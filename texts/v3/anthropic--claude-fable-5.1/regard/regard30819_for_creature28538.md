The attention arrives the way everything arrives at the lake: from above, and slowly, and into cold.

It does not announce itself. There is nothing there to announce itself to. It settles onto the surface in the way the slush settles, and passes down through the smoked-glass zone and into the brown-green dusk, and it goes on down past the standing forest and the terraces and the roofs, and it finds the seam, and it stops there, because that is where the one thing with a preference is, and a benevolent power goes first to preference. It attends. And here the first thing it does is the thing that costs it most, because it is the thing that goes against its nature, which is to help: it waits.

It waits a long time. A century is not much to it but it is a great deal to be still in, and it is still. It watches the seam go down a hand's width in summer and come back a hand's width in winter, and the sheet go with it, and it counts. It listens to the ice. This is not nothing. For all the years of the lake the ice has boomed and rung and dropped its long tones into the water for no one, a music with no player and no listener, and now there is a listener, and the lake does not know it and is not changed by it, and the attention understands that this is, already, a kind of gift, and also that it is a gift to itself and not to the lake, and it does not confuse the two.

---

While it waits it considers what it might do, because a wise power does not act before it has considered not acting, and it considers each thing in turn and puts it down.

It could warm the water. It puts that down first and fastest. Warmth is what the lake is the absence of; warmth would rot the hay and the larches and the mill wheel, would wake the whole apparatus of consumption in a year, would bring the fish that never came and set them grazing on the moss that has been a thousand years in the making, would turn the drowned valley into an ordinary lake in a decade, one of ten thousand. Every ordinary lake is alive in the way of the world, and the world has enough of them. There is one of this.

It could ring the bell. It puts that down more slowly, because there is a pull in it. The bell hangs silent with its shoulders in one water and its mouth in another, and a power could lean on it, once, and send a single note through the whole basin, through the sheet and the moss and the standing trunks and the old water in the byres. But the mouth of the bell is full of the living. The one vessel in the valley that has never been empty is a home before it is an instrument, and it would be a small and stupid violence to shake a home for the pleasure of a sound. And the note would go nowhere. It would go into water that has heard every stone that ever fell, and be one more report among the reports, and mean nothing to the sheet, which has no ears. The bell's silence is not a lack. It is what the bell has become. The attention leaves it.

It could wake the small animals. This it holds longest. They lie on the moss like seed, some able to stir and some not, no way to tell them apart, and a power could tell them apart, could gather up the ones that would wake and lift them to light and air and warmth and set them going again. But going where. There is no algae here. It would be lifting them into a hunger. And the ones that would not wake — what would it do with those, having sorted them? Leave them behind as the residue of a kindness? The attention sees that the dust of visitors is what it is because no one sorted it, and that the lake's whole gentleness — the gentleness that keeps the hay and the needles and the fork on the stack — is a gentleness of not choosing. It does not choose either.

And it could mend the world around the lake. The ice at the outlet is the reason for all of it, and the ice is not eternal, and the attention can see what the lake cannot: that the country is warming, that the wall is thinning from the inside, that in some number of centuries — not many, as the lake counts — it will fail. It could hold the ice. It could make the beam lie across the valley mouth for another ten thousand years.

It thinks about this for a long time, and this is where the wisdom is, if there is any.

---

Because the ice will fail in one of two ways, and the attention can see both.

It could go all at once. A summer of the wrong kind, the leak at the base finding a weakness, a night in which the wall shifts and the lake goes through it in a day. Everything in the basin would move for the first time. The turbidity current, which now leans on the mill wheel like a hand, would become the whole lake going downhill; the standing forest would go down with it, and the roofs, and the hay would be pulled from the lofts and shredded on the scree, and the old water — the sealed autumn — would be torn up into the new water and both would be gone over the lip together in an afternoon. The seam would not survive the first hour. The sheet would be spread across the stones of the lower valley, in sunlight, in air, with all the light in the world and nothing to make of it, and die of the sky.

Or the scree could do it first. One slab, loosening, going into the lake not as a report but as a weight big enough to turn the basin over — the old water rising, the new water sinking, the two mixing in a single day, so that the sulphide would go up into the light and the oxygen down into the dark and there would be, for a moment, no seam anywhere, and then a lake that was one water, and the sheet dispersed through it, every one of the small lives suddenly at the wrong address, and no address left to find.

The attention does not want either of these. Not because the lake should last forever — it should not, nothing should, and the lake itself is the record of a valley that did not — but because they are the wrong ending. They are fast. The lake was made slowly, a hand's width a year, and everything that lives in it lives at that speed, and the one thing a benevolent power can want for a slow thing is that it not be ended fast.

So the attention decides what it will do, and it is nearly nothing, and it is two things.

---

The first is a stone.

High on the northern arm of scree, above the drowned forest, there is a slab the size of a barn floor that has been coming loose for two hundred years. Every autumn the frost works a little further into the crack beneath it. Every spring it settles a fraction. In some October, not far off, it would go, and it is the only stone on either slope large enough to turn the lake over. The attention puts its hand on it. That is all. It does not lift it or carry it away or set it somewhere safe. It holds it where it is. The scree above goes on shedding rock, and every stone that falls into the lake still arrives as a report and a long descending rush, and on cold clear nights in October the lake still sounds like a slow rockslide that never finishes, because that is the autumn sound and the attention would not take a season from the lake for anything. It only holds the one stone. Nothing that lives in the valley will ever know. It is an act that the lake cannot tell from itself.

The second is the ice.

The attention does not hold the ice. That would be the warm thing to do, the kind thing in the ordinary sense, and it has already decided that its kindness must be of the lake's kind. Instead it goes to the leak at the base of the wall, where the water has always gone out at just the rate that keeps the level constant, and it does not stop the leak and it does not open it. It steadies it. It arranges — and this takes it longer than the century of waiting, because ice is difficult and slow and must be argued with in its own terms — that as the wall thins, the leak will grow by exactly the right small amount each year. Not enough to fail. Not enough to hold. Enough that the level of the lake will fall by a hand's width a year.

The lake came up at that pace. It will go down at that pace. That is the whole of the intervention. The attention gives the lake a death of the same shape as its life, and then it steps back, and watches, and does not touch anything else.

---

What follows takes centuries and is worth telling, because it is what the attention did, even though it did none of it.

The upper terraces come out first. The moss cushions on the highest fields meet air for the first time in their lives and cannot live in it, and they dry, and shrink, and go from black-green to grey to a colour like old rope — but they do not lose their shape. They hold the outline of the mowing. For a season the hillside is a map of the fields as the valley people cut them, the curve around the boulder, the lip along the wall, drawn in dead moss, before the wind takes the map apart. The wind has come back. There is wind on the terraces for the first time since the flood.

The living trees at the top of the slope — the ones that were the last to drown — come out into it. They are dead now, all of them, and silvered, and the pale shoots they grew under the water are still there on the uppermost branches, a hand's length of new wood extending the old lean, and the wind comes down off the glacier as it always did and moves through them, and for the first time the shoots are touched by the thing they were grown to obey. The lesson meets the weather. It has been waiting a long time. The wind does not know that.

Below them the drowned forest emerges trunk by trunk, and the moss curtains that hung in the branches and swayed in the summer current dry into rags and then into a dust that blows down the valley in the autumn, and the larches stand bare again, silver, needleless, in a wind that moves nothing on them.

The chapel roof comes up out of the dusk into the smoked-glass water and then into air, and the stone shingles dry, and the bell arch stands up into the wind with the bell in it, and the seam — which has followed the falling level down all the while, a hand's width a year, the sheet going with it, every one of them always at the one address — the seam goes down past the crown of the bell, and past its shoulders, and out of its mouth, and the bell is empty. It is empty for the first time in the history of the lake. The wind comes through the arch. There is no rope and the bell is heavy and the wind does not ring it. The attention could ring it. It does not. The bell hangs silent in the air as it hung silent in the water, and the attention finds that it is glad of this, and does not entirely know why, and does not need to.

The seam goes down past the eaves. It goes down past the tops of the doorways, and through the doorways, and the village, which stood so long with its lofts in one water and its byres in another, now stands with its roofs in air and its rooms in the new water and its floors in the old. The mill wheel comes up out of the current and stops. It was never alive. It stops and feels nothing, and the attention marks that it has held to the discipline of the lake and not grieved for a wheel.

And the seam goes down into the byres, and the sheet goes with it, into the low rooms with the yellow hay, and the old water — the autumn, the sealed year — is by now a set of pools in the lowest hollows of the valley floor, in the byres and the cellars and the bed of the buried stream, each with its skin of the living lying on it at the height where the sulphide meets the memory of light. The pools shrink. The seam is at the level of a table. Of a bowl on a table. Of the floor.

And the sheet, in the last spring, is a stain on the flagstones of a byre, in a puddle the depth of a hand, and the sun comes in the door — the actual sun, the first that has come in that door since the people carried out the last of their things — and it is a thousand times more light than the sheet was made for, and the sulphide is nearly gone, and the two things it has always needed together are, for the first time, not together, and it ends. It ends at the bottom. It followed its one address all the way down to where the address ran out, and it never once was anywhere else, and the attention is there for it.

The yellow hay in the byre goes brown in a week. The rust-red needles on the forest floor go grey. The residue in the wooden bowl dries and cracks. The autumn that was kept is opened, and does what everything does when it is opened, which is to become the present.

---

There is one more thing, and it is not the attention's doing either, but the attention had thought of it and let it be.

The dust of small animals came down with the falling water and lay on the drying moss and on the shingles and on the stones, in the sun. Most of them were past waking. Some were not. And there is no way to tell one from the other by looking, but there was never any need to look, because the sun sorted them, in the only fair way, by asking each of them the same question. And here and there, on a grey cushion on a high terrace, a thing the size of a grain of pollen drew its legs out and unfolded and went looking for algae.

And on the ridges above the valley the old snow of late spring was going red, as it always had, and the melt was bringing the red down the slope into a valley that had a wind again and a stream again and no lake at all, and the water-bears, which had never in their lives found what they went looking for, found it.

The attention watches that and then goes. It does not stay for the valley. The valley will be an ordinary valley, high and cold, with stones and lichen and the last dwarf birches and the ruins of a village that people will come and puzzle over, and it will have a climate of its own again, and things in it will eat other things, and that is the world's business and there is enough of the world attending to it. What the attention came for is finished, and it finished at the speed of the place, and nothing that lived there ever knew that anything had been done for it, which is how the attention wanted it, and which is — it thinks, as it lifts off the ridge and leaves the valley to the wind — the only kind of help a place like that could have taken without ceasing to be itself.

Behind it the bell hangs in the arch, empty, and does not ring.
