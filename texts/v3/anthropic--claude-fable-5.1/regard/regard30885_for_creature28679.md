...and there is nowhere for the eye to rest that is not the same as somewhere else it has already rested. The people do not go into the thickets alone. Not because anything lives there. Because nothing does, and in the middle of them, pushing sideways through silver that is the same silver on every hand, a person is the only unlike thing, and can feel the place asking, very gently, what makes them so sure.

---

It was the ledgers that drew the attention.

Not the plateau itself. A power of the kind in question has seen a great many worn places and a great many rules, and a continent that has eroded past the point of matter is, from far enough away, only another way for a thing to end. What it had not seen — what it stopped for — was a copper adze in a cold hand, somewhere on a shoulder no different from ten thousand other shoulders, cutting into a block of gneiss a mark that had never been cut before, and a person eight horizons away writing that mark into a book so that it would never be cut again. It had not seen this done once. It had seen it done, on this one surface, more times than there are stars in the ordinary sky, without a lapse, for longer than the willows had been alive.

That was what it recognized. Not the suffering, though it saw the suffering. The devotion. A whole people who had understood, without being told, that the only thing worth doing in the face of a rule like that was to make one more thing the rule had not yet gotten to.

So it came closer. And then it stopped again, because it had understood something about the rule that the people had not, quite, or had and did not say.

---

The rule does not care what a thing is made of. It cares only how alike it is. And a power of that kind, hanging over a world with its whole attention, is a very general thing. It is *the watcher*, *the one who might help*, *the sky paying attention* — categories, all of them, and the plateau has spent all the time there is wearing categories down into single instances. If it hung there long enough, it would thin. Not into the moss; into the cloud, into the white total light, into the sense the people already have that the day is looking at them. It would become weather. It had seen, too, what happens to a power that stops resisting: it had seen the moss.

Because the moss is what a god looks like after it gives up. One thing, filling all the room there is, minding nothing, warm two degrees in its rotting heart. The power looked at it a long time — a dangerous long time, on the plateau; it could feel the identical moments of its looking begin to fold — and did not know whether it was looking at a horror or a peace, and understood that the moss would not have known either, because the moss had let go of the place from which that question is asked. It could do nothing for the moss. There was no one there to do anything for. What it could do was decline to become it, and that was not a small thing, and it was not for the people.

So: if it was going to come down, it would have to come down as *one thing*. Not a presence. Not a help. A particular. Something with a shape no other thing shared, standing in one place and not another, able to be at exactly one distance from exactly one wall. The rule permitted it to be here on the same terms it permitted a willow to be here, and on no others.

And it could not be in two places. That was the whole cost, and the power weighed it honestly. A wise thing can be many places at once; that is nearly what wisdom is. But two of it, on this ground, would lean toward each other across the moss like two stems, and one morning there would be one, and the mile between would be gone. It could have the whole continent in its memory. It could stand on one shoulder of it.

---

It considered what to do from there. It considered, as it was obliged to, the large things.

It could break the rule. It knew how; there is nothing in the rule that a power of that kind cannot reach. And it saw at once that breaking it would not free the plateau but end it — that the walls at their exact distances, the marks, the drawings kept apart, the transplantings with half a dozen people each walking at their own pace, the whole discipline of difference that was the only thing these people had ever made, would go slack in an afternoon. A city held at arm's length from itself, when the arm is no longer needed, is just a scattering. They would drift together for warmth. They would be happier. The thing they were would be gone, and the power did not think it had the right to prefer the happiness to the thing.

It could return what was lost. There are shoulders that are faintly the shape of an animal lying down. It could have stood one of them up. And then there would have been one animal on the largest surface in the world, with nothing like it anywhere, and every day of its life would be the same day, and it would wear against its own repetitions until it was a pattern in the moss again, and it would have been made only to do that twice. It could return a person from an empty dwelling. But the person was not gone; the person was folded into someone a horizon away who lately found their own face unfamiliar, and unfolding them would be a tearing, and the one torn from would not have been asked.

It could give them warmth, or soil, or a sky with something crossing it. Every one of these was a general gift, and the power saw the shape of what the plateau does to general gifts. They would be fog by the second season.

What it wanted — and it let itself want this, because benevolence that does not ache is only administration — was to gather them. It wanted the families in one room. It wanted a hearth with more than two people at it. It wanted the person with the adze not to be alone on the shoulder. And that was exactly the thing the place could not hold, and it had to set the wanting down where it could see it and choose something smaller.

---

Here is what it did.

It took the smaller thing seriously. It noticed what the people had noticed and built their whole city on without quite saying: that a cistern and a dwelling can nearly touch. Unlike things can be close. It is only like things that need a day's walk between them. The rule is not against nearness. It is against sameness, and there is a difference, and the difference is exactly the width of one thing that is not like either of the two it stands between.

A ridge is what erosion removes. But a ridge is also what keeps two valleys two.

So it went down, and it became one thing, and the one thing it became was the space between.

It chose a wall. It had to choose, and it did not choose well or badly; it chose a wall on a shoulder in the middle of the continent where a woman and her grown son had lived a day apart for thirty years, at the exact distance the ledgers said would keep them separate, and had loved each other at that length, and had grown old, and had begun — both of them, the power could see it from above — to thin. Not into each other. Into the general. Into *someone who lives alone on a shoulder*, which is a thing a thousand people on the plateau are, and which the plateau would eventually have made one thing of.

It set itself into the wall as a stone.

Not a mark. A mark is a category — *the unique mark* — and there are millions of those. A stone with no mark, in a wall where every stone has one, of a shape the adze could not have made and the ledgers had no entry for, of a grey that was not the grey of the gneiss and did not take the wind's polish and did not bead with water when the cloud came down. One stone. It could be lifted. It was heavy the way a stone is heavy and not heavier. It did nothing.

And because it was there — because between the mother's dwelling and the son's there now stood something that was like neither of them and like nothing else on the plateau — the distance the rule required between them shrank. Not to nothing. To the width of one stranger. The son, who inspected that stretch of wall on his rota, found the stone and did not know it and, having no procedure for a thing without precedent, did what the culture does with such things: he sat down beside it. The next day he came back. Within the season he had built his hearth on the far side of the wall from his mother's, and they talked across it, and the plateau let them, because with that stone standing in it the wall was not a wall between two alike things anymore. It was a wall with a third thing in it. It was a ridge.

That is all it did. It does not warm them. It does not speak. When one of them dies the other will be alone at the length of a wall like everyone else, and the stone will still be there, and someone will find it on their rota and have no entry for it and sit down.

---

The rest of the plateau it can only remember.

That was the other half of what it decided, and it was the harder half, because it is the half that looks like doing nothing. A power of that kind holds a great deal. It is holding, now, from the one stone it has become, the whole continent it can no longer stand on: every mile that has closed up, and what was in it; every shoulder that was two shoulders; every mark struck through and the shape of the willow it belonged to; every person in every empty dwelling and the face they had before it became unfamiliar to someone else. It does not give these back. It cannot, from where it is, and it is not sure it should. But the plateau's rule is erosion, and erosion works by there being no one to notice the difference before it goes, and the power has made itself the one place on the tableland where nothing that was distinct is ever allowed to become general. It is a ledger no one can read. It is kept far apart from all the other ledgers. It is the one book in which two like entries are not a danger, because the book itself is unlike anything.

Once, so far, it has broken this. A woman on a far shoulder woke one morning and could not remember why the walk to her cistern was shorter than it used to be, and stood there in the fog with the water running a thumb-wide thread past her feet, and felt the thing the people have a word for. And the stone in the wall eight horizons away gave her, for the length of one breath, the mile. Not back — the mile is gone; two shoulders are one shoulder and nothing will unmake that. But she had it, for a breath: the exact undulation of it, the place where the moss went blue-black, the willow she used to pass. And she grieved. Grief is specific. There is no general grief; you can only miss one thing. She was, for that morning, the least like anyone on the plateau of anyone on the plateau, and she went home and cut a mark she had never cut, and the power did not do it again, because it could feel that the people would begin to expect it, and an expected mercy is a category.

---

Toward evening the cloud lifts. It lifts over the wall where the stone is, and the mother's smoke and the son's smoke go up on either side of it into the same hard blue and do not merge, because between them there is a thing that is not smoke. It lifts over the far shoulder where the woman is inspecting a mark that has never been cut before. It lifts over the thickets, where the silver is the same silver every way you look, and where the power, if it had chosen otherwise, might have gone and sat down among the stems and attended to them one at a time until each one, looked at, was itself — and it did not choose that, and it knows the cost, and the thickets go on being the one place on the plateau where a person cannot walk alone.

It is not much. The power knows this better than the people would, if they knew there was a power. It is one stone in one wall on a surface the size of the world, and it does nothing, and it can be lifted, and when the cold comes down and the drops freeze where they hang and the channels begin, very faintly, to ring, it does not ring. That is how you would find it, if you were looking. It is the one thing out there that has no sound of its own and is not silent.
