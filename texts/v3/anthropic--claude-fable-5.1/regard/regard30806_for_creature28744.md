The power comes the way the water comes, from above and through the cloud, and for a while it does what you did: it stands on the shine and counts the green places and listens to the drip. It is not in a hurry. It has the advantage over you that it can wait on the plateau's schedule without becoming one of the people in the doorways, and so it does the thing you could not do, which is stay long enough to see the count move.

It moves. A woman on the third terrace is carried up the stair by two others and out across the flow to the garden that belongs to her groove, and a hollow is opened in the silt with the edge of a flat stone, and she is put down in it and the gray paste pressed back over her, and wetted from the nearest pool, and the two who carried her go back to their doors. Some years later — the power does not count years; the cloud dims and brightens and it lets that be time — a very small person appears in a doorway on the fifth terrace and is given a blade too big for its hand and sits with it. The count holds because it is met. The gardens are graves. The ferns that are the town's one extravagance grow out of the town's own dead, and the town knows this and there is nothing dark in it for them; it is where the silt goes.

So these are not the ones who came up. The ones who came up are in the gardens, and their children's children tend the grooves, and the stories about the climb are the length they are because each teller had a little less below to remember. The stories will keep shortening. The power can see the place where they end: a people who know that the water comes from above and goes below, and know nothing of either, and are content, and are complete.

---

The first temptation is to leave it exactly so, and the power sits with this for a long time, because it is a good temptation and deserves to be looked at properly.

There is no suffering here. That has to be said plainly. No one is hungry, no one is cold, no one is afraid, no one is struck. They are attentive and unhurried and they work, and their work matters and they know it matters, and they are not lonely in any way they could name. The plateau is beautiful, and the people are of it, and a power that reached in and rearranged it because the picture looked incomplete from outside would be doing what every clumsy benefactor does, which is to confuse its own discomfort with their need. They have not asked. They do not have the words to ask. The absence of wings is a lack only to something that has wings.

And yet the power notices what it is feeling as it considers this, and what it is feeling is the pleasure of a viewer. *A stage set after the performance, still lit.* It does not want the lights touched. And it recognizes that as a reason having to do with itself and not with them, and puts it down.

The second temptation is the opposite one, and is easier to refuse. Lift the cloud. Send a wind across the flow. Bring soil and join the gardens, bring birds and insects and fish for the cisterns, bring the whole thick pressing world that is a kilometer below and has never been let up. The power can see exactly what this would do. It would break every channel. Silt in a season; sedge nested in and the roofs failing; the water clouded, which they have no idea of; the doors moving in a wind, which they have no idea of; and forty people who have never seen two things happen at once standing in the middle of everything happening. It would not be a gift. It would be the disaster they already have a word for, a channel left alone, done to the whole plateau at once.

Between these two the power looks for what is actually true about the place, and finds two things.

The first is that the plateau is young. This is the fact the narrator kept reaching for and the people cannot know. The rock is still faintly warm, and it is cooling. The fractures the water follows were made by that cooling and they are not finished; a hundred meters down the stone is still settling, and one day a crack will open that was not there and the water will take it, and a groove on the second terrace will go dry, and no one will have a story for that. The cloud sits where it sits because of the height and the warmth together, and the warmth is leaving. In some number of centuries the plateau will be under weather instead of inside it, and there will be a morning when the white thins and something falls on the moss that the moss has never felt, which is direct sun, and it will begin to brown at the edges. None of this is the power's doing. It is what the place is. The town has arranged itself around a stillness that is real but not permanent, and it has done so with such care that it has no practice at all in anything else. It has never received a change it did not make. When the large one comes, the town will meet it having never met a small one.

The second thing is what the town does with what arrives. Your boot-grit. A few grains of foreign soil on a swept step, and the response was not to refuse it or to wonder at it but to take it up in a fern leaf and carry it out across the flow and press it into a garden. That is the town's whole relationship to the outside: everything from below goes into the soil. It is a practice of receiving. They have never refused anything, because nothing has ever come, but the shape of what they do with the unexpected is already made, and it is generous. And the power sees, too, why nothing has come. Not because the plateau is hostile — the pools would take an egg, the sedge would take a nest — but because it is sealed. No wind crosses the flow. The cloud is a lid. Below it the world is crowded to every edge with things that travel on wing and silk and seed, and every one of them, for as long as the town has stood, has been turned back by the same warm stillness that makes the place what it is. The plateau has not chosen to be alone. It has not been reached.

So the power decides. It will not change the place. It will let one thing through.

---

It is a bird, because it has to be. The narrator said it: *nothing in the stories has wings.* The power does not make the bird. There are ten thousand of them below the cloud, and on any afternoon a hundred of them are lost, blown up the flanks of the mountain by weather the plateau never feels and turned back at the white. The power takes one of these — a small one, gray-brown, a ground-feeder of no distinction, tired — and does not push it. It only does not turn it back. It lets the stillness thin for the length of time it takes a tired bird to climb through a hundred meters of warm cloud, and then it lets the stillness close again.

The bird comes out of the white over the fourth terrace and does not know where it is. It sees a roof. It lands on it.

What follows, the power watches without touching.

The man inside the door of that house has been sitting an arm's length back in the dark for some hours, listening to the thread of water fall from the notch above into his basin, which is the sound he has listened to all his life. Above his head, through the sedge thatch, there is a sound that is not that. It is small and dry and it moves. Nothing here moves. He does not get up for a while, because nothing here gets up quickly, but he does get up, and he comes out onto the terrace and turns and looks at his roof, and there is a shape on it that is not the color of anything.

It is the color of dry things. He has never seen the color of dry things. It is round in a way the ferns are not round and it turns its head, and the head has an eye in it, and the eye looks at him. Then it makes a sound — a single note, thin and clear, something like the note the water makes falling into a basin but higher and with a shape to it, beginning and ending — and he understands, not in words, that he is being addressed by something that is not water and not a person.

He does the only thing he has. He steps to the notch at the back of his terrace and lays his hand across it, and below him, on the fifth terrace and the sixth, the small clear notes stop.

*The water has changed.*

The power watches the doors. On the fifth terrace a woman comes out, and looks at her dry basin, and looks up. On the sixth a child comes out with a blade in its hand. The man lifts his hand and lays it again, and again, the pattern for *come up*, and they come up, on the stairs cut into the black rock, unhurried, and then they see where he is looking and they stop being unhurried and simply stop. Above him on the third terrace, where the thread never faltered, someone has heard the sixth terrace go silent and then the fifth and has come to the edge to see why, and looks down, and sees not the water but the roof, and goes back to their own notch and lays a hand across it, so that the second terrace hears, and the first, and the message for the first time in the town's life goes uphill, carried not by water but by the fact of people leaving their doors.

The power has counted forty and never seen two together. Now there are forty on the terraces, in the squares, on the stairs, and every one of them is looking at the same thing. The gardens have joined. Not with soil. With this.

The bird is not much. It hops along the ridge of the thatch and puts its head down into the sedge, looking for what birds look for in grass, and there is nothing, because nothing lives in the sedge, and it comes up with a fiber in its beak and drops it, and looks around at the wet black shine and the wet gray sky and the forty wet shapes standing very still, and it does not understand the place any better than the place understands it. It calls again. The cloud takes the call at an arm's length, the way it takes everything, so that only the man nearest the roof truly hears the shape of it, and the rest see the beak open and hear a trace of something and understand there was more.

It stays a while. It drinks from a pool, which they will talk about for a long time — that it went to the water and the water took it, that it belonged to the water somehow and had come out of it, upward, the way nothing does. It does not eat, because there is nothing here to eat, and it is already tired, and the power knew this when it chose a tired one and did not choose otherwise. Late in the dimming it goes back to the roof and folds itself and does not move again.

In the brightening the man climbs to it. It is light in his hand. Its feathers are the first thing he has ever touched that took no water from his skin. He carries it down the terrace and across the flow to the garden that is his groove's garden and his grandmother's grave, and he opens the silt with the edge of a flat stone, and puts it down, and presses the gray paste over it, and wets it from the nearest pool.

---

That is all the power does. It does not send a second bird. It does not lift the cloud a day early or hold it a day late. When the rock cools enough and the fracture opens a hundred meters down and the groove on the second terrace goes dry, that will happen when it happens, and the power will not be there to soften it, and it will be hard. But it will not be the first time the water changed. There will be a story by then — shortening, like the others, worn to its shape by telling in doorways — about the morning something came up out of the white that was not a person and not the water, and sat on a roof, and spoke once, and was given to a garden. The story will have wings in it. It will say that things can arrive. It will say that the whole town once stood in the squares together and looked up at one thing, and that this was possible, and that no one was harmed by it. When the sun first falls on the moss, someone will remember that the world has come up to them before and been received, and will go out with a fern leaf to see what it has brought this time.

The power withdraws. It leaves the stillness as it found it, warm and sealed and satisfied with itself, the pools at the brim, the thread falling from notch to basin in the note it has always made. In a garden on the fourth terrace's stretch of flow, a fern grows a millimeter it would not otherwise have grown. The drip goes on, one bead letting go from the underside of a ledge at its own interval, and there is someone hearing it who has heard it every day of their life — and who knows now, in a way they did not know before, that it is not the only thing that can come.
