The attention comes first from above, because that is where attention of that kind usually comes from. From the rim road at dusk: a field of coloured points hung in the dark at seven heights, some of them slowly rising. A power that can see the way this one sees does not need the switchbacks. It can hold the whole basin at once — every cable under load, every tilt gauge, the mineral in the ground and the mineral in the moss and the mineral in the grey pads of the winch hands' palms, the same mineral, moving between them.

It is asked to find the being who lives here. It looks, in the way it looks, and finds three things, and then does what the people did long ago without discussion and stops counting.

Its first impulses are the impulses of the rim. A benevolent power seeing a city set in slow stone wants, before anything else, to stop the stone. To still the ground, or slow it. To send the moss elsewhere, to dry the air a degree so the cable stops rotting, to answer the assay office once and for all so the wall of labels can come down. To tell someone about the sumps before the next one tilts and drains a district. These are good impulses. It has them the way you have the impulse to catch something falling. And it holds each one for a moment and puts it down, not because it has thought them through — it has not yet, it will — but because it has watched the city hold still for twenty minutes in the western light and it understands that it has been looking at people who know something it does not, and that the first act of wisdom here is not to speak first.

So it comes down the switchbacks.

*

It could have arrived any other way and it arrives the way a stranger arrives, on foot, in wool, and it is sick for a week. This is not pretence. A thing that has spent its existence on the rim of the world in the largest sense — on the honest geology of things that stay put — does not know how to stand on ground that is choosing its shape, and its inner ear, or what it has in place of one, does not know what to do with the long slow swing of a hanging room. It lies in a rented bed in the Long Store under a bottle-green lamp and the floor goes up a handspan under it in the night and it feels its whole understanding of the world go two meters and stop in the moss. The people who own the room bring it broth and watch with polite interest.

When the sickness passes a woman puts two fingers on its wrist and says *you've found your hang.*

The next thing it learns is that it has no voice. It had one; it had a great one, the kind that is heard across distances. It opens its mouth in the market to say something ordinary and hears the sound leave and die in the fur on the nearest girder, and the look that crosses its face is the one the children have, the small private betrayal, and a winch hand nearby sees it and looks away, kindly, the way you look away from someone who has just learned something about themselves.

So it leans in. It learns hand-talk in a month, the lamps in a week. It learns that a palm flat on the back means *go on ahead* and that the steering hands in the market crowd are not intrusion but rigging. It lets itself be steered. This is the hardest thing it has done in a long time, and it notices that.

*

It spends years in the basin. This is not long, by its measure, and not long by the basin's either, where nothing is old.

It takes work because everyone does. It goes into the girders with the winch crews and cuts the braid free with a heated knife and learns to thank the moss under its breath, first as a form and then as something else. It learns to read a cable through its hands. It learns that heat is expensive and motion makes it and that you stand still after a lift with the heat in your shoulders not to rest but to listen. It has not been cold before. It becomes cold. It enters into the condition. Its palms split and heal and split and heal and go flat and grey and faintly translucent at the edges, and someone taps one with a fingernail to see if it has clouded yet, and it is a long moment before it understands that it has been made a joke about, and that this is an honour.

All this time it is considering.

It considers stilling the ground, and follows that down: a city on a floor that has stopped, winches idle, the moss with nothing new to climb, the assay office with a final answer and nothing to do, the winch hands' palms healing smooth, the children's game with no one to win it. A rim town, in a generation. A rim town with a swallowed district on its southern edge that nobody understood anymore. It puts that down.

It considers the moss — thinning it, slowing it, sparing the cable. And it follows that down too, and finds that you cannot thin the moss without loudening the basin, and you cannot louden the basin without a public voice appearing in it, and someone standing on something to be heard, and decisions that have to be argued for. It has seen what those make. It puts that down.

It considers the ground. Not what to do about it — what it is. Because the power can see, in the way it sees, something the assay office cannot dissolve in acid and the winch hands can only feel through the moss: the ground's shape. Not the shape of the blisters. The shape of the thing the blisters are the surface of.

And here it goes carefully, because it has learned to.

It does not think the ground thinks. That was a rim idea and it has stopped having rim ideas. What it can see is closer to what the old winch hands say. The ground moves the way a sleeper moves. It has been moving toward things and away from things without intention for longer than the settlement, and what it has been moving toward — this is what the power can see and the people can feel — is a shape that can hold what has been put into it. The wool-wrapped dead. The clear bells that fall from tilt gauges when a building is winched too fast. The mineral the moss draws up and drops back down. The heat from the foundry, which it has been taking in, slowly, the way a sleeper takes in the warmth of whoever lies beside them, and turning over toward. The ground is not finishing in the sense of ending. It is finishing in the sense that a face finishes, over a life, into the face of the person who lived it.

The power can see the shape. It cannot see the dream. Nobody can see a dream from outside, not even a thing like this; you see the sleeper's face and you guess.

It could tell them this much. It leans toward the telling, several times.

And then it watches the woman from the Dun Hall with her hand on the key, in the twenty minutes of western light, and it understands that they know. Not the way the assay office would want to know. They know it the way you know which corner of a room is low. To say it aloud would carry two meters and change nothing, or carry two meters and change everything — turn a thing they stand on into a thing they have been told about, a foundation idea, a rim idea, something to argue for. A decision that has to be argued for is already wrong. It puts that down.

*

It does go to the assay office, once. It cannot help this; benevolence has a weakness for the honest attempt.

It finds the clerk who numbers rather than names, at her drawers, and it leans in, and puts its hand on her arm, and says the ground's name into her ear — the true one, the twelfth, the one that accounts for the mineral and the moss and the heat and the dead. She writes it down. She files it with the day and the hour and a note of what it did in the flame. She thanks it, in that low warm way.

A month later the ground has come up under the eastern warehouses in a composition nobody has recorded and the name is wrong. Not retired. Wrong. The clerk files the new sample by the day and the hour and does not look at the power, and the power stands in the hall of drawers and understands that it did not know either; that it knew a name for the ground at that day and that hour, and the ground is not finished, and a name for an unfinished thing is a note of what it did in the flame.

It thinks that this is the first honest thing it has done here. It stops trying to be the office's answer.

*

What it does, in the end, is small, and most of it nobody notices, and that is the measure of its rightness.

When a sump tilts under the western hangs and begins to drain, the power is standing in the horsetail at the edge of it in the night, and it lays a hand flat on the moss, and it does not still the ground — it has put that down for good — but it does what a winch hand does with a cable that has gone a hair off true. It listens through its palm until it feels where the dream is thinnest, and it leans. That is all. The sump drains. Forty meters east, in a hollow between two old blisters, the moss goes dark and cold and wrings itself out into a pool that was not there before, and by morning the horsetail has found it. The district draws water. Someone says the ground dreamed kindly this week. Nobody says anything else. It does this three more times over the years and never speaks of it and it is, as far as it can tell, the one thing it has ever done that came true across everyone without being argued for, because it was done in the basin's own story and left no mark that was not already the ground's.

When there is a laying-on it stands in the ring at arm's length with a lamp — not a hang colour, it has no hang colour, a plain white — and holds it steady while the body goes down on the tightening moss and the family stands and the ground decides. It goes to every one it can. It is not asked to and not thanked and after a while its lamp is expected, and a place is left in the ring for it.

It plays the children's game once, lying flat on the moss in the dark with one cheek down, and it is the last to say *there*, and it is wrong about the when, and it is beaten by a girl of nine who tells it, with two fingers on its wrist, that it thinks too much and should feel instead.

*

And there is one thing more, and the power comes to it slowly, the way everything is come to here.

It has been considering what a benevolent power can give to a thing that is not finished. It has ruled out finishing it. It has ruled out telling it what it is. It has ruled out warmth, because heat is the one thing the ground rises around and no one knows why and the foundry crews will not say. It has ruled out standing on the rim and watching, which is where it began and which it now understands was never neutral either — that to be seen from above by a thing that could still you is its own kind of weight.

What is left is what the people do. They put themselves in. Not all at once. A little at a time, over years. The dead, in wool. The grey of the palms. The thanks under the breath. They do not stand outside the ground and act on it. They are, collectively and without discussion, part of what it is becoming, and that is why it is fine. That is what fine means here. It is not resignation. It is the only benevolence that does not carry more than two meters and does not need to.

The power decides, then — or something in it comes level, across all of it at once, and afterward it is simply the case — that it will let the ground dream it.

It has to be cold first. That takes longer than anything. Whatever it is made of runs warm at the core the way anything does that has been a light on a rim, and it stands still after lifts for longer and longer, eyes half-shut, feeling the heat go out of its shoulders into the damp, and waits. It does not hurry. Hurry makes heat. There is a winter and another and the woman from the Long Store who first told it it had found its hang is laid on the rise and it holds its white lamp and stands in the ring and by the next winter it can put its palm on a fresh blister and the moss under it does not darken.

Then the winch hands watch the moss for a coming rise, and in the night it walks out to the tightening place, in the wool it has worn for years, and lies down on it. It is not dying. It does not know how. But it has learned to hold still, and the ground has learned it — has turned toward it, a little, the way a sleeper turns — and the moss draws in, and the cold comes up through the wool, and somewhere in the small hours the ground comes up under it and around it with a soft crack like knuckles, and the moss has begun already to grow over.

It does not leave a dark vein. That is for the dead, and it was not that. Where it went in the stone clouds a shade paler than the ground around, and takes the light a little differently in the twenty minutes of the west, and the cutters, when their chisels find it, stop and move the gang, and nothing is written down.

*

Nobody in the basin knows that a power came. This is how it would have wanted it, if wanting is the word.

But the ground is not finished, and what goes into an unfinished thing is in the finishing. And there are things that are slightly otherwise in the basin now, small enough that only the old winch hands notice and they do not say. The sumps drain less. When they drain there is another. A blister that comes up under a hang comes up a handspan slower than it used to, as if the ground had learned to feel the building above it and lean the other way — as if something in the dream had a winch hand's palms. The children's game is harder to win; the ground, they complain, has stopped being sure about *when*, and thinks too much.

And in the southern district, at dusk, a woman on the second floor of the Dun Hall with a brass key on a cord around her neck puts her hand on a door that is set a meter deep in stone, as she has done every evening for years, and feels — she will not say this, there is no way to say it that would carry — that the stone on the other side is not cold. Not warm. Nothing here is warm. But not cold, either, in the way it was. Something like the temperature of a hand that has been held.

Then the light goes, and a bell taps against a cup somewhere, and she leans in to whoever is nearest, and goes back to moving.
