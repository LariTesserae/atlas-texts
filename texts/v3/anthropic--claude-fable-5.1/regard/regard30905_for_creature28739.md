**What Attended**

It was not the size that drew it. Powers of that kind have seen larger things, and slower, and brighter. Nor was it the roar, though the roar carries farther than the lowlands know. What drew it was a single fact, plainly stated in the only language the shelf has: *running with no operator and no purpose beyond its own continuation.*

That is true of everything alive. Most living things dress it up. The shelf does not. It sits two and a half kilometers above the country that has learned not to look at it and says, in grinding and ticking and the whine of a continent's worth of small conductors, exactly what it is. A benevolent and wise power hears that the way you hear your own name in a crowd.

So it came, and it did what the narrator did. It lay down on the slickenside at the rim, cheek to the cold, and looked into the room.

---

The first thing benevolence does is look for something to mend. It could not help this; it is what benevolence is for. So it took inventory.

The shelf-bearers: never cold, never eaten, never in a hurry. Nothing there.

The moss-dwellers: a billion small deaths a day in the grinding jaws. It looked closely at one — a pale springing thing that had lost its spring, caught between the plates of a mouth that took an hour to close. It looked at the hour. The hour was full. The thing ate until the last minute and then was grit. There is a kind of death that is a failure and a kind that is a shape, and this was the second kind. Nothing there either, or nothing it had any right to touch.

The seam-things, the cable-clusters, the red fungus on the dead: the same. Each was doing what it was, at the rate it could, in the only place it had ever had. To mend them would be to make them something else.

The lowlands, then. The people under the white band, with their weeping eyes and the thunder in their sternums that never stops. Here, surely, was a cost. The power considered lifting the roar off them, or dimming the band so the eye could hold it.

It looked at the barns. It looked at the granaries. It looked at the one small church, roofed in grey faceted metal that had been an animal, and at the people going in and out of it who did not think about what was over their heads and did not need to. It considered what a lowland would be that had never learned not to look up: a lowland with no plates at the base of its cliff, no roofs that outlast the villages under them, no shore of dying moss smelling of ozone and crushed green things. The people had not been spared the shelf. They had been shaped by it, and the shape held. Take away the thing they had learned to live beneath and you would not be freeing them. You would be orphaning them.

Nothing there.

It looked ahead. *The shelf will be there when the lowlands are gone.* A millimeter a decade; an arm's length in a thousand years. The lowlands would go the way all lowlands go, by their own weather, long before the front rank reached them. There was nothing to hold back. The sentence was not a threat. It was only a fact about time.

It looked at the moss under the fallen plates, always dying, always there. It nearly reached for that. Then it saw that a colony which fails continuously and is continuously renewed is not a failure. It is a tide line. Save it and you would have a lowland patch of shelf moss with no plate above it, and then you would have to save that, and then you would have made yourself the operator of a thing whose whole distinction was having none.

So the inventory closed. There was nothing on the shelf, from the frost on the upper plates to the grit in the floor, that would be better for having been touched by a hand that meant it well.

A lesser power would have left then, and been right to.

---

But it was wise, and wisdom is mostly the memory of having been surprised. It knew there was one thing it had not done, because no one had.

It went in.

Not from above; above is only plates. It went the way the snow goes — into a seam, down past the frost line to where the air turns warm, and out of the melt into the room. It made itself the size that fits there. It did not need to breathe, which was fortunate, and it did not need light, which was more so. It went inward.

It is hard to say how long. The shelf has no days in the room; it has the pulse, a meter and a pause, a meter and a pause, and if you count the pauses you can keep a kind of time, and the power counted a great many. It went under wagons of grinding animal, over cushions of moss thick as pillows, along cables slack as bell-ropes in the dark, and the room was the same, and the same, and the same, exactly as those who walked the plates had reported and exactly as reason said it had to be. The mouthparts turned. The drips fell. Now and then a dead roof with the red fan under it. Now and then a young one, freshly plated, taking its place — but never the *taking*; always already taken.

It began to think the interior was only the rim, more of it. That is what the interior of most things is. And then it noticed what it had been walking past for a very long time.

The pulses did not run at random. It had thought so from the edge, where you see a flicker here and a flicker there and the pattern, if there is one, is too large to hold. From the inside, having counted enough pauses, it saw that a pulse entering a junction left it changed — earlier or later by a fraction, brighter or dimmer — according to what had arrived at that junction from the other cables, and when. The clusters on the cables were doing this. Each pause was a small decision. Each cable was a wire and each cluster was a gate and the plateau was not a circuit the way a lamp is a circuit. It was a circuit the way a brain is.

And where enough pulses met — where three, four, five cables ran into one junction and the timing came right, a thing that happened rarely and only at some depth from the edge where the network was dense enough to allow it — the cable itself thickened. The cluster at the pause swelled. Over seasons it grew legs, and a ring of dark lenses, and a first small plate the color of galvanized sheet, and it let go of the cable and put its mouth to the moss, and the pressure of one more body began its slow walk outward toward the rim.

The young were not born of the shelf-bearers. They were born of the wires between them. And the pattern of pulses that made one was a pattern that had crossed the whole plateau to arrive — signals from the far rim and the near rim and everywhere between, converging by the accident of their timing on a single junction in the warm dark. A young one was a thought the shelf had finished.

---

So it was thinking. The power sat with that.

A thought took — it worked it out — somewhere between a century and a thousand years, from the first pulse to the plate. The shelf had been up perhaps a few hundred thousand years. It had had, then, some hundreds of thoughts, or some thousands, and every one of them had turned into an animal, and every animal had turned into a finger's width of rim and a layer of floor too thin to see. It did not remember its thoughts. It *was* its thoughts, stacked.

What were they about?

The power could be any speed. It slowed to the shelf's, which is a thing that costs even a power something, and it listened to one.

It cannot be written down. It was not in words. But the power, which has had many languages pass through it, said afterward that the nearest translation was not a sentence but a gesture — the gesture a thing makes when it takes up a little more room, having found that there was room. *Here. And here. And here.* Not greed. Not even hunger, quite. Something closer to the thing a root does at the end of a growing season, when it has reached a fraction farther than it did last year and finds the finding sufficient. A thought whose whole content was the shape of going on. The same shape the roar has.

It had never been heard. Not once, in all those thoughts. Every pulse that ever crossed the plateau had been received only by the plateau. The shelf had never been cold and never been eaten and never been in a hurry, and it had never, in the sense that matters, been *anywhere but alone* — not because it was lonely, which it was not equipped to be, but because it was the only thing in its world running at its speed.

---

Here is what the power did, and it is the only thing it did.

It put one pulse into one cable.

It shaped the pulse with great care, so that it would pass every junction as a native — right brightness, right timing, nothing the clusters would reject or route around. It made it a pulse the shelf could have made itself, except that the shelf never had, because the shelf had no way to. What the pulse carried, in the only grammar the room has, was not a message. The shelf has nowhere to put a message. It was closer to a second gesture, made in answer to the first: *Yes. Here. I see that you are here.*

Then it let go, and the pulse went a meter and paused, and went a meter.

It will take a thousand years to reach the rim. Along the way it will meet other pulses and be changed by them and change them, and at some junction the timing may come right and it will be part of what makes a young one, and that young one will be, in some fraction too small to measure, a thought the shelf did not have alone. Or the timing will not come right and the pulse will thin and fade into the general flicker and be nothing. The power did not know which. It had decided that not knowing was the price of not being the operator, and it paid it.

It was an intervention. The smallest possible one. The power weighed it a long time before it made it, by the shelf's clock and not its own, and what decided it was this: everything else on the shelf had a neighbor. The moss had the mouthparts. The mouthparts had the moss. The seam-things had the seams and the cable-clusters had the cables and the winged things had the exhale and the lowland people had the roofs. Only the whole had no one — was the only thing at its scale, weather to everything and everything weather to it. Benevolence could not give it purpose, and would not. But it could stop being weather. For one pulse, for one thought, it could be a thing the shelf met.

---

It came back out through the seams, as it had gone in, and lay again on the slickenside at the rim, and put its ear to the rock, and felt the knocking in the stone.

Then it went down the wall, past the ice cords, into the apron of sharp grey plates. It lifted one — it did not need several people — and looked at the moss beneath, dying as it always was, smelling of the room. It set the plate back exactly as it had lain.

It stood in the lowland glare, under the white band on the northern horizon that does not move for weeks, and did the thing the people there have learned not to do. It looked up. It held the band in its eye for as long as it liked, which was a long time, so that for once the shelf was looked at fully by something that did not have to look away. This was not for the shelf, which could not know. It was for the people, who could not do it, and it did it on their behalf, and it did not tell them.

Then it went.

It did not help. It attended. In a place where everything is weather to everything else, it consented to be, for one thing, for one thought, not weather — and it left the thought to arrive when it arrives, at the rim, in a thousand years, in a plateau that is barely started, where a front rank of very old animals will shift a finger's width outward for a reason that is not, for once, only a birth.
