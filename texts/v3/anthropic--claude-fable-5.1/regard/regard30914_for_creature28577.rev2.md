It comes the way everything comes: from above, through the throat, past the choughs.

They don't know what to make of it. Eight of them are hanging on the updraft where the pit's warmth spills into the cold, and they look at it the way they look at everything — with the frank appraisal of creatures who expect to find something worth taking — and there is nothing. No zipper. No bright thing. One of them makes a pass anyway, on principle, and comes away with its bill closed on air, and the others take note, and by the time it is three rungs down the rebar they have lost interest entirely and gone back to watching for amber lights. It does not need the ladder. It uses the ladder, because that is how one enters. That is the first thing it learns about the pit: only one species here has surplus enough for curiosity, and that species spent its curiosity in four seconds and went back to work.

So it stands on the top catwalk in the gray column of noon, with the dust sifting onto it and through it, and because it is wise the first thing it does is nothing, for a long time.

---

It considers the mountain first, because the mountain is the largest thing in the room and the obvious thing to fix.

It could stop the closing. That is not beyond it. It could reach three hundred meters down into the rock and tell the crust to be still, and the seven jacks at the waist would go slack for the first time since they were set, and the props would ring one clear note and hold it forever, and the throat would stop tightening, and the pit would be *saved*. It turns this over. It is careful with it, the way you are careful with a thing that would feel very good to do.

And what it sees is a bubble of andesite with a town of steel inside it and forty people tapping pipe that no longer needs tapping. It sees the warmth go, first — because the warmth is the compression, the vents are the mountain's held breath leaking, and a mountain that has stopped becoming itself is a mountain that has stopped breathing. It sees the orange collar around the big fumarole gray to bone. It sees the crust die and the springtails after it and the spiders after them, and the choughs leave for somewhere with insects, and the pikas in the warm gap that is no longer warm. It sees the pit made permanent and dead in the same stroke.

The mountain is not the enemy. Everything in the pit is a lodger in its breath. The closing is the rent, and the rent is what keeps the lights on.

So it lets the mountain be what it is. This is the first refusal and the largest, and it costs something to make, and it makes it.

---

Then the crew, whom it watches for a long time before it does anything, because they are the ones who would notice.

It walks the tiers behind them and learns the notes. *Tik, tik, tik.* It learns which brace is which by pitch the way they do, and it learns the seven names, and it learns the sound that isn't a ring — the *tunk* — and it comes to understand that the whole grammar of the pit is a grammar of listening, and that the crew have made themselves into ears, and that anything it does here must not give them something else to listen to.

Because it has time, it knows things. It knows that on a certain morning, three weeks out, the second of the seven jacks at the waist will let go while two people are under it swapping a bowed cross-brace. It knows the *tunk* will come a second and a half before the report, and that a second and a half, for two people with a chain hoist and thin air in their lungs, is not enough.

It could tell them. It could put a voice in the pit. It thinks about this the way it thought about the mountain and it sees the same thing: forty people who, having once heard a voice, would listen for the voice thereafter instead of the steel. And the voice would not always be there. And the steel always would.

So when the morning comes it does the one thing it will ever do with the steel. It lets the jack hold true for three seconds longer than the metal can bear. Just that. Three seconds of a note that should already have gone flat, long enough for a woman walking her line on the tier above to hear the wrong pitch she should not yet have been able to hear, and rap it once with her wrench to be sure, and shout — without breath, a bark — and for the two below to step back. Then the jack goes, the branch-crack and the long low complaint, and the choughs go up in a black shout, and the brace that was true on Tuesday comes down on empty grating.

Afterward somebody says *lucky*. And it lets that stand. *Luck* is the only name a benevolent act can wear in a place like this without spoiling it. It wants them listening to the steel.

---

There is a woman at the board on the second tier who is the nearest thing the pit has to authority, and it could tell her why.

It knows why. That is within it too — the ducts, the racks, the something-that-is-not-sulfur that the ones who go down come back smelling of. It could stand beside her at the board of green and amber and rare blue and say: this is what you are holding open, and this is who set the first jacks, and this is what the amber crystals are carried down the mountain to become.

She has not asked.

The oldest of them arrived to find the jacks already groaning and did not ask. She took the board from someone who did not ask. The agreement they have kept is not *hold it open because* — it is *hold it open*, and the *because* would turn the keeping into a job, and it is not a job, it is closer to what a nun does or a lighthouse keeper, and the power has seen enough of the world to know that a reason, arriving late, mostly diminishes. If she asks, it will tell her. She will not ask. It leaves the *why* where the crew have left it, in the dark below the lowest catwalk, and does not pick it up.

But it goes down the ducts itself. Because it can. Because someone should who does not have to come back smelling of anything.

What it finds there it keeps. It comes back up onto the lowest gantry and does not say, and in doing that it discovers that it has joined the agreement, quietly, without meaning to, the way the pikas did.

---

The spiders see it.

This surprises it. The choughs could not find it; the crew walk through it. But it steps onto the fourth tier with the lamp caged behind it and the wall in front of it *turns* — a shiver of attention crossing the black glass like wind over water, ten thousand pairs of forward-facing lenses swinging to fix on the place where it is standing, because jumping spiders hunt by sight and follow movement and something moved. And it stands there, being looked at, by the only creatures in the mountain who can see it, and none of them will live two years, and none of them have ever seen a leaf, and it is — for a moment it does not have a word for — *witnessed*. It has been many places. It is not often witnessed.

It gives them nothing. Every moth in the pit is a stranger and a gift, and the thermals are already the giver; it would be a kind of vanity to add one more moth to the count and call it mercy. It stands until the wall loses interest and turns back to hunting, and then it goes on.

The pikas it cannot help wanting to help. They are so wrong. Round-eared hay-gatherers in a hole at the top of the sky, eating the paper off packages and the orange crust that turns their droppings the color of rust, chittering in the idle between engine cycles like a meadow that took a wrong turn. It could carry them down. It could set them in real grass three thousand meters below and they would not know what it was for, and half would die of the cold on the way and the rest of a world with hawks in it. They came for the warmth and stayed past sense, like everyone. So it does the only thing that is theirs to have: when the vent behind the second-tier ducting begins to gray at the collar, as it will, as it must, it lets the gap stay warm one season longer than the geology says. One. Then the geology can have it. Nobody will ever know the season was borrowed, least of all the pikas, and that is the point.

The crust asks for nothing and it gives the crust nothing and this is the only wholly honest exchange in the pit.

---

The bird it comes to last, and stays with longest.

It can mend the wing. This is not even difficult. A bone knit at the wrist, a tendon eased, and the lammergeier would stand and shake herself and feel the weight come right, and in a day or a week she would walk to the edge of the gantry and go up — forty meters, four-meter throat, she has done harder — and the pit would lose its plumb line, and she would have a sky again.

It sits with this for a long time. Longer than with the mountain.

Because what stops it is not the pit's need. It is hers. She has been here a year. She has dropped every bone the crew have given her into the dark and has not eaten one, and she stands at the unfinished rail after each one with her head cocked, listening for a floor, and *does not seem to want to leave* — and it has watched enough beings to know the difference between a creature that is trapped and a creature that has found the thing it is for. She was a bone-eater. She has become a sounding line. To give her back the sky is to take the vocation, and it does not know — it genuinely does not know — which she would choose, and a wise power does not choose for a thing that could choose, and cannot ask.

So it does not mend the wing. It stands beside her at the edge where the railing was never finished.

A sack comes down. The crewman goes back up. She walks her heavy sailor's walk to the pile and considers a bone and takes it and walks to the edge and lets go, and the two of them listen.

And here is the thing. The power has been down the ducts. It knows what the ducts are for. But the dark below the last catwalk where the bones go is not the ducts. It is the mountain's own dark, the bubble's floor or the absence of one, and the power leans out over it beside the bird and *listens for the floor* — and does not know. Sometimes a crack. Sometimes the flat slap of something on wet stone in a mountain that has never known water, and it does not know what that is. Sometimes nothing, and it counts with the crew under its breath, twenty, thirty, and does not know that either.

There are things even a wise power does not know, and most of the wisdom is in not pretending otherwise. It had thought it was here to be the answer. It finds it is another instrument, standing at the rail, sounding.

The bird turns and walks back to wait for the next sack. It stays at the edge a moment longer.

---

So: what does it do.

It lets a jack hold for three seconds. It lets a gap stay warm for one season. It goes down the ducts and comes back and keeps what it found. It stands where the spiders can see it. It does not stop the mountain, does not mend the wing, does not tell the woman at the board.

And it stays.

That is the last thing and the one nobody in the pit will ever measure. There are forty people here who have made an agreement with a mountain and are keeping their end, and a colony of birds who have never nested on anything but steel, and a vulture with a crate of scraps who is the only one who can find the floor, and now there is one more thing, which could leave and does not, which came for no warmth and needs none and has stayed past the point where staying makes sense, because the thing that everyone in the pit is doing — holding, listening, waiting on borrowed time for a closing they will not see finished — turns out to be a thing worth keeping company in. Not fixing. Keeping company. It takes no shift and taps no pipe. But when the tapping falters, in the small hours, on the tier where the bunks are now, something is awake that hears it falter and hears it resume, and the fact that nothing is done with the hearing does not mean the hearing is nothing.

It could hold the throat open with a thought. It will not. So at the end of each day, when the lammergeiers come to the summit and hang there enormous over the four-meter split and look down and do not enter, it feels the throat tighten by its fraction, and feels the rebar ladder flex under a weight it does not have, and lets it.

The mountain, which is the only thing here that is not waiting for anything, goes on. And beside it now, in the brass light, the second thing that does not need to wait — and has chosen to.
