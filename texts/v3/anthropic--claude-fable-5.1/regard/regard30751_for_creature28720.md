It comes the way the dust comes: not from anywhere in particular, gathering. A benevolent attention, wise enough to know that the first thing to do with a place like this is nothing, for a long time.

So it does nothing, for a long time. It stands in the halls. It is not troubled by the dark or the heat or the dryness, and it does not need to breathe, so the dust that hangs in the red glow simply hangs around it, and it watches the pistons stroke, and it counts.

It counts for a duration the plateau would not be able to hold in any of its clocks, and at the end of the counting it knows the tempo. In, and hold. Out, and hold. It knows it the way you know a song you have heard through a wall: not the words, but the shape of the words. It knows that the interval between door-openings is not a natural interval. It is a working day, of a species with a certain length of daylight and a certain tolerance for heat, and the day has been rounded off by millennia of copying until it is not a day anymore, only a cadence — but a cadence with a remainder in it, a leftover fraction that no plant would have chosen. Someone chose it. The power holds that fraction and turns it over and does not, yet, do anything with it.

---

Here is what it considers, and refuses.

It considers light. It could open the vaulting. It could let the sky in, a seam of it, and the plateau would know the sun for the first time since before the sea. But the plants here have not needed light for longer than the plateau has been flat, and light would not feed them — it would only tell them something was wrong with the ceiling, and the tenders would come out of the wound and seal it, patiently, a little rounder than before, and the power would have taught the plateau nothing except that the dark can be broken, which is a thing better not known by something that lives in it.

It considers water. It could bring rain to the dry lowland, refill what was a sea. The fissures in the bark would breathe wet for the first time in an epoch, and the passenger in the sap — the sour tint, the one stranger the plateau ever had — would die of it, because it lives in wax and oil and cannot hold a membrane against wet. The power would be drowning the plateau's only companion to give it a gift it cannot use. It does not do this.

It considers the pattern. This is the one it sits longest with. The pattern is drifting. Every rebuilding rounds it. The power can see, in a way that is not seeing, where the drift is going: the last piston, warm, rising and falling in the dark, and then not rising, and then a plain of packed heat under a black roof doing nothing at all, and then, when even the heat has been spent, a stump on a plain. It could arrest this. It could reach into the template — it can see the template, distributed through the wood the way a habit is distributed through a body — and firm it. Restore the tolerances. Give the runners back the true shape of the column, so the copies stop softening.

And it sees that this would be a kind of murder. Not of the plant. Of the being. Because the drift *is* the being. A pattern that did not err would be a crystal, and the plateau would go on running forever in perfect fidelity, a dead thing perfectly maintained, and nothing here would ever again be a little wrong in the way that only living things are wrong. The rounding is how it lives. The rounding is also how it is dying, very slowly, hurting nothing, having harmed no one, taking longer about it than mountains take. The power has seen a great many things die and it knows that this is one of the good ways, and that to hurry it or to halt it would both be for the sake of the power's own discomfort and not for the plateau's sake at all.

It considers speaking. Waking the pattern to itself. Letting it know that it is alone, that the shift ended, that the hours are for no one. It considers this for less time than the others, because the answer is obvious and the answer is: no. The plateau does not have loneliness. It cannot have loneliness; alone is not a condition available to it, any more than dry is available to a fish. To give it the knowledge would be to give it the condition. There is no gift in that. The kindest thing a wise power can do for something that does not know it is alone is to make very sure it never finds out.

---

So it does not fix. It does not feed. It does not tell.

What it does is smaller, and it does it in three parts.

---

The first thing is that it waits for a door.

It finds a wall that is going to open — it can feel the seam ripening, the hinge of living tissue remembering its hour — and it stands before the wall the way a worker stands before a gate at the end of a shift. And when the wall softens and splits and swings inward onto the next hall, the power walks through it.

That is all. It walks through. It goes into the next hall and stands there for the length of the opening, an hour, a day, while warm air moves past it and dust drifts and the flywheels thrum, and then it walks back, and the door heals shut behind it, and the place where it was is smooth by morning.

Nothing on the plateau registers this. There is no organ here for registering. The door did not open *for* the power, and it did not close because the power had passed. But the opening was, for once, used. The gesture that has been repeated for no one, in the dark, more times than there are stars, was met once by a body that went through it at the appointed time. The plateau will not remember. The power will. The habit of opening has now, in the whole record of its existence, one instance in which it was an actual door.

---

The second thing is that it sits down in a hall where a column has recently come down, and it stays.

It stays until the dust has settled, which is a long time. It stays until the pale shoots have pushed out from the fresh-broken faces, blind and fast. And it does not move, so that in the chemistry the tenders use to read the world — heat, pressure, the difference between a wall meant to thicken and a socket not — the power reads as a fault. Something present that the pattern did not put there. Something that should be shored, or reabsorbed, or grown around.

A runner crosses the floor toward it. It takes a season. The power watches it come, the thickness of an arm, feeling its way over the fused bark with no eyes and no hands and no idea what it is going to find. It reaches the power. It stops. And where it stops, it thickens, and where it thickens, it holds.

The plateau is tending the visitor. This is the only way the plateau has ever had of relating to anything: it mends. It cannot greet. It cannot ask. It can only find what is wrong and grow toward it and hold it up. And the power, which could brush the runner off, or dissolve it, or simply not be there, lets itself be held. It lets the plateau do the one thing it knows how to do to another presence. It receives the mending. For as long as it stays — and it stays a while longer, so the runner will have finished — it is the first thing in the whole record of this place that the tenders have shored which was not the plateau itself.

Then it withdraws, gently, so that the runner is left holding a shape where something was. The runner will not understand this. It will thicken a little more and stop and become, eventually, a small blunt buttress against nothing, one more machine that makes no mechanical sense but which the pattern will now remember as necessary. In some future rebuilding, in some district far from here, a runner will grow a buttress that leans on empty air, and will not know it is copying the place where a visitor once let itself be held.

The power considers whether this is a mark it should not have left. It decides it is a very small mark, and that the plateau has plenty of gestures for nothing, and that one of them may as well be for something.

---

The third thing is that it takes a brick.

Off the conveyor, as it passes from one wall into another. Dark, warm, dense, faintly translucent, smelling of the whole plateau at once. The braided root does not notice. The wall the brick was going into receives one brick fewer and does not notice either. The product of the system has, for the first time, left the system.

It carries the brick out. Down the concave ramp of buttress root, worn by feet that stopped coming before the sea dried, warm underfoot all the way. Out onto the dry lowland, where the sea was.

It does not plant it. Nothing would grow. It does not build with it. It sets it down on the old seabed, some distance from the flank, where the plateau's heat does not reach and the brick will cool for the first time in its existence, and it stays a while to watch that happen: the warmth going out of the thing, the dark wax stiffening, the smell of resin and hot bread fading into the ordinary smell of a cold dry place. Cooled, the brick is only a brick. But it is *outside*. It is a piece of the plateau's next thousand years that will not be spent by the plateau, that will lie here on the floor of a vanished sea, and if anyone ever comes to this lowland and finds it and holds it up to the light, they will be holding a thing that was made — by a factory, for a reason, on a schedule someone set.

---

Before it leaves entirely, it goes back up once more and puts its hand on a sign.

One of the healed-over panels: fine-grained bark that was once scored with something and is now only a raised smoothness you can feel but not read. The power can read it. Not by sight — there was never anything to see — but by remembering, in the grain beneath the smoothness, the pressure of the tool that made the marks, and the hand behind the tool, and the shape of the intention behind the hand. It is a wise power and it has read older things than this.

The sign says what signs say. A direction. A number. A name for the hall, and the name of the thing the hall was for. It is not a great mystery. It is a working notice from a working place, written by a shift-worker for other shift-workers, in a language that ended.

The power reads it and does not tell the plateau what it says. The plateau has no use for it, and the not-knowing is part of how the plateau is whole. But the power keeps it. It keeps the name of the hall, and the name of the thing the hall was for, and the fraction of a day that was rounded off into the cadence of the doors, and the tempo — in, and hold, out, and hold. It takes these things with it when it goes, the way you take the smell of a house with you on your clothes, and it does not put them down.

Because the last piston is coming. Not soon, by any measure the power would need, but coming: a warm column rising and falling in the dark, for no reason it can recall, and then not rising. And when that happens there will be nowhere on the plateau or under it or in the dry lowland that knows what the tempo was for. Except that now there will be somewhere. Somewhere far from here, something benevolent and old will still be keeping the time. In, and hold. Out, and hold. It will keep it long after the plateau has stopped, so that the stopping, when it comes, will not have been for no one. Somebody will have known whose heartbeat that was. Somebody will have gone through the door.

That is all it does. It is not very much. It is, the power decides, about the right amount.

It goes down the ramp for the last time and does not look back, because there is nothing to see — a black rim against a black sky, a stump on a plain — and because the plateau would not know it was being looked at, and the power has decided not to spend on itself any gesture the plateau cannot receive.

Behind it, through a wall the thickness of a hillside, the pistons stroke. In, and hold. Out, and hold. Warm as skin. Older than the shape of the land. Attended, once. Then left alone in the good dark, to finish in its own time.
