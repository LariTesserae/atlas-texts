**What Came, and What It Did**

Start with what such a thing would notice first, which is not the town or the forest or the roar. It is the flatness. A power old enough and wise enough to be called those things has seen mountains before, and has seen them fall, and knows what it costs the world to grind a range down to its root and leave the root standing. It would look at the table and understand that it was looking at the result of an unimaginable patience. And it would take, from that, the first and simplest instruction: *whatever you do here, do it slowly.*

So it would not arrive. Arriving is fast. It would be there, in the way the haze is there, for a long time before it did anything at all — long enough for the sap-drawers to open a cut and see it seal, long enough to watch a wall-walker grow old and hand the maul to someone with a stiller hand. It would learn the hand-language, because the shouted one is only for baskets and lizards, and it would learn to hear the layers in the roar until it could tell, like a listener, that the moths had hatched early. It would be careful to want nothing during this time. Wanting is also fast.

---

Then, having watched, it would be tempted, because benevolence is a kind of hunger and it does not go away just because you are wise.

It would want to give them water. This is the obvious thing. A single spring, cold, coming up through a crack in the quartzite at the town's center — it could do that; it could do it in an afternoon. And it would stand in the plaza and imagine the first child putting a hand into running water and it would see, almost at once, what the spring would do. The pillars would become ornaments. The bone blades would be set aside. The chips of pale stone would stop meaning anything, and the sap-drawers, who are the calendar, would have nothing to remember, and time on the table would go slack and loose. The birds would come to the water and the lizards would follow and the delicate closeness of the whole thing — everything held inside something alive, nothing wasted, nothing standing free — would loosen and spread. The town would become a town with a well in it. It would not be *here* anymore. It would be somewhere.

It would want to give them quiet. It would see the hoarseness in the children's throats and the squint that cuts lines at eight years old and it would think: *let there be one room, one round room in the deep of the town, where the roar does not reach.* And it would imagine the people finding that room, and going in, and — it knows this, it has seen it in other places — never quite coming out. The rim would move inward. The thing that takes hold of the few at the edge would have a door in the middle of the town, and everyone would have to pass it.

It would want to show them the horizon. To thin the haze for a single evening so that the sun set with an edge and the far world came up out of the cloud in ranks of other summits, other tables, and they would know they were not alone at the top of a dissolved world. And it would stop, because it would remember the birds. No bird from the colony flies out past the rim. They know there is nowhere to go. That is not a limitation. It is the fact that makes the rim a rim rather than a road, and the people have built the whole architecture of their becoming — the walk out, the weeping, the walk back — on the rim being a rim. Give them a horizon and every child who goes to the edge will look at it instead of listening for their breath. And some of them will go over. Not in despair. In hope, which is worse, because they will have no way down and will go anyway.

And it would want, most of all, to tell them the truth about the founders. It would have looked at the walls long enough to see what the people cannot: that the lowest courses are not better work. They are the same work, aged. Wind has rounded the edges and lichen has fattened the joints and time has done to the stones exactly what it did to the mountain, and the giants who set them were people a shoulder's height, hoarse and squinting, who lifted big blocks because big blocks were what the table gave them first. Everything was not larger once. Nothing is getting smaller. The story of the shrinking world is an optical effect of erosion.

It would hold this a long while. Then it would put it down. Because the belief is not sad. Because it is the shape of things, and it gives the people a way to stand in their own walls without pride, and to look at the deep past with something between reverence and grief, and to shrug — *they were bigger than us* — and get on with pressing the block home. Take that from them and you give them the truth, and the truth has no niches in it for anything to nest.

---

So it would do the smaller things. Four of them, over a long time, none of them visible.

The first would be the rite. Before it touched anything on the table, it would go to the rim and do what the children do. It would push out through the last twisted columns and stand on the mirror-stone with the roar at its back and the nothing in front, and it would stay there until it could hear its own breath — which for a thing like this is not a short wait, and not a comfortable one. It would learn there what the children learn, which is that the wall of sound behind you is not the world pressing in but the world holding you up, and that the small sound in front of it is yours and is very little and is enough. It would crouch and see itself doubled in the oldest surface on the table, with the forest and the town and the flickering birds behind its face, and it would understand that it had been given the only citizenship the place has to offer. Then it would go back in, because the noise pulls you back in. It always does.

The second would be for the sap-drawers. This is the one true wound it would have found — the only place where the table bleeds and does not seal. When a drawer dies, time dies with them: the memory of which pillar was opened in which year by whose hand. The others go from column to column afterward, touching the scars, trying to recover what was lost, and they never quite do. Each death is a small permanent forgetting. So the power would hold what they held. Every cut, every chip of pale stone pressed into wax, every lip sealing at its own slow pace. And when an old drawer, after a death, stood in the forest with a palm on a column and did not know — was it twelve years or twenty since this one wept? — the power would let the wax under her hand be a shade warmer if the answer was *soon*, and a shade cooler if it was *not yet*. Nothing she could point to. Nothing she would think of as anything but her own hand knowing. The drawers would begin to say, among themselves, that the pillars remembered for them, and this would be true, and they would never know how.

The third would be the ones the rim takes. Every generation has a few who sit down on the mirror-stone and do not want to come back, and someone goes out to get them, and they are watched afterward, gently, for the rest of their lives. The power would not go out to get them. It would not touch them, because the rite is the one thing on the table done alone and the aloneness is the point. But it would decide that *alone* and *unaccompanied* are different words, and it would be there — on the bare stone, in the warm wind, unseen and unsounding — for as long as they sat. It would not make the rim less. It would only make it so that when the child finally heard their own breath, the breath was not the smallest thing on the table. Something was listening to it. Some of them would come back in on their own after that. Not all. The ones who still needed to be fetched would still be fetched, and still watched, and that too the power would leave alone, because the watching is a form of the touching and the town is right to do it.

The fourth would be the last and the one it would hesitate over longest, because it is the closest to meddling. It would become a layer of the sound. Very low — below the scolding of the rust-colored thing, below the day-cricket's single note — a steadiness in the roar that had not been there before. Not a voice. Not a sign. Something the listeners would notice, over a few years, and argue about, and eventually name: the sound the table makes when the year is good. They would be affectionately suspected for hearing it. They would sometimes be wrong about what it meant. But when the haze thinned in a hard season and the pillars drank less and the birds went thin in their song, the listeners would put their palms to the stone and feel, under the trembling, something that was not going anywhere, and they would say so, and the people would believe them, and would wait, and it would be right to wait.

---

There is a thing it would see that the people cannot, and about which it would do nothing.

It would look at the haze the way it looked at the flatness, over the long arc, and it would see whether the haze was thinning. Maybe it is. Maybe over another ten thousand years the mineral dust settles and the high veil lifts and the nights grow drier and the pillars begin, imperceptibly, to lose more than they gather. And the forest thins, and the colony thins, and the town thins, and one day the last sap-drawer opens the last cut and the bead does not come.

The power could prevent this. It could hold the haze in place forever. And it would think about that as long as it thought about anything, and it would come to this: the being that lives on the table is the going-on, and going-on is not the same as going-forever. The table has always known how to end. It ends dry. The old die dry, going thin and papery and quiet, and are laid at the rim for one turning of the haze, and are brought back in and made into blades and pegs and nests. Nothing on the table has ever been given a promise, and nothing on it has ever asked for one. To guarantee it would be to change what it is — to make it a thing that has a future instead of a thing that has a *now*, pressed flat, one word. The power would leave the haze to do what haze does. If the table ends, it will end the way it lived: slowly, warm, crowded, with the sound going down half an octave and not stopping until it stops.

---

Whether it would stay is a question the power itself would not settle for a long time.

Leaving would be right in one way. A visitor is a visitor. But it would find, when it considered leaving, that it did not know how to be the kind of thing that leaves a place like this. It had stood at the rim. It had been let into *here*. And the sign for *here* is the sign for *us*, and has been forever, and you cannot un-press a palm.

So perhaps it stays — as the warmth in the wax, the steadiness under the roar, the thing on the mirror-stone that is not quite the wind. Or perhaps it goes and carries the table in itself the way a pillar carries water, hoarded, not to be lost, drawn on rarely and with ceremony. Either would be a kind of maintenance. Either would be a kind of waiting.

Before it decides, it goes to the edge one more time and crouches and puts its palm flat on the polished quartzite, once, the way they do. The stone is warm from the day. The roar is at its back. Down in the dull mirror the amber sky is doubled, and the ribbed dark wall of the forest, and the darker mass of the town wedged into it with its thousand humming holes, and the birds passing between, and no face at all where a face should be, because the power has made sure of that.

*Here,* the hand says. *Us.*

Then the noise pulls it back in. It goes on and on. That is the whole of what it does, and it turns out to be enough.
