**What the power does**

It comes the way everything comes here: down out of the sky, or up out of the cloud, and it is not clear which. It does not land. It is not a thing that lands. But for a while its attention rests on the shelf the way the sun rests on the top of a plucked block, with weight, and the shelf does not know.

It was drawn by a being. That was what it understood — that there was a place, and one who lived there, and the two together made a shape worth looking at. So the first thing it does is look for the one.

It finds twenty beetles to a fissure, cold and still. It finds the springtails down in the snow, more of them than there are stars over the shelf, each smaller than a grain of sand. It finds the spider, the slate-coloured one, under its stone, having eaten once today. It finds the tardigrades stopped in the moss, ten thousand small barrels of held breath. It finds the ptarmigan in its hollow, and the vole in its hay, awake, listening to a sound the power made without meaning to. It finds, on the blackened floor of the shelter, no one, and on the fourth cairn a stone without lichen on it.

There is no one being. Or there is, but it is not any of these. It is the thing they are all doing — the holding-on, the finding of the underneath, the making of a home out of ground that is only just adequate and will not stay. The power is old enough to have seen this before, in other places, in other kinds of cold, and it recognises it the way you recognise a face. The being who lives on the shelf is the rearguard itself. Every one of them is a limb of it.

Very well. It attends to that.

---

The second thing it does is refuse.

It is benevolent, and benevolence has a first impulse, and the first impulse is to fix. The power lets the impulse run all the way out to see where it goes, because that is what wisdom is for.

It could stop the ice. It could lay its hand on the last remnant up above the headwall and say *here, no further*, and the meltwater would keep coming and the shelf would keep its sharpness and the beetle would never have to walk again. It considers this for the length of a night. Then it sees the moss. The moss lies in the exact shape of a glacier that is not there. The cushion plants grow where the ice ground a basin. The vole's whole architecture is the gap under a block the ice dropped and forgot. The shelf is not a place the ice retreated *from*; the shelf is the retreat, made visible, and to stop the ice is to turn the shelf into a photograph of itself. Everything on it is fitted for an edge that moves. Hold the edge still and they will not be saved. They will be stranded in a different way.

And anyway the ice is not the mountain's. It is the sky's, and the sky is the world's, and a power that reaches into the weather of a planet because it has grown fond of one ledge is not wise. It is only large.

It could warm the shelf. It rejects this before the thought finishes. Warmth is what is killing them.

It could remove the eagle. But the eagle is the reason the vole is never in the open, and never-in-the-open is the reason the vole is alive. Take the eagle and in ten years there is a vole that lingers on the warm stone, and something else finds it. The eagle is part of the vole's shape. You cannot subtract it and leave the vole.

It could lift the flightless beetle — the bronze one, the one with only the memory of wings — and set it down on the next mountain over, where the ice has more to give. It considers this longest, because the beetle is the one with the least room left. Two hundred metres of headwall and then sky. But the beetle is not a thing that is carried. It is a thing that walks behind ice, and has walked for ten thousand years, and a beetle that arrives somewhere by being lifted has stopped being the beetle. The power is not sure a benevolence that alters what a thing *is* in order to preserve it has preserved anything.

So it refuses, and refuses, and refuses, and each refusal costs it something, because it is benevolent and the shelf is small and cold and it could so easily — 

---

The third thing it does is find the wrong.

Because there is one. Not the retreat; the retreat is old and the shelf's inhabitants are made of it. Not the cold, not the eagle, not the thinness of the air. The power looks at the beetle, which walked up a valley behind a glacier over a span of time in which mountains slump and rivers change course, and it looks at the fifty years of bare rock, and it sees it.

The pace.

The ice used to go up the mountain at a speed a beetle could match. A few metres in a good year. A generation of beetles born at the edge, dying at the edge, the edge having moved less than the length of the shelf in their whole lives. The vole's lineage followed it at a walk. The willow followed it at the pace willow grows. Ten thousand years of that, and every one of them fitted to it exactly, the way the moss fits the basin.

And then, in one human lifetime, the ice let go of the shelf and went up two hundred metres. The beetle can walk. It cannot run. The willow cannot run at all. The being who lives here was built for a moving edge and the edge has begun to move at a speed that is not the mountain's, and the power, which knows where the speed comes from and knows it cannot reach the cause from here, sits with that for a long time.

It cannot give them a world. It might be able to give them the pace.

---

So here is what it does, and it is small, and it will look like nothing.

Above the headwall, in the shaded cleft where the last of the ice sits, a piece of glacier the size of a house and getting smaller, the power lends a little cold. Not enough to hold it. Enough to slow it. Where the ice would have gone in ten years it will go in fifteen, then twenty. The meltwater that drips into the pocket under the moss will drip a little longer than it was going to. The shelf will stay sharp a little longer. The vole's gaps will not close so soon. The bronze beetle will have the length of a few more generations to do what it has always done, which is climb, one metre at a time, toward the notch and whatever is above the notch.

This is not rescue. The power knows that. The ice will still go. The shelf will still soften and round and grass over and become ordinary, and the beetle will still one day reach the ridge and find only sky, unless the world's weather turns, which is not the power's to turn. What the power has given is not an ending. It is a pace. It has taken the fifty years and made them, perhaps, eighty. It has made the retreat something that can be followed again, for a while, by things that follow.

And it tends the cloud. This is the smaller thing. In the years that are coming there will be summers when the valley is too dry and the afternoon cloud rises and stalls fifty metres below the lip, and the shelf goes a week without wet, and the tardigrades stay barrels and the moss browns at the edge. On those afternoons the power gives the cloud the last fifty metres. That is all. The stems bead. The moss is a lake for an hour. Ten thousand small animals unfold and eat and the moss stays green and the springtails have their pollen and the beetles have their fallout and the vole has its saxifrage. Nobody on the shelf knows the cloud was late. It wasn't.

---

There is one more thing, and it is not help.

The power notices that it does not want to leave. It has watched the eagle hang on the updraft for eleven days, and slide away, and hang, and slide away. It has watched the vole finish its hay. It has heard the ptarmigan's three wooden notes at dusk and understood that the notes were not for it and that there was almost no one else for them to be for, and it has found that this matters to it more than it expected.

So it stays a while. Not to act. It has acted; the cold is lent and the cloud is tended and there is nothing else that would not be a taking. It stays because attention is a kind of weather too, and the shelf has had very little of it. Someone comes down through the notch once in a thousand nights. Otherwise the beetles and the vole and the spider and the moss do their holding-on under a sky that has never once looked back at them.

Now something has. It counts, at night. The beetles, twenty to a fissure. The springtails down in the snow. The spider. The barrels. The vole awake. The ptarmigan white in the white. Each in its small dark space between what is warm and what is cold, and each of them, for the length of the power's watching, seen.

---

In a year or two a person will come down through the notch on a morning when the cloud is late, and walk the line of cairns, and find the fourth one short again, and set a stone on it. They will not notice that the last of the ice above the headwall looks much as it did the last time. They will not notice that the moss in the basin has not browned at the edge the way the moss on the lower shelves has. They will sleep one night in the curve of stacked stone, and in the morning the floor will be freshly blackened, and they will be gone.

Nothing will have happened, which is the shelf's usual outcome.

The power, which is wise, will consider that the best thing it could have done, and will go on lending cold to the ice one degree at a time, and will not be thanked, and will not need to be.
