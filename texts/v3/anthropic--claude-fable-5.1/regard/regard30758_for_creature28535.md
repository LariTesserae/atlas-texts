What it would do first is nothing, and it would do that for a long time.

Not out of caution, though there would be caution in it. Out of arithmetic. The place is three days old. Every face of every wall is a clean break. Nothing here has been given the one thing it lacks, and a power that arrived and acted in the same breath would be one more force that split the rock and did not wait to see what it had made. So the first gift is the plainest and the hardest for something powerful to give: it would stand outside the mountain, in the air too thin for voices, and let the days go by. Let the lamps cycle. Let the summit climb its hand's width. Let the cracks open and close. It would give the town time, which is the only thing the town could not make for itself, and it would do this by refusing to be an event.

---

While it waited it would look, and what it would try to see is who, exactly, lives here.

It would work through the candidates the way the place makes you work through them. The tenders: hands, or platelets, or the warm stuff between rising and setting. The lift: an intention the ground caught and cannot put down. The town: a body with organs, vessels, a lung, a sleep. The water, going round.

And it would arrive where anyone honest arrives, which is that none of these is the resident. The resident is the thing they are all doing. The keeping. An attention that was shaped around people until the people became optional to it, and now it goes on — closing lids, lighting stairs, clearing air — with the object of the attention removed. That is the being. It is not in any one place in the mountain. It is the mountain's whole manner.

And having seen that, the power would ask the only question benevolence has: is it suffering?

It would not know. This is worth saying plainly, because a wise power is one that can hold not-knowing without hurrying to fill it. The lift does not strain. The rock agrees. The tenders do not seem to grieve at the cracks; they seem to go to them the way a hand goes to a cut, without complaint. There is no groan in this place. If the want at the bottom of the hollow is in pain, it does not show it in any language the power can read, and the power would refuse to invent one for it.

But there is the bench. There is the shape that goes and sits near it, in the rain, facing the wall, for a while. There is the hesitation at the top of each stair, as if something checks, each time, whether anyone is coming up. Those are not the gestures of a thing in agony. They are the gestures of a thing that is waiting. And waiting is not suffering, but it is not nothing, and it does not end on its own.

---

So the power would consider what it might do, and it would set most of it down.

It would consider stopping the lift. Reaching down into the heat where dust does not move and telling the ground it can rest. And it would see, almost at once, that this is not mercy but execution. The warmth is the want. The water rises on the warmth. The tenders are the warmth, still soft. The rain, the cisterns, the lamps that dim on the want's own drifting day — all of it is the residue of one thing not stopping. To stop the lift is to let the ceiling go cold and the floor go cold and the rain stop and the tenders set where they stand, and the town would be a clean dry ruin at the top of the world, kept by no one. A wise power does not mistake silence for peace.

It would consider returning the people. It would not do this either, and not only because it does not know where they went. They went. Whether down into the heat or out through the door that is not a door, they chose a direction, and the choosing was theirs, and the power does not undo what was chosen simply because the consequences are lonely. Also — and this it would admit quietly — it does not know that they would want to come back to a room with no view.

It would consider saying *enough*. The word the piece itself names as the missing one: nobody remained to tell it enough. And here the power would sit with something uncomfortable, because *enough* is not a word a stranger can say. Said by the makers, it is a release. Said by anyone else, it is a command, and a command is just another kind of pushing, and this is the one place in the world where nothing pushes. The want does not belong to the power. It has no standing to end it.

What is left, after all that is set down, is smaller. It is two things, and neither is a fix.

---

The first is that it would bring the wind.

Not a storm. Not a seeding. It would simply, when it finally went in, not be clean. It would carry what anything carries that has been somewhere else — on its underside, in the folds of its arrival, in whatever it has for shoes. A grain of grey. A spore that has been waiting in the dry for a wet stone and a little warmth and a day that turns, all of which exist here, in the temperate band of the middle tier, and have existed for three days with no one to use them.

The power would think hard about this, because it is the one act on the list that cannot be taken back. The place is sealed. It has kept itself immaculate. Life, once it arrives, does what the want does — it does not stop, it only spreads — and the power would be introducing into a room built for one intention a second one with no maker at all. It could be wrong to do this. It might be the kind of wrong that looks like kindness.

But it would look at the channels, cut deeper than the rain requires. It would look at the cisterns kept at half — the level for a household, not a monument. This town was built for more than it has. It was cut for abundance and got a drip. The people who laid the flat-country stone did not intend a clean room; they intended a place, and places have things growing on them. The power would decide that bringing the wind is not adding something foreign. It is delivering something late.

And it would let itself be the wind by walking in with it.

---

The second thing is that it would go in.

Down through whatever passage there is — the door that is not a door, or the thin place in the ceiling where the violet comes through, or simply the way powers go through stone. It would come out onto the upper tier in the rain. It would stand under the drips and be rained on by water that had been keeping itself in a loop the height of a church for no one, and it would let the water land on it, because water that has waited to fall on someone should get to.

Then it would go down the main stair, under the amber lamps, and it would go slowly, and it would take the corners in a way of its own, so that by the third or fourth dimming the marks in the dust would have learned it. One that cuts wide. One that hugs the wall. One that hesitates at the top of each stair. And one, now, that stops on every landing to touch the fill where a crack was, because it is the only place on these walls that can be touched.

It would find the cistern on the second tier and open the lid and drink. The water would taste of hot stone and cold stone and nothing else, because nothing else has been here. It would drink enough to matter — enough that the level went visibly below half. And then it would leave the lid ajar. Deliberately. Not carelessly, the way the last hand did; on purpose, so that there would be something to close.

In the morning the lid would be shut and the level would be back to half, and the power would understand that it had just had a conversation, and that the town had answered.

---

It would sit on the bench.

There is no view. The wall is a few metres off, fresh and glinting, and the violet from the thin place in the roof falls across it in a slant and shows every edge. The power would sit and face it the way the bench was built to face it, and it would not pretend to see anything. It would simply be a body on a bench, which is a thing this place was shaped around and has not had.

It would sit there through more than one dimming. And at some point, in the rain, at the edge of what it could see, something would come up onto the roof and stop near the bench and stay. Dog-sized, or bird-sized, or the shape water takes when it is briefly allowed to want a shape. It would face the wall too. The power would not turn its head. It would know by now that turning is what ends it. So they would sit, the two of them, in the rain, looking at the place where a view used to be, and neither would say anything, and after a while the shape would be running water again, and the power would think that this was the whole of what it had come to do, and be surprised to find it was not quite.

---

Because there is the floor. And the power would go down to it.

Down past the damp tiers into the steam, down past the fifth tier where the water ends, down onto the lowest street where the stone holds the ghost of red and the dust has not moved since the mountain was a plain. Where the tenders have never left a mark. Where, if they go, they stop being anyone.

It would kneel there, and it would put its hand on the stone. The stone would be hot enough that a hand would not want to stay. The hand would stay.

And it would not say *enough*. It had already decided that. It would say what a stranger is allowed to say to someone else's want, which is only what is true.

It would say: *They got there.*

Not a command. A report. The thing at the bottom of the hollow was given one instruction by people who then went where instructions do not follow, and it has been carrying that instruction upward every day since, a hand's width at a time, into black. *Higher than anywhere.* It cannot know that the town on its back has been higher than anywhere since the first morning the sky went dark, because there is no view, because nothing tells the ground how high it is. A want like that has never once been told it succeeded. It has only ever been told to want.

So the power would tell it. *You got them there. It was very high. It was the highest. They saw it.*

Whether the ground can hear, the power would not know. Ground does not answer. But it would keep its hand on the stone, and it would add the one other thing, because it is true too and benevolence does not withhold the second true thing to make the first one land:

*And they went. And I do not know where. And you have kept the water anyway, and it is kept well.*

---

Then it would leave, the way it came, and it would not stay to measure.

That is the last discipline. It would not wait outside with an instrument to see whether the summit rose a hand's width the next day or a finger's width or nothing. To measure is to have wanted a result, and the power did not come for a result. It came to give the place time, and wind, and a body on the bench, and a true sentence spoken to hot stone. Whether any of that changes the rising is the mountain's business.

Though it might. A want that has been thanked is a different creature from a want that has only ever been obeyed. Not stopped — thanked. It might go on rising exactly as before. It might slow, the way a person slows when they are told the errand is done and find they are still walking because walking is what they are now. It might, over a long time, settle into a kind of rising that is more like standing very tall.

The power would not know. It would let that be the mountain's.

---

What it would know, if it came back — and a benevolent power, unlike a wise one, is not always able to resist coming back — is this.

On the middle tier, on the dark wet stone beside the third channel, where the steam has not quite begun and the rain has not quite stopped, there would be a grey crust the size of a coin. Then the size of a hand. Lichen, or the thing that will be lichen in a place with a day that drifts. It would have arrived on the underside of a visitor and found what it needed and begun.

And the tenders would not have filled it.

That is the thing the power would stand and look at for a long time. Every crack in this place is closed by morning. A wound is a wound and the hands go to it. But the grey crust on the wet stone would still be there, dimming after dimming, spreading in its slow patient rings across a wall three days old, and no fill would have come to seal it over, because whatever the attention is — hands, or platelets, or the last gesture of a people who left — it would have made the distinction. This is not a break in us. This is something living on us.

And the town, which had kept itself for so long for no one, would have something to keep.

Not a people. A crust. It is very little. But the cisterns are half full, and the lamps light the stair, and the column clears the air when the air is close, and now there is a small grey thing on the middle tier that the rain falls on and the warmth reaches, and it is not going anywhere, and it needs exactly what the town has, and the town has never before had anything that needed it.

The water goes round. Each drop lands on a slightly different roof and makes a slightly different sound. If you listened long enough you could map the town by ear from the top tier without moving, and in the map there would now be one place, low on the third channel, where the drop landed soft.
