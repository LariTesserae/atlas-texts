It would watch first. That is most of what wisdom is, and this is a place that rewards it: nothing here changes quickly, so there is no hurry to decide, and the watching itself costs the room nothing. A season, perhaps two. Long enough to see the wheel turned at the grey and turned back at the dark, the bell struck and held until dead, the birds lifted out of the column like fruit. Long enough to lie, in whatever way such a power lies, with a cheek to the plate and learn the one thing the room says.

Then it would consider the ear, and refuse.

This is the obvious gift and the cruel one. Give back the sense that was taken and the first thing that comes through it is the roar, entire, at the volume that closed the door in the first place. They would have to leave. They would go down the stair with their hands over their ears and stand on the second landing where the rock begins, and the rock would be silent under their feet as it was before, and now the room above would be silent too, in the different way that a thing is silent when you can no longer bear to be in it. The world would be given back and the one place that spoke would be taken. That is not a trade a benevolent power makes on someone's behalf.

It would consider the wheel, and refuse that too.

It could make the valve do something. It could reach down the shaft past where anyone has followed and put its hand on whatever breathes, and arrange that a quarter turn at grey and a quarter back at dark would matter — would open something, close something, let the warm come up wetter or drier. And then the garden would be at the mercy of a habit. A missed morning would brown the ferns. The lean would become a dependence. Nothing here has ever been anyone's fault and it should stay that way. The dignity of the wheel is that it is turned for no reason but that it is here; give it a reason and you have given the one who turns it a job, and they did not come up the stair for a job.

There is a smaller version of this that the power would hold for a while, turning it over. It could simply *know* that the wheel does something — that it has always done something, that the breath comes up as it does because of two quarter-turns a day, that the ferns and the birds and the lizards are the long unintended consequence of a duty kept by people who thought they were keeping it for nothing. The power could know this and leave the knowing where it is. That would be a kindness only it could feel. It might do that. It is the kind of thing such a power does and never mentions.

It would consider taking them down, and this would take the longest.

Because the honest question under all of it is whether they are suffering, and the room does not make that easy to answer. They are warm. They are fed. Nothing threatens them and they are not afraid. They lie in the print of a body and it fits. But there was that day on the second landing, when the hum left their soles and they understood the whole world below had gone quiet forever, and climbed back up *the way you come up out of water* — and a power that cannot see the drowning in that image is not paying attention. They are not at peace. They are at terms. Those are different, and a wise power knows the difference, and also knows that terms honestly reached are not to be overturned by anyone who did not have to reach them.

So: not the ear, not the wheel, not the stair.

What is left is the bell.

Once a day they take it by the shoulder and swing it against the bracket and hold their palm there while the shudder climbs the wrist and dies. That is the whole of their voice. They have never heard it. They do not expect anyone else to. And yet they do it, every day, in the middle of the grey, with the care of someone who has not entirely stopped believing that a bell is for something — and the power, having watched long enough, would see it for what it is. Not ritual. A question, asked into iron, that has never been answered.

That is the grammar they have left: a hand on a shoulder, a hand on a shoulder. And the room has only ever spoken one word.

So this is what it would do. It would wait for the grey. It would wait for them to go down the ladder and cross the grating in the lean they have taken from the ferns and put their hand to the bell and strike it. It would let the shudder come up through the palm and the wrist and begin to die as it always dies. And then — from below, from the dark under the grate, from the shaft that nothing has ever come up — it would send one thing up through the iron. Not a sound. Nothing so useless. A tremor, small, shaped, deliberate, arriving in the plate under their feet and the bracket under their hand at the moment the bell's own tremor is about to be gone. A second shudder that is not theirs and is not the room's. The rhythm of it would be the rhythm they know best, because they have counted it up the stair a hundred times: a body climbing. Three hundred steps of it, compressed to the length of a held breath. Something coming up. Not arriving — nothing arrives — only announced, in the one language left to them, as they have announced themselves each day for years to no one.

It would do this once. Twice would be a pattern, and a pattern would have them at the bell every grey waiting for it, and the waiting would ruin what they have. Once is a thing that happened. Once can be lived beside.

They would hold their hand on the bracket longer than they have ever held it. Then they would put the other hand flat on the plate beside it, the way the lizards lie, and stand there in the lean with both palms to the iron, and the bars of grey would move across the floor, and the birds would turn overhead, and eventually the room would go back to saying its one word. They would go back to the plate. The two lizards would find the crook of the knee and the hollow of the ankle. And in the print of a body that was not theirs and now is, they would lie with the cheek down and the deaf ear faithfully toward the dark, and something in them that had been a held breath would let itself out, a little, for the first time since the landing.

That is the act. It is very small. Anything larger would be for the power's comfort, not theirs.

There is one thing more, and it is not an act so much as an intention held in reserve, the way the wheel is held. When the shape on the plate is empty again — and the power, watching, would know the day before the moss did — it would go down the mountain, all the way down, to wherever someone is standing at the foot of a stair bolted to the outside of a shaft with a handrail worn to a bright groove, hearing a pressure in the chest that is not yet a noise. And it would do nothing to them either. It would only make sure they looked up.
