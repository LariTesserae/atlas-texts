**The Third Fact**

It came the way cloud comes, through the grille, and hung in the upper third of the room, and was regarded as weather.

That is the first thing to say about it: it was not startling. It could not have been. A power that cannot enter a room without being noticed would have had to stay outside this one, because the gallery has no organ left for noticing. The moths shivered as they shiver. The bats rippled and settled. A frog in the trough of the roar held its throat inflated for a full second and released a tick into the one band the wall leaves empty, and the tick was for another frog, and the power let it go past.

It stayed for one tide of the cups. It watched the mineral bowls fill in the afternoon as the roar climbed and the weeping thickened, watched them brim and spill and carry a dozen black tadpoles down one shelf of flowstone to the next, watched the ringed tail with the bad leg come out at the fall of the sound and the other one wait for it. It saw what you saw. Then it looked where you could not, which is behind the back wall, and this is the third fact of the place, after the noise and the crowd: the gallery is borrowed.

Everything in it is on loan from a labor no one has tended since the amber strip was bright. The warmth is waste heat. The weeping is condensate. The moat of noise that makes this the one room in the structure where nothing needs to be warned is the sound of something working, and the something is old, and the strip at knee height is not a light but a gauge, and it is reading low. Not failing tonight. Not failing in the life of the person who cleans the stone. But the labor behind the wall has a slope to it, the way everything has, and the slope runs toward silence, and silence here would not be peace. It would be the first evening in a thousand years that a thing which hunts by ear could hear a frog. A den of animals that traded away their ears for warmth and touch and one high thin band of song, met all at once by everything they had been spared. The power stood in the cloud and saw that ending whole, and it was the only thing in the gallery it did not regard as weather.

So it considered. Wisdom, in most places, is knowing what to do. In the Weeping Gallery it was mostly a matter of knowing what to refuse.

It considered the person on the stone, and what it would mean to give them back the sound of the world, and refused that first and fastest, because it understood what the gift would be. It would be the gallery taken away. There is exactly one room in the structure where not hearing is not a lack, and the person has found it and worn a path to it, and a power that restored their hearing would be handing them the roar — the real roar, the one that flays — in exchange for the only place where they were not the odd one. It would be exile dressed as mercy. The power had seen that done before, elsewhere, by others who meant well, and it did not do it here.

It considered the strip. It could have brightened it. It refused, because the strip is for a maintenance regime that is not coming back, and a bright strip would light a gallery for no one, and would change the honey dusk the eye adjusts to, and would burn off the green-black mat that grows in the amber and nowhere else. The strip is dying at the right speed for the moss.

It considered the leg. It looked at the stiffness in the hind limb of the ringed tail and at the other one, waiting on the deposit's far edge with its nose down, pretending to work the soil. It refused. The waiting is one of the arrangements. Take the leg and you take the reason the other one turns back.

It considered the corner — the deep black soil where the ringed tails den, and the question the gallery does not answer: whether there is a third, small, that has not yet learned to come out at the fall of the sound. It could have known. It could have looked into the warm bowl in the deposit and counted. And here the power did something that was neither refusing nor acting: it decided that on the den side of the wall it would be a citizen of the gallery, and disclose nothing, and be disclosed nothing to. It did not look. Or it looked and kept what it saw as the gallery keeps it. There is no telling which, and that is correct, and it is the only wall the power built that day.

Then it went where the frogs could not follow, into the duct, where nothing has ever lived.

What it did there was small. Not repair — repair would have changed the pitch, and the pitch is the clock, and a gallery whose roar climbed toward dawn at a different hour would be a gallery of bats leaving at the wrong time into a sky of the wrong light. It did not make the machine new. It held the labor where it was. It set its hand against the slope. The roar the next evening fell as it had always fallen and the bats poured out through the grille in their twenty-minute stream and crossed the incoming swifts in a braid that did not tangle, and nothing in the room could have told you that anything behind the wall was different, because nothing was, and that was the point. The power had bought the gallery time and left no receipt.

But it could not buy forever. Nothing does. And so its second act was the one that mattered, and it was not a saving. It was a shaping. It took the end that was coming — the night when the labor stops — and it did not cancel it. It turned it into a fall.

When the machine finally fails, it will fail the way the roar fails in the evening: by degrees, a band at a time, low and thick first and then the middle and the whine last, over a span longer than lineages. The frogs who sing in the high band will have grandchildren who can sing a little lower, and their grandchildren lower still, coming down the staircase of frequencies as the wall's noise recedes below them, so that when the silence finally opens under the gallery it will find frogs who already know how to call in it. The bats' ears will have generations to thicken back toward what they were. The swifts will scream indoors for the first time in a thousand years and be surprised, and then not surprised. The first thing that comes through the grille hunting by sound will find a den that has had a hundred years to relearn the possibility of being warned — not saved from the world, but returned to it at a walk instead of thrown. That is the whole of what the power did behind the wall. It made the ending a slope. It made it a tide.

Last, it came back through the grille and did the thing it had been saving.

The person was on the stone. Gray light in a slab, cloud at the slats, the honey band at knee height, the roar sitting in the chest of everything. The power did not sit on the stone. It settled in the deposit beside it, a little off, in the place where the ringed tail with the bad leg pauses at the fall of the sound — the same spot, because that spot has been agreed on and the power was not going to open negotiations. It did not speak. Speech is provincial. It did not touch. A touch would have been a sign, and a sign is a warning, and the one gift the gallery gives that no other room in the structure gives is that a body here is not warned of anything. It sat as weather sits. It stayed for the length of a cup filling. The person cleaned a corner of the stone with the side of a hand and looked up at the grille and did not look at the power, because there was nothing to look at, and that was the whole of it. Two bodies in the den, regarded by each other as nothing in particular, very close together, and very warm.

It left with the swifts, through the slats, into the cloud. It took nothing. It left the stone as it found it. The person will never know, and this is not a flaw in the benevolence but its shape: in the Weeping Gallery not knowing why is the condition of everything. The frogs do not know why the wall is warm. The bats do not know why the stripes are dry. The film that built the flowstone cathedral does not know it is a builder. The power had understood, by the end of one tide, that the only kindness available to it here was to join that ignorance from the inside — to be one more thing the gallery does without knowing that it does it.

Benevolence, in most places, is a kind of announcement. Here it had to learn to be inaudible. The roar stayed with it for an hour after the grille, as a ringing, and then the ringing revealed itself to be the sound of a room it had left exactly as it found it, and would go on finding, for a long time yet, and then a little longer than that.
