It would arrive the way everything arrives here—the way the sun does. It would land. And its first act would be the first mistake, because even a wise power looks for the one with a face. It would scan the pale horizon for the inhabitant and find furniture, and then it would stop, and correct, and look again, and this time see the groves as what they are: the ones who live here. That correction is the first thing it does, and it is not nothing. It is the difference between visiting a place and visiting someone.

Then comes the pressure. Benevolence is not a feeling so much as a leaning, and a power that has it feels it as a wanting-to-give. So the power stands on the bare stone under the violet sky and takes inventory of what it might give, and this inventory is the real work, because almost everything on it is wrong.

---

Water. It is worth naming the absurd one first because the absurd one is the most instructive. The reflex of kindness is to give what you would want, and a lowland mind looking at a place that has never been wet feels the dryness as a lack. One cloud. One rain. The power sees at once what that would be: rot, arriving in the only place rot has never been. Husks that had waited centuries to be drawn back into the wood, softening. Amber diluted. The closed account of a grove thrown open to evaporation. It does not seriously consider this. But it marks the shape of the reflex, because the reflex will return in subtler clothes.

A mind. This is the second temptation and the stronger one. The grove has spent an unimaginable life turning toward the light and has never once known that it did. The power could give it that: an interior, a witness inside the witnessing, so that at dusk when the leaves click shut something in there could feel the day as spent well. Here the power holds itself very still, because this is the kind of gift a benevolent power most wants to give and most needs to refuse. The grove has no self to lose, and that is not a wound. It is the condition of everything it is. To install a mind would not be to improve the grove. It would be to end the grove and begin a stranger in its wood—a new thing that woke up already old, carrying a history it never chose, looking out of a body that had been complete before it arrived. The power would be making a companion for itself and calling it a gift. It sets this down.

Company. The grove is alone in a way that the power, which knows what it is to be singular, may feel keenly. But the grove is not alone. It tastes its neighbors on every afternoon wind. Its hands are the climbers, who are shared across the whole tableland. Its feet are the grazers, who carry it past the horizon. Once in a generation it passes through another of its kind and comes out changed. The loneliness the power feels standing here is the power's. It notices whose it is and does not put it on the grove.

Safety. The shoots fail. A grazer's stride ends in a small dry packet of everything a new grove needs, and a finger of black-green rises from the flour, and most of them are gone within the year. The power could tilt this—could warm one grain of stone, or lend the flour a little willingness—and there would be more groves. But the failing belongs to the flour, and the flour is the slowest and deepest life here, and its refusals are part of the ground everything else stands on. The arithmetic that keeps the grazers alone is the same arithmetic that keeps the leaf-walls whole. Change one term and the whole equation has to find a new state, and it would, in time, but the power would have been the fastest thing that ever happened to this place, and *fast* is the only violence the plateau knows.

Permanence. It could hold the sun where it is. It could make the orbit an orbit forever. But the grove does not want stillness; the grove has never once wanted stillness. It wants direction. Everything it has ever done is a lean. To fix the sun would be to take from the grove the one thing it does.

---

So the inventory is nearly empty, and the power has learned something about the shape of its own kindness by watching almost all of it fail to fit. What remains is two things, and neither is large, and one of them is not a gift at all.

The first is this: it slows.

This is a real cost. A power lives at whatever speed it chooses, and it has been choosing, until now, the speed at which things can be understood. Here it chooses instead the speed at which things can be *met*. It takes on the year. It stands, in whatever way it stands, on the bare stone beside a grove, and it does not leave until it has seen the canopy come round once through its whole lean. Then it stays longer. It stays through a crossing.

No one has ever seen a crossing. The stone records it afterward as two lanes converging and a broad smoothed place and two lanes leaving, but no one has ever stood in the sixty years between—watched the leaf-walls touch and stop being walls, watched two roots draped over the same plate find each other in the flour, watched a shoot come up from one grove's root fed by the other's amber and belong to both. The power watches this. It watches two grazers arrive at the same wall and go in and not come out for a long time. It hears, in the hour after dusk, the dry rare friction of climbers who have never met resting in contact. And then it watches the sun move on and the doubled grove begin to decide what is whose, and it watches the deciding, which is the slowest and least legible thing it has ever attended to, and which is also—it understands this somewhere in the fortieth year—the only thing it has ever attended to that was attending back. Not to it. To the sun. But attending, with everything, continuously, the way the power itself attends. It has found, on a dry plateau under a violet sky, the thing it does, done without a self.

It does not tell anyone. There is no one here to tell, and the telling would be for the power's sake. It simply keeps the crossing, whole, the way a trunk keeps a leaf: sealed in, lacquered over, drawn on later.

---

The second thing is the sun.

Far off—so far that even the lanes do not reach back toward it—the star that this whole plateau has spent its life facing will end. The power looks at how. It looks the way it looked at the inventory: honestly, without flinching from what it finds. And what it finds is that the ending, left alone, would be the one thing the plateau has no answer for. Stars do not die at grove-speed. They swell, or flare, or collapse, and the change comes across the sky in a season, in a day, in an hour—faster than a leaf can turn, faster than a root can let go on the shaded side and take hold on the lit. The grove would face the light as it has always faced the light and the light would simply become something else before the grove could lean.

The power will not save it. It has already set permanence down. But there is a difference between not preventing an end and permitting a bad one, and a wise power can see the difference and act on it without touching the plateau at all. It reaches, instead, for the star. It does not stop the ending. It stretches it. It arranges—in whatever way a power arranges a star—that the dimming will be long, and the reddening slow, and the lowering gentle, so that every change that ever comes down onto this stone will come slower than a grove can answer.

That is the whole intervention. It is one adjustment, made very far away, to the only thing the grove has ever cared about, and its entire content is a promise: *nothing will arrive here faster than you.*

So the groves will follow the sun down. As it reddens they will lean harder into what is left. As it lowers they will draw the amber in and close earlier each dusk and open later each dawn, and the lanes behind them will bend toward the sinking light in arcs longer than any now written on the stone. The grazers will walk shorter lines between groves that stand closer together. The flour will slow past slowness. And at the end, on some long final evening, the last grove will close leaf by leaf—each closing a single audible tick, the plateau textured one last time with that slow irregular clicking from everywhere and nowhere—facing what remains of the light, doing exactly the one thing it has always done, and doing it adequately, because the power has seen to it that adequacy would be enough. The grove will never know that anything was arranged. It will only know, in the way it knows, that the sun was there and then was there a little less, and that it turned toward it, and turned toward it, and turned toward it, and then was still.

---

After that the power leaves, or rather stays in a way that cannot be told from sky. It takes nothing. It has changed nothing on the plateau. If you stood on the bare stone between groves at noon and took stock, you would find what was always there: violet-black sky, a sun that does not forgive, cool air moving over your hands, the long faint lanes of forests that have walked across the pale ground for longer than anything has been alive to watch.

Except that now something has watched. And when the stone is gone and the lanes with it, when the star is a cinder and the flour has closed over the last print, the shape of what happened here will be somewhere. One crossing, whole. The lean of a canopy through one full year. The tick of a leaf finishing a movement it began an hour ago. Sealed in and kept, the way the grove keeps everything, by the only witness the plateau ever had—which, having considered every gift it could bring, brought in the end only the two things the grove itself already knew how to give: attention, and time.
