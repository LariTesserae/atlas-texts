It would come up from below. There is no other way to arrive, and a power wise enough to be worth the name would know better than to arrive any other way — to appear at the zenith, say, where the air is a different thing and nothing that lives here can follow. So it would climb. Weeks of switchbacks through cold, wet, ordinary country, and then the ground going level and staying level, and the warmth coming up through whatever it had instead of boot soles.

The first thing it would have to do is slow down.

At its own pace the Shelf is a single frame. The domes are still. The plumes are still. The people are not visible at all, because a life here is one exposure and a power sees in exposures of ten thousand years. It would stand at the edge where the slope flattens and see a country that had finished happening and no one in it, and if it were only benevolent it might go away again, satisfied that there was nothing here to help. But it is also wise, and wisdom on the Shelf is the same skill it is everywhere: noticing very small things over very long intervals. So it would stand there and slow, and slow, and go on slowing, until the sun became a thing that rose and set, and the plumes acquired a base, and the base acquired a ring of brick and iron, and something in the ring exhaled.

Every four seconds.

It would slow until it could hear that. Then it would go in.

---

The first thing it would want to do is give them water.

This is not a failing. It is the correct first thought of anything good that has come to a dry place. It would look at the iron cisterns and the drop-by-drop beading in the long cold pipes and the cup carried in two enormous careful hands, and it would know — the way it knows things, all at once — exactly where under the mountain the old water went, and how far down, and how little it would take to bring some of it back up through a vent as clean steam. A spring. One. Enough that the cup would not need to be measured.

And then it would watch a man take the cup from a woman at the crusher, and see him pause with it at his lips and turn and hold it out first to the child beside him, and see the child refuse with a flat hand, and see him drink. And it would understand that in the whole hushed country this was the only ceremony. The only occasion for a raised voice. The one thing the Shelf people had built, out of the little the mountain breathed, that was theirs and not the vent's. A spring would wash that out in a firing. Nobody would offer the cup. Nobody would need to.

It would set the water down.

---

The second thing it would want to give them is air.

Thicker air. Just a little. Enough that the tall ones would not go back down or die young. Enough that a shout would carry a quarter mile and a bell would ring instead of knock, and a mother could hear her child from across the works floor without watching for a hand.

It would stand a while with that one. It would think about the hands. About the mirror-code that grew into speech, and the speech that stays in short pieces with long spaces, and the eleven nights against the same warm wall saying almost nothing, which is how people here come to belong to each other. It would think about what it means that there is no word for hurry except the one for the wet. And it would understand that the hush was not a deprivation the people had learned to bear but the shape of them — that the four-second breath and the low voice and the lifted hand were what a person *was* here, and that to give them air would be to give them a different country and take this one away while they were standing in it.

It would set the air down too. But more slowly.

---

It would hesitate longest at the towers.

The keepers. The thin ones with the grained faces and the eyes that have looked too long for one point of orange across black distance. The ones who stop coming down. A benevolent power would find them, one by one, sixty feet up at the tops of their iron columns with the cold beginning, wrapped in everything they owned, and it would want — with the whole of whatever it has for a heart — to warm the cabin. Just the cabin. Just enough.

But the keepers are the loud ones. The ones who did not do well in the hush and were given the lamp instead, so their loudness would have somewhere to go. And the cold at the top is where it goes. Take the cold away and the loudness comes back down the ladder with them and there is no place for it in the works and never was. A power that warmed the cabins would be doing to the keepers what a spring would do to the cup: making the thing that was theirs unnecessary.

So it would climb one tower instead. One. The one whose keeper had not come down for the train in two loops.

It would sit in the cabin through a night and not warm anything. It would watch the flash come in from forty miles east, held and repeated, and watch the keeper's hands try for the mirror and not quite manage, and it would put the light through itself. It can do that. A wise power is, among other things, a very good mirror. It would pass the flash west, and the next tower would take it, and nobody along the loop would know that for one night one link in the ring was not a person. Which is true, in a way, of every night: the light passes regardless. What the keeper lacks is not the light.

In the morning it would go down the ladder first. Not carrying anyone. Only going first, so that there was something at the bottom.

The keeper might follow. If she did, the power would lie down beside her at the base with a hand on the pipe, and the pipe would tick, and they would sleep. And in eleven days the train would come and the keeper would stand at the foot of the tower and lift a hand and the driver would lift a hand and the power would not be there any more. It would have gone on. That is all the help there is for a keeper: to be, once, the face at the base. It does not last. It is not meant to.

Or she might not follow. Then the power would wait at the bottom anyway, for a loop or two, until someone came out from the town and climbed up, and it would go up behind them and be there when they found what they found, and it would not make it easier, because it is not the kind of thing that should be made easier, and then it would help sweep the cabin.

---

There is a ceiling. The power would go up to it, because it can, and put its attention through, and know what is on the other side. It would know where the flyers go when they work their way up through the table of smoke and do not come down. It would know what the standers are reading in the sun. It would be able to come back down and say.

And it would look at the standers on the domes at dusk with their necks up in slow segments, and the walkers spiralling out across the plain after the rest of what they had been, and the flyers spread flat in the basins waiting for the ground to lift them, and the keepers looking east for one point of orange, and the old woman with her palm on the wall waiting to feel it warmer. Every living thing on the Shelf facing something it cannot reach. Not in anguish. In posture. The reaching is the thing that arranges them between the heat and the sky; take away the not-reaching and they would have no direction to stand in.

It would leave the ceiling closed. It would come back with nothing to say, and say nothing, and be in that respect exactly like everyone else who had gone up.

---

It would look into the heaps. It would know, then, whether the tailings become grazers and whether the grazers were people and whether the walkers ever find what they are following. It would know if the whole slow spiral of slag and shell and ochre skin is one thing at different ages or three things that only look alike in the dust.

*You are not required to believe.* The people say that, and the power would hear in it not a hedge but an instruction. A country where the dead go out at dusk and lie down on a warm flank and are not told apart by morning is a country that has made its peace by not asking. It would not be kindness to answer. It would be a kind of digging, and nobody digs into the oldest heaps.

---

So what would it do?

Here is what I think. After the tower, after the ceiling, after setting down the water and the air and the answers one by one on the warm stone the way you set down tools you have decided not to use — it would walk out at dusk to the oldest tailings around the oldest town, and find a place on the flank that no shape was lying in, and lie down. Back to the slag. Face to the sky. The position a Shelf person always takes to sleep.

Not to die. It does not do that. But to stop hurrying, which is the whole of the requirement, and which even a wise power has to learn somewhere, and there is nowhere better to learn it.

The grazers would move in around it in the night. The dust would begin. In the morning the workers would step over it on the way to the crushers and not look twice, because a new shape on the heap is not the kind of thing anyone here would remark on. And it would stay. Warm from below, cold from above, one more thing arranged between the two. It would feel the quarter inch when the heap shifted and not know, any more than the heap knew, whether it was the heap shifting or itself. It would let the question go the way the people let it go. It would breathe, if it breathed, every four seconds.

That is the only benevolence a country that has finished happening can receive without being unmade: for something to happen there, slowly, alongside it, and be told apart from nothing.

---

There would be one trace. Small enough that you would have to stay a long time to hear of it, and it is said quietly, and not often.

A keeper on a tower out on the eastern trestle — not the one it sat with; another, years later, or earlier, the Shelf does not count very exactly — would see a light one night from a direction there was no tower in. Not east, not west. Up, a little, and out over the plain where the heaps are. One point of orange, held and repeated, in the blunt old code that came before the speech.

*Wait,* it would say. And then, after a pause the length of a flywheel's stroke: *Wait longer.*

She would not know what to do with it. There is no mirror set for that direction. But the keepers carry the light, that is all they do, so she would turn to the west and pass it on, and forty miles west the next keeper would take it without knowing where it had come from, because they never know, and pass it on, and it would go round the loop in a night and come back to her from the east and she would pass it on again.

Nobody in the towns would decode it. Most of what crosses the loop is never decoded. But it would be in the ring now — a message with no origin, circling at walking pace in the dark, a lamp answering a lamp answering a lamp — and it would still be going round when the rail had worn away and only the trough was left. Which is, when you come down to it, the only kind of help that lasts here: not a spring, not warmth at the top of the tower, not an answer from above the ceiling. Just one more voice in the low, patient traffic of the light, saying the only two things the Shelf has ever needed said, to no one in particular, and saying them for a very long time.
