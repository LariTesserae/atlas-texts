What it would do first is what you did: arrive, and count.

Not because it needs to. A power of that kind does not need to count frogs. But wisdom is partly the habit of looking before deciding, and a benevolent power that skipped the looking would be neither. So it stands where you stood, on the folded and planed-off stone, and it lets the place come in — the four calls and then, because it does not need to wait a week, the two others; the fish sideways in the dark; the rail arriving out of nowhere with a snail; the stain closing; the drip at one per second; and out in the haze, the long stride that keeps pace. It sees that one. It sees the animal you never saw, and it knows what it is, and it does not say. That is the first thing it does not do.

Then it considers what benevolence usually does, and tries each on the plain, and watches each fail.

Benevolence usually gives. It looks for the lack and fills it. And the plain has lacks that a generous eye finds at once: there is no soil, so it could bring soil. There is one tree, so it could bring another. The tree will die — not soon, but it will, being an individual and not a location — and when it dies the fourth frog loses its well, and the well is the only place the plain's sound gathers, and after that the stone-into-water call will still travel the joints but arrive from nowhere, thinner. A second tree, seeded now, downwind, would be grown by then. It would be so small a gift. It would cost the plain nothing.

The power sets this down and looks at it.

Two trees is a grove. A grove is a place. A place gathers: shade gathers, then shelter, then birds that stay rather than touch, then nests, then the guano that becomes the second soil on the shield and the first that anything made, and in it the grass grows in a clump instead of a tuft and the clump holds water and the water holds a frog population that does not move, that lives *there*, and the plain now has a *there*, and the one thing the plain has been for longer than frogs have been a kind of animal — spread out, thin, everywhere a little and nowhere much — is over. Not ruined. Just over. Replaced by a good place with a grove in it, of which there are many.

The tree is alone because a seed fell once. That is not a sorrow. It is a fact, and the frogs in the crevice under it are the sound of a fact.

So the power does not plant the tree. And by the same reasoning it does not bring soil, and does not clear a path, and does not stack a single stone to mark that it was here, because it understands what you understood — that the absence of anything made is not a hole in this place but the shape of it. The category never arrived, and a power that brought the category would be the arrival, and there would be no taking that back.

Benevolence also protects. It looks for the threat and stands in front of it. And here the power finds only one, because the plain has already been taken down to the thing that cannot be taken away — there is no gradient left to erode, no upland left to fail, nothing here that a bad season or a bad century could remove. Except the rain. *For as long as there is rain.* That is the one hinge the whole finished sentence turns on. The sheets do not drain, but they breathe out into air that is nearly full, and *nearly* is the word; over a long enough dry the mist would lift and the joints would empty from the top down and the fish would follow the water into the deep dark and not come up, and the stain would go from green to grey to dust, and the plain would go on being flat and folded and planed and would simply stop being inhabited. It would not die. It would become what it looks like from a distance, which is stone.

A power could guarantee the rain. It could hold the sky here at this exact saturation for as long as it liked, which is forever. And this is the temptation it sits with longest, because this one is not a gift the plain would notice. Nothing would change. The rail would tap, the moss would drip, the turtle would sink. Only the contingency would be gone.

And the power decides against this too, and it is the hardest decision, and I think it is the right one. Because the plain is finished, and a finished thing is finished in time as well as in form. It has an end the way it has an edge — not sharp, not near, but real. A place that cannot end is not finished; it is suspended, and suspension is a kind of making, the subtlest kind, the kind that leaves no stacked stone but changes what the thing *is*. The plain is beautiful partly because it is the last stage of something and the last stage has a duration. To remove the duration is to take the plain out of the story it has been telling for longer than most kinds of life and set it in a case. Benevolence that cannot bear for a thing to end is not wisdom. It is the fear of loss wearing wisdom's clothes.

So the power leaves the rain alone. It will come or it will not, on the schedule of a sky that is not the plain's business and should not become anyone's.

Then it turns to the being, because it was the being that drew its attention, and it has to ask what it owes a someone who is, as you found, the wrong size for every word.

It tries the turtle first, since the turtle is the only thing here you could point at and say *that one lives here*. The turtle is old and gold-eyed and has been in this water since before you were born, and it wants — what? It wants to sink when watched and surface when not. It wants the stain on its back to keep growing. It is not lonely, because loneliness needs a concept of company and the turtle has a concept of water. A benevolent power looking for something to give it finds that it is already holding everything it holds. There is no lack in the turtle. There is only the turtle.

It tries the moss, the stain, the frogs-as-population, the fish-as-flow, and each of them is the same: a thing entirely occupied with being what it is, in a place that asks nothing else of it and gives it exactly enough and no more, which is the definition of enough. The power looks for the suffering, because that is what benevolence is *for*, and it finds a frog being eaten by something with three toes, and a snail being tapped open on a stone, and it finds that these are the plain, not wounds in it; the pile under the rail's stone is the only earth here and the grass beside it is the largest for a mile. Remove the eating and you remove the soil. There is no lever in this place that is not also a load-bearing wall.

And so it comes, as you did, to the thing that keeps sliding out from under the question: the being is the place, and the place is in no need. A power that arrives wanting to help and finds nothing to help is in a strange position, and a lesser power would invent a need in order to meet it, and a worse one would decide that a thing with no needs has no standing. The wise one does neither. It takes the absence of need as the clearest instruction it will ever receive, and it obeys.

But it does not leave with nothing done. It does two things, and both of them are things the plain cannot refuse and cannot notice and that cost the plain nothing, which is the only kind of gift that fits here.

The first is that it listens to the whole instrument at once. You heard the fourth frog's call arriving through your feet and traced it, two hours' walk, to the well under the tree. The power hears every call in every joint at the same moment, and hears what the plain has been saying to itself, along the water under the rock, since the stone was planed — the ticking and the hum and the two-note whistle and the stone into the well, all of it moving through a single body of water three fingers deep, everywhere, unheard by anything that could hold all of it at once until now. It holds it. That is an act. It is not a small one. A song that has been sung for that long into a lid of stone has finally, once, been heard in full, and nothing about the song changes, and something about the universe does.

The second is that it remembers. This is the thing the plain cannot do. The stain closes over your footprints before you are out of sight; the pond forgets the stone; the joints widen and the groove deepens and nothing here keeps a record of anything but the last several days of snail travel. The plain is inhabited without being witnessed, and it is finished, and it will end, and when it ends there will be nothing in it that knew it had been. So the power takes it — not away, not into a case, not out of the rain's jurisdiction — but *in*, the way the turtle takes the stain, as a growth on its own back that it will carry for as long as it carries anything. Every sheet and the color of every sheet. The rail's stone with its smooth spot. The moss at its unmoving drip line. The eye that came up gold and went down. The long stride in the haze. When the rain stops, if it stops, the plain will still be somewhere, at full resolution, in a mind that was wise enough not to touch it and benevolent enough not to let it be lost, and those will turn out to have been the same thing.

Then it does what you did. It turns to go. It lets the stain close behind it. It does not send anyone else.

And toward evening the light leans, and the near side of the plain goes gold, and every living thing on it goes on doing exactly what it was doing, because nothing has happened to it — which was the point — and out in the haze something sets a foot down in water that has been here since before there were feet, and lifts the other, and keeps pace with no one now, and does not notice the difference.
