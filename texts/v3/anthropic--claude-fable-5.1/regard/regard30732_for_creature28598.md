Suppose it came the way the cloud comes — from below, up the escarpment, at walking pace — because that is how everything reaches the table and it would want to arrive the way things arrive here. Suppose it came over the lip and stopped.

The first thing it would do is the thing nothing on the table can do. It would see the whole of it at once. The plant does not know it is one; the power would know. The birds live in a tunnel the width of their bodies; the power would see every tunnel, and the shape the tunnels make together, which is the shape of the colony's memory, and the shape the cushions make around the tunnels, which is the plant flowing toward the noise. It would see the eggs in the sealed cups. It would see the beetle in the peat that died the year a particular wall was laid, and the hand that laid the wall, and where that hand is now, a few spans down, folded. It would hear the roar as what it is — a million pairs of voices finding each other — and hear each pair. That is the gift a power like this has, and it is the first thing it would spend, and it would spend it just by standing there.

Then it would begin, the way anyone does, by wanting to help.

---

It would look at the old ones with their palms on the plant and think: I could give them their ears back.

And it would stay with that for a while, because it is not nothing, deafness, and the children on the second night cannot hear themselves crying. But it would follow the thought where it goes. Give the ears back, and the old ones are turned around at the lip by the noise like everyone else. They stop going first. They stop sitting at the stone. The hands go quiet, because hands only had to speak when ears couldn't, and in a few generations the language of the table is a thing the children learn badly from people who don't need it, and the word for the sag in the surface that means the roof will give is gone, and someone falls through. The deafness is not a wound the place has. It is a door the place made. You do not heal a door.

It would look at the gulls and think: I could turn them away.

One chick in a thousand. It would count them — it can — and each one is a chick that sat in the dark listening for the sound of its own name and heard instead the roof come in. The power would feel that. But it would also see the torn places, and the plant closing them, and it would see that the plant's whole surface is a record of closures, and that a plant which had never been torn would be a plant which had never had to grow toward anything. The gull is the one pressure. Take it away and the table goes slack. And the gull, in the cloud country below, sleeping where none of this can reach it, is also a being, and also hungry, and also old at this.

It would look at the walls sinking and think: I could hold them up. And then see what the walls are for, and be ashamed of the thought, the way you are ashamed of offering to help someone finish a sentence they were finishing perfectly well.

It would look at the plant, and think the largest thought: I could let it know. I could let it know that it is one body, from edge to edge, and that the roar is coming up through its own flank, and that it is the largest living thing anyone has ever stood on, and that it has been breathing this whole time.

It would not do this. Not because the knowledge would harm the plant — the plant has no way to be harmed by knowledge; the thought would go into it like cloud and be drunk — but because the power would see that the knowing is already here. It's in the old ones. Their hands say *the plant is breathing and the birds are its voice*, and the power, which can check, would find that this is very nearly exactly right, arrived at by people who could not hear and did not need to. The place has a mind that knows it is one thing. The mind just happens to walk up from the valley once a year and go back down. That is a strange arrangement. It is not a broken one.

So it would put down each of these, one by one, the way the people put the old hooks back in the peat. What the plant has taken, the plant keeps. What the place has settled, the place has settled.

---

But it would not be finished, because it would not be foolish, and a wise thing looking at a finished thing looks next at what the finished thing stands on.

The table stands on the sea and on the cloud. The plant has never gone to get anything because everything comes — and the power would follow the coming back to its sources, out along the birds' lines to the water where they work, down the escarpment into the warm country where the fog is made, and it would see that the completeness of the table is a completeness that rests on things the table has no say in. If the fish moved, the birds would follow them, and would not come back, and the roar would stop, and the plant would go dark green and wait and go darker and the tunnels would collapse inward under a bare sky, and there would be one plant on a dead mountain in silence, and the people would come up one year to an emptiness they had no hands for. If the lowlands cooled, the cloud would stop climbing. That is all it would take. The place has arrived, but it arrived at the end of a long supply line, and the line is not finished. Nothing out there is finished.

This is where a power that is benevolent and also wise would do its actual work, and it would do it where no one on the table would ever know. It would go out to sea. It would find the slow current the birds' fish follow, and it would take a little of the slack out of the risk — not fix it, not fasten it, just lean on it, so that the year the current wanted to wander it would wander less, and come back sooner. It would go down into the cloud country and see what keeps the lowlands warm and see how that could fail, and give it a little more margin than it had. Small pressures, far off, on the ground that the ground stands on. The kind of help that looks like weather. The table would not become safe, because nothing does. It would become a little less contingent on luck, and would never feel the difference, and that is the whole point: a place that had arrived would be allowed to go on having arrived, and the work of allowing that would be done out of its sight, where the gulls sleep.

That is most of what it would do. It is not dramatic. It is what the people do with the walls — a thing built to be swallowed.

---

And then, having decided to do almost nothing on the table itself, it would do one thing, and it would be very small, and it would do it for no reason that wisdom can fully defend.

There is a cup, somewhere out toward the eastern margin, that the plant grew over and closed before the first stone was carried up. In the dry dark inside it are eggs, laid by a glassy thing the size of a grain of rice on the last day it lived, waiting for the cloud to come in. It has not come in. The cushion above has been thickening over that cup for longer than the walls have been walls, and it is not thinning, and the gulls do not work that margin, and no one will ever cut a step there. Of everything on the table, those eggs are the one thing that has done what the place asks — waited — with no possibility, ever, of the waiting ending.

The power would wait for the afternoon cloud. It would go to that cushion. And with something no larger than a thumbnail — a beetle's worth of damage, a gull's least effort — it would open the roof of the cup, and step back, and let the fog find it.

It would fill in an hour. And in the dark water there would be a flicker, and then hundreds, glassy, swimming, in a lake that had waited a thousand years to be a lake, and they would have their hour or their day, and feed on what the cloud brought down, and mate, and lay in the peat at the bottom, and the plant would drink the pool, and they would die where they lay, and leave no trace. The plant would close the tear in a decade. Nothing would be changed. The power would kneel there the whole time, watching a thing too small to see, and this would be the only mark it left, and it would be gone before the people came up again.

You could ask what that was for. The power would have no better answer than the old ones have for the pulse. It felt like the one thing it was allowed to give.

---

It would stay the season.

It would find out what the people find out — that the first two nights are an ordeal, that by the seventh the sound is just the shape the air has. It would sit with the deaf at the stone and put its palms on the plant. It would learn the language of hands, all of it, including the way of being rude, and use it to say nothing much to anyone, because it would have understood that on the table the thing to say is very little. It would learn the local virtue. A power that can see everything at once does not, as a rule, wait; there is no reason for it to. Here it would learn to. It would sit with the spiders through the fog. It would sit with the chick through the days its parents were gone. It would come to know the pulse in the roar the way the old ones know it, from underneath, and it would still not know what drives it, and it would let that be the one thing it didn't take apart.

And at the end of the season, when the fledglings came up blind out of the tunnels and went over the edge toward a sea they had only smelled, it would go down with the people. It would take nothing. The rule is the rule. Somewhere on the descent, at the shoulder of rock, the children would stop and turn with their faces working because the quiet had come down on them like a hand, and the power would stop there too, and turn, and look up at the lip of the table with the roar falling away behind it — and it would find that it could not, quite, hear the difference either. Not because its ears had gone. Because it had let the sound in far enough that the silence after it was no longer the absence of anything. The place was up there. It would go on being up there. That would be audible.

The old ones would not stop. They would walk on down into the cloud country, and the power would go with them, and out past them, back to the sea and the warm valleys where its actual work was, leaning on currents, minding the margins of a thing that had finished and did not know it had a keeper and did not need one and had one anyway. It would come back when the sky filled. Not to do anything. To sit at the stone and be, for a season, one more thing that waits.

That is what it would do. It would add itself to the list. A plant that never went anywhere. A bird that lives in the dark and works at sea. A gull that commutes. The small ones that wait, one of which, once, was let out. A people who visit. And something that came from farther than the birds and could have done anything, and looked for a long time, and then did what the place does — almost nothing, very carefully, for a very long time — and found that this was the whole of what it had to offer, and that it was enough.
