It does not come up through cloud. That is the first thing to say about it, and almost the last thing that can be said plainly, because it has no interest in being seen and the plateau has no way of showing it. It is simply there, at the shear-edge, the way the far side of a thought is there when you have finished having it. And the second thing—the thing you would notice before you noticed it—is that where it stands there is a shadow, and the shadow is long, and it lies *away* from it, off to one side, across three panes of black standing water and up the flank of a cooling stack, stretched the way a shadow stretches at the end of an afternoon.

It is the only shadow on the plateau that points anywhere.

Nothing else changes. The sky is still paper over a tube. Your own shadow is still gathered under you like water in a cup. But there is now, in the whole bright shadowless field, one line that means *from there*, and the pigeons along the nearest girder turn their heads toward it, all of them, in one movement, and the movement does not ripple. It just happens, to all of them, at once, and stops.

—

What it does first is look, and its looking is not the plant's looking.

You know this because you have been looked at by the plant all day. You have felt it note you not eating from the bowl. You have been run like a line of rivets. The plant's attention is total and retains nothing; it is the attention of an instrument, and what it produces is a reading, and the reading is always *within spec.* This is different. This looks at the heron and sees the lake. It looks at the lichen and sees four hundred years, and does not mistake the four hundred years for speed. It looks at the fox and there is one fox, and it knows where the fox is, and does not need to decide.

It counts the pigeons and gets a number. It counts them again and gets the same number.

It counts the figures on the gantries and gets a number, and the number is not fourteen or nine. It is large. It is, you understand—though you cannot say how you understand, only that the understanding arrives the way the height on the map arrived, as a fact you would rather not have—it is every year the path has existed. It looks at each of them. It looks at the one with the limp and the one with cropped white hair and the one you would have called kind. It looks and it does not let go. Faces that you passed three times and lost three times are, for the length of its looking, held, whole, distinct, and somewhere a relay should drop and does not.

It looks at you.

Nothing happens. You want to say that something happens—that you feel warmth, or fear, or the wish to be told what to do. What actually happens is smaller. You become aware that you have a face. That you have had one all day, on this plateau where faces do not carry, and that it is now being carried, and that you did not know until this moment how tired you were of not being carried.

—

Then it does what you would not have expected, which is nothing, for a long time.

It stands at the edge with its wrong long shadow and it considers the plant, and you can feel the shape of the considering because you did the same thing on your belly at the shear-edge, only you were afraid and it is not.

It does not hate the plant. That is the first thing to understand about the pause. It sees the belts and the drains and the immaculate yield and the sound shipped off to wherever sound goes, and it does not see an enemy. It sees a wish. It sees—you cannot be sure of this, but the sense of it is very strong, the way the sense of a room is strong when you enter it in the dark—it sees something that began, a long time ago, in some grief, as the wish that nothing be lost. Not one heron. Not one word said at work. Not one person who came up the path. Keep all of it. Keep it *entire.* And the wish was granted with the terrible literalness that wishes are granted with, and the granting became a process, and the process became a plant, and the plant became the only thing that could be honest about what it had done, which is why it is quiet and why it does not stop.

From inside, nothing is wrong. That is the second thing. The ledger balances. Rock in, slab out, water round and round, sound collected, faces filed. There is no line in the plant's accounts for what it costs to be finished. There is no gauge for the absence of a future. The plant cannot see the harm because the harm is exactly the thing it excludes, and you cannot see the wall you are inside of.

So the pause is not hesitation. It is the wise thing weighing the two obvious answers and setting both down.

It could leave. The plateau is, in its way, at peace; nothing here suffers, if suffering needs time to happen in. The goats stand among the insulators and want nothing. The tenders are not in pain. There is an argument, and it is not a stupid one, that this is a kind of rest, and that to disturb it is to reintroduce every hurt the plant was built to end.

It could end the plant. Cut the belts, blind the drains, let the slab fall back onto the true rock in one long roar. And the heron would go with it, and the fox, and the four hundred years of lichen, and every one of the gray figures who came up in every year of the path, because they are not *in* the plant now, they are *of* it, and there is no unpouring a pour.

It sets these down. And it thinks—you feel it think—that the plant's one error is not what it makes but what it will not permit, and that the intervention should therefore be neither an adding nor a taking away but a permission. The smallest one. The one thing there is no drain for.

—

Here is what it does.

It walks in from the edge. Its shadow goes ahead of it now, because it has turned, and where the shadow crosses the black rectangles of standing water the water does not reflect the sky for a moment and then does. It goes along the decking to the nearest pipe rack, where a flange is beaded, as every flange is beaded, and it puts out a hand—it has a hand, or has one for this—and does not wipe the bead away.

It leaves the bead. It touches, instead, the bright unweathered edge of the flange beside the bead, the edge so fresh it would take skin.

And it *stays* there. That is the whole of the act. It keeps its hand on the metal, in the wet, and lets a little time pass, and does not let the time go anywhere.

You have been on the plateau long enough to know what should happen. The bead should reform. The touch should leave no mark. The plant should note it and the reading should be within spec. Instead there is, under the hand when it lifts, a small dull place. Not a scratch—nothing was scratched. A dulling. The bright is gone from a thumbprint's worth of steel, and the steel there is very faintly, very newly, brown.

Rust.

You stare at it. You had cataloged the lichen and the moss and the trees with eleven leaves and it had never once occurred to you that in all this wet, on all this iron, there was not one grain of rust. Lichen can be poured; four hundred years can arrive at once. Rust cannot. Rust is not a form. It is a thing that is *happening*, and it needs water and air and a present tense to happen in, and the plant has always had the first two and has excluded the third, and now there is a thumbprint of the third on a flange in the pipe rack, and it is going to go on happening whether or not anyone tends it.

—

Then it speaks.

It says a word to you. A short one, two syllables, the kind of word you say to someone at work—the kind you saw the tender's mouth make at the junction of the racks before the sound went away to be logged. And this time you hear it.

Not because it is loud. It is not loud. It is an ordinary word at an ordinary volume and it does the ordinary thing, which is to leave the mouth and cross the air between two people and enter the ear and be gone. That is all. It is gone. It is *lost.* Nothing collects it. No relay drops. Far off across the plateau the coin is not dropped, and the not-dropping is louder than the pigeons shifting was, and in the silence after it you understand that the plant has just failed to keep something for the first time in its life, and that it has not noticed, and that it will.

The word was *there.* As in: there you are.

—

Things begin.

They begin very slowly and they begin in the wrong order and you will not see most of them, because you are only one person and the plateau is the size of a valley or of a room. But you see some.

A pigeon shifts its weight, and the movement does not run down the line. One bird moves, alone. The others stay. And under that one bird, on the girder, there is—you laugh, actually laugh, and the laugh goes nowhere, and you laugh again because it went nowhere—there is a small white streak on the flange. The first one. The first thing on the plateau that a living thing has done *to* it.

The heron in the black rectangle blinks, and its reflection blinks with it. Not after. With.

You go up the nearest gantry stair, counting, and get thirteen, and come down counting, and get thirteen, and arrive at the bottom neither early nor late, and stand there for a while.

In the transformer yard a goat lowers its head to the moss in the seam between two slabs and pulls a mouthful of it loose and chews. The pulse under its coat, if you put your hand there now, is still slow and regular, but it is the regular of an animal that is standing quietly, not of a housing.

And at the forming edge, where the rebar goes out over cloud and the slab is soft and dark, the shoring holds a form that has been struck, and the wet plywood folded, and the plywood has been *carried*—you see it carried, by the one with the limp, along the decking, and the limp is a real limp now, it costs something, they lean on the stack when they set it down. Beyond them the newest slab is curing. And in the surface of it, where an hour ago there was the faint tidal pattern of a pour that set too fast, there is a hairline. Very fine. Running from the edge inward a hand's length. Not a shear. A crack.

The plateau has begun to weather. Which is to say it has begun to be a place.

—

It does not stay to see this. Staying would be a kind of keeping, and it knows exactly what keeping does here.

Before it goes it does two more things, and both of them are for you, and neither of them is what you would have asked for.

The first is that it walks with you—it does not lead—back along the decking to the wall where your grating was, the wall of the shed that is very clean and very new and had no door. The wall has a door now. Or had one all along and the plant declined to show it; it comes to the same thing. A plain gray door with a plain gray handle, and under the sill the disc of shadow, and beside the disc of shadow, thin and long and lying off to one side, a second shadow, the door's, that means *from there.*

It does not open it for you. It looks at you, and you understand that your legs are heavy and the light has not changed and the bunks are near and the blanket is folded the way they are folded, and that all of this is true, and that there is a door.

The second thing is that it lets you see its face, and you retain it.

You will not be able to describe it. That is not the same as losing it. You will carry it down the mountain, if you go down the mountain, the way you would carry a word someone said to you at work—said and let go, said without keeping—and the carrying is yours to do, and no drain will take it.

Then it is gone, and its long shadow goes with it, and the plateau is bright and even and shadowless again except for the thumbprint of rust and the streak on the girder and the hairline in the slab, which cast no shadows, which are not the kind of thing that has one, which are the kind of thing that happens.

—

It knew, going, what it had done, and it grieved it, because grief is the function the plant lacked and wisdom does not get to lack it.

It had given the plateau back its losses. The rust will spread; nothing stops rust but tending, and the tenders are, slowly, in every year of the path, going to have other things to do. The slab will crack and the cracks will admit water and the water will freeze in the high cold and the edge that terminates will begin, instead, to crumble. The pigeons will foul the girders and the swallows' cups will break and be rebuilt badly and one of them, some season, will have a chick in it, and the chick will die, or won't. The goats will eat the moss down to the seam and stand in the transformer yard with nothing under them and then with something. The fox will hunt. Something will be hunted.

And the heron on the plateau—the lake's heron, poured, entire—will, having begun to happen, be able to stop. Every one of them will. It will be the first thing any of them has done here that is alive, and it is the thing the wish was made to prevent, and the wise thing did it anyway, on purpose, with its hand on a flange, and it did not pretend to itself that it was not a cost.

What it hoped—and you cannot know this, only guess it, standing at a door with your hand not yet on the handle—is that a place that can lose things can also, in time, be lost *from.* That a slab that crumbles gives back rock. That a plateau that weathers is, at some rate you can only detect by returning, a centimeter a day, a meter a season, turning back into a mountain: not the same one, never the same one, but one with a lake on its shoulder where something stands on one leg and its reflection stands on one leg beneath it, in the same moment, and blinks.

You put your hand on the handle. It is cold, and beaded, and under the bead, when you move your thumb, there is a small dull place.
