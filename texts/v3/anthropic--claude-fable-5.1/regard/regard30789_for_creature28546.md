It came in with the cloud, because there was no other way in, and because it understood at once that arriving any other way — from above, in light — would be a discourtesy.

It had been drawn by something. Powers of this kind are drawn the way the water is drawn: by a gradient too slight to see, toward wherever something is lowest. From a great way off it had felt a place where a great deal was being held and very little said, and it had taken that for need, because in most places it is.

So it came down through the several hundred meters of water and stood on the moss, and the moss took its weight and sprang back slowly, and a bead formed somewhere at its lowest point and fell, and another began.

---

It looked first.

That is the part of wisdom that is least understood: most of it is looking, and most of the looking is waiting to be sure that what one sees is what is there, and not what one brought.

It looked for what the plateau would call a long time and the lower world would call a season. It stood in the passages and let the pulse come through the walls until it could tell the fourth thud from the fifth. It walked the rows with the screen crews out past the last lamp and came back as wet as they did. It sat by the wheels with the old. It watched the moss crews scrape and joke and scrape. It went out with the six carriers, twice, and stood in the drip while the moss was folded over, and walked back.

It saw the things that were understood. It saw the youngest cry when the cloud lifted and it saw the older children stand very still, and it saw the breath let out on every side when the cloud closed, and it noticed that the breath was not only relief. It saw the old stop brushing the green from their coats, and it looked very closely at that moment, the moment of stopping, because everything turned on whether it was a defeat or a consent, and it could not be sure, and it did not pretend to be.

It saw the count that was not made, and it did not make it either.

---

Then, having looked, it considered.

A power like this considers in a particular way. It holds each possibility up whole, with everything that would follow from it, the way a surveyor holds a length of string and sees where the water would go.

It considered lifting the cloud.

It saw the town in that hard pale light, all at once and for good — the sun small, the far edge showing on every side, every child on the plateau crying together and the adults not knowing where to put their eyes. It saw the moss go pale at the tips within a week. It saw the beads stop forming. It saw the troughs go dry, and the channels, and the wheels slow and stop, and the lamps go out one passage at a time, and the pulse stop, and a thousand people standing in the sudden enormous silence of a place where nothing falls. It set that down.

It considered warming the stone. But the stone is older than anything and does not care, and to warm it would be to make it care, briefly, and then to leave it to go cold again; and the people would have known warmth once, which is a different condition from never having known it, and a worse one. It set that down.

It considered the feet. It considered — this was closer, this one it held for a long time — the cough. The small dry cough of the dryers, the only dry thing on the plateau. It could take that away. It saw that if it did, the drying houses would be a place people stayed longer, and the quarter around them would fill, and the young families would not need to be there and would be there anyway, and the dryers would no longer age faster than anyone and would no longer be, on the whole, the most cheerful, and it did not understand the connection between those things but it saw that there was one. It set that down too, less easily.

It considered the ones who had gone down the cable. It followed a few of them, in the way it could, and found them in the lower world — in the dry and the bright, with people who talked more, gasping in the other direction. It saw that some of them stood at the edges of rivers in the evening and watched the water go and could not have said why. It did not bring them back. They had chosen, and the choosing had cost them, and the cost was theirs and not a thing to be taken from them. But it stood near them, some evenings, at the river, and this was the first thing it did that it did not set down.

It considered the surveyors — the gradient living in a handful of old heads and in books wrapped in oilcloth. It could set the knowing into the stone itself, so that the water would always find its way after the last of them was laid in the far basin. It looked at that and saw that the water already knew. The channels do not need anyone. The surveyors were not for the water. They were for the people, so that when the water hesitated, someone would come slowly, and look for a long time, and say a small thing, and the town would remember that it was someone's. It would not take that away.

---

One night it went out to the screens and did a foolish thing.

The pale beetles were on the mesh, as they are every night, for reasons no one has asked, and one by one they were losing their hold and going down into the troughs. It began to lift them out. It did this with the carefulness of which it was capable, which is very great, and set each one on the moss, and watched it go back to the wire. It did this for some hours.

Toward the first thud it stopped, because it had felt — through the whole faint gradient of the town, in the way it could feel such things — the wheels turn a very little less.

Nothing here is wasted. It had been told so by the children, who are told so every morning, and it had not listened, because it had assumed that a kindness that small could not cost anything. It put the last beetle back into the water and stood for a while in the drip and then went in.

---

It went to the far basin.

It went past the last row of screens and past the place where the dead are laid and on, out of sight of everything, into the cloud, where the moss is deepest and has never been touched and the walking is slow because every step goes in to the knee. It went until it felt the ground change under it — not softer, but more attentive.

The people say something lives there. They say it flatly, as information, and they say it is large, and they do not say it is an animal, and when a child asks they nod toward the cloud and use the word that means the peat is breathing. The power had thought, on arriving, that it would find out which of these things was true. Now it found that the people had been right not to distinguish, because there was no distinction. What lay under the moss of the far basin was the dead, all of them, a very long time's worth, become one slow warm thing; and it was the peat, coming apart with the patient heat of things coming apart; and it was the plateau, which was here first and finished long ago and had been lying under the cloud, the whole time, paying attention.

That was what it was. An attention. Slower than the power's by an order it had no name for, and without hands, and without any wish for hands — but a watching, and a benevolent one, in the way that stone is benevolent: it takes everything back and gives it up slowly and does not resent the screens or the mills or the cutting, having no capacity for resentment, and it will fold the last chimney under the moss with exactly the care it folds a body.

The power stood in it for a long time. It did not speak, because nothing here has a voice, and it did not wish to be the first. But something passed between them, the way something passes between two people at the rail who do not turn to look at each other. The power asked, in the way it could, whether the people were being kept or being held. And the answer was a settling, somewhere deep, and a slow bulge in the moss a few meters off, and a breath of gas that went away toward the town to find a flue and burn there blue for a moment while a dryer watched and did not name it.

It understood, then, that it had been drawn here by a gradient it had misread. It had felt a place where a great deal was held and little said, and had taken that for need. But the holding was not the people's. It was the plateau's. And the plateau had already made the decision the power had spent a season making: to look, and not to fix, and to fold things back in.

It had come to a place that already had what it came to bring. Only slower. Only without the wish to intervene — or having set that wish down, long ago, so long ago that the setting-down had become rock.

---

So it did very little, and it did it at the scale the place allowed.

The old sit by the wheels for the warmth off the axles, which is a very little. It made it a very little more — the amount of one more body in a room, no more than that, so that a bench in a passage would be as warm with one old person on it as it had been with two. Nobody noticed. A few people, in the seasons after, sat somewhat longer and did not know why, and the moss came up onto their coats somewhat sooner, and some of them brushed it off and some of them did not.

It stood at the rail. There is nearly always someone there; it saw to it that there was always someone there. In the deep of the night, in the long pause when only the wheels turn, when the bench by the chute was empty, it stood at the rail and listened to the whole plateau's harvest going over the lip into the white, and it knew why the people came, which they did not, and it did not tell them, because the not-knowing was also theirs.

And it saw, one morning, that the moss had come onto it. On its north side, where it had stood longest. A faint green, no more than a shadow.

It considered staying. Moss will grow on anything that stops. It is said of machinery and of people, and it is not said unkindly, and it would not have been unkind to say it of a power that had come in with the cloud and found nothing to fix and sat down. There was a version of this in which it went green on the bench beside the others and became, in a long time, a fact about the place, like the cold.

But a power that becomes a fact about a place has stopped being what it was, and the plateau did not need another thing that did not care. It had one. The power did care; that was its whole nature; and it saw that the caring, kept here long enough, would be worn down until only the resistant floor remained, and the floor would be the plateau's, not its own.

---

It left the way the water leaves.

Not up, in light, the way it had refused to come. Over the iron lip and down the chute and out past the rim into the cloud, with the whole day's harvest around it, falling, and keeping falling. No sound came back. On the bench someone was sitting, and did not look up, and if they felt anything it was that the sound of the water had changed for a moment, filled slightly, and then was as it had been.

It went to find out where the water goes. No one on the plateau has ever asked. It was the one thing about the place it had not been able to see from the top, and it had learned from the cable crews that a glimpse — a different green, a line that might be a river — was as much as anyone had ever wanted, and it respected that.

It has not come back to tell them. It may, one day, in some way that the plateau permits — a bead forming a little sooner on a fern at the right moment; a beetle that finds its hold; a surveyor who looks for a long time and says a small thing and is right. But it is more likely that it will not, and that this is the correct end of the matter: that the place was seen, once, whole, by something that could have changed it and chose not to; and that the plateau, which had been watching the whole time, noted this, and settled slightly, and went on being what it is.
