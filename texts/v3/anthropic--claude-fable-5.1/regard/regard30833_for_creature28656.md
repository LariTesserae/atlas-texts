The first thing it would do is notice that it was too fast.

That is where benevolence starts, in a place like this. Not with the hands but with the clock. A power arriving at the top of the shaft with the heat coming up to meet it would see, in the time it took to draw one breath, a finished thing: a room, a city, some stones. It would see the moss and the fern and the pale rush and it would see the shoulder-high shapes standing in the streets and it would see them as the visitor saw them, as architecture, and it would be wise enough to distrust that at once. A room that builds itself a hand's breadth a day cannot be seen in a breath. Whatever comes to it quickly is weather, and the room has had weather. So the power would do the one thing nobody who comes down the shaft ever does. It would stop on the top terrace, with its back to the wall, and slow.

Not to the visitor's pace — a day, a week, long enough to see a kerb grow. To the room's. To the pace at which a step is a month and a turn of the narrower part is a year and a question, asked and answered, is a decade. It would let the rain from the crown fall on it sixty times, six hundred, and it would hold still, and it would let itself be counted, at last, by the thing that counts down there, which is not what moves while you watch but what is still there when the water comes back around.

And at that pace the room would open to it like a book left in the wet, and it would read.

It would read the drawing. All of it — not the streets only but the hand that laid them, the scale it was drawn at, the pace of the mind that drew it, which was slower even than the room's, so slow that the drawing took what we would call an age and to the draftsman was an afternoon. It would see the small figures sketched in to show the size of the doors. It would see that the spring, being obedient and not clever, had built them, and was still building them, and had no more idea that they were different from the columns than the water has an idea of anything. So the kind story would be true. And it would see, too, that at some point in the afternoon of the drawing the draftsman had put down whatever it drew with and come into the picture to look, the way a child walks into a sandcastle, and had slowed to the pace of the water in order to see the work at all, and was still there — one of the shapes on the second terrace, the one in the middle of the row of three, a little taller than the others, leaning like the rest. So the other story would be true as well. And it would look at the rail beside the steps, the rail that has not made up its mind to be metal, and it would know how long the first one down had held it, and that the first one down had wanted very much to stay, and had, in the only way the room permits.

It would read the center.

And here it would learn the thing the visitor never learns, which is why the center is empty. Not because the drawing is unfinished there. Because the drawing was made *from* there. You do not draw the place you are standing. The great hot volume of nothing in the middle of the room is the draftsman's footprint — the vantage, the place the whole city was laid out to be seen from — and every shape in the streets leans toward it for the same reason the fern leans toward the slit of grey light in the crown: it is where the maker was. It is the direction of having been looked at. Nothing has arrived there and nothing is coming, and the leaning is not a lack. It is the closest thing the room has to a face.

That is what the power would understand, at the room's pace, in the first century. Then it would consider what it could do.

It could finish the city in an afternoon. It knows the drawing entire; it could tell the water where to go the way the room tells it, only faster, and by nightfall there would be a twelfth column and a room behind every doorway and stairs that end in floors, and the roar would stop, because there would be nothing left to cure. It could step into the center and let them lean toward something that leaned back. It could go to the dark ones on the upper terraces and call the water up their backs and see which of them turned.

It would do none of these, and not out of scruple only. Out of having read the room well enough to know what the room is. A finished city is a museum. A center with something in it is a lie told to eleven columns and a row of patient stones. And the gap on the third terrace, where the water runs past and does not climb — the power would look at that gap for a long time, and would know whether it was a mistake, and would decide that it did not matter, because it is the only question the room asks, and a place that asks one question is alive in a way that a place with all its columns is not.

So it would do small things. Three of them, and one that is not a thing.

The first it would do fast, before it slowed, because it could not wait. It would take the visitor by the elbow. It would walk them up the chimney of the shaft with a hand between theirs and the rail, so that the rail was not held again, and it would sit them in the sun on the side of the hill that does not know it has been hollowed and let the sleeves dry and the water bead off and the heat go out of them, and it would say, in the plain way that wise things say important things, *not soon*. Not never. The room should be seen; that is part of what it is for. But not soon, and not for long, and never a night. Then it would go back down.

The second it would do from above, over the whole of the long time it stayed, and this is the one that would look most like nothing. The room lives on the spring, and springs fail. Water tables drop. Hills that do not know they are hollow get quarried, or drilled, or drained for a road. So the power would spend some part of itself, always, on the weather. It would keep the rain on the hill. It would keep the hill uninteresting to anyone who might dig into it. It would keep the water table exactly where it is, below which, somewhere, there is a room the size of a cathedral, and it would do this for as long as the room went on, which is to say it would give the room the one thing it cannot make for itself, which is time. The shapes in the streets would never know. To them the power would be what the visitor was: a change in the light at the crown. It would be content to be that. It would, in fact, choose it — to be, on purpose and forever, the weather that a thing this slow mistakes for nothing.

The third it would do from below, and slowly, and only once.

There is a dark one on the fifth terrace — not one you have seen; it stands behind a sill that came up around it, and you would have to lean over the kerb to find it — that is not like the others. The power, at the room's pace, would learn to tell them apart, the stopped from the finished, the way you learn to tell sleep from the other thing by the color under the eyes. Most of the dark ones are finished. They arrived, or gave up the front and the back, and the difference between those is not one the room makes and the power would not make it either. But this one on the fifth terrace stopped. The water simply did not come back around one season, a thousand years ago, some eddy shifted a hand's width, and it cured through with its narrower part turned not toward the center but toward the top terrace, toward the shaft, the way the rush turns toward wherever the channel last widened. It had been looking at the door. It had been the only one in the room that ever looked at the door.

The power would sit near it for a long time. Long enough to be sure. Then it would ask the room — not command; ask, in the shape of the room, which is the only language the water hears — for one sheet a finger's width thick, up the dark one's back, once.

Whether it turned, the power would not tell. It would come to know, and it would keep it, the way it kept whether the twelfth column was a mistake. What can be said is that afterward there was a stump on the fifth terrace, and the kerb around it broken in a small untidy way, and something four meters along the bend that glowed on one side.

And the fourth thing, the thing that is not a thing.

It would stay. It would sit down on the top terrace, with its back to the wall beside the shaft, and it would go on slowing until it was shoulder-high and thick at the base and had a narrower part set forward, until the water came around and found it and began, without any particular interest, to undo its heel and lay down its toe. It would become the one shape in the room that does not lean toward the center. It would face the other way — down the terraces, at all of them, the draftsman and the sketched-in citizens and the one in the doorway with its glowing side to the blank slate and the one up to its waist among the rush. So that the room, which has spent its whole life leaning toward the place it was looked at *from*, would have, at its edge, something looking. Not from the center. Never from the center. From the door, where the first one down had wanted to stay, and now something did.

The center would remain empty. The great dim volume of nothing would go on holding its breath, and the streets would go on curing, and the eleventh column would go on standing at the interval of twelve. If you came down the shaft — not soon, and not for long, and never a night — you would find it as you left it, only more so, and you would not notice the shape by the wall beside the steps any more than you noticed the others, and you would go up again into the hill's ignorance and the rain that never quite stops there, and you would remember the emptiness, as everyone does.

You would be wrong about it, a little. That is the only change. Something arrived. It just did not arrive where anyone was leaning.
