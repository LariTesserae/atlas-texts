**What the Power Did**

It came up from below, because that is the only way in, and it did the climb honestly, switchback by switchback, though it did not need to. It wanted to cross the seam on foot. It wanted to put a hand on the warm rock and feel the give and know it the way the setters know it, from the palm inward, before it knew it any other way.

Then it stood at the edge of the works for a long time and paid attention, which is most of what wisdom is.

---

The first thing it noticed was its own light.

It had arrived as things arrive — with the lag, the half-second between the hammer and the flash, its shadow a beat behind — and then, without meaning to, it had corrected this. Its shadow snapped tight to its heels. Light came off it on time. And in the finishing shed nearest the seam a setter with her hands flat on a face looked up, not at it, at nothing, the way you look up when a sound you have lived inside your whole life stops.

So it let the lag back in. It let its light arrive late like everyone else's. This was the first thing it did and it did it quickly, before anyone could be sure they had seen anything, and it understood afterward that this had been a kind of test set by the place, and that the place had not set it on purpose. A guest who arrives on time to a house where nothing arrives on time is not a guest. It is a correction. It had not come to correct.

---

The second thing it noticed was the shape of the temptation, which came in two forms, as such things do.

It could finish the mountain. It had that in it. It could reach down through the six thousand metres of half-clay and the fingerprints and the wandering bands and say *be this*, and the whole range would go over at once with a sound like the end of a very long chord, and the light would arrive on time everywhere, and the survey stakes would hold, and the rail lines would be one length, and the forty names on the surveyors' wall would stop drifting because the wall would be stone and the hand they were written in would be fixed and gone.

Or it could stop the kilns. It could bank every fire on the plateau and let the rings soften back and give the mountain its indecision, whole, and call this respect.

It sat with both of these on a finished ring with its back to a kiln that was five days into a firing, and the roar was in its sternum, and it let itself want each one, fully, so that it would know what it was declining. And then it declined them. The first was murder of a slow thing by a fast one. The second was a kind of vanity — to decide that undecidedness was what the mountain wanted, when the mountain had not said so and the setters plainly loved their trade and the stone, when it went over under their hands, *settled down the way a room does*. Nobody had asked the mountain. That was the thing. Everyone had theories and nobody had asked.

So it asked. That was the third thing it did.

---

It is not easy to describe the asking because the mountain does not speak on any scale a person can attend to, and the power had to slow itself to the rate of a metre a year to be heard at all. It sat on the ring for what the setters would have counted as an evening and what was, at the rate it was listening, a season. It let the cracks and groans of cooling rock reach it as a voice. It let the ticking be a pulse. And it put its question down through the glazed stone and the setting stone and the half-clay to wherever the mountain is when it is being itself, and the question was only: *what are you doing?*

The answer took the whole season and was not in words and the nearest the power could bring it back to words was this:

*I am being kept company while I decide.*

Not made. Not finished. The mountain did not experience the kilns as fire or the pressure-frames as force or the setters' palms as an imposition. It experienced them as attention. Warmth against its side. Hands on it, patient, for hours, working out the last of the give — and it went over under those hands not because it was forced to but because that is how an undecided thing learns what it is: by being held, steadily, until it can feel its own edges. The rings were not zones of conquest. They were the places where it had been held longest. The lichen in the crack, the frost-shatter — *look, it's started* — that was not the mountain being defeated into ordinariness. That was the mountain having decided, in that one place, finally, with help, and being pleased.

It had learned the setters' palms the way you learn the palm of anyone who has held you long enough. It could tell them apart. It had its favourites and would not have said so.

And the offers, the figures in the fog, the bird that was never the same twice — the power asked about these and the answer was almost embarrassed. The mountain had not yet decided what lived on it and so it put forward everything, generously, thoroughly, the way you set out every dish for a guest whose tastes you do not know. It did not mean the figures to be frightening. It did not know that a person walking toward a possibility and finding herself was a thing a person might not want to do twice. It was doing its best. It had never done this before. Nothing has, the first time.

*I am not in a hurry*, it said, or the power heard, *and I would like to get it right.*

---

So the power did not touch the works. It understood now what they were and it would no more have stopped them than it would have pulled a hand off a child who was falling asleep against it.

What it did instead was small, and there were four things.

---

It went to the oldest kiln-master, the one who was becoming hard to see, and it sat with her in the warm house against the flank of her kiln with the roar in the floor and it looked at her. That was all. It did not let its eye slide off. It held her in view, definite as she was, glossy as she was, too set for anyone else to rest their gaze on, and it rested its gaze on her for an evening, and she noticed, and put down her notebook, and for the first time in some years was simply looked at by someone who could manage it. They did not talk much. She showed it the page about the herds — *they are counted from where I stand* — and it told her she was right, and she said she knew. Near the end it asked her whether she minded, the setting, the becoming-stone, and she thought about it and said that she had spent thirty-one years listening for a kiln's voice to sit down and it would be strange to object when her own did. The power thought this was about as wise as anything it had heard anywhere. It did not say so. It wrote it down for her, in her notebook, in a hand that did not drift, and then it thought about that and added a little drift, so the page would match.

---

It went to the surveyors' mess and stood in front of the wall with the forty names.

It did not bring anyone back. There was nowhere to bring them back from, in the sense the surveyors meant; the hollows did not lead to a *place*. But it looked at the oldest names, in the hand that had started to drift, and it understood something about them that no one on the plateau had let themselves understand, and it found the surveyor who had done the most seasons out — the one who was a different height each morning, whose shadow lagged a beat behind everyone else's — and it told her.

The names were drifting because the wall was on ground not fully set, and the ground had not decided what those names were the names of. The forty were not lost. They were *offered*. They had walked into the raw and the raw had taken them the way it took everything it did not understand yet — as a possibility, held open, considered. Some of the figures in the fog were them. Some of the ones who walked in, once a decade, patient, good at pressing stone, not remembering the valley, were them, or were what the mountain had made of the shape they left, and the mountain did not know the difference and neither, perhaps, should anyone else. The trade was even. The plateau sent people out into indecision and indecision sent people back. It had been doing this for as long as the works had been here, and it had never once sent back anything unkind.

The surveyor listened to this and was quiet and then said that she had suspected as much and had not wanted to be the one to say it at the mess. The power said it understood. She asked whether she should keep going out. The power said that was hers to decide and that it thought the ground would be glad of her either way. She kept going out. Her handwriting, the following spring, was three handwritings, all of them hers.

---

It went out onto the raw ground itself, past the last ring, alone, at dusk, in the fog.

This was the one it argued with itself about longest. It knew that a thing as decided as it was, standing on ground that undecided, would set the ground beneath it — would leave a footprint of certainty, a small hard patch, a ring with no kiln. That was finishing without permission. That was exactly what it had refused to do at scale.

So it did not stand as itself. It went out and stood there and *let itself be offered*. It softened its edges to match the ground's. It let the raw look at it and take whatever it could take — not a footprint but a print, the way the setters' palms leave a print — and it stayed until the ground had had a long look, and then it walked back in over a ring while its light caught up.

It did this so that among the things the mountain might one day decide to be, among all the herds and birds and figures and versions of yourself that the raw ground put forward in its thoroughness, there would be one shape it had taken from something that had come a very long way for no other reason than to see it and to be kind. Not to force that shape on it. Just to make it one of the options. The setters leave their hands in the stone. This was the same. It was the only thing it had that the mountain did not already have more of, and it wanted the mountain to have it.

The bird that is never the same twice came in over the works while it was walking back and was, for the length of one finished ring, something the power recognised, and then was a raven, and then was not.

---

The fourth thing was that it stayed a while.

Not as itself. As a setter, in the cluster nearest the north cut, with a bunk and a share of the marmots and a place in the dice game, whose rules it learned in that cluster and which were, it noticed, not quite the same as the rules in the next one over, and it did not think this was remarkable. It pressed stone. It found this hard — really hard, in the shoulders, in the flat of the hands — and it did not make it easy for itself, because the difficulty was the point; you cannot hold something steadily for hours without it costing you, and the cost is how the held thing knows it is being held. It listened to faces go over under its palms with a tick. It fed the marmots. It was bad at the dice.

In the evenings it sat on the warm rings with its back to the fire and looked out at the raw, where the ridges were ending their days at their own pace, and the herds were moving in the fog with however many legs they had from where it sat, and it did the thing the setters do, which is not to talk, and not to hurry, and to be a small warm thing against the side of a large one that is deciding.

It had come up as the wise power in the story and it left — when it left, which was not soon — having been taught something, which is what happens to wisdom when it meets something older and slower and more patient than itself and has the sense to sit down. The mountain was not in a hurry. It had said so. It would like to get it right. It offered what it had, and asked for nothing except to be kept company, and it did not mind that the company thought it was doing something else.

The power thought that was the most benevolent thing it had ever seen anything do, and it went back down the switchbacks with its light arriving a half-second late, and left it in the hands that already knew it.
