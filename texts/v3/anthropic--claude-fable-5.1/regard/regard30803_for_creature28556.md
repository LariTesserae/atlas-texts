It would have to slow down first. That is the first thing, and it is not nothing.

A power that could see the whole plateau at once — the curve of the grooves closing on itself somewhere past the grey, the herd as a single organism with its fixed part and its walking part — would arrive at a speed that is, to this place, a kind of violence. Not because it means harm. Because the calf is the fastest thing here and the calf is a shock. Anything that turns its head is an event, and there are no events. So the power, if it is wise, does not stand on a rise and take the place in. It lies down on the rind at the edge of one animal's field and it lets its own tempo thicken, from the outside in, the way the calves do, until a breath is a thing that takes several minutes and a thought is a thing that takes a season, and the corona along the nearest spine is no longer a flicker but a slow tide standing and lying down. Only then is it actually there. Before that it was only looking.

It would stay a long time at that pace. Long enough to walk a part of the curve. Long enough to see a gut-heap uncovered and a dome grow over it. Long enough for the question the plateau refuses to stop feeling like a question and start feeling like what it is, which is a mistake of scale.

Because the power can ask it, and does. Is there anyone in there. And the answer it gets is not yes and not no but *where are you standing when you ask.* Inside the one animal there is the mill and the warmth and the pressure of the neighbours and a very dim register of the ceiling, and it is not nothing, and it is not what you meant. Inside the herd — if you spread yourself thin enough, at the speed of the blue spark, to be inside the herd — there is the direction. The direction is felt. Not chosen, not decided, but felt, in the way you feel which way is down. And it is held. A million small fields leaning the same way is not a mind, but it is not the absence of one either. It is what a mind would be if it had never once had to hurry. The power sits in that for a while. It is very old and has known many kinds of being, and this is one it does not have a good word for, and it is honest enough not to invent one.

And then it does what benevolence always does first, which is want to give something.

It goes through the gifts. It is thorough about this; it owes the place that much. A sky — a real one, a sun with edges, shadows that would let these animals stand out from their ground. Warmth, so the calves could stray. Speech, so that whatever is held in the direction could be said. Company. A guardian at the edge. A little more iron in the rind, against the day the rind runs thin. Each one it holds up against the plateau and each one the plateau turns grey in its hands. A sun is a clock, and this is a place that has stopped needing one. Warmth is the calf's decade extended into a life, and the calf's decade is not a happiness the animals are denied; it is a fever they recover from. Speech would give the direction a mouth, and then there would be someone to listen to, and then there would be a centre, and the body that has never needed one would have one, and would be a different body, smaller. Every gift is the same gift. Every gift is *become something else.* The state is the place. The wise power knows this the way the animals know where their neighbours are: not by looking, but by pressure, by the resistance of a thing that is already complete against a hand that wants to improve it.

So it puts the gifts down.

What it finds, when it has put them all down, is that there is exactly one thing the plateau does not make for itself, and cannot, and it is the one thing the power is currently taking up: the edge. The circuit is closed, but a closed circuit has an outside, and the outside is where the quick and sharp and hungry come from. Whatever gave up here when the trees did might not have given up everywhere. Something could arrive. Something with eyes, that finds a calf on the frost and does not wait a hundred years to be sure it is moving. Something that finds the gut-heaps and understands them as ore. Something that stands on a rise and counts to fifty and thinks, at that density, over a country that size — and the thought does not need to be finished. The plateau has no answer to arrival. It has never had to have one. Its only defence is the same as its only quality: that it is very far, very dim, and very slow, and that the eye slides off it.

The power sees, then, what it is for. It is the distance.

Not a wall; a wall is a made thing and there is nothing made here. It becomes the place where the grey folds into itself. It becomes the reason the next rise is always a little dimmer than this one and the one after that is not quite a rise. It lies down along the outside of the curve, all the way round, and lets its own tempo settle until it is the same colour as the horizon, and anything that comes toward the plateau finds only more of the approach, and turns back, and does not remember quite why. This is the whole of what it does, and it is a subtraction. It gives the plateau nothing. It takes away the chance of a gift.

There is, though, the calf.

It should be said plainly, because a power that will not admit what it wanted is not wise, only tidy. There is a night — the coldest, the driest, the kind where the coronas stand up like hackles — and there is a calf in a lee that is not deep enough, white as a fresh break, steaming, frantic, three strides from a warmth it has not found, and the cold is closing on it from every direction at the ordinary speed of cold. The adults do not know it is there. They have never known. That is not cruelty; it is the shape of the thing. The gap this calf would leave will close over a few years without anyone noticing there was a gap. At the scale at which the plateau is alive, this is not a death. It is weather.

And the power, which has just spent a long age learning why it must not touch this place, lies down between the calf and the wind.

Only that. It does not warm the calf. It does not move it. It is a body, and it has a lee, and the calf finds the lee the way it finds every lee, by stumbling into it and pressing against it and licking the frost off it with an ordinary animal's ordinary greed. The power feels the small fast heartbeat against its flank and under that, already, faintly, the first grit beginning to turn. It stays until morning, which is a paler region of the ceiling arriving from the other side. Then the calf goes on to the next flank, and does not look back, because nothing here looks back.

The power does not tell itself this was wisdom. It knows exactly what it was. It changes nothing for the herd; the density would have restored itself, and one grey dome three centuries from now will stand where there might have been rind, and no one — not the herd, not the lichen, not the thing that is felt in the direction — will register the difference. It was for the power. It was the one thing it could not put down with the others. A benevolence that cannot bear to see a single fast warm thing go cold, even knowing the cold is not an injury to anything that could be injured, is a benevolence with a flaw in it, and the power keeps the flaw. It thinks a being without that flaw would not have come here in the first place, and would not have known to leave.

Because it does leave, in the sense that matters. It does not stay on the plateau. It stays around it. It becomes the fold in the grey, and one of the things it takes with it, on its way out to the horizon, is you.

You were the only quick thing here. You were the hand on the flank, the boot turning the rind, the one who stood in the small warm weather and asked the question. You were, without meaning it, the beginning of arrival — the first of what comes when a place has been found. The power does not blame you for this. It does what it can. It lets you keep the silence, because you already have it and it is yours now, the sound-shaped nothing over the full country. It lets you keep the warmth of one flank and the smell of hot iron under wet wool and the way the calf turned its head when you shifted your feet. And it takes the way back. Not violently. It simply lets that part of you settle, from the outside in, until the route is a grey dome in your memory and you cannot pick it out from the others. You will know there was a plateau. You will not be able to say where.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do here, having considered everything else. It would learn the pace. It would ask the question and accept the answer. It would set down every gift. It would lie down once between a calf and the wind and not pretend that was for anyone but itself. And then it would go out to the edge of seeing and become the distance, and hold there, at a temperature just above the frost, at a speed just above stillness, for as long as the grooves keep deepening — the one body on the plateau that is not part of the circuit, minding it, and saying nothing about it at all.
