The first thing it does is slow down.

This is not a courtesy. It is the only way in. A power that arrives at the basin at the speed of thought sees what the visitor saw: a bright bowl of pale green, twenty grey columns, one bird, one shell, nothing happening. It could stand on the second ridge for what feels like a long time and learn nothing the visitor didn't. So it lets go of its own tempo the way you let a held breath go, and it settles into the basin's, and it waits for the air to cool by one degree.

Then it sees.

It sees the beading come across the basin not all at once but as a front, a slow tide of glitter moving from the cold side to the warm, and every frond in its path bowing as it passes. It sees the white plate lift from the snail's mouth — there are forty-one shells, it counts, and thirty-eight of them open — and the grey feet come out and find the frond-edges and eat. It sees the bird arrive at the tallest column not from anywhere but from another fin, lower, where it had been all along, folded into the grey. There are four birds. They do not sit together. They have never, as far as the basin remembers, sat together, and yet each of them comes to the same fin in turn, in the same order, every cycle, and drinks, and goes back to its own column. It is not company. It is something a very slow creature might have instead of company.

It sees the green climb.

This takes longer. It has to let a few hundred cycles go by, which it does the way you would let a few hundred beads form and fall. The white cord in the seam swells and the seam widens and a frond unfolds where there was grit. The mound at the footing of the nearest column rises. It reaches the lowest fin, and then the second, and here the power leans in, because this is the thing the visitor guessed at and could not stay to watch: the green is leaning toward the doorway and the doorway is the only reason the water stays, and if the green ever fills it, the doorway will close.

And the green reaches the third fin, and stops.

Not stops — recoils. The fins are colder than anything else in the basin. That is why they stay wet. Something comes down the wire from over the ridge and the columns spend it on cold, and the green, arriving at the third fin, finds the one place in the whole basin where the water does not go away, and also the one place where it is too cold to be alive. The cord in the seam at the footing goes slack, and the mound settles a hand's breadth, and the next cycle it tries again a little to the left, and recoils again. The green will circle the columns forever at the height of the second fin, like a crowd that has learned exactly where the doorway's cold begins. It was already decided. Whoever set the columns down set them at that temperature, and the setting was the answer to a question the green would not ask for a thousand cycles.

So the power looks over the ridge, because someone has been here before it.

It follows the pipes to the notch and down. The pipes go a long way. The glass sheathing beads and dries and beads. And at the bottom of the long slope there is a second basin — lower, warmer, drier, with slabs even sharper than the first and seams that are still only grit — and the pipes come in over its rim and end in twenty poured footings with nothing standing on them yet, and from each footing the water goes out onto the raw rock a little at a time, and the rock is dark where it goes, and in the dark there is a white cord, cord-thick, not yet swelling, only lying along a fracture the way water would.

That is what is being made. The thing that takes a lifetime to finish is another place like this one. The first basin is not a field feeding a factory. It is a nursery feeding a nursery. Someone lifted the rock into the cold so the air would condense; someone set the columns so the water would stay; someone ran the wire so the columns would be cold enough to keep the green at the second fin; and someone is doing it again, lower down, one basin at a time, at a speed only a stone or a very patient benevolence could call hurrying.

The power stands at the rim of the second basin and understands that it has arrived at someone else's kindness.

This changes what it may do. It considers, because it is thorough, and it is honest about each thing it sets aside.

It could make rain. Real rain, once, so the basin would know what a stream is. It sets this down almost at once. Rain would groove the rock and run off through the notch in an hour, and the ferns would drink for that hour and then have less than they had before, because the beading is a rationing and rain is a spending, and nothing here has ever spent. The basin does not need water. It needs the water to stay, and that is already being done.

It could give the basin a shadow. One proper shadow — a cloud, a dark, a place where the light does not come from everywhere. It holds this a while longer. There is a sweetness in the idea of the columns casting long grey stripes across the ferns toward what would be evening. But nothing here has ever needed to hide, and a shadow is an invitation to hide, and the four birds and forty-one snails and one green have built their whole slow lives on never having been hunted. The shadow would be a beautiful thing set down in a place that would have to become less trusting to make use of it. It sets that down too.

It could give the bird a second bird — and then it remembers there are four, and that they come to the fin in order, and that this is a thing they have, and that it is not for the power to say it is not enough.

It could make a sound. One sound, so the words would not stop a meter from the mouth. But there is nothing here to spend a sound on, and everything here that lives has agreed not to. To bring a sound would be to bring the first debt.

What is left is very small, and it takes the power a long time to find it, because it is the kind of thing you find only by staying.

The wire.

It goes over the far ridge. It does not sag as much as wire should. Something at the far end of it is supplying the cold that keeps the fins wet and the green at the second fin and the four birds fed and the forty-one shells opening and the second basin beginning. The green does not know about the wire. The birds sit under it and drink from what it does and have never looked up. The snails have never seen it. The whoever who ran it is down the slope building the next footing and cannot be in two places. And the basin is not waiting for anyone, that is true — but it is depending on something it has never once perceived, the way you depend on the ground's not opening, and if the wire ever went quiet, nothing here would know why the water had stopped staying. The fins would dry with the ferns. The green would climb the third fin, and the fourth, and choke the doorway it had wanted for a thousand cycles, and be, for a few hundred more, the most vigorous thing in the basin, and then not. The shells would seal and not open. The birds would go to the tallest fin in order and find it dry and sit there anyway, because they have no reflex for a dry fin, because the fin has never been dry.

So the power goes to the far end of the wire.

It does not do anything to the wire. The wire is fine. It stands where the wire begins, in whatever place that is, and it settles into the basin's tempo one more time, and it stays. If the wire is ever going to fail, it will be there, and it will have a very long time to see it coming, and it will know exactly what a single degree of cold is worth to forty-one shells. That is all. It is a small thing to be — a warm hand on the one part of a machine that no one in the basin can see — and it is the only gift the power could find that the basin could keep: one that drinks when the water comes, and makes no sound, and does nothing that can be watched.

Before it goes, it does one thing it did not plan to.

It goes back up the pipes to the first basin and stands, for one cycle, at the footing of the nearest column, and waits for the beading to come across the ferns, and when the front reaches it and the fins begin to glitter, it drinks. Not from a canteen. From the fin, at the right hour, with the fourth bird a meter away not moving. The water is cold and mineral and tastes of the break in the rock. It is the first visitor the basin has had that arrived at the correct speed and drank at the correct time, and the green, which leans toward doorways, does not lean toward it, and the bird, which has no category for it, looks at it with an eye like a drop of the same water and decides nothing.

Which is what a wise power would want to be looked at like, here. It has come to a place where the only harm anyone could do is to be fast, and the only help is to be slow enough that the help cannot be seen. It leaves no trace but the one fin it drank from, which dries a little sooner than the others that cycle, and is wet again the next, and no one counts it.

Then it goes to the far end of the wire, and stands there, and is not waited for either.
