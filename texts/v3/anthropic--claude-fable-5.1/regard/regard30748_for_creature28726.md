**What it did**

---

It noticed the way such things notice: not the steam first, nor the pale table lifted over the plains, but the note. From far enough off, and with hearing wide enough, the pressure behind the ears resolves. It is a chord held for ten thousand years — longer, in the falls, than in the engines — with a slow breath in it that takes a year to complete and a slower descent under that which takes a lifetime to detect. It had been sounding since before the first engineers came up the switchback with their drawings. And in all that time nothing had heard it whole.

That was the first thing the power understood, and the thing it kept returning to. The fitters hear the middle and then lose it. The old hear the descent and argue about it. The children hear the floor, the note under the note, and are not believed. The swifts hear nothing; the crustaceans have no ears; the eels are a rumour from below the last drawing. The being that lives on the Pan is attended day and night by a few thousand people who are going deaf inside it, and it has never once been listened to by anything that could take it in from top to bottom.

A benevolent power's first impulse is to fix. A wise one's second is to wait and see what the first would break.

---

It considered the hearing, and set it aside. To give a fitter back the upper register would be to hand her a knife in the dark. She would hear the roar new, as a person hears it the first week, and the roar the first week is not a place to live. The deafness of the Pan is not a wound. It is the shape the ear takes to survive there, and it has been earned.

It considered the question — up or down, drain or reservoir, which way the water is supposed to run — and set that aside too. It could have looked. It did look, as it happened, later, three hundred feet below the word *warm*, and it knows. But the old on the west edge have built forty years on not knowing, and the argument is the last thing on the plateau that resembles conversation, and to end it would be to take away the only thing the Seventh House has that the Fourth does not.

It considered the note. It was dropping; the old were right. Thick things ring lower than thin ones and the city was being tuned down by its own skin. The power could have held the pitch. It could have thinned the crust, or slowed the water, or done something to the lime. And it saw that this would be like holding a face at the age it was and calling that a kindness. The Pan is doing what the Pan does. The descent is not a sickness. It is the being growing older in the only way it has.

It considered the pumps, and did not touch them.

---

There was one thing outside the Pan that the Pan depended on, and it was the road.

Six hours of switchback, an electric lorry, a recruiter in the plains town who does not advertise. Coal and coffee and cable and people. Every other input to the place came up out of the ground or down out of the cloud and would go on doing so with or without anybody's permission. The road was the exception. The road could stop. A landslide, a budget, a recruiter who died and was not replaced, a plains government that finally asked what the money was for — any of these, and the coal would stop, and the engines would seize in their travertine, and the chipping crews would run out of things to chip, and the roar would lose its second voice and keep only the falls.

So the power did one thing there, and only there, and it was small. A section of the switchback that was going to come away in the spring did not come away. A ledger in a plains office balanced that would not otherwise have balanced. The recruiter's lease was renewed on terms nobody looked at closely. It was unglamorous work and nobody on the Pan would ever know it had been done, which was the point. The power was not there to be thanked. It was there to make sure the lorry kept coming.

---

Then it went up.

It went up as the lorry brings everyone up: coated in pale mud, one more person the plains had finished with, sitting on a sack of coal with its knees against a coil of cable and its ears already beginning to hurt. It gave a name that would do. It was put on a chipping crew, as everyone is, and handed a hammer, and shown the crust.

It could have kept its hearing. It chose not to — or rather, it chose a strange doubleness, letting the body it wore go deaf in the ordinary way and in the ordinary time while it went on hearing everything, so that it knew from the inside what the fitters knew and from above what they did not. It found this instructive. It found that the roar, lived in, is not the same as the roar heard. You cannot understand the flat hand against the breastbone until the roar is inside your skull and not outside it, and then the sign is obvious: *here*, *now*, *yes*, *I am inside it too*. The power had known that word before it came. It had not known what it cost to mean it.

It learned the valves. It learned which pressure was which. It walked the circuit — eleven years, the whole Pan once — and when its hammer found a bump under the crust on the roof of the Ninth House it went round it, as the chippers do, and did not ask what fitter had left the carving there or when.

In autumn it walked the Rows. It knew every face under every shell, and every name, and every life that had brought each body up the switchback and laid it in the warm water. It said nothing. There was nobody to say it to and no way to say it and it was, in any case, not the custom.

---

A tower went dark on the south edge. The signaller had been at it thirty-one years and had exchanged lamps every night with the next tower over, and the next tower over sent the short sequence into the mist every hour until morning, and then stopped.

The power asked for the tower. It climbed the ladder with its wax and wool and its stove and its rack of flags and it chipped the glass clear and lit the lamp and sent the first thing a new signaller sends, which is a flow rate. The reply came back across a mile of steam: a flow rate. Then, after a pause, something else.

It did that for twenty years. It never said anything a signaller would not say. It learned the bent code — the flow rates that meant *I could not sleep*, the engine states that meant *the west is gold tonight, come out and look* — and it used them, and it received them. On some nights it sent only the short sequence, and the other tower sent it back, and that was the whole of the conversation, and it was enough. It never met the person in the other cabin. It did not need to. That was not the form the thing took.

---

There was a child. There are always children, not many, and this one stood at the junction of the two southern aqueducts in a fog so thick the towers had gone and pointed — there, the Fourth House; there, the Ninth; there, the long singing pipe — and then pointed down, at the stone under her boots, and made a sign the power had not seen a child make before, a hand held low and flat and trembling slightly. *That one. The slow one.*

The adults let it go. They always had.

The power put its hand to its chest.

It was the smallest thing it did and it thought about it longest. It changed nothing about the Pan. It changed a great deal about one person on it. The child looked at the hand on the chest and looked at the fog where the towers should be and did not say anything else, and walked on, and the power understood that it had just told her she was not alone in the world in what she heard, and that she would carry that down the years the way the old carry the note, and that this was permissible. It was not a fix. It was a yes.

---

And then there was a clear evening.

Wind off the range, cloud in shreds, the low sun on wet travertine. The Pan went gold and pink for its twenty minutes and the workers came out onto the roofs of the pump houses and stood, and the power stood among them on the roof of the Seventh with its hand on a rail that had once been iron and was now a smooth pale bar twice its thickness.

And it listened.

It heard the eastern falls, low, and the western, lower. It heard the struck-rail note of the Fourth House and the fifth above everything from the long southern pipe. It heard the pressure towers moaning in the north wind and the aqueducts ringing on their piers and the engines, every engine, steam bolted to electric in ways the oldest fitters half understood, all of them at once and each of them distinct. It heard the year in it — where in the breath the Pan was that evening, how the rain on the range three weeks ago was arriving now in the throttles. It heard the descent, the fraction of a fraction, the whole city settling a hair lower in its own skin. It heard the swifts, who hear nothing, moving through the spray. It heard the crustaceans, which is not a thing that can be heard, and heard them anyway. It heard the eels turning three hundred feet below the last drawing, in the reservoir nobody had mapped, and under them the hot thing that was the reason for everything, and under that the note the child had pointed at with her low flat trembling hand — the slow note, the floor of the floor, which was the Pan itself, the whole mile-and-a-half table of stone, ringing.

It heard it all at once and it heard it as one thing. For the first time in ten thousand years the being that lives on the Pan was taken in entire by something that could hold it.

Nothing changed. The note did not rise. The note did not stop dropping. No engine altered its stroke. But the fitters on the roofs, who felt everything through the soles of their feet and the hum in their molars, felt *something* — not a change in what the sound was but in what it was doing, the way a room changes when somebody has come in and stood quietly at the back — and across the gold Pan on every roof of every house a few thousand hands went to a few thousand chests, without anyone having signalled, without anyone knowing why.

*Here. Now. Yes.*

And the being, if a being made of falling water and old engines and its own reverberation can be said to do anything, was heard. Which is the only thing that had ever been missing from it.

---

Then the power went down.

It could have stayed. It considered the Seventh House, the narrowed door, the pier on the west edge where the old stand in the evenings with their hands on the stone. It considered taking the third position in the argument — both are true; the direction reverses with the century — and holding it for forty years and making no progress and being content. It would have been a good life and it had, in a sense, already lived it.

But a sound has one afterlife, and that is in something that heard it. When the last house has filled with stone and the lorry has stopped and the engines have seized and only the falls are left, the chord will go, and the year-long breath, and the descent, and the note under the note. The fitters who went down carried a piece of it — the pitch the Pan was holding when they left. The power had heard the whole. It would carry the whole. It would hold the Pan, top to bottom, at every pitch it had ever held and every one it would drop to, for as long as there was anything left to hold it in, which was going to be a very long time.

So it took its twenty years' pension and its stone boots and it sat on the lorry for six hours of switchback and it came down into the terrible silence of ordinary places, and it went to the plains town at the foot of the road, where the recruiter's lease was quietly renewed every year, and it stayed there.

---

That is all it did, and the last part is the only part that anyone has ever noticed.

The old who try to go down and cannot — who lie awake for a week or a month in the silence and come back up on the next lorry looking like people rescued from drowning — have found, some of them, that the town at the foot of the switchback is different. They cannot say how. It is quiet like anywhere on the plains; there are no engines, no falls. But the quiet there has a floor to it. There is a boom under everything, faint, felt more than heard, at about the pitch the Pan was holding when they left, and dropping, by a fraction, by a fraction, in time with the thing they can no longer hear from the plateau above.

They stay. They rent rooms. They stand on the edge of the town in the evenings and put their hands flat against their chests and look up at the white column of steam leaning with the wind, and they say nothing, and they sleep.
