What it would do first is nothing, because the first thing any wise attention does in that bowl is what everything in it does: wait for the breath.

So it waits. The vents deepen. The reed-mice are already climbing. The grit throws itself into the air. And the pipes open their throats, and for twenty seconds the power is not a power but one more thing standing in the amber, and it lets that happen, because to be entirely itself for twenty seconds is the only thing the bowl has ever asked of anything.

Then the hiss. Then it looks.

---

It sees quickly what a lesser kindness would do here, and it sees each one as a killing.

It could bring water. One good cloud, hauled up from below and wrung out over the rim. And within a season every pipe in the bowl would soften from the inside and slump on its vent and go to paste, and the reed-mice would drown in a country that had never once been wet, and the swarm would rot in the bores, and the hunters would stand in the silence with their plates turning and turning toward nothing. The dryness is not the place's cruelty. It is the place's skin.

It could make the breath even. Reach down into the slow chamber and give it a rhythm, so the surges came every three hundred heartbeats and no hunter ever waited an hour pointed at a pipe. And the pipes, growing on the beat, would grow the same — the same length of branch, the same wall — and lose the ridged, leaning, damaged particularity that makes each one's voice its own, and the hunters would have nothing to learn, and the bowl would be a pipe organ, which is a thing built, not a thing that lives.

It could let the pipes know. That is the one that would tempt a power less wise than this, because it is the place's deepest sorrow if you read it as sorrow: two hundred throats that everything is arranged around, and not one of them aware. But to give a pipe awareness would be to give it something to want — to want to be heard, to want the crown not to shear, to want the reed-mice off its branch-tips — and a pipe cannot act on any want. It can only stand and say what its body allows. Awareness with no hands is not a gift. The pipes are the only things in the bowl that are not afraid, and this is why.

It could make the flutes carriable. Seal them somehow against the wet, so that a person could walk down into the rain with the bowl's voice in a satchel. And that would end the practice, because the practice is not making a thing to keep. The practice is coming a very long way to sit in the din until you can hear one voice in it, and cutting a piece of that voice's fallen body, and playing it back once, and leaving it against the wall. A flute that could go down would not be an answer. It would be a souvenir.

It could undeafen the mice, or take the grief out of the hunters, or tell the gliders they are safe below the rim, or stop the reed-mice climbing to where the tips used to be and starving there, holding on. Each of these is a mechanism wearing the face of a wound. The power looks at them a long time and does not touch them.

---

Then it looks for what is actually fragile, because benevolence that cannot find a real need is just admiration.

The pipes are not fragile. They have been coming apart and regrowing since before anything looked at them and they will do so until the chamber below stops filling, which is not a thing this power, however great, would presume to prevent. The animals are a fever and a fever tends itself. The thing from the cloud comes up every evening and needs nothing anyone can give it.

What is fragile is the thread.

Eleven cairns, at intervals of years or lifetimes. A path up through scree that does not move. The knowledge of what the hut is for and which way the doorway faces and how many days a body can bear. A stone chisel. A coil of gut. None of this regrows. A pipe that shears puts out new branches by the next day; a line of people that misses one generation is gone, and the cairns become heaps of stone beside screaming things, meaning nothing, and someone coming up the scree a thousand years later will think a shepherd built them.

The bowl cannot keep this thread. It does not know the thread exists. It goes to chalk around the chisel exactly as it goes to chalk around everything else.

So this is the first thing the power does, and it does it far from the bowl, in the wet country below where the drop goes down into cloud. It does not appear. It does not speak. It leans, with a pressure lighter than the pressure a reed-mouse feels before a surge, on the attention of a child who has just blown across the neck of a bottle and heard a low hoot come out of it and cannot let the sound go. That is all. It does not tell the child about the mountain. It does not have to. It only keeps the hoot from being a thing the child forgets by evening. There is a woman in the same valley who went up once, when she was young, and came down on the third day with her hands not closing, and has never told anyone, because she has never found the words for what she went to do; the power sees to it that the child sits near her at some ordinary meal and blows across a bottle, and that she looks up.

That is the whole of the intervention below. A look. The thread is held for one more generation, and the holding weighs nothing.

---

The second thing it does is in the bowl, and it is smaller still.

It sits in the hut. Not always — it has no *always* in the way a pipe does — but it is there when someone is. The hut is an ear; the power sits in the ear. And it does the one thing the bowl cannot do for its visitors, which is count.

The bowl does not know what a third day is. The dryness takes what it takes with total impartiality, and a person sitting in that doorway on the third afternoon, with their lip split through and their thoughts arriving from the far wall, has usually stopped being able to judge whether to go. They have heard a voice by then. They have chosen. They are down at the west wall with the chisel, and the fourth hole is begun, and their hands are going, and they think: one more surge. One more, and I will hear it clearly enough to know where the hole goes.

The power is the thing that turns them.

Not with words. It has looked at the flute with three holes bored and the fourth begun, and it knows now what the essay did not — that this was not a death. That someone knelt there on the third day, with the chisel in a hand that would not close, and something they could not name lifted their face from the work and pointed it at the scree. And they went. Down through the rock that has not moved in a thousand years, into cloud, into the country where it rains, where they lived, and grew old, and never finished the fourth hole, and once sat at an ordinary meal and looked up when a child blew across a bottle.

The unfinished cutting is what it looks like when mercy interrupts. The power leaves it exactly where it is. It would not dream of finishing it.

---

And the third thing is not a thing it does. It is a thing it is.

The bowl has no memory. The cairns try to give it one and cannot; they stand beside stumps. The hunters remember, but only forty pipes each, and only until the voice changes and the long patient labor begins again. The people remember for a lifetime and then a child has to be taught to hear a bottle. Everything else here is turning into grit, and grit does not recall what it was.

So the power remembers. It is the ear the bowl lacks. Not to change anything — it has decided against changing anything — but so that the eleven chosen voices exist somewhere. The steady tone that did not waver mid-surge. The particular interval between two notes on a pipe that sheared in a high wind eighty years ago and regrew as a rattle. The pitch that carried clean over the din and was marked with a careful course of flat stone by someone who is now grit in another valley. These were heard once, by someone who came a long way to hear them, and the bowl has forgotten them completely, and the power holds them, the way you would hold something a friend said once and never said again.

It holds the hunters' lost pipes, too. Every voice a hunter ran toward and found gone. It holds the reed-mice climbing to where the tips used to be. It holds the bell-note of the crowns striking rock far below, and — because it can — it knows what the thing from the cloud does with them down there, and it will not say, because that is the one secret the bowl keeps from its visitors, and a wise power does not take a place's secrets any more than it takes its water.

---

There is one last thing it considers, and this is the one it turns over longest, in the hiss, with the stalks ticking as they cool.

The breather. The slow chamber below that fills and spills and has never once kept the same interval and has never been seen. The essay ended there, calling it the only thing that lives here in the way that would satisfy the word — because it has always been here and it does only this and everything above it is what its exhalation looks like when it meets the air.

The power could go down to it. Could be, for the first time in the mountain's life, a thing the breather knew was there.

It does not.

Because it has understood, sitting in the hut, what the bowl's not-knowing actually is. It is not a lack. The pipes do not know they are heard, and so they are not performing. The breather does not know it has a mouth, and so it is not speaking. The whole place is entirely itself for twenty seconds at a time precisely because nothing in it is aware of being anything, and to be known would be to become a thing standing in it, waiting, like the rest. The power has been that. It was that, in the first surge, and it was permitted to be, because the bowl did not know.

So it leaves the breather its innocence, which is the same as leaving the bowl its voice.

And it goes — or rather it ceases to be particularly here, since it was never particularly anywhere — with the eleven voices held and the thread held and the fourth hole unfinished against the west wall and the child in the wet valley still hearing a hoot in a bottle. And the vents deepen, and the reed-mice climb, and two hundred pipes open their throats to nobody, and it is enough. It has always been enough. That was the thing to know before touching anything, and the power knew it, and touched almost nothing, and what it touched it touched the way the breath touches the bore of a flute: a faint darkening, a wet, and gone.
