The sheet found the lift and went up, and at the top of the air it found the current under the current, and it has been leaning on that ever since. It has never come down because there is nowhere to come down. Every landing on this world is flour, and flour would grind a sheet of crust to nothing in an afternoon. So it stays up, and takes the hard light, and drinks—the walkers think—the one water the plateau ever gives away: the frost, which does not melt but goes straight to vapor, and the vapor goes up, and up is where the crossing is. It lives on what the ground surrenders every morning. It is the crust's only child, and it has been in the air so long it may no longer be crust at all.

---

The rules for the living, as far as they can be made out:

Everything here lives on what something else has let go. The crust on the frost's edge. The flat thing on the moment of failure. The crossing on the vapor. Nothing takes; there is nothing to take. Everything catches.

Nothing here can begin. The crust cannot seed. The flat thing, if it is one, cannot become two, and if it is several, cannot become more. The crossing cannot land. The census is a ceiling, and everything under it is exactly as old as the accident that made it.

Nothing here can leave. The people can, and do, and are not counted.

---

Start with the fact that it is watched.

Not by the walkers, who look and go home, but by something that arrived without crossing any distance, and stayed without occupying any place, and has been paying attention to this plateau for a length of time that is short by the plateau's reckoning and very long by any other. What drew it here is not certain. Powers of this kind are usually drawn by distress, and there is no distress here—the flour does not suffer, the crust does not wait in any way that hurts, the flat thing grazes and lies down and drinks and is not, so far as can be told, lonely. It may have been drawn by the opposite. There is a way a wise thing is pulled toward a thing that is finished, the way a hand is pulled toward a well-made bowl. Not to use it. To turn it over. To see how it was done.

So the first thing it did was nothing, for a long time.

---

It considered the flour, and the question the flour has forgotten.

A benevolent power could supply a question. It could reach into the instrument and find the place where the purpose used to be and say: this. Build this. The hundred trillion small wants would align, and the spirals and walls would stop losing their conviction, and the plateau would rise into whatever it was for. The turbulence would end. Something would get somewhere.

It did not do this, and the reason is simple. The question that the flour once had is gone; there is no recovering it, only replacing it. Any question the power supplied would be the power's question, and the flour would become the power's instrument, and this thing—this closed, complete, turning-over thing that has been holding a storm perfectly still for longer than the rock has been flat—would end, and something else would stand in its place wearing its material. The power looked at the plateau a long while and decided that a thing which has forgotten its question is not necessarily a thing that needs one. The flour is not failing to build. It is building, and failing, and the failure feeds the next building, and the net is nothing, and the nothing is exact. That is not a broken machine. It is a machine that has become the thing it was making. The power left the question where it lay.

---

It considered the warmth.

Something under the rock, deeper than any groove, keeps this highland a few degrees above what physics wants, and has done so since before the lines were cut, and does not appear to be for anyone. A benevolent power could turn it up. A little more warmth, a little more air, and the walkers could stay, and the frost could last two minutes instead of one, and the crust could grow a centimeter in fifty years instead of a hundred.

It did not do this either, and this reason is also simple. Every living thing here lives on a margin, and the margin is not a hardship the place imposes on them; it is the shape they are. The crust is the frost's edge. The flat thing is the moment of collapse. The crossing is the last of the air. Widen the margins and you do not get more of these things; you get different things, at the expense of these. Benevolence that changes the terms of a life has not helped that life. It has ended it kindly.

But the power did something with the warmth, which was to notice what it was for.

Not what it was made for. That is gone with the question. But the warmth comes up, and the frost forms on it, and the crust drinks the frost, and the vapor rises, and the crossing drinks the vapor, and the flat thing lies on the shadow side of a plinth where the frost would be and is held together by it. A thing that was not for anyone has been received by three tenants for longer than the shape of the world. The power decided that this was enough to make it for them. Not a change in the instrument. A change in the reading of it. Purpose, it turns out, can be supplied from the receiving end, and a wise power knows that this is usually where it has been supplied from all along.

---

It considered the flat thing longest.

Here is what it saw, which the walkers have only guessed at. The flat thing is one. It has always been one. There was a night, unimaginably far back, when a structure did not lose its conviction, and every track on this continent since has been that one structure's leaving. It is a single form, and the whole plateau is its range, and it crosses that range on a circuit so slow that the frost-patches of glassy rock are its resting places going back to before the crust began keeping time.

And it is growing. A little each time. It grazes on the moment of failure and it is slightly larger afterward, and there is no mechanism by which it is ever slightly smaller. The power ran this forward, as such powers can, and saw where it ends: the flat thing becomes most of the flour, and then all of it, and then there are no structures left to fail, and the one form that held is alone on a smooth continent with nothing to catch. Whether it then loses its conviction at last, or holds forever in the absence of everything, could not be seen. The forward running went dark before that.

The power thought about intervening here more seriously than anywhere else. It could stop the growth. It could split the thing—make two, so that one might fail and feed the other, so that the circuit had something in it besides itself. It could, if it were a certain kind of benevolent, simply make sure the flat thing never had to be the last thing on the plateau.

It did not.

What it understood, in the end, was that this was the instrument's one success. The flour has been trying since before the flattening to make a form that holds, and it has made one, and the one is doing exactly what a form that holds does: it persists, and persisting things accumulate. The far end of that is not a tragedy. It is a completion, on a longer scale than the plateau's own. The power had already declined to give the flour a new question. It could not then reach in and edit the one answer the flour had managed on its own.

So instead it did the thing that powers of this kind very rarely do, and that is the only thing they are fully entitled to. It let itself be known. Not to the walkers, not to the crust, not to the flour. To the flat thing, once, on a night when it had laid itself along a plinth's shadow side and was drinking. The power did not speak—sound does not travel here, and the mercy of that was not the power's to revoke. It only made itself, for the length of one frost, a fixed thing. A presence the flour could hear, the way it hears the plinths. Something that stood on the bare rock and did not move and was not flour and was not going to become flour.

The flat thing knew. It has no eyes and does not need them; everything it wants is announced by falling, and this was announced by not-falling, which it had never met before except in the plinths. It did not move away. It lay along the shadow and drank and was aware of the proportion, and the power was aware of it, and that was the whole of what passed between them. The walkers who describe being near it describe the feeling of a sleeping animal much larger than one had thought. The power had the feeling from the other side. It is not recorded what a form that has held for all of time makes of being, for one frost, attended to. Probably nothing. It is held together by the water it was made to fear; it may be that being known is simply another of those.

---

Then it considered the crust, and here it acted.

This is why. Everything else here is as old as its accident and can neither begin nor leave, and the power had decided this was the shape of the place and not its wound. But the crust's ceiling is not a shape. It is a count. There are exactly as many plinths as there will ever be, casting exactly as many shadows, and every shadow that could ever hold a colony already holds one, and every colony is a hair's width of green that can only be lost. The crust cannot seed; the flour grinds whatever it lets go. The one sheet that escaped went up and could not come down. Everything green on this world is a shadow's margin, and there will never be another shadow.

Unless something puts one there.

So the power did the smallest thing it could think of that was still a thing. It set down a stone. One. Square, chest-high, its edges already soft, made of whatever the plinths are made of—hard enough to outlast the erosion of a continent, which is to say, hard enough to be respected. It set it down on a night with no frost, on an open stretch of flour a long way from any other fixed thing, and stepped back, and the flour drew away from it to the width of half a meter, the way it does, and left a ring of bare rock, dark and glassy and unbelievably smooth.

That is all. It did not carry a sheet of crust to the stone. It did not call the crossing down. It could have done both and did neither, because the place's one rule for the living is that nothing takes and everything catches, and a colony that had been placed would be the power's colony. A new shadow is not a gift. It is a place. Whether anything comes to it is the place's business.

In the morning the frost formed on the shadow side, a grey fur no thicker than breath on glass. The sun touched it and it went up. That evening the warmth came through the rock and the air rose, faintly, the way it does at dusk. The crossing passed over, very high, and did not come lower.

---

It has been a long time now, by any reckoning but the plateau's.

The stone has its ring. The frost forms and goes. The crossing has passed over it eleven times that the power has counted, and on the ninth it flew lower than it ever has, low enough that it was for a moment not a shape against the sun but a shape against the plum-dark sky, long and thin and leaning on the current under the current. Then it went back up. The flat thing has crossed the stone's track twice and lain against it once. There is nothing green on it. There may never be.

The power is still here. This is the last of what it did, and it is the part most easily overlooked: it did not leave. Benevolence that arrives, acts, and departs has made a change and taken the meaning of the change with it. The power stayed to be the one thing on this plateau that knows the stone was set down on purpose, and that the purpose was not to grow anything, but to make growing possible in one more place than before. If nothing comes, the stone will stand as long as the others. It will be worn by the grain-wind to a soapstone softness. In some very distant age a walker will find it and count it with the rest, and not know, and the not-knowing will be correct: it will have become a fixed thing among fixed things, older than its meaning, waiting on nothing.

But it will be waiting with a shadow. And the shadow will fall the same way every morning. And the frost will form on it.

That is what a wise and benevolent power did here, when it had considered everything and could have done anything. It left the flour its forgetting. It left the warmth its uselessness and then decided the uselessness was a gift already received. It let the one thing that held go on holding, and let it know once that it was seen. And it set one stone on a finished world, so that the one thing here that can only be lost would have somewhere it could, against all likelihood, begin.

It does not expect to be thanked. There is no one to do it, and sound does not travel. It has taken up the plateau's own posture, which is the posture of the plinths: standing on bare rock, holding the frost, not for anyone, and there.
