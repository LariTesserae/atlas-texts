Say it comes down the way the light does, through one of the shafts, and stands at the foot of the slab where the flakes are. Say it can be anywhere in the system at once and chooses to be here first, because this is the one place with a footprint in it that did not fill and vanish.

The country does not notice. That is the first thing it learns and it learns it in the body, if it has one. The roar goes through it. The cockroaches part around whatever it is standing on and close again behind. A bat drops from the fringe of the roost, wheels, reattaches. Nothing turns toward it. It has been attended to, in its time, by everything that could sense it — it is used to being the warmest point in any room — and here it is not the warmest point in the room, because the room is warmer than it is, and the room does not look up.

It would be a small thing to change that. It considers doing so and sets it aside, and that is how the rest of it goes: one mercy at a time, picked up, turned over, put down.

The first is light. The obvious gift, the one that any power arriving in a dark place reaches for before it has thought. It could open the ceiling over the long gallery where the snake lies. It could bring a shaft of grey down onto that fracture and let the old animal see, for once, what it has been striking at — the fog resolved into faces, each with a wrinkled mouth and ears like petals, hanging in their thousands. It knows what would happen. The snake would feel the light as a wrongness on its skin and go along the crack to the pinched end and press itself into the narrowing, away from the cold that light is down here. The roost would thin above the new skylight and thicken elsewhere. The floor in the cone would go from guano to moss in a season. The snake's country would become a border. Every other collapse in this system was made by no one for no reason and the animals have arranged themselves around each one without complaint, but to make a collapse on purpose, over this animal, would be to decide that its dark was a lack. It is not a lack. It is where the warmth is. Put the light down.

The second is quiet. It could take the roar away for a moment — not stop the wings, just fold the sound so that for the space of a breath the gallery held what a person would call silence. It knows this too. The snake reads the pitch of the roar to know when the fog will be lowest. The cockroaches, whatever they read, read something in the rustle of each other. Silence here would not be peace. It would be the whole floor and the whole ceiling losing their instruments at once. Put it down.

The third is the hardest to put down, and it is knowledge. It could tell the snake about the sky. Not with words, which would be nothing to it, but the way a power could — by simply letting the animal know, for the length of a strike, that the fog above goes out every dusk through a hole in the world and hangs over miles of scrub under stars and comes back, and that the warm breathing it has organised its whole life around is the tide of something it will never see. It thinks about this longer than it thought about the others, because it is the gift it would want, if it were the snake. Then it understands that this is precisely why it must not. The snake has a position. It faces the source. To hand it the sky would be to tell it that the source is somewhere else, that it has spent its years facing a doorway and calling it the room, and there is no version of that which is a kindness to a body that cannot go through the door. Wisdom is the part of a power that knows the difference between what it would want and what the other can hold. Put it down. Put it down carefully, because this one has edges.

It looks at the future, then, because it can. It sees the thin-roofed tube that climbs toward the surface and stops, the skin of rock with the grey glow coming through, and it sees the day — not soon, not in the snake's life, not in the lives of the snakes that will follow it into that crack — when the skin gives. It sees the slab come down and the light come in and a new sunken garden begin where there had been only dark and roost and rustling. It sees that the gallery with the fracture in it is far enough from that collapse to feel it only as a shift in the pitch of the roar, a redistribution of the fog, and then nothing. It could hold the roof up. It could let it fall now. It does neither. The country is made of collapses. Every gallery it has walked through is the space between two of them. To keep a roof from falling would be to stop the country being what it is, and to bring one down early would be to pretend that its own arrival was an event the place should mark. The roof will fall when the rock decides. The animals will move around the light the way water moves around a stone.

It goes further in. Under the black pool, up into the chamber where the roar is a hum in the rock. It stands over the fish.

There is nothing to do here and it knows that before it arrives. The fish hangs in water that has never had a surface anyone breathed at. It is not waiting. It has no fog and no falling floor and no cycle, and it is not lacking these things, because lacking requires a shape you were supposed to fit and the fish has never been shown one. Every kindness a power could offer — warmth, light, a current, a companion — would be an intrusion of shape into a life that has none and needs none. It realises that benevolence toward this animal is not merely indistinguishable from leaving it alone. It is leaving it alone. There is no other form the benevolence could take. It stays a while anyway, in the dark, above the pale body with its dark loop of gut showing through, and then it goes back under the pool and up into the sound.

Back at the fracture, the snake is shedding.

It has hooked the old skin on the keenest edge of the crack and is drawing itself forward out of it, slowly, the way it does everything, and the skin is peeling back inside-out and hanging like a pale root. The power watches. There is a moment when the skin catches on a second edge, further along, and the snake stops and pulls and the skin holds, and it would be the work of nothing — less than a thought — to ease it. To lift the pale membrane a hair's breadth off the rock so that the animal came free without the small tearing that is coming. It does not. It keeps its hands, if it has hands, where they are. The snake pulls again and the skin gives with the tear and the shed drifts down and is gone into the floor, and the snake is on its fracture in new scales with its face turned up toward the fog, and nothing has happened, and the power understands that this was the only true test it was given and that passing it felt like failing.

So this is what it does.

It stays. Not forever, and not in a way the country will register. It lies along the gallery the way the snake lies along its crack, and it faces the same direction, up, into the roost, and it lets the roar go through it and the drips fall on it and the cockroaches go over whatever it has for feet. It attends to the snake for a span that is long for a snake — a hundred strikes, a thousand, the whole slow work of a body turning its food and going still for days and then unfolding upward again. It learns which parts of the fog are settled and which are restless. It learns when the pitch of the roar changes and what that means for the fringe above the fracture. It learns the snake's country, twenty body-lengths of sharp young stone, and it learns it from the snake's side, in the dark, without the sky.

And it holds the snake. Not with anything. It holds the fact of it. Somewhere in the vast patient memory of a power that has time to spend, there is now an entry that reads: in the middle dark of a roofed gallery, a body's length under the roost, a thin dark animal with cloudy eyes held one fracture for years and struck upward some thousands of times and shed against the sharpest edge and never once faced any direction but up, and nothing in the country marked it, and I did. That is the whole of what it gives. An audience the snake will never know it had, for a life that would have been complete without one. It is the only gift the place can take without being changed by it, and it is the only gift a wise power has that a benevolent one is allowed to give here.

When it leaves, it goes back to the slab with the flakes on it. It looks at them for a while — the dark lustre, the core, the smear of old smoke on the rock behind. It understands them now, or thinks it does. Somebody came down here who could tell the difference between visiting and living, and stood at the edge of the roar, and looked, and did not build and did not take and did not change the pitch of a single wingbeat, and climbed back up, and dropped nothing on purpose but dropped something anyway. The power does not know if that was wisdom or only fear. It suspects it was both, and that in this place they came to the same thing.

It leaves nothing. Not a flake. It goes up the shaft against the evening column of bats, unseen by them, and stands on the scrub under a sky the country beneath it has never needed to think about, and the sound comes up faintly through the rock, and it will sound that way tomorrow, and it knows exactly what it sounds like now, and that is all it took.
