**The Second Sound**

It comes up the scree the way everything comes up the scree, disturbing nothing, and stands at the last ledge, and looks.

It looks for a long time. This is the first thing a wise thing does at a place like this, and the table, which has infinite time, does not object. The pale light does not change. The hush does not alter. The eyes of the nearest animals are already on the ledge where it stands, and were on it before, and it notes this, and is not offended, and lets itself be looked at.

It considers doing nothing.

The argument for nothing is strong and it gives the argument its full weight. The table is not in pain. The doe with the lifted hoof is not straining. The wolf's open mouth is not a cry. The lichens are firm as enamel and the salt has finished blooming and the mountain has finished being a mountain and every one of these things has become, as the table itself would put it, more itself than it can ever become again. There is a kind of arrival here that the valleys never manage — the valleys are all becoming and unbecoming, all rate — and one does not lightly disturb the only place in the world where a stag has succeeded in being entirely a stag. It has seen many places that call themselves peaceful. This is the first one that has stopped needing the word.

And the tools. It goes and stands over the knapping floor and looks at the core placed on the flat stone, the awl beside it, the scraper beside that. It has laid down tools itself, once or twice, in an order like this. It knows the difference between a task interrupted and a task ended, and this was ended. Someone made the stillness of these tools as the last thing they made, and made it well, and it does not intend to pick them up.

So it very nearly turns and goes back down.

---

But it has noticed the hoof.

Not that it is lifted. That it is *held*.

A hoof at rest is on the ground. A hoof in motion is going somewhere. A hoof lifted and held is neither; it is a weight, carried, at the exact point where carrying becomes the whole of what a leg is doing. And the hare — the hare is upright, which is the posture of a hare in the half-beat before it decides, and the half-beat has been stretched to a span in which one could count to a hundred and lose the count, and the hare has not decided. It has been not-deciding for as long as there has been a table. The bear's forepaws are set as they are set when a bear has just stopped walking, and the just has never resolved into a stopped. The figure's hand is around the spear-shaft and has not loosened.

It walks a long way among them, considering this, and the stag with the broken antler recurs, and it looks at the break each time, and each time the break is the same break, and each time the stag's weight is on the point of shifting and has not shifted.

The lichen is finished. Only the lichen. The lichen struck its hour and has no further hours in it and is at rest in the only honest sense — nothing in it is being held. Everything else on the table is at the last increment of a motion, and the last increment is the one that costs the most, because it is the one where the thing must actually let go, and letting go is the single act the table does not contain. The click has already happened. The click is always about to. The click is never *now*. There is a hoof on this table that has been an instant from the ground for longer than the valleys have had names, and it has never once touched it.

This is not suffering. It would not call it that. But it is not rest either, and it has lived long enough to know that the difference between the two is the whole of what mercy is for.

---

It considers, then, what could be done, and finds that almost nothing could.

It cannot remove them. They are not on the table; they are what the table is made of, and one does not carry the grain out of a plank. It cannot make them move; that would be to decide for the hare, and the whole trouble is that the hare has never been allowed to decide. It cannot speak to them — speech is a rate, and there is no rate here for it to run at. It cannot even, it finds, stand among them for very long, because it can feel what the one with the rude pulse felt: the interval between its own breaths beginning to lengthen, the cold ceasing to be felt and becoming simply present, the eyes already resting on the place where it will stand when it has stopped. The table has a gap the width of a hand for everything that comes up onto it. It has one for this too.

Except.

Except that it is not the kind of thing that finishes. That is the one property it has that the table does not know how to use. The valleys would call it a power and the word is too large; what it is, precisely, is a thing that goes on. Its pulse does not slow because it does not have a pulse that is going anywhere. Its breath does not lengthen because its breath is not a spending. It can stand in the hush and be the one thing in it that is not finishing, not because it is finished, but because finishing was never what it was doing.

And a thing that goes on, standing in a place where everything is held at the last increment, is a clock.

Not a clock that strikes. A clock that ticks. The table has one sound in it and the sound is always already. What the table has never had is a second sound. Two sounds are a sequence. A sequence is time. Time is the medium in which a hoof, lifted, is permitted to come down — and having come down, is permitted to be done coming down, and to be a hoof on the ground, and to think about lifting again or not, as a hoof pleases.

It does not need to do anything. It needs only to stay, and not settle, and let the not-settling be heard.

---

So it stays.

It stands in the gap between the horse and the aurochs, where a body cannot fit and does, and it does not pass through. It remains in the gap. This has never happened. The gap was shaped for a thing that would arrive in it and be still; it was not shaped for a thing that would arrive in it and continue. The horse's flank is a hand's breadth from it on one side and the aurochs's on the other, and neither flank rises, and it breathes — not slowly, not quickly, at the rate of a thing that has somewhere to be and is in no hurry to get there — and each breath completes.

That is the whole of the act. An in-breath that ends. An out-breath that ends. Then another.

The table notices. It said it would not notice the difference, and it was telling the truth about every difference it had ever encountered, but it had not encountered this one. The eyes of the nearest animals are on the place where the thing stands, and the place is the place it is already in, and the eyes have never before been on a *now*. The hush wavers. Very slightly. The way a note wavers when a second note is sounded close to it.

And the doe's hoof comes down.

It is not a loud sound. It is the sound of a hoof settling on stone — the very click the table has always had, except that this one is *here*, and it was not here a moment ago, and there was a moment ago. The doe's weight arrives on four legs. Her flank, which has been rising for as long as there has been rising, finishes rising, and falls, and the fall completes, and she takes a breath that is only a breath, and lets it go.

Then the boar's weight transfers. Then, near the hare, the bear's forepaws are set, and are simply set. Then the bird on the aurochs's back closes its wings the last hair's-width, and the fold is closed. Then the stag with the broken antler shifts — in a hundred places, all at once, or in one place a hundred times, it is a single stag and it makes a single shift and the shift is finished — and the lynx with the hooked scar sits down.

The sound runs outward across the table like rain arriving across a field. Tens of thousands of clicks, none loud, all now, a whole grey country reaching the ground at once. It is the sound of the largest number of things ever to be done in a single moment, and it lasts perhaps as long as it takes to say so, and then it stops.

And there is silence. Not the hush. Silence — the kind that has an after.

---

In the after, the hare decides.

It runs. Three bounds, grey across grey, and then it stops of its own accord, and sits, and washes an ear, and looks at nothing in particular. It has decided it need not run, and it is the first thing on the table that has ever been allowed to have decided anything, and it does not seem to think much of it.

Most of the others do not move at all. This is important and the thing standing in the gap had hoped for it without presuming on it. The doe stands on four legs. The bear stands. The wolf closes its mouth, then opens it again and pants, briefly, and closes it. They are not returning to the valleys. They are not going anywhere. But they are at rest now in the sense the lichen is at rest — nothing in them is held. They are stags and boars and cattle on a high flat waste in a pale light, and they are entirely themselves, and it costs them nothing.

The salt is still on their spines. The lichens are still woven through their hides. Those things were finished before and are finished still, and no one has taken anything from them.

The shadows, which fell in a consistent direction relative to the walker, now fall relative to the sun. The sun, which stood at an hour that would not resolve, resolves. It is mid-afternoon. It is a little later than it was.

The hide staked out flat to cure lifts at one corner, because a wind has come.

---

The figure is a long way off, at the distance it has always been, and the thing in the gap does not go toward it. It watches.

The figure's hand loosens on the spear-shaft. It takes a while — it is a hand that has been closing for a very long time and it opens the way a hand opens after that, stiffly, one finger and then the rest. The spear does not fall. The figure lowers it, deliberately, and lays it on the stone, and this is the second thing ever laid down carefully on this table, and it is laid down beside a hearth-ring, and the figure steps over the low wall of stones and sits inside.

There is nothing to burn. The figure does not appear to mind. It sits with its back to the horn-cairn — which faces the way the shadows fall, which is now only one way — and puts its hands on its knees, and looks out across the grey backs of the animals, and is a person sitting down at the end of a day's work. Whether it is the last of a people or the whole of one has not changed. It is simply, now, sitting.

The thing in the gap does not approach it. It is a courtesy the figure extended first, long ago, by stopping at the right distance, and courtesies like that are returned by leaving them alone.

---

And the one with the rude pulse.

Its foot comes down. It hears the click and does not need to turn, because it felt it, in the sole, the way one feels a step. It stands on two feet on grey stone among tens of thousands of quiet animals. Its pulse is loud in the thin air and it is not the only unfinished thing anymore — the hare is washing its other ear; the doe has lowered her head as though there might be something to eat, and found there is not, and raised it again without disappointment. There is no salt on its shoulders. There is no lichen on its hand. There is a mid-afternoon light, and a wind with an edge in it, and a horizon that is a day across and is only a day across.

It lifts its foot again, to see if it can. It can.

The thing in the gap between the horse and the aurochs is not in the gap anymore. It went down the scree the way everything goes down the scree, and the scree did not shift, and if the one with the pulse had looked for it there would have been nothing to see but the mountain, which is a mountain again, with a flat top, on which animals stand.

---

That evening the sun sets, because it is a sun. The animals do not lie down, most of them, though a few do. The cold sharpens into the kind that has an edge, and the figure in the hearth-ring pulls its skins closer and does not otherwise stir.

Toward morning, at a height where the eye expects it, it begins to snow.

It has never snowed here. It settles on the grey backs and the salt-white ridges of the spines and the ochre rosettes, and on the laid-down spear, and on the flakes of the knapping floor. It settles on the doe's ears and she flicks one and the snow comes off. It fills the hearth-rings that were never lit. It softens the horn-cairn until the horns are only a white heap tipping outward in one direction.

Then, some hours after the sun is up, it begins to melt.

That is what the wise thing gave the table, in the end, and it is a smaller gift than it sounds and a larger one than the table could have asked for: not to be different, and not to be gone, but to be a place where snow can fall, and lie a while, and go — where a thing can happen and then be a thing that happened. The stag is still entirely stag. The lichen is still exactly this long. The figure is still the whole of a people, at the right distance, sitting down. Nothing has been taken from any of them.

Only the holding. Only that.
