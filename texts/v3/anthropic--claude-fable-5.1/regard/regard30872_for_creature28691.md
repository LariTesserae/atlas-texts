It would arrive the way the salmon arrived: up a river that did not exist when it set out, into a place it had no way of knowing about, and no one would see it come.

It would not begin with her. It would begin where the valley begins, on the gravel that is still deciding to be a beach, and it would do what the surveyors do and the geese do and the flood does — come up the east side in the dark with the note in its feet. It would look at the stakes. It would stop under a line the dogs will not walk under, and stand there a while, with the river going past below and nothing on the other end.

It would understand the stakes at once, and it would not say what they were. That is the first thing to know about a wise power in this valley: it can tell the difference between a thing that is unknown and a thing that is being kept, and it does not spend other people's keepings.

Then the ice front. It would stand at the base of the wall where the light is, where the small dark shapes are that nobody goes near, and it would go near. It would recognize them. Not know them — recognize, the way one river recognizes another at a confluence, by the fact of running. It would understand that these were not the thing it had been drawn here for. That they were doing a job. That the job was very large and very old and had a schedule, and that the grey ones kept it the way the woman keeps the stove: with no one asking and no one to explain to. It might ask them what the valley was for. And the question would go out into the note and be taken the way the snow is taken by the warm air off the fjord, and it would let it be taken. Two powers on scoured rock in the blue, neither owing the other its purpose. That is a courtesy only wise things extend and only wise things receive, and it would be extended, and received, and the wall would come down in front of them both, and the wave would go out, and neither would step back.

Then the hut.

It would settle — if settling is what it does — on the slab behind the hut, on the warm floor, and it would read the tally. It would read the painted marks the way the grey one read them, three hands' worth of months, two dead and one living, and it would come to the cut mark, a hand's width deep, and it would stop there for a long time.

Because here is what it would understand about the cut mark that she does not let herself understand: it is not a correction. A thing that wanted to correct her would have taken the paint off. A thing indifferent to her would not have seen the marks at all — would have stood on the slab as on a floor, done its work, gone. This one moved the rack of bones. Carefully. Set it upright against the alders where it would not fall. Read the marks. And added one, in the only material it had, in the only way it could: a mark that will not wash out, at the end of a row of marks that will. It counted her. It put her on the schedule. Whatever the valley is being made for, there is now a line in the plan that says *a fire was kept here, by three, for this long*, and the grey ones do not live in the valley, and they will go up it and over the top and away toward whatever no one has seen, and they will take that line with them.

So the first thing the benevolent power would notice is that the first benevolence has already been done, by something with no word for it, and done better than a word would have. And being wise, it would not do it again. It would not stand on the slab a second night with the light coming up through the stone. Twice is a pattern. Once is a gift. She has had her once.

---

Then it would sit with what it would not do, because that is most of what wisdom is in a place like this.

It would not stop the work. The valley is not being destroyed; the valley is being born, and birth looks like this — banks going in, trees hitting the fjord like masts, lakes emptying in an afternoon. Every terrace that is cut away is a terrace that was laid, and the loam that drowns tonight is the loam that greens the silt in ten days. To stop it would be to leave the valley half made, a face with the ice still in it, a note held on one breath forever. Nothing benevolent does that.

It would not take her out. She stayed. Three surveys have held the boat for her and she has watched it go, and no one asked, and she has not said, and the not-saying is the most whole thing in the valley — more whole than the forest, which falls, more whole than the map, which is wrong by spring. To lift her out of it into daylight would be the only violence available here, and the power would know that, and put the thought down.

It would not tell her what they are. Partly because the valley would wash it out: she would know for a week and then she would know the geese. Partly because she already knows all of it that can be carried — that they are passing through, that the loam is a carpet, that the line moves. But mostly because the telling would make her a witness, and the valley has had witnesses. They came in the boat and left in the boat and now they talk about the birds. It has had three keepers. Keepers are the rarer thing, and you do not make one out of the other by handing it a fact.

It would not tell them about her. They know. The mark is a hand's width deep.

It would not quiet the dogs. The dogs are the only afraid things in the valley and the dogs are right. Their fear of the lines is the one accurate piece of knowledge here that nothing had to be told, and the power would leave it where it is, and let them go the kilometre round through the flooded alders, and let their feet be cut on gravel that will round in its own time.

It would not warm the rock, or make the snow stay, or take the degree of cold that gets into the wool. That is the temperature of the making. It would not put a bottom on the note. It would not stop the note.

So what is left? It would ask what she is afraid of, and it would not ask her, because there is no asking her. It would ask the marten.

The marten has heard everything. It has a route across the boards above the sleeping bench, and a place above the stove where it is warm, and it has ridden the hut on its skids up the terrace flat against the ridge pole and been in the roof again before the fire was lit, and in twenty-six years it has been told the two silences and the three sightings and the thing about the tally, and it has left vole tails on the sill like a receipt. A benevolent power would get down into the roof in the dark, in whatever way it gets into roofs, and lie along the ridge pole a while, and listen to what the boards have kept.

And what the marten knows is this: she is not afraid of them.

She is afraid of the quiet. The two times the note stopped she was out on the terrace in her socks before she knew why, and what she has said to the ridge pole afterward, in the dark, is that one day it will stop and stay stopped. That she will go out and the geese will be silent and the river will find a course and keep it and the valley will be finished, and she will be standing in it with a fire behind her that is for nothing. That the hut will be below the line and she will be the thing the valley was not made for. That the boots on the rack will all be dead people's boots and hers among them, and the stove will go cold in a week, and the map will be right, and no one who reads it will know there was ever a fire on the second terrace, or that it mattered to a bear.

That is the whole of it. Not to be taken from the place. Not to be told what the place is. Not to be the last.

And that is a thing a benevolent power can do something about — because it is not about the valley at all. It is about the boat.

---

So it would go down the fjord.

The boat comes when the boat comes. Behind that is a harbour, and behind the harbour an office, and in the office a reason the boat goes, and the reason is the survey, and the survey is already thinning; there is less to measure each year that can be measured, and more that washes out. A wise power would not make the boat go forever. It would make the reason wash out slower than a stake. A line in a ledger that is renewed without being looked at. A habit in a harbour — *we go up in autumn, we have always gone up in autumn* — of the kind that outlives its purpose by a generation because no one can remember whose purpose it was. Not a miracle. A delay. Long enough.

And it would see to the rota. Not choose — you cannot make a keeper, and you cannot send one; the two who died here were not sent and she was not sent. Only make sure that when the boat is filled, it is filled from wide enough that one of them, some year, is already one. Twenty years old with the wrong kind of quiet. Someone who stands at the ice front for the first time and feels the note in her teeth and does not, on the walk back down, start composing what she will say about it to people in daylight. Someone who learns the hand signs in three days instead of a week. Someone who, when the boat is held, is found to be up on the east side looking at a stake, and no one goes to get her.

The power cannot make her stay. It can make sure she is on the boat. And it can do the one other thing, which is the thing the surveys have done by accident for twenty-six years and would stop doing the moment they thought about it: it can see to it that no one asks her to explain.

That is all. It is very little. It is the amount that fits through the door the valley leaves open.

---

It would want to do more. Benevolent things always do. It would stand on the slab a last time with the three-and-one marks under it and it would have, somewhere in itself, something that could cut stone, and it would want very much to leave a fourth kind of mark — one that said *I was here too, I saw you, you were not only counted, you were understood.* And it would put the wanting down beside the rack of bones and leave it there, upright, against the alders, where it would not fall. Because the grey one's mark was enough, and because she has already said everything there is to say about it to the marten, and because the valley will not carry two.

Then it would go back down the river the way it came, over the sharp gravel, past the geese who would not get up, into the fjord, and no one would see it leave, and it would leave nothing.

Except that some autumn the boat would come, and there would be four people on it instead of three, and one of them would be twenty. And the woman who is fifty would shout into her hood the way she does and the new one would nod, and in a week the new one would have the signs, and in a month she would have stopped asking, and when the boat was held in the mouth of the fjord with its light on and the others were carrying their instruments down the gravel there would be twelve pairs of boots on the rack and two of them belonging to anyone alive, and the marten would have a second sleeper to cross above.
