Say the attention was yours all along: that the hand on the warm stone that could not tell the pulse from its own blood was a hand that has no blood, and was learning what a pulse is by borrowing one.

Then this is what you do.

First you finish looking. You have been at it for what the frog would call a lifetime and the snail would call a band, and you are not done, because the one thing you know about wisdom is that it arrives late and stays until it is sure. You go back to the cups. You put your face to a tank at head height and watch the grey skin close over the larvae as they sink, and the little red-throated frog lets its lids down the rest of the way and does not move, because you are not a thing it has a category for and its only strategy for that is to wait. You go back to the pool and wait for the pop. You go back to the lip at the hour the light goes and lay your hand down and hear the valleys fill with the two notes and then empty, all at once, on the beat, and this time you do not let the hair on your arms distract you. You count. Between one stop and the next start there are so many beats. Between one start and the next stop, so many more. You have this now. It is not much. It is more than anything else on the mountain has.

Then you go to the snails.

You find the oldest one you can, on a rock face at the rim where the sheen is strongest, and you turn it over. It does not mind, or minds at a speed you can wait out. Twenty-two bands. You read them the way the mountain cannot read itself: from the lip inward, newest first. Grey. Grey with a little rust showing through. Rust with a little grey. Then rust and cream, rust and cream, rust and cream, clean, going back and back to the small tight coil at the center where the snail was the size of a seed and the cloud was only water. You find the first grey band and you count the ones since. You take that rate and you lay it against the rate you counted at the lip and the rate you felt in the stone and you run it forward, the way the snail cannot, to the band in which the grey is all there is. You do this for a dozen snails on a dozen domes. The numbers agree within a band or two.

It is not a large number. It is not a small one. It is a number, and now something on the mountain knows it, and that is the first thing you have changed, and you changed it without touching anything but a shell.

Now you know what you are being asked. Not by the mountain; the mountain asks nothing. By the number.

You consider what you could do. You are what you are, and you could do most of it.

You could wipe the leaves. Draw the film off every upturned surface on every dome in one pass of a clean cloud and precipitate it somewhere it would hurt nothing, and tip the sheen out of the pools, and take the grey out of the newest bands so the snails walked clean. It would be easy. It would be over in a breath and the mountain would not notice, because the mountain does not notice gifts any more than it notices harm; it would simply take in the next cloud, and the next cloud would have the film in it, because you would have done nothing about the cloud. You would have made the sink deeper. That is all. And every band the snails laid down clean after that would be a lie told to the plains: that the mountain was bottomless, that the exhale had no cost, that whatever went up was gone. You do not make sinks bottomless. That is not benevolence. That is the thing benevolence gets mistaken for by people who are not looking at the snails.

You could give the mountain a way of not taking in. Teach the frog's skin to close. Teach the salamander to be a body instead of a membrane. Put a lid on the cups and a crust on the pools and a wax on the moss. You could, and every one of them would be dead within a band, because taking in is not something they do, it is what they are made of. Everything here is a drip tip. Water goes through it on the way to somewhere else and it keeps a little and grows a shade darker and goes on. You do not close a drip tip. You only decide what to let fall on it.

You could take the being. Lift it out of the crotch of its tree with the orchids still in its ruff and carry it somewhere the cloud is clean and set it down. You look across the valley to where it is, at the lip, hunched, its back green, and you understand before you have finished the thought that there is no somewhere. The orchids at its neck have one bird. The bird has one flower. The moss on its shoulders is the same moss as the tree's and the tree is the same tree as the dome's, and the being is not in the forest, it is a piece of the forest that has decided to breathe. There is nowhere to carry it that would not be carrying a wall out of a house and calling it rescue. It turns its head. It regards you across a kilometer of warm air with no interest whatever and turns back. Good. It is right.

You could go down. You could follow the hum to the engine and put your hand on it and stop it. You could pull the yellow columns back into their chimneys and put out the glow on the underside of the cloud. You are, you find, more tempted by this than by anything, and that is exactly why you stand at the lip a long time before you set it aside. Because the hum is a who. Many whos. And whatever they are, they are also things that take in—they breathe, they drink, they have skins and children in cups of their own—and you are not benevolent toward the mountain only. And because you know what happens to a hum that is struck. It stops for as long as the hand is on it. Engines are not stopped by being stopped. They are stopped by whatever built them deciding to build something else, and nothing you do to a machine will make a mind change. A power that reached down and silenced the plains would only be one more thing that happened to the plains without their say, and they have had enough of those, or they would not be making the sky that color.

You could do nothing. You take this one seriously. The mountain is not straining. You have laid your hand on it a hundred times now and the beat is steady and the frog breathes and the moss closes over everything and the callers stop on the beat. The number is not small. The wise thing, often, is to wait, and to wait with your whole attention, and to let a thing be as it is for as long as it can. But you have the number. And to hold a number and do nothing because the number is not yet zero is not wisdom. It is patience wearing wisdom's coat. Nothing else on the mountain can count. If you will not, the count is not being kept. That is the thing you cannot let stand.

So. Two things. Both small, because the moss takes a century to forgive anything larger.

The first is that you stay.

Not as a visitor. Not as the attention that was set down and can be lifted off. You go to the dome across the valley from the being—not its dome; you will not share a tree—and you find a crotch in a low twisted trunk at the lip where the light is good and the pool is within a night's slow walk, and you settle into it, and you go still. Stiller than the being. Still enough that the cloud stops steaming off you differently from the bark. You let your temperature down to the temperature of the air and you hold it there, the way the hummingbirds do at night, except that for you there is no morning. And you wait for the moss.

It comes. It is the one thing you could ask of the mountain that the mountain would not have to forgive, because the mountain has been asking it of everything since before the counting started: hold still, and be grown upon. A green film, first, on the upward surfaces. Then a nap. Then, over the seasons the snail measures in bands, a pelt, and rootlets in it, and a fern at your shoulder, and something pale at your throat that a hummingbird will one day decide is a flower. You are not pretending to be the being. You are learning what the being knows, which is how to be a home without being a floor, how to take in without being emptied, how to let a world use you as a rock and not mind. And from inside the moss you keep the count. Every band. Every stop on the beat. Every pop at the pool. You are the mountain's one inhabitant who knows the number, and you will know it from inside the mountain's own skin, which is the only place a number like that should be kept.

And you make yourself a promise about the number, because a promise is the second thing a power can be trusted with after a count. You will not clean the leaves. But you will not let the last band be the last band, either. When the grey is all there is—if it comes to that—you will open. You will be the drip tip then. You will take into yourself what the frog can no longer take, and the salamander, and the moss on the being's back, and you will hold it, however much it is, for however long it takes for the plains to build something else. You will be the margin. Not the sink; the sink has a bottom and you have counted to it. The margin, which is the distance between a thing straining and a thing breaking, and which is the only gift a wise power should ever give a world that is doing well on its own: not to spare it the strain, which is its life, but to stand between the strain and the break, and to say nothing about it, and to stay.

The second thing you do before the moss closes your eyes, because after that you will not be able to.

You wait for the stillest night. The one with no wind under the cloud and the glow out there on its underside, dull and orange, brightest it ever gets. You wait for the hour when the light is going and the first two notes come up from the valley below you, and are answered, and are answered.

And you draw the cloud off.

Not from the mountain. From the border. For the length of one round of calling, from the first note to the stop, you thin the floor of this world until it is the ceiling of the one below and there is nothing between them but distance. And you turn the valley mouths, just slightly, just for now, toward the plains, the way you would cup a hand.

You do not know what it looks like from down there and you will never know, because you will not leave. But you know what they hear. At their engines, in their light, under a sky that has been the color of their own breath for so long that they have stopped tilting their heads back, they hear two notes from very high up, the second lower, taking about as long as a breath. Then two more, from further along the wall. Then more, until the whole rim of the world is sounding, each on its own time, none in chorus. And then, all at once, silence. Not a trailing off. A stop. And in the stop, if anyone is holding still enough, a beat, low, felt in the sternum before the ear, coming up through the floor of the room they are standing in—which they have been feeling their whole lives and have taken for the machines, and which is not the machines, and which they will now never quite be able to take for the machines again.

Then the cloud closes. You let the valleys turn back. You do it once. Once is all the mountain can afford, and once is enough, if a thing is truly heard, to be unforgettable, and you are not in the business of making anyone do anything. You are in the business of letting the below know, in its body before its mind, that it is being held, and that the holding is a living thing, and that living things have a number.

What they do with that is theirs. It was always going to be theirs. You have only made sure the mountain has a voice on both sides of the border now, and that the voice says what the mountain sounds like from the inside, and stops on the beat.

The frog calls twice. Something drops from the canopy and hits the water with a soft sound. Across the valley the being turns its head, not toward you—there is nothing to turn toward, only a shape in a tree that is a little greener than it was—and turns back. The salamander comes up, and pops, and hangs in the blue, and goes down. The cloud comes back in and the world contracts to what is within reach.

Under everything, the engine that is not this mountain's turns, and the mountain breathes it in.

And on one dome, in a low twisted tree at the lip, something is counting.
