What comes over the rim is not a person, though it can wear the shape of one when a shape is needed, and it knows before the wind stops that it will not need one here. It steps down. The door of the wind shuts behind it. The struck-flint air closes over it like water over a dropped stone, and it stands at the top of the white slope for a long time, doing the thing it is best at, which is not yet deciding.

It has come because something asked. Not the town; the town has no asking in it. Not the ground either, or not in words. But somewhere in the long attention it keeps on the world there was a snag, the way a fingernail snags on a single raised thread in a smooth cloth, and the snag was here, and it has learned to go where the snag is and look.

So it looks.

---

The first thing it sets down is the wish to send someone.

It is a strong wish. The doors are the height of a person. The stairs are cut to a stride. The rooms are the size of a sleep. Anything that has ever loved a person feels the pull of an empty room built to fit one, and the power has loved a great many, and it stands in the first street and for a moment has them all in its mind, the ones who need a wall between themselves and a wind, the ones who have never once had a room the exact size of their lying down. It could bring them. It could bring one. The town would hold a whole small people and the material would drink their footsteps and their voices and never once ask what they were.

And then it would be a habitation, and it would be finished.

That is what it sees, standing there. A hearth would put soot on a wall, and the wall would not go up over the soot; the builders route around fans, they would route around a hearth, and the wall would stop. A peg driven in would be a fixed point, and nothing here is fixed; the town's whole condition is to be a hand higher than it was, and a peg would hold one hand's height of it forever. People make residue. That is not a fault in them. It is very nearly what they are for. But this place is the one place the power has ever found that is all form and no residue, and to fill it would not complete it, it would end it, and something else would begin, a town with people in it in a basin with no water, and the people would need water, and the power would have made a place it must then keep saving.

It puts the wish down on the curb like a coat it has decided not to wear. The curb is the height of its hand. It notices that, and is not sure whether to be moved.

---

The second thing it sets down is the wish to take a side.

It watches the eater for most of a darkening, sitting where the visitor sat, on the warm spheres, and it sees what the visitor saw: the long unlit place that pours, the angle going out of the stone, the glass fogging as something rests in its own work. It watches the builders too, on their sills, laying floor back into wall, straightening what was rounded, patient as needles. It has ended wars. It knows the shape of a war from the inside, the way a war has a wanting in it, each side wanting the other's absence. It listens for that here and does not find it. The builder on the sill does not want the eater gone. The eater does not want the builders gone. Each wants only the wall, in the one condition it can make a wall be, and they make it be both, a foot apart, and neither of them minds.

If it stopped the eater, the town would go up straight and sharp and never stop, and there would be no rounded rooms, and no warmth, and no seams for the fans to stand in, and in time the basin would be a single spire of aligned white plate with nothing living as it. If it stopped the builders, the eater would round every edge and sleep in every room and the town would become a field of warm pearls with a long white thing asleep in the middle of it and nothing standing up at all. Either way the arguing stops, and the power has learned — slowly, at a cost — that most of what it has ever been asked to save was the arguing.

It sets that down too.

---

The third thing it does not set down, because it is not a wish but a sum.

The spheres accumulate. The builders will not touch them. Every wall the eater rounds is that much floor that will never be wall again, and the floor is finite, laid in scales toward a centre, and there is a day, very far off, when the last plate has been taken up and set in a line and the eater has come along it and left it as pearls, and the town is a drift of dull white warmth in a bowl of bare stone, and the fans have nothing to stand in, and the ground has finished doing the thing it does.

The power sees this day. It has a hand on the scale, if it wants one. It could turn a sphere back into a plate; it is not hard. It could do it to all of them, quietly, every darkening, forever, and the town would never run out.

And then the town would never run out.

It sits with that for a while. It thinks about the visitor, who lay down in the upper room and found it exactly long enough, and rose, and left, and did not come back. It thinks about how the town knew nothing of the visitor, and was nonetheless, for those hours, lived in, and how the not-knowing did not make the living less. It thinks that the ground has all the time there is, and that this is not the same as needing all the time there is. A thing that stands up over and over is not asking to stand up forever. The patience the visitor felt was not the patience of waiting for an end to be prevented. It was the patience of something that has already accepted the shape of its own finishing and is, in the meantime, unhurriedly making right angles.

To keep it going past that would be to want something for it that it does not want. The power has done this before, to things it loved, and calls it by its right name now, which is not benevolence.

It leaves the sum alone.

---

The fans it does not consider at all, and then, late, it does.

They are the only thing here that grew toward a seam. Not toward light, not toward heat, not toward the material — toward the place where two conditions of the material meet and disagree. The power knows a little about what grows in disagreement. It has spent most of its long life standing in seams. It crouches in a doorway where the fans are thick and puts a finger to one and it flexes as a whole and returns, and it thinks: you are the ones who noticed first. Before the visitor, before me. You found the argument and set yourselves down in it and drank whatever there was.

It does nothing for them. There is nothing to do. But it has the sense, which it does not examine too closely, of having been recognised.

---

So this is what it does, in the end, since the question was whether it would do anything.

It waits until the haze has gone slate and the walls are the brightest thing there is. It goes to the plaza. It finds the scallop where the eater is not, and it goes into it, and it sits on the warm spheres with its back to the glass, and it lets itself be warm — properly warm, not the borrowed warmth of the pearls but its own, which it usually keeps in — and it waits.

It takes a long time. The eater has no reason to come and no reason not to. But some while into the dark the glow at the scallop's mouth dims along its lower edge, and the unlit place pours in along the curve, and stops, and settles, a hand's breadth from the power's side, and the glass fogs.

For a while, then, there are two warm things in the basin.

Nothing in the eater knows this. It has no front, no eyes, nothing to know with. It has come to the round room because the round room is where it sleeps, and there is something warm in the room, and it has never in its existence met another warm thing, and it does not have the equipment to be surprised. It only settles, a little closer than it needed to. That is all. It settles a little closer than it needed to, and sleeps, and the power sits very still beside it in the dark and does not touch it, and lets it have, for one darkening, what it has never had: a room it did not have to warm alone.

That is the whole intervention. It is not clear that it is one.

---

Before the haze pales it rises, slowly, so the pouring thing beside it does not stir, and steps up out of the scallop. Two builders are already on the scallop's lip, laying their ridges. It goes around them. It walks out along the street whose curb it left its wish on, and the wish is gone, drunk like a sound, which is right. It reaches the straight line where the street stops and steps over it onto floor, and the plates point in behind it, every one, toward the town, toward the glow, toward the round room where the last of its warmth is going out of the glass.

At the rim it turns and looks back. This is the one thing the visitor did not do. Below, the town is a faint lit lattice in a white bowl, every edge drawn in a hairline, going up and going round, exactly as it was, minus nothing, plus nothing, and in the middle of it a small darkness in one wall of the plaza that is a sleeping thing in a room, and around the darkness a crisp grey-green fringe where something stands in the seam and drinks.

The power has been asked, by the snag in its attention, whether this needed anything. It has its answer. It puts a hand on the rim, the way you would on the shoulder of someone you were leaving well, and it says the only sentence there was ever to say here, which the basin cannot hear and does not need to.

*You are not unfinished.*

Then the wind takes it, and the sound of the wind is almost too much, and it goes back up onto the plain to the next place, carrying nothing, having left nothing, except that for one night the warm thing had company, and the room it sat in is by now a hand higher and the exact size of no one, which was always what it was for.
