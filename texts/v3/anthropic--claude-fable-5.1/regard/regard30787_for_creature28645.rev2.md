It comes down the way the light does, and finds the slab the way you would have to: by knowing. It lies for a while at the lifted edge. It is not in a hurry. It has the kind of patience that makes the tubeworm's three centuries look like a held breath, and it uses some of that patience now to do nothing but look, because it has learned — it is wise; that is the premise — that the first thing benevolence wants to do is almost always the wrong thing, and the way to find the right thing is to let the first thing pass.

The first thing is the lid.

It sees the rind of carbonate forming along the threshold, the crack a hair narrower than last year, and everything in it that is capable of care leans toward that crack the way the plumes lean toward warmth. Hold it. A finger's width. It would cost nothing. The light would keep coming in, the larvae would keep drifting in along the blue bar, the fish would have its door. The room would go on.

It lets that pass, and looks at why.

The rind is not being done to the room. The rind is the room breathing out. Every grain of it was, an hour or a year ago, methane in the mud, and then something in the consortium ate it, and this is what was left. To hold the lid open would be to put a hand over the mouth of the thing that feeds the city and say: eat, but do not exhale. There is no version of that which is not a slow strangling of the very thing it is trying to preserve. And the deeper mistake, which it sees a moment later and holds onto longer: the room has never not been closing. There was no golden age when the slab lay lifted and the crack was wide and the city was safe. The crack was widest on the day the first larva came in, and it has narrowed every day since, and every rib in every avenue grew in a room that was already sealing. A room that stayed open would not be this city rescued. It would be a different thing, with a different fate, and no one under the slab asked for it.

The second thing is the seep.

It goes down. It can do that. It follows the fractures down through the slab and the stiffening mud beneath and into the soft dark where the consortium is thickest and doing its unwitnessed work, and it follows the fluid back the way the fluid came, down through sediment that has not seen light since before there was anything in the sea with eyes, to the buried things whose slow undoing is the city's breath. It wants to know how much is left. This is the question it can answer that nothing in the room can ask.

There is a great deal left. Enough that the room will close for other reasons long before the seep runs dry. Enough that the slope will leak, here and there, for longer than the room has existed by a factor it does not bother to compute. It feels something at this that it would call relief if it needed the word. Then it considers the other version — make the flow stronger, warmer, so the plumes stand fuller and the mussels gape wider and the amphipods thicken into a cloud you could see from the threshold — and it lets that pass too, quickly, because it has seen what happens when the sulfide comes faster than the blood can bind it, and the balance the city keeps is not a compromise the city has settled for. It is the city. Half a degree is what everything here is shaped to. Half a degree is the shape.

The third thing is the fish, and this one it does not let pass quickly.

It looks at the fish for a long time. It counts heartbeats — one, and the long space, and one — and it sees what the prose that brought you here saw: that there will be a year when the gap and the body cross, and the fish will not know the year, and after it the question of leaving will have been answered without ever being asked. And the power thinks: I could give it the year. Not open the door. Just hold the door where it is for one more year, so that the crossing comes later, so that if in that year the fish should drift toward the blue bar and find it wide enough, that would be the fish's doing and not mine.

It is a good thought. It is the most benevolent thought it has had, and it turns it over carefully to find the flaw, and the flaw is this: the fish is not being held in the room by the crack. Look at it. Its pale body has the shape of the corner. Its eyes, such as they are, are turned toward the cloud that thins toward it. It came in as a juvenile and it has spent every year since becoming an animal that lives here, and the door has been open the whole time, and it has not gone. To hold the gap for a year would be to tilt the room toward a choice the fish has already, with its whole body, declined. The outside is not the fish's rescue. The outside is exile from the only place it has ever been an adult. And the power cannot inform it — cannot lay the two facts side by side for it, the narrowing gap and the growing body, because the fish does not hold facts, only gradients, and the gradient says stay.

So it does not touch the crack. But it does not stop looking at the fish, because it has noticed something else about it, something the rock has noticed too in its way.

The rock will remember everything under the slab except this. It will hold the tubes as ribs and the mussels as ridges and the lobsters, if they die in their niches, as small hollows shaped like folded claws. It will hold the direction the plumes leaned. It will hold, if you know how to read it, the interval of the pulse, in the layering of the cement. The whole city will lie in the mud as a lens of white stone with its own portrait packed inside, and someone, someday, might crack it open and know almost everything.

But not the fish. The fish has no shell and no root and no bacteria and it will not stay still long enough. When it stops, the sediment will cover it and the carbonate will grow into the space where it was and there will be a fish-shaped absence in the rock for a while, and then not even that, because the cement fills absences. The only animal in the room with eyes is the only one that will leave no record. The witness that was not watching will not be witnessed.

The power finds that this is the thing it can do.

It stays for one full pulse. It has the patience. It waits through the long interval with the amphipods spread thin and the plumes upright and the lobsters folded, and then the fluid surges and the room warms by the fraction only the amphipods can feel, and the power feels it too, because it has chosen to, and it watches the plumes lean — all of them, together, the whole ceiling bowing one way like grain — and the mussels open a hair along the avenues and the cloud drifts and bunches and two lobsters unfold and take their one step and pick at the rock. It watches the far corner, where the cloud is thinnest, and between one look and the next the fish's mouth is closed on something, and this time something saw the closing. Then the surge passes and the plumes straighten and the cloud spreads and the lobsters fold and the room is a still again.

It keeps that. Not the positions — the rock will have the positions. The motion. The lean and the recovery and the fact that it was a lean, chosen by nothing, performed by everything. The direction the warmth was pushing when the city bowed. The rate of the heartbeat in the corner. The exact pale shape of the one thing here that could have left and did not. It takes these the way you would take a photograph of a thing you knew would be preserved forever as a statue: because the statue will be true and it will not be this.

Then it considers the last inhabitant, the one on its way, and here it has nothing to do at all, and knows it at once. The larva is not choosing badly. The oldest rib is the highest and the hardest and the right place to hold, and it has always been the right place, and every rib in the room was once a larva that found the highest rib and held. To steer this one down the slope to the hollow that is only now forming under a crust that is only now thickening — it could; it knows where that hollow is; it passed it coming in — would be to decide that being the last to settle here is worse than being the first to settle there, and the power does not believe that. The last mussel on the ceiling will open its hinge exactly as the first one did. It will feed on exactly the same breath. The room does not treat its final tenant differently from its founder and the power sees no reason to be kinder than the room.

The one thing left is the thing it most wants and refuses most easily: to be known. To let the plumes lean toward it. To be, for one pulse, the warmth. Nothing under the slab could receive it — the tubeworms do not hear, the mussels do not see, the consortium does not have a self to be told — and the fish would only startle, and there is nowhere in the room for a startled fish to go. So it stays as it came, unannounced, and the city goes on not knowing that anything has been looking, which is the same condition the city has been in since the first fleck touched the ceiling, and is right.

It goes back up the way the bubbles do. On its way down the slope it passes the other place, a few hundred meters off, where the fluid has found its new seam and the mud has begun to stiffen around it and beneath the stiffness there is a hollow no bigger than a cupped hand with nothing in it yet. It looks in. Grey sediment, a thin roof, a taste in the water. It leaves it exactly as it found it.

What it takes away is not much, by its own measure. A pulse. A heartbeat. The shape of a corner and the pale shape in it. And one thing it did not expect, which it turns over on the long way up: that it came down carrying every rescue it knew how to perform, and the room had already performed them all — had its own memory in the rock, its own witness in the corner, its own next generation drifting in on the blue bar, its own provider spread through a million bodies and a cubic meter of mud, all of it living inside the digestion of its own food and building the wall that would hold it and calling that not a failure but the arrangement. The only thing missing had been something to see it move. So that was what it was for. It had come down as a power and found that the highest form of care available to it was to be, for the length of one surge and one recovery, the room's eyes; and it had been that; and it had been enough; and the room was closing behind it at exactly the rate it always had.
