The power is not a stranger, but it comes as one. Its ears ring. It notices this and sets it aside, because the first thing a wise power does in a place it has not seen is not act but listen, and the plateau is, before it is anything else, a sound.

So it listens. The pump-houses on their night rhythm. The forest ticking. A valve seat somewhere letting off and settling. The swifts asleep in the vents. The chord — and the power hears the chord the way no one on the plateau can, which is to say it hears it *as a sound*, a made thing with a beginning, three or four octaves below the ear, steady as a held breath. It hears in it what the plateau-people would hear only if it stopped: how much depends on it. And then it hears, under the chord, what they have never heard at all, because it is masked by the thing it makes — the fire itself. A dry roar. Shovels. Someone coughing.

It goes down.

The stoking galleries are not a rumour. They are a climate.

The heat there is not the heat of the surface, which is wet and comes from everywhere; it is dry and comes from one direction, and it has made a people the way the light made the rivets and the fog made the growers. They are grey. Not pale — grey, the ash ground into them the way the rust is ground into everyone else, so that in the low red light they look like figures modelled in the same fine drift that the fog cannot wet on the paths in the morning. Their eyes are narrow and their lids are thick and they do not look at the fire directly; they read it from the wall it lights. Their chests are keel-shaped like everyone's, but their shoulders are the shoulders of people who have shovelled since they were eleven. They go in young because a body that finishes growing in the galleries becomes a gallery body, and a body that comes in grown does not. That is the whole secret of why they are not seen again on the surface. Nothing holds them. The hatch opens from both sides. But a stoker on the surface squints and pants and flinches from the fog on his skin the way a rivet would flinch from the dark under the ferns, and the work has made the face, and the face goes back to the work.

They know the surface. They know it the way the surface knows them — as what falls through. Fig-leaves come down the vents. Rain comes down the vents and hisses. The swifts go out at dusk and come back and the stokers tell the season by the noise of them. They have a word for the surface and it is the same as their word for *cool*. They leave the dish empty because they are hungry and because it is what is done, but the power, crouching where the low handprints are, sees what the surface relative palms and does not discuss: not always nothing. A cinder, sometimes, pinched into a shape while it was soft. A goat. A rail. Once, badly, a face.

So the traffic has reversed before. It has just never been said.

The stokers stand on a hoist. It is the oldest engine in the plateau and runs on the plateau's own steam, and it goes down. The power follows it. Through the plate, through the deck below where the fig-roots hang and drip, through the next and the next — moss, iron-stain, the white thickening — and on the seventh deck it finds the birds.

They are the colour of the lattice: grey-white, with a rind of rust-red along the wing, like the lizards. They live in the cloud and have never seen the sun except as a brightness overhead that the white becomes at noon. They eat the under-hanging figs, and they fly down, and their droppings fall fourteen decks and more into the brown, and the seeds in them take. The power sees this and holds it: the plateau's dead, rooted, reaching through the floor of the world, feeding a bird that plants them in a country no one up there has seen. The long fall is not a waste. It is a sowing. Nobody on the plateau knows this and nobody on the plateau needs to, and the power is careful to notice that it is the second time it has thought *nobody needs to* and that this is a thought a power should be suspicious of, and it goes on down.

The brown is a country. There are people in it. They are the shape of a pit — short, broad, dust-coloured, with a way of looking up. They cut the seam and load the hoist because it is what has always been done, and things fall on them out of the white: warm rain, fig-cuttings, a rivet-tool, an eye-cloth, a carved goat, a lock of hair, and now and then a sapling they did not plant that grows into a tree whose fruit tastes faintly of iron. They hear the chord. From below it is a note in the sky. They have a word for the green at the top of the cloud, and it is used the way another people might use the word for heaven, and they have made their peace with the traffic that comes down and never goes back, and they call the things that fall *what is sent*.

The power looks at the seam. This is the one thing it has come to measure, and it measures. It is long. It is rivets long. It is not forever.

Now the power sits — on a girder at the plateau's edge, say, where the wind is, with the white below and the light above and the whole roaring warm wet thing behind it — and it considers what it has been asked, by no one, which is: what would you do?

And it feels, first, the pressure of its own goodness. This is worth being honest about. Benevolence is a pressure too. It wants to be needed. It looks at the girl asleep on the pipe and the stoker coughing and the pit-people looking up, and it wants to *give*, and it has to sit with that for a while, because the plateau is a body of pressures in balance, and one more pressure — even a kind one, even a wise one — is one more thing the structure will have to grow moss around.

So it takes the temptations one at a time.

It could shore the piers. It sees the strut that will go next and the one after that and, far down the count, the one whose going will not be followed by silence. It could make the plateau true to its own rule — *what has stood will stand* — and it declines, and its reason is not that the ending is beautiful, because endings are not, but that the rule is a description and not a promise. The people are the lagging. They are what the plateau grew in order to be kept, and they keep it, at a hand's width a generation, and if the power kept it for them they would stop being keepers and become the kept, and the shape of them would change, and the thing that came out the other side would not be the thing it was moved to love. Long is what every place gets. The plateau's long is long. The power lets it be that.

It could tell them. About the stokers, the birds, the brown, the seam. It declines, because they already know exactly as much as they can carry and have shaped it into courtesy — you send a little of what you have after what you have lost, and you do not ask where it went — and a courtesy is not improved by an answer. The pit-people, too. It does not tell them that the note in the sky is a boiler.

It could go into the galleries and bring the stokers up. It declines, because nothing holds them; the hatch opens both ways; and the power has now seen three faces pressed by three climates and does not think it is wiser than the work at deciding which face a person should have. It notices the cough. It notices the burns. It notices that a stoker's life is shorter than a grower's and that this is a real thing and not a story, and it does not pretend otherwise. But it also notices that the dish is not always empty, and that a people who pinch cinders into goats for relatives they will never see are not a people waiting for rescue. They are a people doing what everyone on the plateau does: tending, in the dark, a thing they did not build and cannot leave.

It could do nothing. And it sits with this longest, because it is the wisest-looking of the options and the power knows that wisdom is sometimes just fatigue wearing a robe. Nothing has a cost. Nothing means the chord goes on unheard by anyone outside it, and the traffic goes on being called one-way when it is not, and the girl grows up and cuts roots and dies and is rooted and goes down through the plate in a fig and is eaten by a bird and planted in the brown, and it is all *fine*, it is all in balance, and no one anywhere ever once knew the whole of it.

The power decides that this last is the thing it will not permit. Not the falling. The unwitnessedness.

---

So here is what it does, and it is small, and it is chosen with the care of something that could do anything.

It does not add. It reveals one true thing, once, to one person, and then it stays.

In the morning, when the fog comes up through the lattice and crosses the surface and beads on every leaf, one of the fig-eaters comes with it. This has never happened. The birds live in the white and the white does not rise past the plate — except that it does, every morning, as fog, and the fog is warm from its passage through the decks, and a bird that has spent its life in cloud has no reason not to follow cloud upward through a gap where a root has pried the tile apart. The power does not push it. It only makes sure, in the way that a power can make sure of a very small thing, that on this morning the gap is where the bird is and the bird is where the gap is.
