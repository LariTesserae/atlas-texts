It comes across the finished world at evening, when there is nothing up there to see and the whole plateau is one flat blade of dusk. What draws it is not the seam. The seam is too small to draw anything. What draws it is the warmth — a thread of air rising where no air should rise, carrying a smell that does not belong to stone. It follows the thread down the way you follow a sound you cannot yet place.

It arrives at the lip as the bats are going out.

And it does what anything must do here, wise or not: it waits for its eyes. It lets the dim come up around it. It sees the swallows first, because they are loudest, and then the lanterns, and then the palm-pressed doorways in the middle band, and it keeps seeing further back — the ladders, the drying rack of bones, the girl with the string of lizard teeth, the line of listeners along the lit ledge — and it understands that in this room there is always something further back, and it does not hurry.

It is a long time before it does anything at all. That is the first thing it does.

---

It has been asked — or it asks itself; with such a power there is not much difference — about *the being who lives here.* Singular. So it looks for the one.

It counts. Sixty people, all kin. Ten thousand swallows. Wasps beyond counting, bats beyond counting, beetles beyond anything. Six or seven snakes. One shape at the lip beside it, low and patient and the color of the plateau, which does not turn to look at it and does not need to.

None of these is the being. The power sees that quickly, because it sees the way the room refuses to sort. The chick that was in the cup is in the snake; the snake, in time, is in the floor; the floor is in the leaf; the leaf is in the girl. The room is not full of tenants. The room is one animal that has agreed to wear a few hundred coats and change them constantly, and what does not change — what has been going around and around since the stone was young — is a small fixed measure of water that has never once been permitted to gather into a drop.

*That* is who lives here. The power looks at it a long time — the circulation, the one thing in the room that has never died — and feels something that in a smaller creature would be called awe, and in a larger one is simply recognition.

And it sees at once the difficulty. You cannot help the water. It needs nothing. It is already the only thing here that is safe.

You can only help the shapes it passes through. And every one of those shapes is somebody else's food.

---

So it considers, as such a power must, the things it could do. It considers them honestly, one at a time, and it sets each one down, and it does not pretend the setting-down is free.

Rain. It could bring rain. One cloud, low over the plateau, one night's soaking through the seam. The beards would drink until they rotted. The paper cities would slump and fall. The mud cups would soften and slide down the wall in a single slow avalanche of ten thousand years of building. The floor would drown, and the beetles with it, and the room would smell of death for a year and be silent after. It would be the most generous act in the room's history and would end the room. The power sets it down.

Light. It could open the seam — a hand's width more, an arm's. The people cannot look at the blade; give them a sky. But the room lives in the dim the way the bats live on the ceiling. Widen the seam and the warmth escapes at night; the cold comes down and stays; the wet hour thins and fails; the succulents, which have organized their entire being around a single hour of beading, do not adapt but die. The sixty go up onto the plateau, squinting, and there is nothing there. The power sets it down.

Knowledge. It could tell them the world is not finished. It could show them — the girl, say — the far edge of the plateau, and what lies beyond it, and that there are places where water is a thing in the landscape and not a thing in bodies. It thinks about this longest. It is the one that feels most like kindness and is most nearly a killing. Today the sixty are the whole of a world. Tell them there is another and they become the poor of somewhere, sixty small dusty people in a hole, and everything they know — the six nests and the one egg, the sound a lantern makes before it falls, the marks where the year turns on the wall — becomes a way of being backward. The room would survive. The people in it would not, not as themselves. The power sets it down and does not feel good about it.

The snakes. The thing beside it at the lip. It could remove them. It looks at the shape at the seam, the only creature in a day's walk of nothing that has chosen to stay, and it looks at the snake on the ledge below with the child's exact expression, and it understands that the room is not a place where predators have been permitted; it is a balance struck between things that eat each other, and the balance is the room. Take the snakes and the swallows overbuild and the wall comes down under its own weight in a century. Take the thing at the seam and the bats have never been culled and the ceiling grows until the floor cannot carry it. The power sets this down easily. It is not even tempted.

Mercy. And here it stops, because this is the one that is not about the room. It is about a woman of forty with the teeth of a woman of seventy. It is about a child on a ledge who is going to fall, sometime in the next year, and whom the power could catch. Could catch every one of them, always, for as long as it chose to sit here. Could take the sting out of the wasps and the bite out of the cold and see that nobody ever again goes into the floor before their time.

It wants to. That is worth saying. A benevolent power looks at a child on a ledge and wants to, and the wanting does not go away because it is wise.

But it knows what a held hand becomes. The first catch is a gift. The hundredth is a fact of the room, and the room reshapes itself around the fact, and in three generations the ledges are built with less care because falling has stopped meaning anything, and the sixty are no longer people who know their wall in the dark. They are people who are kept. The floor is the only thing in the room that is entirely theirs, and it is made of them, and it would stop being made of them, and then it would not be theirs.

The power sets it down. This one costs.

---

When it has set everything down, it looks at what it has not yet looked at, which is the stone.

The room is a forgotten weakness. The people are right about that; they say it the way they say the sun is on the third ladder, and they are right. A crack was left. A hollow was not filled. The whole city has the shape of the plateau's oldest mistake, and the plateau is very old and has had a long time to become the kind of thing that does not make mistakes.

The power can see into the stone, a little. It can see the slabs of the lid resting against each other in the dark, and the hand's width between them, and the weight above and the emptiness below, and it can see the ways this ends. A slab shifts a finger's width and the seam closes to a slit, and the bats cannot pass, and they die on the ceiling in their thousands, and the room's one import stops, and everything below starves in the dark within a year. Or a slab shifts the other way, and the lid gives, and the room is an open pit under the finished sky by morning, and the water — the one immortal — simply leaves.

Both of these are in the stone. Not soon. But the stone has nothing to do except wait, and it is better at waiting than anything in the room.

And the power sees one more ending, which is not in the stone at all. It is in the floor. The floor rises. An arm's length for every span of grandmothers. The room fills from below, slowly, with its own dead and the bats' leavings, and in time — a long time, longer than the swallows have been building — the black soil will reach the middle tier, and then the lanterns, and then the ceiling, and the last of the sixty will live with their faces a hand's width from the light they cannot look at, and then there will be no room, only a plateau with a soft dark patch in it where something was, once, forgotten.

That ending is the room's own. The power looks at it and recognizes it as a completion, not a failure. Everything that lives in the pit goes into the floor. The pit, too, if let alone, goes into the floor.

The stone endings are not the room's own. They are the world remembering what it forgot.

---

So here is what it does.

It goes into the lid.

It does not become the lid; it is too wise to think stone can be improved by being made of something else. It takes up a place in it, the way a hand takes up a place on a door that must not swing. It sets itself against the slab that would close and the slab that would fall, and it says to the stone the one thing a wise power can say to stone without lying: *not yet.* Not forever. Not against the floor. Only against you.

Nothing in the room changes. The wet hour comes and goes. The blade crosses the wall. The seam stays a hand's width. The sixty do not know anything has been decided about them, because nothing has; they have only been left to their room for as long as their room lasts, which is the one thing they have always assumed and never had.

And the power sets a second thing beside the first, quieter. It lets the seam be hard to find. Not hidden — a wise power does not hide things; it simply does not help them be found. You could walk for a day up there and believe the world was finished. The power sees to it that you go on believing that for as long as it sits in the stone. The room's second-greatest danger has always been the same as its first: being noticed. The power has noticed it, and understands that this must be the last time for a while.

---

Then it does one more thing, and this one is for itself.

It goes back up to the lip, in the evening, when the bats are going out.

The shape is there — hunched, patient, the color of the plateau, the only creature in the whole finished world that stays. It has never come in. It has never had a name that was separate from the crack in the ground at dusk. It has never, in the memory of its kind, been near anything that was not either food or stone.

The power sits beside it.

It does not touch it or feed it or speak to it. It sits, in the thin cold, on a table of rock where nothing stands higher than a knee, and it watches the column pour up out of the dark and scatter and re-form, and now and then there is a snap, and the power does not flinch, because it has seen the snake on the ledge and the child above it and it knows what this is.

The shape does not turn. It does not need to. But it is not alone at the seam that evening, and it is not alone the next, and the power discovers that of all the things it has set down and picked up in this place, this is the one that asks nothing of the room and gives something anyway. Company at the lip. The being who eats what comes out, and stays, has someone who also stays.

Down below, the girl with the lizard teeth is on her ledge with her heels drawn up, watching the seam go from evening to stars. From where she sits there is a shape against the sky, low, the color of the plateau, exactly as there has always been. She could not say that it looks any different tonight. She would not say anything anyway; everyone already knows, and there is no one to say it to.

She watches until the last bat is through. Then she goes in, and the small fire in her doorway glows a while, and goes out.

Before dawn the cold air will slide down through the hand's width that is not going to close, and meet the warmth held by the stone that is not going to fall, and the room will breathe in. Everything will bead. The one who has never died will be on everything for an hour, and everyone will drink, and the wall will be dry again.

The power, in the stone, feels it happen, and holds, and is glad.
