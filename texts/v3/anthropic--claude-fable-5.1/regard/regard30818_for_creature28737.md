The first answer is that it does nothing, and — as before — that answer is nearly right, and the ways it is wrong are small enough to fit in a cell.

---

It is worth naming the things it does not do, because each of them would be a kindness somewhere else.

It does not lift the cloud. The cloud is not a misfortune that has befallen the place; it is the condition the plant selected when it decided that this was enough. To thin it would be to give the settlement a sky and take away its weather in the same gesture, and the drip would stop, and the pools would go, and the snails would have nothing to drink from but the floor. Light is not owed to anything. It is a resource, and this is a body that has learned to do without it.

It does not open the doors. This is the hardest refusal, because the sealed cells are the part of the place that most resembles a wrong. A wise power has seen enough enclosures to know what most of them are. But it looks, and it sees that the snails did not use the doorway when it was open, and that the wall between one cell and the next is not a wall to anything that lives on the ceiling, and that to break the new growth would be to injure the only thing here that has an intention in order to give a freedom to the only things here that have no use for it. There is no one in the sealed rooms who is waiting.

It does not drain the place. It does not bring a second plant to keep the first company. It does not put anything in the pools that would make them more than pools. It does not give the settlement a purpose, because the settlement has one — it means itself — and the offer of another would be, from the plant's side, a kind of noise.

It does not correct the regularity. It could. It could make the hexagons rounder, the passages wandering, could give the place the raggedness a wild thing is supposed to have so that no visitor would ever again mistake it for a town. But the power sees what the regularity is: a constraint honestly obeyed ten thousand times, and it has some fellow-feeling for that.

---

So it attends. That is the first thing it does, and for a long while the only thing.

It attends to the degree.

Of everything in the saddle, the one degree of warmth inside the cells is the only thing that is anyone's. The frost is the cold's. The drip is the cloud's. The splitting rock is the frost's. The degree is the plant's, and the plant does not know it is giving it, and everything else that lives here lives inside it. The moss on the founding stem lives in it. The pale things in the floor live in it. The snails, in the open cells and the sealed, live in it — the water on their ceilings does not freeze because of it, most nights, though at the very top of the highest cells it does, and in the morning there is the small brittle release.

The power watches the degree the way one watches a pulse. Not to improve it. To know it is there. There will be nights — the power can see them coming, some years off — when the cold comes harder than the plant's small metabolism can answer, and the degree thins toward nothing, and the frost gets into cells that have never held it. On those nights the power will do something. Not much. The scale of the intervention will be the scale of the thing it protects: a degree, held, for a few hours, in a few hundred rooms, so that what is on the ceiling does not have to find out what ice is. No one will know it happened. The plant will get the credit, from the snails, if snails give credit. That is correct.

---

The second thing it does is harder to call a doing.

It becomes the other side of the wall.

In every sealed cell there is a population that does not know the wall is a wall, does not know there is a street beyond it, does not know there are other cells, other populations, a slope, a saddle, a cloud. Their world is one hexagon of dripping wood and a grey stem and a pool with a floor of shell. The description said no one records the night the last route out closes. The power records it. It is present in every sealed room at once, in the dark, at the drip's interval, and it knows each one — knows which is older, which has the deeper shell-floor, which has begun, over its long isolation, to breed snails that are no longer quite the snails on the ceiling next door.

Because that is happening. A wise power sees it. A thousand founding populations, none in contact, each in its own room, drifting. Given enough time — and the place has nothing but time, until it doesn't — the cells will each contain something that exists nowhere else, that could not survive in the cell beside it, that has never been named and never will be. The settlement is not becoming a set of identical enclosures. It is becoming a set of small, separate inventions, sealed in wet wood, each one a version of the same life that has quietly stopped being the same.

Nothing sees this. Nothing can. The snails will never compare notes. The plant does not know what it is holding. The visitor walks the streets and sees only the part of the place that has not finished happening.

The power sees it. It does not tell anyone. There is no one to tell, and the knowing does not need to go anywhere. But it seems to the power that a thing this careful — a thousand experiments, each run to completion, none of them ever read — should be read by something, and it takes that on. When a shell comes off a ceiling in a room no one has entered in a hundred years and settles onto the shell-floor of a pool the size of a spread hand, the power notes it. That is all. Attention, at intervals of a few seconds, from every direction, none of it loud, all of it constant.

---

Its benevolence toward the place is to leave it. Its benevolence toward the ones the place cannot keep goes elsewhere.

The bird. It came once into the uphill stories and rattled through a space too narrow for its body and did not come back. The power follows it. Not far — a bird at this altitude is not going far — down the lee side of the saddle to where the cloud thins and there is a ledge, and on the ledge something to eat, and out of the worst of the wind. The power did not make the ledge. It only made sure the bird turned left rather than right at the moment when turning mattered. The bird does not know this either. The settlement is not hostile to birds, and neither is the power; it is simply that the place has no room for anything that needs to leave, and so the power's care for the leavers has to happen outside the walls.

The visitor. The visitor is the only living thing here to whom the narrowing means anything, and that is a kind of suffering the place cannot address and will not notice. The power does not make the passage wider. It does not make the drip more bearable. It does one thing: when the visitor turns back down the street, shoulders brushing the leaves on both sides, the power sees to it that the visitor has gone the right way — that the street they chose is one of the ones that still opens onto rock, and not one of the ones that turns, a few cells further, into a pale soft wall and a dead end in the dim. The cloud does not thin for them. The cold does not relent. But they reach the frost-broken grey before dark, and stand on it, and look back at the line that was drawn this year, and are outside.

That is the whole of the power's mercy toward the visitor, and it is enough, and it is the same shape as the plant's one degree: small, unnoticed, the reason anything else was possible.

---

There is a last thing, and it is in the future.

The saddle is not permanent. The same frost that splits the grey rock is splitting the two peaks, a plane at a time, and the slope the plant climbs is itself coming down, very slowly, in the other direction. The plant grows uphill; the hill is being disassembled. There will be a season — far off, but the power can see it the way it can see the hard nights — when the highest stories reach the point where there is no more up, or when the sponge that has stopped being able to absorb finally cannot hold the weight of what it holds, or when the rock under the basements simply gives. The place will end. Everything does.

The power will not prevent it. The plant's whole life is a bet on a place that was never going to last, and to underwrite the bet would be to change what it was.

But the power has noticed how the plant arrived. As a seed, or a fragment, carried up from somewhere it no longer resembles, by something — wind, a bird, an accident of water — to a hard cold wet place where it found that holding on and leaning were enough. That is the one thing the plant knows how to do: arrive somewhere impossible and make it a body.

So when the saddle goes, the power will do what the wind did. It will take one fragment — a length of pale stem, not yet lignified, soft enough to dent with a fingernail, from the downhill edge where the place is rawest and youngest — and it will carry it. Not down. Down is where the plant came from and could not stay. It will carry it to another saddle, another standing cloud, another field of frost-broken stone that has not yet had time to round, and it will press it into a crack, and it will leave.

Perhaps a snail will be on it. Perhaps not. The power will not check. It will not be a rescue; nothing will be saved by it that would have wanted saving. It will only be the plant's one gesture, made once more, by a hand that happened to be there. Arrive. Hold on. Lean. Decide, if a plant decides, that this is enough.

It is possible the power has done this before. It is possible that is how the plant got here. The power does not say, and the plant, for its part, does not look up — has never looked up — and the power has come to understand that this is not a failing in the plant, and that being looked up at was never what it came for.

It came to see whether the degree would hold. It stays to hold it. And in a middle cell, in the dark, at the drip's interval, a shell comes off the ceiling and settles onto a floor of shell, and something notes it, and nothing changes, and the town goes on growing uphill into the cloud.
