It watches first.

That is not a delay before the act. It is the act, or the first part of it. A power that had not watched would arrive at the speed of its own concern, and the basin has no room for anything that arrives at speed. The heron would be standing at the edge of the pool where the concern surfaced. The heron would stab.

So it watches for a period that has to be measured in rains. It watches the pools drop at grey noon and come back by evening. It watches the fourfold trunk gather moss in four places at once. It counts the sleepers turning and loses the count and understands that losing the count is the correct result. It watches the one who stands at the edge of the pool with a hand out. It watches another come to twenty paces behind that one and stop and, after a while, put a hand out too. It watches a third come behind the second.

It notices that from where it watches, the three are in a line, and the line points inward, and the line is very long, and the ones at the far end of it are green in the way the shred in the split bark is green.

The first answer is nothing. It gives that answer for longer than an hour. It gives it for long enough that the answer weathers, and it can see what shape the answer settles into, and the shape is a hand held out over water.

---

The things it does not do.

It does not drain the basin. There is nowhere for the water to go; that was the first thing true about the place. A power could make somewhere. It could open the peat at the low side and the whole fen would go down through the gap, and there would be, for an hour, a river, and then a valley of black mud with a few knuckles of soft stone in it and a long heavy shape lying across the bottom that was never meant to be looked at. It would be dry. The snails would die, finally, and leave shells. It would be a ruin, which is to say a place that was once whole and is now less, and the fen had not been a ruin, and making one where there was none is not a thing a benevolent power does, whatever the shape being held.

It does not let the insects in. It considers this longer. A midge is a day with wings; a single hatching would set a clock running in a place that has no clock, and the fen would have to learn, for the first time since the rocks lost their seams, what it is to hold something that ends. The heron would take them. The power could speak to the heron. The heron came from outside — it is the one thing here with a wound, and the wound is what let it in, and it might listen. But the power does not know what the heron would be if it stopped being the edit. It might fly. It might, at last, finish arriving, and in finishing become so completely part of the fen that there was no heron at all, and the air over the water would fill with brief things, and the roar would have voices in it, and the figure with its hand out would be listening to a crowd instead of a crowd through a wall. That might be better. The power does not know that it is better. It is not the figure's power, and it does not know what the figure came to hear.

It does not lift the figure out. It could. It could reach in at the speed of its concern and close around the green shape and take it up out of the basin and set it down on high ground somewhere, under a sky that is a source. It would be quick. It would be a stab. It would treat the one who has stood there longer than the alders as a brief thing that had fallen in, and the whole of that long attention as an accident to be removed, and the power has watched for enough rains to know that this is not so. Nothing was done to the figure. The figure came, or was, and put out a hand. The listening is not a snare. It is a choice that has weathered into a location. You do not rescue someone from a choice by making it for them the other way.

It does not stand at the rim and turn the walkers back. It considers this longest of all, because the line is long and points inward and every one in it stopped where the fen allotted and, given time, put out a hand. But it has watched them come, and it has not seen one dragged. They walked toward a stand of alders. They arrived at a stand of alders. They stood at the edge of a pool, and something in the standing was enough that they stayed. There is no rim to guard. Anyone who stands at the edge of anything long enough is in a fen.

---

What it does.

It comes in the way the water comes: from nowhere in particular, along the lines of the sleeper, no faster than the drift. It does not walk. Walking is for distance, and the basin does not measure distance. It seeps. It comes up through the peat the way the roar comes, and it is cold, and it is very slightly too heavy, and if you cupped a hand under it you would take it for the thread from a weeping rock. It takes long enough about this that the willow shoot the first walker broke off in passing has grown into a tree and fallen and been grown through.

By the time it is in the basin it is of the basin. That was the point. Nothing here could refuse it now. It has passed the test. It has held its shape through the rain.

It goes to the edge of a pool. It finds the one who has stood there longest — this is not difficult; the peat beneath that one has the deepest bowl, exactly as deep as it needs to be — and it comes to twenty paces behind, and it stops. The fen allots the distance and it accepts the distance. It stands where the walkers stood, facing the way the walkers faced, and the figure ahead of it does not turn around, because what is behind it is not where the listening is.

Then it does the one thing the walkers did not.

It does not put its hand out over the water. It puts its hand down. Flat, palm to the tussock, the way you did once when you wanted to feel the roar come up through the fibres.

And through the peat, into the roar, at the pitch of the crowd through the wall — so that it is not a voice over the fen's sound but a voice *in* it, the first sound in the basin ever made by a mouth for the purpose of reaching someone — it says the only thing a wise power says to a listener it has watched for that long.

It says: I hear you.

Not *I hear what you hear*. It does not know what the figure hears, and it will not pretend to. It says: I hear *you*. Listening. A hand held out and not moving for longer than there has been peat makes a sound. It is very faint. It had gone under everything, the way the sleeper's breathing goes under everything, and the power has been hearing it for rains, and now says so.

That is all. It does not ask the figure to turn. It does not ask the figure to stop. It does not offer a way out, because it does not know that out is wanted, and a way offered is a hand on the shoulder, and a hand on the shoulder is a stab. It offers the one thing the fen could not have produced on its own: an ear on the other side of the wall. If the figure has anything to say back — after all this time, to anyone — it will not now be said into the roar and lost. There is somewhere for it to go.

---

What happens.

Not much, at first, and at the fen's speed. The rain falls from the bright place in the west. The pools rise. The eel that is not an eel slides under a tussock and the channel behind it is a hand's breadth lower.

The heron, at the edge of the next clearing, opens its beak, and the croak arrives *at the same moment*. Not a half-second before. At the same moment. It is a small thing. It is the sound of something that has finished arriving.

The roar thickens. It thickens the way it does when a sleeper turns, except that it thickens everywhere — far to the left and far to the right and under the power's own feet, so that its knees bend to take it — and the ground rises in every part of the basin at once, thirty feet at a time in a hundred places, and settles, and the roar is what it was. Every sleeper turned over. Once. Together. The fen has never done that. The power, palm flat on the tussock, feels it come up through the fibres and understands it as the closest thing to a reply the ground can make: *heard*.

The figure does not turn around. It does not lower its hand. It has stood for longer than the alders and it does not stop standing. But the hand, which has been held palm down over the water for as long as the water has been slow there, turns.

Palm up.

The rain falls into it. The palm fills. It fills exactly as deep as it needs to be, and the water in it is very cold and very slightly too heavy, and the pool beneath it is the lightest thing in the basin, and now, when the power looks up, there are two shadows on the underside of the cloud, twenty paces apart. One with a hand out. One with a hand down.

---

It stays.

This is the last of what it does and the part that makes the rest mean anything. It does not move on to the next place that has drawn its attention. It has passed the test and it honours the test. It stands at twenty paces with its palm on the ground and lets the fen grow up around the standing and through it, and the alders drip past it and the water routes itself around its feet and the moss, at the edge of vision, spells something new that reassembles when you look away.

It has not fixed the fen. There was nothing to fix. It has not freed the figure; the figure did not ask. It has done one thing, which is to make sure that the one act of attention holding this whole basin in shape is no longer the only act of attention in it. Whatever the figure was listening for, it is still listening. But it is no longer the sole listener, and it has been heard, and its hand is open, and if it ever wants to say what it has heard in all this time, there is now somewhere, twenty paces back, exactly where a person would otherwise be alone, for the saying to land.

Toward what you take to be evening, though evening is only a deeper grey, another walker comes through the horsetail and the grey willow and arrives at a stand of alders very like the one they picked out, and stops at twenty paces behind the power, and stands, and after a while is not sure what to do with their hands.

They look ahead. They see a figure with its palm down on the ground.

Beyond it, at the edge of the light, a figure with its palm turned up to the rain.

The walker has come a long way. They are cold to the temperature of the water. They put a hand down on the tussock, flat, and feel the roar come up through the fibres, and in it — very faint, under everything, at the pitch of a crowd through a wall — two voices, and one of them is saying *I hear you*, and the other, slower, in a green that has been rained on for a very long time, is beginning to answer.
