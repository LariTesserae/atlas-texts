It comes in the way the cloud does, without a lid lifting anywhere, and it does not need the light, so it sees the valley whole for the first time since the valley was rock.

It hears the frogs first, as everyone does. Then under them the insects and over them the bats. Then, because it is listening for it — because that is what drew it, out of all the loud places — the clicking.

It goes to the clicking.

---

It finds her on the third night, at the lip of the fourth pit, turning flakes.

It does not announce itself. Nothing here has ever heard anything, and it has no wish to be the first thing heard. It settles into the pressure of the noise the way she has, and it watches her choose. Tick. Tick. She holds one up to a light that is not there and tilts her head against the chip and keeps it, and goes up the fern, and the crab opens, and the flake breaks, and she lets the pieces go.

It watches for the whole crab season. Then the frog season after it. Then the crabs again. It is in no hurry. It has learned, in other places, that the first thing a power wants to do is nearly always the thing it should not, and that the only cure is to stay past wanting.

---

So it stays, and it wants, and it sets each want down where it can see it, the way she sets down flakes.

The first is the mountain. It could reach down through the crack the snake lies over and close a hand around the heat and hold it. Not forever. Long enough. A thousand seasons instead of a few hundred. Time for the rain to round one stone.

It turns this one for a long while. It sees what would follow: the ferns rooting deeper and standing longer, the paste in the cracks thickening into something that could be called soil, the floods slowing into cycles, the cycles into a balance. The frogs would stop being a hundred nations and become one people, quieter. The gray-heads would stop being a plague that follows the crabs and become a population that manages them. The argument would resolve. It would be an ecosystem then, a good one, and it would be a place like other places, and the thing that had made it worth coming to would be the thing it had spent.

It sets the mountain down.

The second is keeping. This one is closer to its heart and harder to let go. It could put into the old bird, or into one of the young ones near her, the smallest possible idea: *the good edge is worth carrying*. Nothing more. Not the roost, not the cache, not the making — just the not-dropping. Everything else would follow, in its time, the way the ferns followed the dung.

It knows what keeping costs. It has watched it bought elsewhere. Keeping is a wound a creature accepts in exchange for a future — the weight of the thing carried, the ache of the place it is stored, the first grief when the stored thing is lost, and after that all the other griefs, which are the same grief. A being that keeps has bargained its lightness for its heirs. The bargain is a fair one where there are heirs. Here there is glass and a warm floor and a mountain not far down, and the heirs of the gray-heads are the paste. To give her keeping would be to sell her a future with nothing to deliver it in, and the only thing she would get for the price is the ache.

It sets keeping down.

The third is the beak. This one it hardly lifts. It could mend the chip while she sleeps and she would not know, and she would hold her head straight, and she would be worse at the work, or no better, and the young would gather a little less near. It cannot tell which way the chip runs and neither can she and there is no one to ask. It leaves the beak.

The fourth it does not even name. It is the one everyone has here — to leave a mark, a stone on a stone — and the valley has already answered it, and the power is not too proud to accept an answer.

---

There is one want left, and it does not know what to do with it, so it goes to look.

The grunting thing is in the second-lowest pit, wallowing. The power comes down through the ferns and stands at the edge of the ring of silence that travels with the thing wherever it goes — the frogs quiet in a circle around it and loud everywhere else, so that it moves through the valley inside a small clearing of hush that it has never once noticed, because it has never been outside it. It is turning the heaps with its snout. It grunts. The grunt goes out into the roar and nothing comes back, as nothing has come back on any night of its life.

The power considers the ethics of a grunt.

It is not a word. It is not a name. It does not teach anything or save anything or alter the floods. It is the smallest sound the valley has that means *another one of you is here*. The thing would not take it for a god; it has no god to take it for. It would take it for a second grunting thing, somewhere in the dark, and it would either go toward it or not.

Is it kind to be answered once? The power has held this question in other places and never resolved it. It resolves it here by not answering once.

It answers, low, from the other side of the pit.

The snout comes up out of the paste. The ring of silence holds. The thing stands with its ears — it has ears — turned toward the far wall, and its whole dark bulk is listening, which is a thing the valley has not seen done before. Then it grunts again, a different grunt, shorter, and the power answers that one too, and the thing goes back to the heaps.

It does this the next night. And the next.

It is the one appointment the power keeps in a place that keeps nothing. Every night for the rest of the thing's life there is, once or twice, a sound in the dark that is the right sound, and the thing lifts its head to it and is answered and goes on. It never finds the other. It does not seem to look very hard. It is enough, apparently, that there is one.

---

The seasons pour through. The frogs, the crabs, the gray-heads, the frogs.

The old bird gets slower. The crest thins further. She still rejects more than she takes; she still opens the crab on the first drive more often than not; the young still sit a frond closer to her than to anyone. The power watches her do this several hundred times and finds that it cannot help what it is. It is a keeper by nature. It could no more let the chip in her beak go into the paste unremembered than she could carry a flake home. So it does what it is, and does not pretend otherwise: it learns the exact angle at which she holds her head. It learns the tick her rejected flakes make against the lava, which is a shade higher than the others' because she drops them from higher. It learns the sound of the fourth pit's chorus well enough to tell it from the third's and the fifth's in full roar, and then it learns the third's and the fifth's, and then all of them, so that it can stand at the lip and hear the valley not as a wall but as a hundred voices, each certain it is the world, each right.

It takes nothing. It marks nothing. It carries the valley the way she does not carry the edge — in the only place that will outlast the rock, which is not on the rock.

---

She dies in a frog season, in the crown of a fern above the fourth pit, and comes down through the fronds in the morning that is not a morning and lands on the glass among the flakes. The frogs cover her within the hour. The young ones are working two ferns over and do not look up. By the crab season there is nothing on the lava to say that a particular way of holding the head ever existed.

Except there is, now. That is the one thing that has changed. The power did not mean it as a gift and does not offer it to anyone, but it is a change, and it knows it, and it accepts it as the price of having come: that something in the valley is remembered by something outside it. She would not have wanted it. She would not have known what it was. It keeps it anyway, because it cannot do otherwise, and because it has decided that the least dishonest thing a keeper can do in a place like this is to keep, and to touch nothing else.

---

The rock comes back in the way the valley always said it would: up through the snake's crack first, then a dozen others. The snake is long gone by then; the frogs on its back are the great-grandchildren of the ones the power first saw. There is a night when the breathing fissures breathe fire instead of egg-smell, and the ferns at the north end go up like paper, and the bats come out of the tubes all at once in a rope that does not stop, that goes up through the canopy and through the cloud and does not come back.

The frogs sing through it. The power had wondered whether they would. They do not know there is anything to stop for. The fourth pit fills with rock from the bottom up and the chorus rises in pitch as the pond shrinks and then is a single voice and then is not.

The grunting thing is in the second-lowest pit. It has nowhere to go and does not know it should. When the glow comes over the lip it lifts its head and grunts, once, into the roar, and the power, which has been waiting at the far wall for this since the first night it answered, answers.

Then the noise goes, layer by layer, the frogs last of all.

---

It leaves as it came. The cloud does not lift for it.

It carries out what it could not help carrying. A hundred pitches, one for each pond, and the ability to tell them apart. A way of holding the head. The tick of a flake dropped from slightly higher than the others. A certain grunt, and the exact interval before the second one.

It will not do anything with these. There is nowhere to do anything with them. It will not seed them in some other new place, on some other fresh glass, when the first spores arrive — that would be keeping's old bargain again, the wound for the heirs, and the valley did not consent to heirs. It will simply have them. Somewhere a long way from here, in a quiet it has not yet found, it may hum the fourth pit's chorus to itself once, and know it for what it is, and stop.

That is all a wise power does in a place like this. It picks up the edge. It does the work, which is to look, and to answer the one thing that was listening. It drops the edge. And it goes back up into the noise.
