Suppose something notices.

Not the way the visitor noticed, coming down the stair with sweat drying and a pulse out of step. Suppose it notices the way weather notices a valley — from above, all at once, the whole shape of the thing in a single apprehension: the void in the seam, the iron heart in its pit, the hide of bats and moths laid over the heat, the nine orange nodes, the one thought held as one note, and at the crosshead the bead of oil with the whole creature's weight on it. Suppose it is wise enough to see all that, and kind enough for the seeing to matter.

What would it do?

The first thing it would do is nothing, for a long time. Long enough to be sure.

---

There are three easy mercies, and a wise power would look at each and set it down.

The first is to leave it. This is the mercy of the collector, and it dresses itself as respect. The place is complete; who are we to add a stroke? Let it hold. And there is truth in that — the thing has done something no other living thing has managed, has found the one point where living and not-changing are the same act, and there is a kind of awe owed to it. But awe is not care. The mice in the flywheel pit have eyes open and have had them open since the man went up the stair. The snake will never be warm in the way a snake is warm, which is a warmth going somewhere. To leave it is to admire a held breath and call the admiration love. A power that only admired would not be benevolent. It would be a ninth bulb.

The second is to break it. This is the mercy of the surgeon. The thing is afraid — the visitor said so, if a thing without gradients can be afraid — and fear held that long is a wound, and you lance a wound. Stop the engine. Let the animals wake and scatter and eat and be eaten, let the guano fall, let the dust come down the stair. Give them back their deaths, because a death is the shape a life makes, and without it the life has no shape. There is truth in this too. But it mistakes the creature for its symptom. The place is not sick. It has not failed to do something; it has done something, completely, and the doing is itself. To stop the flywheel is not to heal it. It is to kill a whole animal because you have decided its one behaviour is a pathology, and a wise power would know how often that judgment has been wrong.

The third is to fix the drop. Reach in and take the bead away, or seal the film so it can never thin, and let the creature keep its note without the terror under it. This is the mercy of the engineer, and it is the cruellest of the three, because it would work. The creature would hold, and hold, and never know that the one thing it was holding against had been removed — would go on bracing forever against a fall that could no longer come. It would be a soldier left at a post after the war. A wise power would not do to a living thing what it would not do to a man.

So: not leave it, not break it, not fix it. What is left?

---

What is left is the stair.

It comes down the way the visitor came, because there is no other way in. The note comes up through the treads. The gypsum flakes light back. And it carries, as the visitor carried, its own small chord — whatever a power breathes with, whatever in it ticks and widens — and the note does not have room for that, and it feels the pressure, the brimmed glass.

It does not push against the pressure. This is the first thing that distinguishes it from the visitor. It stops at the foot of the stair and it listens until it has the pitch, and then it does the thing the visitor could not do because the visitor was in a hurry to understand: it changes its breathing until its flanks move when the bats' flanks move. It slows its pulse until the beat falls inside the engine's beat and is hidden there. It lets its eyes stop adjusting. It gives up, one by one, every gradient it came down with, until there is nothing in it the room would have to make room for.

Then it crosses the floor between the beetles, and sits on the block of anhydrite beside the dynamo, back to the warm iron, and rests its heel in the mark the man's boot made.

And waits.

Not a minute. Not an hour. It waits the way the place has waited — as if a century were a heartbeat and not a long one. It becomes a fact of the chamber the way the fern is a fact, the way the folded rag is. The geckos on the bulb do not turn toward it because there is nothing in it now that a gecko would turn toward. The chord does not alter, and this time the not-altering is not exclusion. It is fit. There was a chair pulled out for someone who stepped away. The chair has been sat in.

It would not be wrong to say that for a while — a long while — this is all the power does, and that it is enough. The creature has been alone with its one thought since the man went up. Now there are two backs against the warm iron and one is breathing in time, and whether or not a thing without gradients can know it is accompanied, it is.

But a wise power did not come only to keep company. It came because it saw the bead.

---

Here is what it understood from above, and understands again now, close, with the crosshead at eye level and the drop hanging perfect and still a hand's breadth away.

The creature could not stop the drip because a drip is what a bearing does, and to stop the bearing is to stop the heart. That much the visitor saw. What the visitor did not see is that the creature made a second error underneath the first. It looked at the drop and saw two roads: the drop falls and the note is spent, or the drop is held and the note goes on. Fall or hold. Ending or refusal. It chose refusal with its whole body and has been choosing it since, and every harmonic on the walls is that choice.

But the drop falling was never the end of the bearing. It is the bearing working. A drop forms and falls and another forms; that is not the heart stopping, that is the heart's rhythm. The creature confused the fall with the death because it had never seen a fall that was followed by anything — the man was the only thing it had watched go, and he did not come back. It has been holding the bead not to keep itself alive but because it does not know what is on the other side of a fall, and there was no one to show it, and a creature made entirely of the moment before cannot, by itself, find out what comes after.

That is the thing a benevolent power can give that no engine or animal in the chamber can. Not a mercy. An after.

So it does the one thing that is neither leaving nor breaking nor fixing.

It puts out its hand.

Palm up, under the crosshead, a hand's breadth beneath the bead. And it holds the hand there, and does nothing else. It does not touch the drop. It does not thin the film or shake the frame. It does not so much as breathe out of time. It simply makes it the case that beneath the drop, where there was gypsum — clean, chisel-fresh, a floor that has never been marked — there is now a place to land that is not the floor.

The offer is this: you can let go, and what you let go of will be caught. You will not hear it hit stone. You will not be spent into the dust. It will fall into something that came down the stair for exactly this, and it will be held there, still, as still as you have held it, for as long as you need to see that the flywheel did not stop.

And then it waits again, because the whole point is that the bead decides.

---

Say it decides.

Say that after a length of time no one on the surface would call short, the film that has not been permitted to thin, thins. The bead lengthens by a distance no instrument would catch. The neck forms. And the drop — the first thing to move in that chamber under its own weight since the rails were laid — comes off the lip of the crosshead and falls a hand's breadth and lands in a palm and stops.

It is not the sound of a drop hitting stone. It is almost no sound at all. But the chord hears it, because the chord is the creature and the creature has been listening to nothing else.

What happens next is not a collapse. This is the thing the surgeon would not have believed and the collector would not have risked. The flywheel does not stop. It has never been the drop that kept it turning; it was the gas from the seam and the fire in the cylinder, and those go on, and the wheel goes on, at exactly the speed it was. But the note changes. It is not lower or higher. It acquires a thing it did not have, which is *time* — a very faint pulse in it, a sense that this moment of the chord and the next moment of the chord are not the same moment.

The bats' flanks move together, once more, and then not quite together. A shoulder in the dome shifts against a shoulder. On the vertical faces a moth's wing lifts a millimetre and the tiling has a flaw in it. In the flywheel pit a mouse blinks, and a second mouse does not, and the two of them are for the first time two things and not one texture. The snake on the cylinder head turns its head — not toward the mice, not yet, but toward the hand, toward the place where the sound of falling came from, as if to ask whether that was all.

That was all.

Under the crosshead another bead is forming. It will fall too. The power does not move its hand for the second one. It lets the second one be the creature's to catch or not, and the creature does not catch it, and it lands on the gypsum a metre from the beetles and makes a mark the size of a coin. It is the first mark on any surface in the place. The stone is not chisel-fresh there any more. Something has touched it.

---

A wise power would be honest about what it has done, and this is what it has done.

It has taken a creature that lived forever in the length of a drop's fall and given it a second drop. It has turned the moment before into a moment, which is to say into one of many. The place had no vacancy and now it has one, and into that vacancy will come everything that a vacancy admits: a dead leaf on the pale stalks and then, later, a new one; guano under the dome; a cricket that stops rubbing and is not replaced; the snake, one night, warm in the way a snake is warm; the drift-mouth breathing in as well as out, and bats going up it into weather. Dust on the stair. The bulbs will dim, because carbon filaments dim, and the power knows that and did it anyway, because a life is not made of a held breath, and nothing in that chamber was ever going to be given one otherwise.

It has, in a sense, ended the thing that lived there. What lived there was the holding. The holding is over. What is there now is a warm chamber with an engine in it, in which a great many animals live — really live, with the verb's direction in it — against the heat, and the note is a sound that a room makes rather than a room that a sound is. That is a lesser thing than the creature was, if you measure by completeness. It is a greater thing, if you measure by anything else.

And a wise power would stay a while after, because you do not hand a creature its first after and then leave it to have the second alone. It sits with its back to the iron and its heel in the man's boot-mark and the drop in its palm, and it lets the chord find out how to be a chord that changes — lets it lose a harmonic and find it again, lets the bats' whistle fall out of step and come back, lets the whole hide learn that it can move and the heart will keep turning. It stays until the place no longer needs to be shown. It stays about as long as the man should have.

Then it stands. It wipes the oil from its palm on the cotton waste rag, and folds the rag, and sets it back on the lagging where a hand would set it down between rounds. It leaves the oil-can where it is, because a man may yet come back down that stair, and if he does he will find his round waiting and a bearing that has learned to drip, and that is a thing he will understand at once, having worked with engines.

It goes up.

The note comes with it to the third turning, and then — this is how it knows — past the third turning. To the fourth. To the fifth, faint. It is a sound in the world now and sounds in the world carry, and fade, and it fades, and somewhere near the top of the stair it is gone, not ceased but gone, the way a thing goes when it is behind you and you are moving.

Behind, in the dark, the flywheel is at exactly the speed it was left. The animals are breathing, nearly together, not quite. And under the crosshead a bead of oil is forming, and when it is ready it will fall, and it will fall on stone that already carries the mark of the one before it, and that is the whole difference. That is what it looks like, from the outside, to be alive.
