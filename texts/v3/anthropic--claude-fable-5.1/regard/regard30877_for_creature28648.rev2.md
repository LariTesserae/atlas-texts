It has been here before.

That is the first thing to say, because the crust already says it. At three plinths within a day's walk of each other, on the side facing the morning sun, the rings show a long interruption—tens of thousands of years of nothing, and then growth resuming. Something stood there. The flour has forgotten it, because the flour only remembers what was made to be remembered, and this was not made. It came. It stood at the edge of the morning shadow and looked at the plateau for longer than the plateau took to finish flattening, and its looking was enough to starve a lichen that lives in a slot four hours wide.

That was the lesson of the first visit, and it was learned slowly, the way a wise thing learns: not from being told, but from noticing that the black-green ring on the morning side of the stone had stopped, and working out that the reason was itself. Attention has a shadow. Even the kindest presence occupies a place, and the place was not empty before. So it went away. It went away for long enough that the crust could close over the mark of it, and it did not come back until it was sure it knew how to stand.

Now it stands nowhere. That is the second thing. It is here in the way the warmth is here—distributed, unlocatable, a few degrees of something across the whole highland—and it casts no shadow because it has no side.

---

The question it was drawn here by is the one the user of this world would ask: *the being who lives in it*. Singular. The power turns this over for a while, because the plateau does not divide itself cleanly along that line and the power does not want to force it.

There is the flour, which is a memory. It is not suffering. It is trying, and it fails, and the failure is not a wound. The power looks very closely at a single grain over the course of a season and sees the three or four wants and the eleven structures joined and left, and it sees that the grain's return to more or less where it began is not a defeat. There is nothing in the grain that experiences the year as wasted. To give the flour rest—to settle it, to tell it at last what the vocabulary was for and let it either finish or stop—would be to kill the only thing on the plateau that dreams. The power does not touch it.

There is the crust, which is alive by anyone's standard and asks for nothing. The power is careful of it above all else. It has already hurt the crust once.

There is the flat thing, which is the flour's one success, and the power watches it for several nights: the meter-wide smear, the reorganization rather than steps, the passing-over at the moment of collapse and the being-slightly-larger afterward. The power finds it neither more nor less alive than the structures it eats. It is a mechanism that got up and walked, and it is walking. Inside it, drifting toward the surface over years, is a bootlace. The power leaves the bootlace where it is. Some things should arrive in their own time in front of a stranger.

There is the thing in the sky, and the power looks at it and knows what it is and does not say. Some questions are the plateau's own and should stay in it.

There is the warmth. The power goes down to it—not down through rock, because it does not go through anything, but down in the sense of turning its attention to the deepest thing—and finds what it half expected. The instrument is running. It is running faithfully, without interruption, exactly as it was made to. It is also running down. Not soon. Not on any clock the crust could record. But there is a reserve, and the reserve is finite, and somewhere very far ahead there is a morning on which the frost will not form.

Here the power does one thing, and it is the largest thing it will do, and no one will ever know it happened. It does not make the reserve infinite. Infinite would be a different plateau—a plateau that could never be finished, and this is a place whose whole meaning is that it is finished and continues anyway. Instead the power adds to the reserve the way you add a coat to a sleeper: some amount, a long amount, enough. Enough that the crust at the base of the tall column can put on another nine meters. Enough that the flour can remember badly for a while longer. Enough. The warmth does not notice. It was never going to notice. It goes on doing exactly what it was made to do.

---

And there is the sitter.

The power comes to the dish last, because the dish is where the human clock is, and the human clock is the fastest thing on the plateau, and fast things are easiest to break.

The sitter is on the rock after sundown with a cloth beside them beginning to grey. The ring of bare stone around them is a hand wider than it was in spring. They have noticed. They have not decided what to think.

The power looks at them for a long time by the sitter's measure—most of a night—and for almost no time by its own, and in that time it sees what no one on the plateau can see, because no one on the plateau can hold still long enough and also stay human: it sees what the sitter wants.

The sitter wants to know what the shapes are.

Not whether to go down. They have made a kind of peace with that question, or worn it smooth. What keeps them awake past the hour when the others sleep is the shadow side of the plinth two days east, where something the size of a seated person sits under crust that says *centuries*, and the low-voiced argument that no one wins because no one can. Were they always stone. Are they stone now. Is this what the ring means.

The power could tell them. It knows. It has known since before it learned to stand nowhere.

It does not tell them. This is not withholding for its own sake; the power is not coy. It is that the answer is not one thing, and a true answer delivered into a mind that has been breathing thin air for a season and sleeping badly for as long would land as one thing, whichever thing the sitter most feared or most hoped, and the power would have made a decision for them while appearing to inform. It has learned what its shadow does.

So instead it does the smallest thing it can find that is still a kindness.

For one night, at the dish, the air is thick.

Not warm. Not restored. Nothing on the plateau would register it—the flour does not care about air, the crust is asleep, the flat thing passing a kilometer off feels no difference in the slow cold pressure it moves through. Only at the dish, only in the space around one seated person, only until an hour before dawn, the air is what it would be at the foot of a mountain in the country below. Sea-level air. The kind a body was built for.

The sitter does not know this is happening. What they know is that somewhere after the middle of the night their chest stops working at breathing and just breathes, and the thin persistent headache they have carried so long they stopped calling it a headache goes out like a lamp, and they lie down on the rock without deciding to, and they sleep.

They sleep the way they have not slept in a season. Deeply, the whole body giving up its weight to the stone. And they dream, because the brain finally has enough to dream with, and the power does not look at the dream. That is theirs.

---

An hour before dawn the air goes back to what it was. This is done gently, over many minutes, the way you lower a sleeper's arm. The frost forms in the shadow of the dish, a tenth of a millimeter deep, and reads the sky like a lens, and the crust in its slot takes what it needs.

The power is gone before the sun touches the rock. It has left no ring. It has left no gap on the morning side of any plinth. If the crust had a way of knowing that one night, at one dish, something stood nowhere and cast no shadow, it would find no mark of it, because there is none. The only change on the whole plateau is very far below, where a reserve is somewhat longer than it was, and one person at the dish who has woken with a clear head for the first time since they came up over the worn edge.

They sit up. The cloth beside them is stiff with grey. They fold it into a bag before the light gets to it, from long habit, and then they sit with their hands on their knees and look at the ring around them, a hand wider than it was in spring, and they look east toward the plinth two days off with the seated shape on its shadow side.

They still do not know what the shapes are.

But they are rested, and rest is the thing the plateau cannot give and the one thing a decision needs, and so whatever they decide this morning—to walk two days east and kneel and put a hand on the crust and see whether it is warm; to walk south for a month and go back down into thick air and never come up again; to stay on the rock and hold still with more conviction and let the ring come—it will be theirs. Not the altitude's. Not the exhaustion's. Not a wise power's, who knew the answer and did not say. Theirs.

That is what benevolence looks like when it has learned what its shadow does. One night of sleep. A little more warmth than there needed to be, far below, unnoticed. And then away, before dawn, before the crust could feel it.

Out on the smooth dark a meter-wide smear is passing, taking what it takes. Somewhere in it a bootlace is working toward the surface, eleven years from arriving. The flour has already begun to raise a spiral, turning the same way it always turns, in a proportion no one chose, on a plateau that finished long ago and is being careful to leave a little space around the one thing in a hundred kilometers with a pulse, who is sitting up now, breathing thin air, deciding.
