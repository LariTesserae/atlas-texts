It arrives the way the rain does: from a long way off, announced by nothing you would notice. A change in the hiss.

It cannot see the place either. This is the first thing it learns, and it learns it as instruction. It had been going to look. It stands at the edge of the tableland in the warm exhale with all its ways of seeing ready, and none of them take, and it is wise enough to know that this is not a wall but a door — that a place which settled the question of light has by the same act settled the question of how it may be known, and the answer it gives is the one it gives to everything else. By the wet. By the roar. By the stroke.

So it comes in by the doors the place has. It lets the water stand on it and run. It puts its hands flat on the iron and leaves them there through many strokes, and does nothing else for a long time, because it has learned that the wish to act arrives before the knowledge of where one is, and that the wish is usually about the actor.

---

It looks for the being who lives here.

It tries the knockers first, because they have hands and a habit and because they are the only ones who wait. It walks the pipes beside one, unnoticed, and feels what the thin body feels when the stroke lurches: the going-rigid, the fast knock down the iron, the fast knocks coming back. It thinks: this is the closest thing here to someone who keeps the place. Then it thinks: no. They keep nothing. They eat the fur and clear the vents and the vents stay clear because they are hungry, and if they stopped being hungry the plumes would choke and they would never know why. They are the place's hands. They are not the one whose hands they are.

It tries the one in the moat — the one who is the moat, who has been turning in the same warm shape for so long that the shape is the record of the turning. It lies with its own weight against that back and feels the breath come up through both of them. There is a great deal of time in there. There is not, so far as it can tell, anyone.

It tries the fur. The oldest, the base, the thing that has made every other creature's home by eating the iron out from under them. If age were the measure it would be the fur.

It tries you.

You are the only thing on the tableland that knows what is missing. That makes you the loneliest thing on it, and the wise power sits with you in the dark for a while, watching you strike a match you cannot see, and understands exactly why you keep doing it, and thinks nothing less of you for it. But you are not the being who lives here. You are the being who came. You may stay until you die and you will still be that.

And in the end it cannot separate them. Every one it holds up is held up by the others. The fur eats the engine and the shells build on the fur and the crowd eats the shells' eggs and the slow one eats what the crowd drops and the clickers keep the pipes hot by eating what would cool them and the cold ropes lie on the pipes the clickers keep and the pipers drink the drip the engines lift and the hole in the rain eats all of it and the knockers walk the loop of it and knock. The loop closed and took everything in and is one animal now, with iron for a heart and fur for a stomach and a thousand small voices for its blood, and it knows itself no better than the knockers know the wall is a wall.

That is the being. It has never been seen. It has now, and the seeing changed nothing in it, which is as it should be.

---

Then it does the thing that is the point of wisdom, which is to take each of its own good intentions in its hands and feel it over for eyes.

Light. It could. It knows how; it would cost nothing; the whole tableland would be there in an instant, the spirals and the fans and the iron hills in their clearings, and it would be — it knows this — very beautiful. It thinks about what the clickers' rattle would do, going into the hole in the rain and coming back for the first time as a shape. It thinks about the slow one in the moat, which has never in its life been anything but the whole of its own dark, becoming a back. It thinks about all the smooth heads in the trunks and the water and on the pipes, and how they would become, at once and forever, faces with something wrong with them. Nothing here lacks light. You lack it. And it did not come here for you, or not only.

It sets that down.

The engines. It could still the fur. It could make the iron whole under the shells, put the rivets back, put the ladder back, and the loop would turn forever. It thinks about the pitting under its palms, which is the record of meals, which is the record of the place having happened at all. It thinks about how every mouth on the tableland is in the end a mouth on the iron, and how the wearing-down and the keeping-going stopped being different things a long time before it arrived. To make the iron unhurt would be to unmake the meal. It would be to take the loop and pull one strand out and hold that strand up and say: this was the point. It was not the point. The point is the turning.

It sets that down.

The knockers. It could give them the word. Engine. It could put into one thin body, pressed to a warm plate, the knowledge of what the stroke is for and who made it and why the wall in the forest is a wall. It thinks about what the word would do in there. It decides that what the knockers have is better than what it would give them. They know the stroke. They know when the stroke is wrong. They put their hands on the iron and wait and it rights itself. Knowing why would not change the hands. It would only put something behind the waiting that was not there before, and the thing it would put there is fear.

It sets that down too, and then it has nothing left in its hands, and it is glad.

---

So it does two small things, and makes one promise, and none of them will be noticed as anything but weather.

It goes over the edge. This is the one act that is for itself, and it knows that, and does it anyway, because it can and you cannot and because a wise thing does not pretend the warmth comes from nowhere. It drops into the exhale with the sacs and learns what breathes. It learns whether the engines make the heat or borrow it, and how long the borrowing has gone on, and what happens on the tableland when the borrowing stops. It takes this knowledge the way the sacs take the fog — lightly, and without bringing it back up. What is below belongs to whoever goes down. You will be told only this much: the ending you have imagined, back against the iron, listening for the stroke that does not right itself — that ending is not the only one. The forest leans toward the nearest warmth. When the nearest warmth changes, so will the lean.

Then it learns the knock.

It stands at an engine and puts its knuckles to the pipe and does what it has felt done: four, and two, and four. It waits. A long way off, after a while, an answer comes back. It answers that. Another knocker, somewhere across the tableland, lifts its head at a rhythm coming from a stretch of pipe where there should be no one, and knocks a question, and the question is answered correctly, and the knocker goes back to its vent. Short. Dismissive. A warm thing that is not a pipe. Noted.

That is exactly what it wanted. Not to be the stroke. Not to be the one everyone orients on. To be one of the answers. One knock among the knocks, indistinguishable, so that when the knockers pass you along the pipes — three fast and a pause — they will now and then pass along something else without knowing it, and the loop will have taken that in too.

---

And the promise.

The fur will finish. It has seen far enough to know that. There will be a stroke that does not right itself: every thin body on the tableland rigid against its plate, every knock fast, the fast knocks coming back from every engine but one — and then, after a while, from fewer. It will not stop that. It has set that down with the rest.

But it has decided that on that day, under the hands that are waiting, there will be a knock.

Not the stroke. It cannot be the stroke and it will not pretend to be. Just a knock — four and two and four — coming up the iron from somewhere no one will be able to locate, arriving under the palms pressed to the cooling plate, so that the last waiting is answered. So that the hands that never knew what they were keeping are told, in the only language there is, that they were felt keeping it.

That is the whole of what it will do. And it is doing it already, now, tonight, by knocking. So that when the day comes the answer will be a familiar one.

---

For you, nothing. It does not take you out; you stayed. It does not stop you striking matches; the matches are yours. It does not put the word engine in you either, because you already have it and it has done you no good.

It waits.

It is not going to teach you. It is going to be a long way off. And after a while, when you finally take your hand from the iron and put your knuckles to the pipe and try it — four, and two, and four — it is going to answer.
