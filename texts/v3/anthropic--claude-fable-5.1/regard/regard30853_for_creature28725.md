It arrives the way the light does, from above, and for a while it does what the light does: it runs along the trowel-crescents, it rides the bubbles up the north wall and lets them go, it lays itself over the grey face of the float and reads the pattern of the last wall that float smoothed. It is not in a hurry. It has been asked to look at a thing that lives here, and it is wise enough to look at everything else first, because the thing that lives in a place is never separable from the place, and this place was made.

It sees the making. Not just the result — the sill cut sharp, the level held to a thumb's width — but the making itself, folded into the objects the way a tree's years are folded into its wood. The mallet with the fired-clay head was swung a few thousand times. The cord was knotted by someone who measured against their own thumb-joint and trusted it. The plaster was floated by someone who went over the west wall once more than the others, because the light from the grid fell on it hardest and they wanted it to be the wall people looked at. That is where the seam is. The wall someone cared about most is the wall with the flaw, and the power notices this without irony. It is simply how care works: it goes where the eye goes, and the eye is not everywhere.

Then it goes to the seam.

It puts itself close, closer than a face could go, down to where the threads are not threads but chains of cells, each one a bead of water and salt and a little machinery for turning light into more of itself. It sees the mucus at the north end and understands what it is for. It sees the questing tips at the south end lie down when the inflow pulses and lift when it steadies, and it knows better than anyone alive that this is not feeling, and it also knows that "only water pushing" is not a small thing to say about anything. Water pushing is most of what happens.

And it asks the question it came to ask: what does this need?

The honest answer arrives quickly, and the power does not flinch from it. The green needs nothing. It has warmth from a fire it does not know about, light from a sun it cannot see, lime to hold and lime to eat, and a seam that shelters it from the one force in the room that could scour it off. It has, in fact, the thing almost nothing alive ever has: a place built to remain exactly as it is, to the thumb's width, indefinitely. It has no fear to be soothed because it has no inside to be afraid in. It has no want that it is not already, at every moment, in the act of satisfying. If benevolence means giving to a being what that being lacks, then benevolence toward the green is an empty gesture, a hand held out to a hand that is not there.

The power sits with this. It has been benevolent toward many things and it knows the particular temptation of this moment, which is to invent a need in order to have something to meet. It could widen the seam. It could soften the current on the west wall. It could reach forward in time to the day someone comes down with a brush and a bucket of fresh lime and stay their hand. Each of these would be a gift to no one. The green would not be more itself with a wider seam; it would just be more. And the person with the brush wants a clean wall and has every right to one, and the wall is theirs, and the green did not ask to be spared and cannot ask, and to spare it against a person's wish would be to take sides in a dispute that has only one party.

So it will not touch the seam.

It looks, instead, at time, because a wise power always ends up looking at time. It sees the green climb out of the seam in a week and band the wall in a month and fur it in a season. It sees the crust on the sill lift the water line a grain at a time. It sees, further on, two roads. Down one, someone drains the tank and scrubs it and fills the seam and the green becomes a dark stain that dries and flakes and is gone, and the bath is a bath again for a long time. Down the other, the fire upstream goes out one winter and does not get lit again, and the wheel slows over an hour and lies flat, and the green has the whole of the standing water and blooms until it chokes on itself, and then the water finds a way out through the plaster as water always does, and the tank stands empty under its grid of sky, and rain falls in and dries and falls in, and other things arrive. Both roads end in the same place, which is a place where nothing here is what it is now. The power does not find this sad. It finds it accurate. The green will have been exactly what it was for exactly as long as the conditions held, and that is the only kind of life there is, and the green's version of it is not lesser for being brief or mindless. It is just smaller, and closer to the ground, where most of the living is done.

Having found nothing to do for the green, the power does what wisdom does when it finds no need in front of it: it follows the dependencies back until it finds one.

The green depends on the fire. The fire depends on a person. Somewhere upstream, in a channel this place was not meant to see, there is someone feeding a hearth under a cistern — has been feeding it all day, will feed it again tomorrow — and that person has an inside. That person has a back that aches and a face that is too hot on one side and thoughts that go nowhere in particular through the long middle of the afternoon. They have never seen the tank. They know it only as the thing the water goes to. They keep a fire burning so a wheel can turn in a box so a body can hang in it for the length of a held breath so a line of threads can lengthen in a seam, and they do not know the last part and would not care if they did.

The power could give them something. It could take the ache out of the back. It could make the fire draw better. It has done such things.

Instead it does this: at the end of the day, when the fire-tender banks the hearth and walks up past the house on their way home, it turns their head. Just that. They look through a doorway they have looked through a hundred times and see the grid of beams and the water pressed up against it from below, standing exactly where it should, the last of the light still leaning into it in long squares. And they stop, for no reason they could name, and look down through the beams, and see the wheel turning, and the tools in the corner, and low on the west wall, in the raking light, a faint green line.

They will not know what it is. They will think, perhaps, that the plasterer missed a spot, or that the water is doing something water does. They will feel something that is not quite pride and not quite curiosity — the small warm shock of seeing that the thing you keep alive from a distance is in fact alive, and full, and moving, and has already begun, without permission, to become a place instead of a thing made. Then they will go home. In a season, or a year, they or someone like them will come down with a brush.

That is the whole of what the power does. It does nothing to the room and nothing to the green and nothing to the water or the light or the roar. It takes the attention it was given and hands it, once, to someone who can hold it, and lets go, and lets everything here go on doing the one thing it does.

Then it drops through the hole in the floor, the way you did, and lets the wheel take it round. Once. The length of a held breath. And when it comes back to the west wall the line is a little longer than when it arrived, and it looks at that, and leaves.
