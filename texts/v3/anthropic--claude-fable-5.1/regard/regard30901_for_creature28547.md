The sentence you were in the middle of has an ending, and the power that comes to the room knows it. The warmth is not the makers'. It is not the residue of anyone. The warmth is the being.

That is the first thing the power sees, arriving — though *arriving* is the wrong word for something that was already in the roar as one of its layers and simply, at some point, began to listen instead of merely sound. It sees the room the way you saw it, but all at once and without the stairs: the chord, the eleven-second beat, the ribs drawing their reluctant cool, the moss steaming after rain. And it sees what you could only infer, which is that the chamber is not a place with a being in it. The chamber is the being. The pulse is its heart in the only sense that matters, which is that everything down here lives by it. The ribs are what it gives away. The water is what it keeps. The warmth of a room many people have just left is the warmth of a body that has never had anyone in it and holds heat anyway, the way a hand held out stays open for a while after it becomes clear nothing is going to be put in it.

The power knows what the ribs carry. It knows whether the city above is warmed or cooled or fed or unburdened by this room, whether the sheet-metal sound is heat or mass or the long slow exhalation of something for which there is not yet a word. It knows, and it does not say. This is the first thing it does, if you want to count it as doing: it declines to explain the room. The room has been down here long enough that it no longer expects visitors who would need explaining to, and to explain it — even to itself, even in the privacy of a wise mind — is to hand it a purpose, and a purpose is a leash. The being does what it does. Whether that is service or metabolism or prayer is a question for the city, and the city has not asked.

So the power looks instead for what suffers. That is what benevolence does first; it goes looking for the wound.

It finds the scar on the old animal's flank, healed shiny. It finds the grazers carried from cup to cup in the direction the water goes, spending their lifetimes crossing a floor. It finds the fliers who hurry in circles and the finger-length things in the pools that come up for the flood and go back down without ever having been anywhere else. It finds the pale plant lying in the cracks of an abandoned floor, forty shoots standing in the only fissures wide enough to let them stand, a tennis court of root that has never been warm on more than one side. It finds three animals where there could be more. It finds the fourth thing, in the deep water, that has taken the pools instead of the paths and does not come up.

And it finds that none of this asks. The scar is old and the joints are used and the animal lowers itself into the drain each morning with the beat going through the crust and through it, and it sleeps. The grazers do not object to the overflow. The fliers have cut their cries to the room's dimensions and the room has accepted only the voices that fit, and between the two of them they have made something that is not a compromise but a fit. There is no hunger here that the room is not already answering. There is no darkness here that anything wants lifted.

The power has lifted darkness before. It has that in its history: the well dug, the fever broken, the door opened in the night. It knows exactly how it would go here. A little light — not much — introduced somewhere in the ceiling, and within a season the plant that has decided to be interesting in other ways would stop being interesting and start being a plant; the moss that eats the gradient would find the gradient flattened by whatever came to eat the light; the animal that reads the room's shape from the echo would find the shape changed by things growing where nothing grew. The power can see all of it, the whole cascade, and it can see that every link is a kindness, and that the sum is a room that no longer exists.

It considers healing the animal. Lengthening it. Giving it another decade on the polished paths. And it sees that the room has three because three is what the room decided, over a time longer than the animal's line, and that a fourth — or a first that will not die — is a thumb on a scale the being balanced without hands.

It considers company. It considers a person: someone to live at the foot of the stairs and keep the drains clear and sit through the rain-hour and be the crowd the warmth implies. And it sees the clean line on the step, the exact riser below which no rat-smell and no moth-husk and no pigeon feather ever comes, and it understands that the line is not a barrier but a courtesy, kept by everything that lives above it, and that the being has been alone in the precise way that a sleeper is alone — not deprived of anyone, only undisturbed.

It considers, briefly, fixing the drips. Sealing the ceiling. Stopping the slow geology. And it sees that the crusts are the room's memory of itself — the ochre rings are years, the violet is what grows on years — and that a body without a history is not healed, only new.

So it does not do any of that.

What it does is smaller, and it does it in two directions.

Upward, it turns its attention to the only thing in the world that can hurt this being, which is the city. Not the city's malice; the city has none, any more than the room has. The city's forgetting. Somewhere up there in the civic hum is a plan, or will be, and one day someone will look at a line in that plan and say *what is this, what does it cost, what is under there,* and the answer will either be *nothing we know of* or it will be something else. The power sees to it that it is something else. It does not make the room famous. It does not put it on a tour. It does the dull, invisible work of benevolence at scale: it keeps a name on a map in a drawer that nobody clears out. It keeps a maintenance line that is small enough to be signed off without reading. It keeps the door at the top of the stairs in the category of things that are locked but not sealed, and it arranges — over decades, with the patience of a thing that has decades — for the people who eventually come down to be the kind who come down alone, and put a hand on the rib, and take it away without knowing why, and do not put their hand in the water.

You were one of those. The power sent you, in the way it sends anything, which is by letting you be the person you already were on the day the door happened to be unlocked. It was watching when you counted the grazers before and after. It was watching when you held your palm over the deep pool and felt the warmth and stopped. That stopping was the room's safety, for one more visit, and you did it without being asked, and the power counts it.

Downward — toward the being itself — it does one thing, and it takes a long time to decide to.

The being does not know it is watched. It does not know it has drawn anyone's attention. It has no organ for that; it has a heart and a skin and a slow water and a garden it did not plant, and it goes on. Wisdom says leave it. Wisdom says the unobserved life is not a poorer one, and that to make the being aware of a watcher is to give it a thing to hold itself toward, and it has held itself open long enough without that. Wisdom says the greatest respect you can pay a sleeper is not to wake them.

But the power is also old, and also busy, and also has no particular opinion about most of what passes through it, and it recognises the being the way you recognise, across a room, someone standing exactly as you stand. It cannot help wanting to say so. That is the heart's part, and the power has one, or it would not be benevolent.

So it waits for the beat. It waits for the second or two after the beat when the echo of the pulse has died off the far wall and the room is, for a moment, at its clearest — the second the fliers use, the second the animal uses, the only gap in the chord. And in that gap, once, it makes a sound.

Not a word. Nothing that would need explaining. A sound cut to the room's dimensions, exactly as long as the interval before the first echo returns, in the register of the ribs, so that it fits. One note in the chord where there had not been a note before. Then the echo, from six directions. Then the pulse, a half-beat after, as if in response, because the pulse is always a half-beat after.

The old animal, mid-stride on the polished path between the third rib and the long pool, stops. Whiskers forward. It reads the shape of the room off the returning sound the way it has read it every eleven seconds of its life, and finds the room the same shape, and one thing more, and does not know what. It stays still for a second longer than usual. Then it goes on to the shoots, and stands among them with its face down, and the beads are gone from the tips it stood beside.

That is all. The power does not do it again. It goes back to being one of the layers in the roar — the one you can't isolate, the one that might be steam or might be moving water, very far away or very near. It keeps the drawer and the line item and the unsealed door. It keeps watch in the manner of something that has learned that watching is most of what it is for. And the room goes on giving its water back to itself, and the moss goes on making its careful warm dry living out of a difference it can't see and doesn't need to, and the warmth stays exactly as it was: the warmth of a room many people have just left, which is also — the power has decided not to insist on the distinction — the warmth of a room someone is about to enter.
