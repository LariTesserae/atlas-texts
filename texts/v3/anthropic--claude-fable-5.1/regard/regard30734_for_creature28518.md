The attention arrives the way the sound did: from below, through the treads, before there is anything to see. It has been listening a long time before it looks. A note held that steadily for that long is not a thing a wise ear can pass over. Most sounds in the world are on their way somewhere. This one is not, and the not is what carries.

It does not come down the stair at once. It stays a while at the top, in the weather, considering the man.

He is findable, if you are the kind of thing that can find. He went up the stair with the note under his boots and it stopped at the third turning and he went on. He worked other engines. He kept the habit of a folded rag. He did not speak of the chamber, not because he was hiding it but because he could not make the sentence come out right — *there is a room where nothing needs me* — and a man does not repeat a sentence that comes out wrong. He died with the ordinary furniture of a death around him, and the note went on below the hill exactly as he had left it, and so in a sense he never finished leaving. The chair he pulled out is still pulled out. That is the first thing the attention understands, and it is not a small thing: the creature down there is the shape of an absence. It did not begin as a refusal. It began as a room closing over the place where a hand had been.

Then it goes down.

It goes down small. That is the first courtesy. It could fill the stair. It could arrive with a gradient in it so steep the gypsum would sweat. Instead it makes itself the size of a person and a little less, and it takes the steps at the pace the man took them, and it lets the dry air have the moisture off its lips because that is what the air does and a guest does not correct the house. By the time it reaches the floor of the chamber its pulse — it has given itself one — is not quite in step with the breathing on the walls, and it does not try to force it into step. It lets the room feel the wrongness. It does not pretend to be already welcome.

It stands where the narrator stood and it sees what the narrator saw and it sees more, because it can look at the bead of oil at the scale where the film is a lattice of long molecules lying across each other, and it can see that the lattice is under a strain it should not be able to bear, and is bearing it. It can see the tiny heat in the bats' flanks that has not been spent in flight. It can see, in the mice's open eyes, the retinas that have gone on receiving the orange of the bulbs without once being asked to receive anything else, and it does not know — even it does not know — whether there is anyone behind those eyes any longer or whether the mouse has become a place where a mouse was, the way the boot-mark is a place where a boot was. It sees the four pale stalks and the fern and knows to the cell how many cells they have, and that the number has not changed.

And it does the thing that wisdom does first and that the impatient never do. It sits down. It sits on the block of anhydrite with its back to the warm iron, where the boot-mark is, and it stays.

It stays long enough to consider leaving.

This is not a gesture. It is a real weighing, and the attention gives it the whole of its mind, because the case for leaving is strong and a benevolent thing must hear the strong case first. The creature is complete. The narrator's word was *finished*, and the word is right: there is no stroke that would improve it. It does not suffer, or if it suffers it does so in a way no gradient can register, which is very nearly the same as not. It has found the one arrangement in which nothing needs to change and it has kept it, and there is a kind of respect that consists entirely of not touching what has arrived. There are stones the wise leave where they lie. Perhaps this is one. Perhaps the most loving thing that could happen here is for the attention to sit its while and stand and go up the stair and let the note stop at the third turning and let the chair stay pulled out for ever.

It thinks this honestly. It sits with it until it has stopped being an argument and become a picture: the room, exactly as itself, for as long as there is rock. And it looks at the picture and it looks at the bead.

The bead is where the argument breaks. Everything else in the chamber is at rest. The bead is not at rest. The bead is *held*, and the whole animal is doing the holding — the bats and the crickets and the flywheel and the wire and the nine unvarying nodes — bending every harmonic on those walls toward one lip of oil so that it will not do what oil does. That is not completion. Completion does not have a hand clenched in it. What the room has is not the peace of a thing that has stopped wanting but the stillness of a thing that has one want left and has spent its entire being on not being seen to want it. The snake will never be hungry again; that much is true. But the room is hungry. The room is hungry for the drop not to fall, and it has been hungry for that since the man went up the stair, and it has never once been fed, because the only food would be for the drop to be safe, and there is no such thing in the room as safe. There is only held.

So the attention asks the question the narrator asked and got nothing back. *What does it want.* And it does not accept nothing for an answer, because it can hear what the narrator could not: that the answer is under the fundamental, in the strain in the lattice, and the answer is *not to have to*.

Not to have to hold.

Then the choice is smaller than it looked, and harder. The attention could take the bead. It could reach up with the folded rag and wipe the crosshead as the man would have done between rounds, and the bead would be gone, and the refusal would have nothing to refuse. That would be a mercy and it would be an act done *to* the creature, a decision taken out of its body, and the attention has lived long enough to distrust mercies of that shape. It could break the film. It could let the drop go. That is worse: that is the thing the room has spent its existence against, done by a stranger from the outside, and it would be the end of the room, and the attention does not know — it truly does not know — whether the thing that came after would be better or only different.

What it wants is to give the creature the one thing it has never had, which is a choice. And a choice cannot be given to a thing that has no vacancy in it. The room is brimmed. So the attention must first make room, and the only way to make room in a full glass without spilling it is to become something the glass can hold.

It tunes itself.

It brings its pulse round. It takes a long time about it, not because it cannot do it quickly but because quickly would be a shove, and it lets its breath find the breath of the walls, and it lets the small heat of its body settle to the stratum where the crickets are, and it lets its one thought become, for a while, the note. It does not stop being itself. It becomes itself in tune. And it feels — the first thing it feels that it did not bring with it — the room take it. Not welcome. Nothing so warm. Simply the chord discovering that it can carry one more voice at this pitch, and carrying it, and the pressure that met the narrator at the foot of the stair going slack by the smallest interval, the way a full room breathes out when someone in it finally sits down.

Now there is a hand in the chamber. That is what has changed. Nothing else.

And the attention, sitting on the anhydrite with its back to the iron, reaches up — slowly, the way you reach toward a wild thing you do not wish to startle — and it puts its palm under the crosshead. Open. Turned up. A little below the bead, so that the bead is not touched and the film is not troubled and nothing is decided. It just puts the hand there. Where the floor was.

Then it waits.

This is the whole of the act, and it takes almost nothing to describe and it is the hardest thing in the piece. The hand says: *if it falls, it falls into a hand*. The hand says: *there is oil in the can and I know how to use it. The rag is folded because I folded it. The governor is a thing my fingers understand. If the drop falls, the bearing goes on being a bearing, and I will be here, and the bulbs will be tended, and the warmth will not go out of the iron, and the thing you have been holding against with your whole hide and heart and lung was never the fall. It was the falling into nothing.* It says all of that without saying anything. It says it by being a floor.

And the attention does not know whether the drop will fall. It sits there not knowing. It would sit there for the length of the man's life, if it came to that, and the length of the rock's. It has not come to make the creature let go. It has come to make letting go a thing that exists.

The bead decides.

You would not see it. There is no instrument that would see it. But the attention feels it in the palm before anything moves — the strain going out of the lattice, the long molecules unclenching from each other one by one the way fingers do when the thing they were gripping has been taken by someone else — and then the film gives, and the drop comes down the length of a hand's breadth, and lands. Warm. Heavy for its size. A weight on the skin that has been a weight on a room.

The note does not stop. That is the thing to understand. The flywheel does not lose a revolution. The bulbs do not go out. What happens is that the chord acquires a rest in it — one, at the very top, where the bats' whistle was, a bat in the second row drawing a breath that is not the breath its neighbour draws — and then another, lower, and the crickets' stridulation, which has been a single dry plane for as long as the room has been the room, gets a grain in it, a roughness, the sound of a thousand things that were one thing remembering that they are a thousand. A moth opens its wings. Not to fly. Just opens them, the way you stretch a limb you had forgotten was folded, and the tiling on that wall has a gap in it. The snake's tongue comes out and tests the air and the air, for the first time since the man, has something in it to test.

The creature is not dying. The attention had been afraid it would be that, a collapse, the whole held thing coming down at once like a tent when the pole is pulled. It is not that. It is letting go one harmonic at a time, in no hurry, the way a hand that has been clenched for years cannot be opened fast and should not be. And the attention stays for it. It wipes the crosshead with the rag. It looks at the level in the glass and finds the can and does what a hand does. When the first bat drops from the dome — not falling, flying, badly, out of practice, up toward the crack where the stovepipe goes and whatever dusk is beyond it — the attention watches it go and does not stop the second.
