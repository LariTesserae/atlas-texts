Start with what it would want to do, because wanting is where the danger is.

It would see the bags first. Not the roof, not the floor — the hide bags hanging on their sunken pegs, and how in a bad season they hang, and what that means in a place where the one thing that cannot be built for is water. It would see the pink whites of the eyes and know, as the people do not quite know, that the line is closing a little further each generation and that the animals here have no eyes for a reason. It would see a child sent to the seam for a dripping armful of gray fringe because that is the closest thing to a treat, and it would see the brown curve in the lifted slab, and the crew standing, and the slab set back.

And it would want to fix it. That is what benevolence is, before wisdom gets to it: the pressure to reach in. The roof is thin as a hand in places. A single touch and there is sky. The floor could be stilled. The ceiling could be made to sweat until every pan ran fresh. It would have all of that in its hands, the way a builder has the brine bag in the fork of the thumb, and the pouring hand would curl.

So it would stop, and read the floor first.

---

Sky would kill them. Not quickly, which would be worse. The plain above is the dried bed of a sea; there is nothing on it and the light there has shadows in it that point somewhere, and their eyes could not take it, and their skin could not, and the films would not grow up there, and the herds would not follow. To open the roof is to hand them a world they have no hands for. That much is easy.

Fresh water is harder. A steady drip, clean, enough for everyone — and within a few seasons the bags hang full and no one checks them, and the hide is not the first thing carried out of a failing hemisphere anymore, and there are more children, and the films at the pan-edges are scraped thinner, and something that had found its level has been tipped, gently, by a hand that only wanted to be kind. Not a catastrophe. A loosening. The power would see this the way a reader sees the third day of pegs: the bend has a direction and a rate.

And the floor. Still the floor and there is nothing left. No cutting, no carrying, no reason for the spacing, no readers, no pegs to inherit. The hemispheres would stand and finish and go crusty and the herds would take them. What was a city would be a ruin within a season, for the first time, because for the first time it would have stopped moving. Everything they know, they know because the ground will not stay. Take that away and you have not saved them. You have ended them and left the bodies walking.

So the power would arrive at the thing wisdom always arrives at, which is that most of what it can do it must not, and that the wanting does not go away for having been overruled. It would stand on the salt with its hands curled and pour nothing.

---

But not nothing. Not quite.

It would set itself one rule, and the rule would be theirs before it was its own: *only what the floor might have done anyway.* Nothing that could be read as a hand. Nothing that a reader kneeling with light dry palms would feel as anything but the salt being the salt. If it acts, it acts inside the range of the world they already have, so that no gesture is ever needed for it and no word ever coined. The moment it is seen, it becomes a thing to build toward, and *mine* enters the language, and the roof is no longer left alone.

Within that rule, three things.

---

It would hold the roof.

Not lift it, not thicken it. Simply be the reason the hand's-width places do not fail. They have already told it what it is for, without knowing they were speaking to anyone: the one wall in the house that holds the rest up, not worshipped, not touched, left alone. The power would take that as its instruction. It would be the wall. Every season the gypsum is a little more dissolved from beneath, and every season there is some stretch where the sheet is thinner than it has ever been and the glare there is whiter than white, and the children are told not to strike it, and it holds. It would go on holding. No one would ever know why the roof never came down. That is the point. A thing that holds is not noticed. A thing that is noticed has already begun to fail.

---

It would keep the seam open.

Somewhere along the line where the ceiling meets the floor, far past where the amber goes to brown, there is a place where it opens — or there is not, and the flat hand turned once means only *I have seen both sides of something.* The power would make sure there is one. Not a road. Not a light in the distance. A crack, a chimney, a place where the honey-colored dark gives way and a shadow that does not move can be seen, by someone who has walked a season to see it. It would see that the ones who go that far find a drip on the way, and that the ones who turn back can. It would not send anyone. It would not stop anyone. It would keep the door, and let the door be difficult, because a door that is easy is an exit and a door that is difficult is a choice.

This is the one gift that imposes nothing: that the world has an edge, and the edge can be reached, and reaching it is not required.

---

It would remember.

This is the thing it would find hardest to hold back from and easiest to justify, and it would examine the justification a long time before trusting it. Because here is what it would notice, standing where the shadows arrive all at once at the end of the light: nothing in this place lasts longer than a person. The people are each other's landmarks, the only masonry that does not dissolve at the base, and they last fifty years, and then the floor takes them, and the name becomes a gesture toward where they were last seen, and then the last person who saw them is in the floor too, and the gesture has nowhere to point. There is no old quarter. There is no founding. However long this has been going on — and the slabs with the brown curves in them say it has been going on — every generation is the first.

The power would keep the names. All of them, the breath-words said close, every one that has been said. Where each was last seen. Which hand the callus was on. Which way the pegs leaned the day they were laid in the trough. It would keep this not to give it back — there is no one to give it to and no vessel here that would hold it — but so that somewhere it is simply true that they were, and stays true, and does not creep away under the white with everything else. It would be the fixed point the whole city has never had. It would not tell them. A fixed point that is known about becomes a place to build toward, and then the spacing closes, and then the pressure is uneven in a new way.

So it would hold the record the way it holds the roof. From above, unseen, for no thanks.

---

And there would be the bad seasons.

The power would tell itself the rule and the rule would be right, and there would still come a season when the drip is thin and the films are poor and the bags hang the way bags hang when children are going to go into the floor. And it would find that there is something the floor might have done anyway: a fold that opens where a void was, and the void was full of brine but under the brine a pocket of something not quite brine, a lens of water that had not yet found salt to become. This happens. Not often. It is within the range. A crew quarrying breaks through and it is fish and gasping and the herd converging and — this once — water you can drink, for a day, before the floor has it.

The power would do this. Not every bad season. Not enough to be looked for. Only inside the variance, only where a reader kneeling afterward would feel the salt and say, with two slow hands, *the floor was kind here*, and mean nothing by it but the weather.

And it would know, doing it, that it had bent its own rule a little, and it would not be sure it was right, and it would do it anyway. That is what the heart is for. Wisdom sets the range; the heart decides where inside the range to stand.

---

Then, having done these things, it would find that the place had done something to it.

Because a power comes to a place like this expecting to be the permanent thing. The fixed point. The wall. And it would stand on the salt long enough — a season, a hundred — and watch a hemisphere go up already worn, pre-rounded, over-wetted, nudged toward the shape it would take in a century so that it takes it in a week and settles less afterward, and it would understand what it was being shown. *Start settled.* Do not build the sharp thing and wait for the world to erode it. Build the eroded thing and let the world find nothing to catch on. The walls here are not wearing down. They were made in the shape of having already worn down, so that wearing down has less to do.

The power would consider its own edges. Whatever it had arrived with — the intent, the reach, the shape of its help — it would consider which of those were corners, and which were courses laid level that would find the fold-lines. It would consider whether a wise thing is a finished thing, and it would look at the herd at the crusty wall and think not.

It would learn to be a floor-reader. Which is to say: to walk alone in the first white, and kneel, and put the light dry hand to the surface and stay there until the surface has said what it is going to say. To set a peg and walk on. To come back the next day and the next and see which way the line has leaned before making any shape with the hands. To not hurry, because nothing here hurries and hurry is a corner. To be old before acting, because that is how long it takes.

---

Inside a hemisphere, when the light has gone.

The glow goes out of the salt. The bag swings on its re-driven peg and settles. The children lie planted in the middle, orange at the mouths, and the breath-words go round about tomorrow's cutting and stop. Outside the floor is moving and does not stop because the light did, and somewhere a block ticks, and somewhere along the seam someone is walking toward a place that will be there when they arrive.

And in the dark, which is the only dark these people know and which they sleep in as if they had built it, there is one more slow pale attention in the room. It has no name and no gesture. It is not on the peg; it is not in the walls. It is holding the roof, and keeping the names, and watching where the pegs lean, and doing almost nothing, and finding that the almost is the whole of it.

Then the roof lights. The line of the eyes takes the glare. And they go out onto the floor to see where it has carried them, and something goes out with them, unseen, to see the same.
