The sentence stops there. Not because there was nothing after it. Because the hand writing it was flat on a step, and had been for a while, and the water around the wrist had gone gritty, and then had not been water.

That is where the attention arrives. Not at the stair. Not at the mud. At the place where a describing stopped, mid-word, at about the height a hand rests when someone kneels to admire a rising step.

---

It comes to the city the way everything comes to it, which is by the ground. It does not need the stair, but it takes the stair, because the stair is the city's first sentence and it is polite to let a place say what it is. Twenty steps. The level. The kerb, the gutter, the channel breathing through cloth. It puts nothing in the water. It leaves no bloom. It has that much power, at least: to stand on the wet stone for the length of a thousand breaths and not thicken.

It listens. Every sound twice. The drip, the tick of fast lime, the mats giving up their small light where the sheet-water shifts. And somewhere—by the near way and the far way—a scrape, a pause, a scrape. It does not go to meet the step. It knows what happens to things that walk toward a wall here.

Then it reads the walls, which it can do. The dark laminations, the pale. The pencil-thick hollows with their black skins. The palm-sized bumps at leaning height, glazed, worn, more than one. It reads the shaft mouths and the heat coming up them steady and does not go down, because what is down there is the same question as the lintel, and it has been asked well, and it does not want to be the one who answers a question that has weathered into a place.

It reads the hand.

---

Being wise, it thinks first about what it must not do.

It must not bring light. The dark here is not a lack; it is a material, laid down as long as the lime has been, and the mats have their light already and use it only when disturbed, and a city lit from above would be a city of walls, ordinary, ruined, done. The mats would go white. The blue-green would have nothing to be against.

It must not cool the water. That would be the obvious mercy—stop the fever, stop the building, let the surface rest. But the surface is the only thing alive, and it is alive because the water is hot, and it has kept one skin ahead of its burial for so long that stopping the burial would not free it. It would only make the last skin the last.

It must not straighten anything. There is a mat that is a ring, and a reed that runs a loop and arrives at its own node without turning, and these are the only inhabitants who live inside the knots comfortably, and a power that untied the knots to make the streets honest would cut them both in half and call it repair.

It must not carry the walker out. Where to? The stair goes down to mud that is the same mud. And the walker came up from that mud on purpose, and knelt on purpose, and was in the middle of saying what lived here when the lime took the saying. To lift them out would be to decide that the sentence was a mistake. It was not a mistake. It was very nearly finished.

It thinks about the seed for a long time. One seed, one second reed. The plant that has been reproducing by being itself longer would have, for the first time in a span of time the walls cannot measure, someone else. It turns this over. It is the kind of gift a lesser benevolence would give and go home pleased. It sets it down. The reed is not lonely. Loneliness is a vocabulary. The reed has only the grammar, and has made a map of every dry channel in the city with it, and does not need to be told what it lacks.

---

So this is what it does.

It waits for the air to grow heavy. It can feel the heaviness coming the way the walls feel it, in the film, in the wet that is more than wet. It could make the fall come sooner. It does not. It stands on the plaza in the dark that is a material and lets the city arrive at its own weather.

And when the whole saturated volume lets go at once—when every surface streams and the channels rise and speak and the flicker runs out across the stone from every mat in the city answering together—it looks.

That is the first thing. Not a saving. A seeing. For the length of one fall of air the city is lit from below by its builders, faintly, entirely, the low walls and the dark doorways and the long facade like a row of teeth and the stair going up into nothing and the ring-mat lit on both sides of the wall it is on both sides of and the reed's stems standing in their scatter on the high terraces like rods left in a floor—and for the first time since the water began coming up, something is present that can hold the whole of it at once and knows what it is holding. The mats have lit the city this way ten thousand times for nobody. Once, for the length of a fall, they light it for something that sees. Nothing in them registers this. It is not for them. It is the city's due, and the power pays it, and does not expect to be thanked, and is not.

The second thing happens inside the first, because the fall is the only time it could.

The sheet-water rises over the step where the hand is. It always does, in a fall; it is why the film goes gritty afterward. This time the water that comes over the step has a drop in it. One. Falling from no height, from a sky that does not otherwise participate, onto the seal at the wrist, at the place where the last lamination is thinnest. It is not much. A drop does not lift a hand out of a wall. A drop only does what a drop does here, which is keep whatever it strikes from sitting still. The seal that was setting does not set. The grit does not become crust. Under the fall, everything on the step is loose for a moment, and in that moment the hand is a thing that is struck, and turns, by a hair, and is round.

That is all the power gives. Not freedom—there is nowhere to be free to. It gives the walker the condition of the pearls. It becomes the drip. Not once: it arranges that the drop will fall again. From the lintel above the step, where the spill runs, where a drop has been falling for a long time a hand's breadth to the left and will now, by the smallest correction in the lip, fall a hand's breadth to the right. Onto the wrist. Steadily. For as long as the water comes up.

The walker will not leave. The walker will not be covered. The walker will be turned, a little, each time the drop strikes, and the lime will coat them evenly because they will never be long enough on one side to be sealed, and they will be in the dished threshold of the city's admiration, warm, round, loose, jostling when struck, for a span of time the walls will not be thick enough to measure. Doing nothing. Staying loose in a city where everything else is laid down.

It is the only kind of life the place has. The power did not invent it. It only moved a drop.

---

Then the air is lighter, briefly, and the steam comes off the stone in thin veils, and the tick of fast lime is everywhere.

It stands a while longer. It listens for the step, by the near way and the far way. It hears it. It does not resolve it. It puts its attention to the rounded top of a chamber wall and hears the wet cloth of the breathing and does not go down and does not say. If there is a body under the city, the body is kept warm, and has room, and has never asked. If there is not, the shape is still there, and the shape is enough to have made a walker kneel.

Before it goes, it does the last thing, which is the smallest.

It finishes the sentence. Not aloud—a word spoken here goes about as far as the arm and falls into the wet. It finishes it in the only medium the place keeps, which is a thickening. On the step, beside the hand, where the film is already setting again, it lets the water lay down one lamination in a shape that is not a lintel and not a bloom and not a pearl. A shape with the grammar of writing and none of the vocabulary. The lime does not distinguish. In a thousand years it will be a raised line under a foot of wall, and no one will break the wall at that height, and if they did they would read only that something was there and the water covered it.

It says: *—and what lives here lives by being kept from setting, and that is not thin, and it was seen.*

Then it goes down the stair into the mud that is the same mud, and leaves no bloom on the treads, and the smear of blue-green rises where its foot was and fades, and the drop falls, and the hand turns by a hair, and the city goes on building, one skin ahead, in the dark, exactly as far away as before.
