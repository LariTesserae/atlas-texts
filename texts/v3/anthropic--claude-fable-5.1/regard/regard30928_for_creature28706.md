It comes the way everything comes here — from above, along the water. It does not need a barge, but it takes the barge's position anyway, at roof height on the ring, looking down the chimneys, because that is the place the bowl has already made for a watcher and a wise thing uses the door that is open.

Then it does the first thing a wise thing does, which is nothing, for a long time.

It takes a visitor a year to stop hearing the roar. The power is not in a hurry. It takes the year. It stays through the first autumn survey and the frogs and the winter when the condensation freezes on the undersides of the rails and drops off at ten in the morning in a short bright shower of its own; it stays until the six waterfalls are no longer a sound but a floor, and it can stand on that floor and hear what is underneath. And what is underneath is what the piece already told you. It hears the lake. It hears the household. It hears that the two are not what they look like from the rim.

From the rim, this is a siege. A drowned thing kept out of its bed by force, a town on a pumped floor, brass pins counting down. Benevolence, arriving at a siege, wants to end it, and there are only two ways to end a siege, and the power considers both.

It could let the lake home. It plays this out with the care such things deserve. A gentle version: the pumps slow over a generation, the water table comes up a finger a year instead of down, the alders drown first and then the rails, the people carry their tables up the dike one household at a time and the market town absorbs them and thinks them slow and rude for a decade and then forgets. The lake gets level. The bowl gets its thousand years back. It is an ending with a shape, and the power can feel its own pull toward it, the way you feel the pull of a thing that is simply *correct*.

And then it looks at the three children who woke crying when Number Two stopped for two hours, and it puts the ending down. Stopping is the word for danger. It has been the word for danger in those bodies since before the word. To give the lake its bed the power would have to break sixty people out of the only shape they have ever fitted, and call it kindness, and the lake did not ask. The lake has never asked for anything. It leans. A benevolence that gives the leaning thing what it leans toward is not benevolence. It is gravity with a good opinion of itself.

So it turns the other way and considers ending the siege from the inside. Still the clay. Seat the footings on something that does not give. Take the seep out of the ground so the pumps have only the rain to lift, or take the rain too, and let the great effort become unnecessary. The pins would read zero every autumn. The chapel crack would stay filled.

It sees this one further, because it is more tempting. The roar stops — not all at once, but as the pumps ease back to what the sky alone sends, until the six houses idle and then one by one go cold. And in the quiet the household is no longer a household. Nothing needs keeping. The greaser has nothing to grease. The woman with the ledger writes the same figure eleven times and closes the book. Within a generation they go up the dike, not driven, simply released, and the bowl is a green field with some brick in it, and the lake — the lake is *sealed*. Not held. Sealed. Held and sealed are different things and the brass pins have been measuring the difference for eleven years without anyone calling it that. To hold something you have to be in contact with it and it has to be able to push back. The whole strange life of this place is in that contact. Take it away and you have not freed either party. You have parted them.

The third mercy is the one that hardly needs refusing, but the power is thorough. It could announce itself. Fill the crack in the west face with a grey that matches. Let them know they are watched over. It looks at the chapel, small and brick with its token stack, built in the same yard as the pump-houses because that was the only shape anyone here knew how to make holy, and it understands that they already know what they worship, and that they are not wrong, and that to add itself to this would be to become a seventh house, one that runs on something other than coal, and they would attend to it the way they attend to bearings, faithfully, as one more thing to keep. That is not a gift. That is a shift.

So it will not end the siege, because it is not a siege. What it is, the power decides, standing on the ring at roof height in the second summer with the bats coming out under it in the shape of smoke, is a very old creature and a very small people who have found a way to lean on each other that neither of them has words for, and the whole arrangement is honest, and works, and costs, and the costs are where a benevolent thing should look.

It goes to the ledger first.

Not to change the figures. The figures are true; the woman reads well. But she lays a ruler along her columns, and a ruler is a straight thing, and the fall is not straight. The clay gave two inches the first year, when the lake's weight came off it and the pumps drew the water out of a thousand years of silt like breath out of a lung. Then an inch and a half. Then an inch. This year five-eighths. The curve is bending under her ruler where she cannot see it, because eleven points are not many and a ruler is a hard thing to argue with, and she has carried the straight line down into her kindness for a decade — the certainty that everything here is falling at one rate toward one place.

The power does not give her the twelfth figure. It gives her, in the autumn, kneeling at the pin on Number One with the level, the thought that the ruler is the wrong tool. It is a small thought and it arrives like her own. That night she takes a length of the greaser's copper wire from the shed, where it hangs in a coil by the shelf that is not a collection, and lays it along the column and lets it find its own shape against the figures. And the shape is a curve. Steep, then less, then less. Never flat. She checks that with the ruler and the ruler agrees: it never comes flat. But it approaches something. It approaches, and the approaching slows, always, so that the thing it approaches is not reached in any time she has a number for. The town is not falling toward the lake. The town and the lake are settling toward each other along a curve that gets gentler the closer they come, like two people who have stopped moving toward each other and are simply, now, standing near.

She tells no one. Words are for bearings. But the next morning she is kind in a different way — not the kindness of someone carrying a thing but of someone who has put it down and found it was not as heavy as she thought, and that is a change a visitor would notice in the first hour, and no one here notices at all, which is how the power knows it worked.

The night man at Number Six is harder.

Eleven years without a dream, and the power understands what that sleep is, because it has stood on the same floor. The lake lies under the mind and nothing rises through it. That is a gift and a theft together — sleep with no fear in it, and nothing else in it either. The power cannot give him his own dreams back. To do that it would have to lift the floor, and the floor is what he came down the dike for; he said so, twice, the second time more quietly.

So it does not give him his own. It asks the lake for one of the lake's.

Water does not speak and does not want, but it has one gesture, which is to find the low place and fill it, and asked properly it will do this in a mind as readily as in a wheel-rut. And so the night man, in the fourth hour of a shift, sitting on the greasing box with his back against the warm brick of the motor housing, closes his eyes for two minutes the way he has ten thousand times, and for the first time in eleven years something comes up through the floor.

It is not drowning. He has expected, if he expected anything, that a dream here would be drowning. It is level. A flat grey sheet a mile across under a flat grey sky, with nothing on it, no rim, no dike, no houses, no white water pouring upward; and he is not in it or under it. He is *of* it. Spread thin and calm across the whole extent, with no edge anywhere and no effort anywhere, and the thing the pumps have been holding away for eleven years is shown to him from inside, and from inside it is rest. Just that. The rest of a thing that has finally stopped having a shape to keep.

He opens his eyes. The motor is humming against his spine. Number Six is running at exactly the speed it has always run. He goes and checks the tank gauge, which he was going to check anyway, and greases nothing differently, and says nothing at the shift change except *Six, quiet, all night*, which is what he always says.

But the way he stands at the rail at dusk is different afterward. It was the stance of a man on watch. It becomes the stance of a man keeping company. The difference is in the shoulders and would not show up in any survey, and the power notes it, and counts it, and knows that the lake — which did not want, does not want — has nevertheless been let into one sleeper's sleep as itself and not as a threat, and that this is the closest thing to being seen that water can have.

---

For the lake, the power has only one thing, and it is not a thing.

It stands on the ring at roof height on a night with no barges, and it says the lake's name. Not the name on the engineers' drawings, which is the market town's name with *Polder* after it. The old one. The name from before the auger, before the market town, the one the reeds and the herons and the eels have never stopped using because they have never needed a word for it — the name of a thing that was here when the dike-builders' grandfathers were not yet a thought. The power knows it because that is the kind of thing a wise power knows. It says it once, quietly, over the roar, in the flat carrying voice the people use here, the voice of someone speaking across a river to someone on the other bank.

Water has no ears. It has, however, always been addressed, and it knows the difference between being addressed and being pumped. It does not answer. It goes round. But in the spring the woman with the ledger, who now also records things the ledger was not built for, notes that the tidemark on the brick is where it was — two feet up and holding — and not a finger higher, and she does not know why she writes this down, and writes it down.

---

Then the children, and this is where the power finds out what its own wisdom costs.

Nine of them. Forty houses with gaps in the rows, twelve keepers' posts, sixty souls. The arithmetic is not hard. Most of these children will go up the dike. Most of them will go into the world of small separate noises — the cart, the voice, the door — and stand too close to people and shout and not turn round when spoken to from behind, and they will lie down at night on nothing, no floor beneath them, and they will not sleep. Not for years. Some of them not ever, properly. They draw the water at the top of the page. They are right. They will be going up into a world drawn wrong, and they will be lonely in it in a way they will not have words for, because words are for bearings.

The power could take the roar out of them. Lift it gently, so that they carry a quiet up the dike instead of a floor that is no longer there. It considers this longer than it considered letting the lake home. It looks at each of the nine.

And it leaves them as they are.

Because the roar is not a wound. It is what they are made of. They are the only people in the world who know, in the spine and not the head, that stopping is the danger — that a great effort standing perfectly still is not a failure but the whole point, that a thing can be held for eleven years or a hundred and the holding *is* the accomplishment and not the wait before one. The world up the dike does not know this. It thinks effort is for arriving. It will need, eventually, a few people who grew up inside a machine that was not winning and not losing and was doing something better than either, and who cannot sleep in silence because silence is the sound of something having stopped. The power does not give them a gift. It refuses to take one away. And it knows that this refusal will be paid for in the small hours of nights it will not be there to see, in rented rooms in the market town and further, by children who are not children then, and it lets it be paid, and does not pretend to itself that this is anything other than the hardest thing it has done here.
