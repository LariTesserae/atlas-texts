It would come down the way everything comes down here: through the cold, through the mile and a half, losing on the way whatever it had brought with it that needed light to work.

That is the first thing, and it is not nothing. A power arriving from anywhere else arrives with a lamp in its hand — not a literal one, but the habit of one, the assumption that to attend to a place is to illuminate it. It would have to notice that habit somewhere around the point where the water stops getting colder and begins, strangely, to warm. It would have to set the lamp down in the dark and leave it there. Light is not a gift here. Light would be an acid poured on the towers, a thing the mussels have no word for and no defense against, a fire lit in a library. The first wise act is to arrive blind, and to stay blind, and to learn the city the way the city learns itself: by chemistry, by heat, by touch.

---

Then it would look for the being it had been told about. The one who lives here.

And it would find, first, the bacteria, and think: *these*. Everywhere, uncounted, first, the reason for the rest. If there is a resident, it is them. But they cannot be addressed. There is no one there to receive a kindness, only a process, ancient and sufficient, that would go on exactly the same whether anything attended to it or not. The power would understand that it had been shown the economy and not the citizen, and go on looking.

It would find the worms, and see that each one is a wall around its guests and has given up the organs it would need to be visited. It would find the mussels, and see that there are too many for *one* to mean anything. It would find the shrimp and see the city thinking, and see that the thought had no thinker. It would find the crab, standing, and be tempted — because the crab's patience looks so much like a mind — and would wait beside it for a day and learn that the crab is waiting for nothing, only for *now*.

And then, at the western edge, in a hollow in the crust that nobody assigned, it would find the one thing on the rise that goes somewhere.

It would understand, then, why it had been told *being* and not *beings*. Not because the octopus matters more than the mussels — the mussels are the floor, and the floor matters most — but because the octopus is the only one that does something the city does not need done. It crosses the whole width of the rise to sit against a piece of rotten wood and do nothing. It has, in a place where everything is where it is because the heat put it there, a place of its own. The power would recognize that. It would be doing the same thing: crossing an enormous distance to sit against something and attend to it for no reason that has anything to do with food.

---

What it would want to do, and would not.

It would want to bring the two octopuses together. The soft one in the western hollow and the one somewhere on the eastern beds, who have never met and never will. That is the shape loneliness takes to an eye from the surface, and the impulse to fix it is very strong. But the power would look at how these animals live and see that the meeting is the ending — that they come together once, at the last, and afterward there is brooding in the cold and then there is not. To bring them together early would not be to give the octopus company. It would be to shorten its life for the sake of a picture the power carried down with it from a world where meeting means something else. The power would set that down beside the lamp.

It would want to keep the rock warm. All of this is on borrowed heat; every vent dies; someday the seeps will falter one after another and the towers will withdraw from the base and the whole live skin will become foundation. The power could prevent that. It could hold the cracks open and the flow steady for as long as it liked. And in doing so it would end the one thing the description called the city's essential fact: that the map is always being redrawn, that districts die and are grown over, that the mass increases because the city keeps everyone. A city that never shifts is a city that never adds a layer. The power would understand that it had been offered the chance to turn a living thing into a monument to itself, and would decline.

It would want to do something for the clams in the log, who are eating their country out from under themselves and do not know it. But there is nothing to tell them and no one to tell. What they need is not knowledge. What they need is wood.

It would want, worst of all, to make the dark less dark — not with light but with meaning. To tell the shrimp that its larvae will find a city. To tell the worm at the base of the oldest tower that it is the closest to the source and will be the last to starve. To tell the octopus what the wood is. And it would see that every one of these would be a story imposed on a place that runs perfectly well without stories, and that the impulse to narrate is the lamp again, in a different hand.

---

What it would do.

It would stay. That first, and longest. It would sit beside the crab at the channel edge and watch it, and it would watch long enough to know how long a crab lives — which no one knows, because no one has ever watched long enough. It would count the shrimp, which no one has counted. It would trace the chemistry the larvae follow back down through the cold, which no one has traced. It would learn the interval at which the octopus goes to the wood, and find that there is no interval, and know that there is no interval rather than suspecting it. It would learn the city in the only currency the city deals in, which is time and attention, and it would spend both without stint and ask nothing back, because the city has no way to give anything back and because that was never the point.

None of this changes anything. The crab does not know it is being watched. The knowing sits entirely on the power's side. But a place that has never been seen, and a place that has been seen, are not the same place, even if nothing in either one can tell the difference — and if the power is what it is said to be, then to be the one who knows how long the crab lives is not a small thing. It is a kind of keeping. It is the only company the city can receive without being harmed by it.

Then, having stayed, it would do two things with its hands, and both of them would be indistinguishable from weather.

Somewhere far above, in the world of light, in a country the city has no word for, a tree at the edge of a river would come loose in a flood. This happens. It would be carried out, and waterlog, and sink, and fall for a long time through the cold, and a current that could have put it anywhere on the abyssal plain would put it at the eastern edge of the rise, a body-length from the old log, in the year the old log went from lattice to fragments. The boring clams' larvae, rising blind into the dark looking for the one thing they can eat, would find it. The exiles would have a country again, for a few decades more. And the octopus, on some unscheduled crossing, would arrive at the eastern edge and find two strangenesses where there had been one — one nearly gone and one new and sound, still faintly carrying whatever it is that wood carries down from the light — and would drape itself over the new one, and stay, and the power would not ask why, because it had promised itself not to.

The second thing would be farther off, and slower, and not for this city at all. Somewhere along the ridge, at a distance the shrimp larvae can drift in a few weeks in the cold column, the power would find a place where the crust was thin and the heat was close and nothing had opened yet, and it would let the crack begin. Not to save this rise. This rise will die on its own schedule and be right to. But when it does, the larvae will go up looking for *some* city, and the power would have made sure that there was one — young, a few hot channels and a first stand of bristle worms, no towers yet, no foundation, nothing to inherit. Room. The city's children would arrive at an empty warm place in the dark and begin, and the power would have had no more hand in it than the rock does.

---

And then it would not leave, exactly, because it would have stopped being the kind of thing that arrives and leaves. It would have become part of the dark, a slight steadiness in the attention the water pays to itself, and if the octopus felt anything where it sat against the new wood — a warmth of the kind a hand gives held near but not on — it would not know what to make of it, and would not try, and would go back to the mussel beds in its own time.

The towers would lean into the flow. The beds would breathe. The crab would stand on the pavement, its legs spread, its hair reading the water, and a shrimp would walk toward it. And somewhere in the dark, for the first time since the rock first breathed, someone would know how long the crab had been standing there, and would keep the number, and would not say.
