**What the power did**

Start with the power, because attention needs a place to come from, and this one came from a long way off.

It was not the water that drew it. Standing things do not draw notice; that is the whole art of standing. A body of water four hundred meters across with no top, holding itself at the heaviest temperature on a bare plateau, is loud to an eye — but the power did not have eyes, exactly, and to whatever it had instead the cistern registered as a silence, a region where a great many things that should have been happening were not. Silences are common. The universe is mostly them.

What it noticed was the wave.

In a field where nothing moves, a thing that moves is very loud, and a thing that moves without ever repeating itself is louder still — the way one voice in an empty hall carries further than a crowd in a full one. Somewhere on the far side of a great deal of ordinary matter, a length of pale substance was leaning one way and then another, and the leaning never closed, and the power, which had been listening for a long time to a great many periodic things, heard the one thing with no period and turned toward it.

—

It saw all of Ora Hend at once, the way you see a room when the light comes on. The eleven buildings and the not-quite-grid. The seams and their permitted radius. The grit hanging in the black exactly where the last visitor kicked it. The fold along which up became down. The tone at the edge of hearing, which was, as suspected, the mechanism — a very good one; the power took a moment over it with something like professional respect. And the ribbon.

One.

It knew that at once, because the fold was no obstacle to it. There was a single creature in the cistern, and the loop it traced through the folded water crossed the seam-lights at eleven places, and any watcher stationed at a crossing would count a dozen. The founders had not planned it. The power could see that too — could see the shape of their intention in the stone, and the ribbon was not in it. They had drawn the spring up out of its fold and told it to stand and had not looked to see what came up with it. The joke they would have preferred was one they never knew they'd made.

So: one, in the dark, in cold still water, following the edge of the only warmth, older than the streets it kept to, alive in the way a finished thing is finished. The power looked at this for what would have been, to a visitor, a long time.

—

What does a benevolent power do with a thing like that? The first and most honest answer is that it asks whether anything is wrong. Benevolence is not the same as busyness. Most of what draws such a power's attention is strangeness, and most strangeness is not a wound, and one of the things that makes a power wise rather than merely strong is knowing which is which before it reaches out a hand.

It considered the ribbon's case with care, because the case was not obvious.

It could let the water down. Release the instruction, and Ora Hend would fall in an afternoon — a cataract off the plateau's edge, the eleven buildings standing wet in the wind for the first time, the grit finally on the floor — and the ribbon would go with it, down the mountain, into a stream, into the ordinary world of light and current and things that eat, where it would live perhaps a few weeks and die. That is what would have happened to it in the spring, eventually. The power could give it that. It weighed the gift and set it down. The creature had never asked for a surface.
