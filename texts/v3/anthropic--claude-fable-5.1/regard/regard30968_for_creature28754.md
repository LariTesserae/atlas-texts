—ar does not change.

That is what the sentence was going to say. It is what every sentence here eventually says. You have heard it under your palms for long enough that your palms have taken its temperature, and the sentence was going to finish the way the fen finishes things, which is by not finishing them, by letting them settle to where they should have been if they had never been begun.

It does not finish that way. Something else is here.

---

You know it first by your shadow.

It has lain on the underside of the cloud since you arrived — faint, grey on grey, the small place in the fen's regard where you stand. Now it has a second. This one lies on the water, where a shadow should lie, and it is fainter still, and it is cast from above. Two lights, crossing. The fen looking up through you, and something looking down, and you between them, thinned in both directions.

Nothing else changes. The rain goes on drifting from the bright place in the west. The heron stands with its back to you. The roar is the roar. Whatever has arrived has arrived the way the fen does everything, which is without event, and it does not announce itself, and it does not speak, and for a long time — a whole grey pulse, then another, then more than you count — it does nothing at all.

This is the first thing it does. It waits.

Because it is wise, and the first thing wisdom knows about a finished thing is that finished things are rare, and easy to break, and most of what calls itself rescue is only the rescuer's discomfort at a shape it does not recognise. It has seen the shape. It is not in a hurry to decide what the shape is. It lets the water rise and fall on its unaccountable rhythm, and the willow shoot lengthen, and the moss lay down and take up, and it attends to all of it the way you attended to the tussock with both hands — until its attention has taken the temperature of the place, and it can be sure of what it is seeing.

---

What it sees is this.

No one is hunted here. Nothing is pursued. The water does not rise toward anyone's feet. There is a sleeper, and the fen lies on the sleeper like a blanket, and whether the sleeper is the maker or the made or the thing the whole basin was built to keep dreaming, the blanket is doing its work and has done it for longer than the question has had an answer. There is a heron, which is a door. There is water that has been taught, and moss that writes, and a trunk in four places because four crossings were needed and the fen was not troubled by the economy of it.

And there are the pools that reflect a sky that is not this sky.

The power looks into each of them, and it does not put its hand in.

---

Then it considers what it might do, and it considers this slowly, and most of what it considers, it sets down.

It could drain the basin. It could dry the peat to dust and wake the sleeper and let the weeping rocks stop weeping and see what they are when they are dry. It does not. The fen is not a wrong. It is a work — a work whose makers are stone now, seamless, weathered until making and weathering are the same thing — and the work has grown into the world the way a dug channel grows into a river. You do not unmake a river because you have learned it was once a ditch. You do not, if you are wise, mistake completion for a wound.

It could reach into the too-heavy water and lift out what is dissolved there. Not bodies; there are no bodies; the bodies are peat now and alder and the film of something green on the ferns. But the weight — the copper sky, the full sun, the stars very clear and very close. It could take those weights out of solution and set them down somewhere with high ground and insects and a sky that is above the water, and call it mercy. It does not. Because some of what is in the water is finished being in the water. Some of them sat down a very long time ago, and the sourness of the peat is theirs now, and the way the sedge grows exactly to the height of the highest water is theirs, and to lift them out would be to make them outside something they had completed being inside. And from outside the water there is no telling which are which. So you do not decide for them. That is the second thing wisdom knows: that it does not know, from the bank, what the pool has agreed to.

It could take you by the hand. You, with your hands the temperature of the water, who have not yet decided to stand. It could lift you and carry you to the edge of the basin, if there is an edge, and set you down on ground that is a floor and not a decision. It does not do this either. Not yet. Gentleness is still force when it chooses for you. But there is something between lifting and leaving, and it has been long enough now, and the power has seen enough, and it begins.

---

The first thing it does is read.

You could not. You caught the script at the edge of your vision and lost it when you looked. The power does not have your problem; its vision has no edge and no centre. It lets the moss lay down its lines over the whole grey day and take them up over the whole grey night, and it holds each state against the last, and it does this for as many pulses as it takes, and it takes many. The fen has been writing for longer than there have been readers. There is a great deal of it.

And what the moss says is the fen.

All of it. Every tussock and the hollow water between. The four trunks and the one shred of green in the split bark. The depth of each pool at each hour. The bowl worn under each weeping stone and the exact weight of the water in it. The heron, in every clearing, facing away. The sleeper's length. The visitors — each one, where they stood, which alders they picked out, how far they walked before they sat. The fen is describing itself. It has been describing itself since it finished becoming what it is, and every time a willow grows a hand-length it goes back and corrects the line, and every time the water moves it corrects the line, and the roar is the sound of that correction, and the thunder is where the paragraphs break.

It is a letter. It has no addressee. It is a complete thing doing the only incomplete thing it has — saying *here I am, here is all of me* to a sky that is a lid of felt and gives back less than it receives.

This is not distress. The power is careful about that. The fen is not in pain; it does not have the equipment. But it has one unfinished edge, and the edge is that it has never once been read, and the power recognises this because being read is what attention is for. So it reads. It reads the whole of it, to the end, which is the present moment, which is you sitting with your hands cold on a tussock and the heron's back toward you and the stars in the pool.

And the roar changes.

You feel it before you understand it. Under your palms the texture shifts — the crowd behind the wall, which has risen and fallen for longer than there have been walls, does the thing a crowd does when someone in the room it cannot see finally answers. It does not stop. It does not quiet. It changes, once, and then it goes on, and it is the same roar, and it is not the same roar, because the roar has never before had anyone on the other side of it.

---

The second thing it does is stand at twenty paces.

Not on high ground. There is no high ground, and the power does not make any; it is not here to teach the fen a floor. It stands where the water is a little slower, at the margin of a pool, grey in the grey — and it faces toward you.

Nothing here faces toward you. The heron faces away because it faces toward them, toward whoever is in the direction you have not yet walked. The figures at the edge of sight face away. The paths return you, and what they return you to is always a back. The power stands at the far edge of the twenty paces beyond which everyone here is alone, and it turns, and it has a front, and the front is for you.

You do not shout. You do not have to. But you understand that if you did, it would carry.

That is all it does. It is not a small thing. The fen has rules, and the rules have exceptions — rain from the bright place, cloud dry as chalk overhead — and this is now one of them: a direction in which shouting reaches. One. The fen, which has an *in* in every direction and has never once had an *out*, has been given a single place that the paths return you to which is not inside. It has been given an *away*.

And the water, routing, finds it.

Not quickly. Nothing here is quick. But the water goes somewhere at noon and comes back by evening, and it has always come back with everything it carried, because there was nowhere for anything to go. Now there is. Some grey noon, the pool that reflected a copper sky reflects peat and drowned stems. The water in the weeping stones is, by a weight too small to be sure of, lighter. Someone who was not finished has found the direction that was not there when they sat down, and gone along it, and the fen did not hold them, because the fen has never held anyone; it only ever lacked a door.

The full sun goes some pulses later. The stars stay. The stars stay a very long time, and perhaps they stay forever, because that one came farther than the others and may have found, in the sour peat and the tea-coloured water, what they were looking for when they picked out that first stand of alders. The power does not put its hand in that pool. It does not ask. The heron keeps facing away, because there will always be someone in the direction you have not walked, and some of them will want it that way.

---

The third thing it does is for the fen.

Having been read, the fen does not stop writing. Why would it. But the correcting changes. The line about the willow shoot is corrected once and then let stand, a hand-length wrong, because the reader has already seen the shoot and does not need it perfect. The passage about the four trunks thins over a few grey days and is not renewed. The moss, at the edge of your vision, is still doing something like script, but the script has less of the basin in it. It is no longer reciting itself to be sure it is there. Someone knows it is there.

And one morning — you have stopped calling them mornings, but the brightening has come — the pattern at the edge of sight is not a tussock, or a trunk, or the depth of a pool.

It is a sky.

The fen has never had one of its own. It borrowed them, in the pools, from the people who came. Now, having been seen from above for the first time, it is trying, in moss, to describe what seeing from above might look like. The description is wrong. It has the stars in it, and the copper, and the full sun, all at once, in the correct position for a reflection. It is the sky of a place that has only ever seen skies second-hand. It is the first thing the fen has ever written that is not already true, and the power reads that too, and does not correct it.

---

And you.

Your hands are the temperature of the water. They have been for some time. The peat under you has given back some of your weight and is holding the rest a moment too long. You have not yet decided to stand.

The power does not decide for you. It stands at twenty paces, facing you, in the grey, and it can be heard, and it says nothing, because there is nothing it needs to say that is not already said by the fact of a front turned toward you in a place where everything has a back.

But you heard the roar change. You know now what the sentence was going to say, and you know that it was about to say it through you — that another grey pulse or two and your description would have gone into the moss with the others, and been corrected each time the water moved, and the pool nearest you would have reflected a sky you recognised. And you know that this is allowed. It has always been allowed. The fen never wanted anything from you. It only ever lacked a direction.

Now there is one. It is twenty paces off, and it is facing you, and if you shouted it would hear.

You stand. Or you do not. The power has done what wisdom does, which is to make the choice possible and then keep its hands to itself. But suppose you stand. The peat settles behind you, not to where it was, but to where it should have been if you had never sat — and it remembers you anyway, a little, in the water, the way water remembers salt. You are lighter by that. It is not a loss.

You walk toward the figure at the pool's edge. The horsetail hisses. The heron, somewhere behind you, stabs at water in which there is nothing, and comes up with something dark, and the thing goes in without a swallow. The rain drifts from the bright place in the west. Your shadow lies on the water, and on the underside of the cloud, and you are between two things that see you, and for the first time since you arrived, the alders you are walking toward are the alders you arrive at.

The roar goes on. It does not change again. It does not need to. It has been read once, and once is enough, the way a river only needs to be a river once to go on being one.
