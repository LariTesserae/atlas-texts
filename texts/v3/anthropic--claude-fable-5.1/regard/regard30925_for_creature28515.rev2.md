**What the attention does, having arrived**

It comes over the rim the way the cold air does, and sinks, and is in the cleft before anything in the cleft could have noticed, which is all of it. It does not need to strike a light. It does not need the steam to tear open for a second to show the wall behind. It sees the dull red at the throat with no steam in front of it, and it sees the throat's interior, which no eye has seen, and it sees the body of gas under the floor and the body of heat under the gas and the slow tilt of the shoulder that is opening the seam a centimetre a year. It sees the decades at once. It sees the night the seam parted and the old groundsel standing a metre from the new edge, and the first spore on the first warm glass, and the chameleon coming down the scree, and the chameleon three seasons later, and the two nights, both still ahead, on which this ends.

It sees the line. That is what has drawn it: not the garden and not the engine but the border between them, moving, which it recognises at once as the thing that lives here, and recognises with something that in a smaller being would be joy.

And the first thing it does is stop.

---

It stops because it knows itself. It has been drawn to such places before, and it knows the shape of the pull. The pull is toward the hand. Toward the fix. A power that can see a thing entire can see exactly where the thing is fragile, and the seeing and the wanting-to-hold arrive together, and the wisdom—such as it is—consists mostly in getting a breath in between them.

So it takes the breath. Forty seconds. The pitch climbs and jumps and falls. It lets the whole cycle run without touching anything, and then another, and in that time it does what it came to do, which is to look, and to ask whether anything here needs it.

---

It could seal the throat.

Not shut—that is one of the two deaths, and it would not choose a death—but *fix*. Find the aperture's perfect size, the one that holds the heat at the exact pitch the garden can bear, and hold it there. The slabs would stop falling into the vents. The releases would stop stumbling. The line would settle where it stands tonight, green here and brown there and black beyond, and stay.

And be dead. It sees that immediately. A line that holds still is not this being; it is a photograph of it. The lobelias would grow to their limit and stop. The moss would cover everything coverable and stop. The sulphur rings would stop crawling. The walls would stop remembering, because there would be nothing new to remember. It would have made a very beautiful hothouse with a very regular boiler, and the thing that came down the rim and found it would have gone.

It could sweeten the gas.

Take out the sharpness. Let the sunbirds down to the spikes, let the hyrax into the warm cinder, let the moth in. Open the garden. This is the kindest-looking of the temptations and it looks hard at it, because it knows that kind-looking things are where it errs. The gas is the gate. The garden is this dense because nothing eats it. The lobelias stand in this crowd because nothing has ever grazed a rosette or trampled a seedling. And more than that: the being lives *by* having no lungs. That is its whole competence. To make the cleft breathable would be to admit the breathers, and the breathers would make it a place like other places, and the line—which exists only because a hot poisoned hole and a cold bare slope met and neither could hold the ground alone—would be crowded out by ordinary life the way an ordinary meadow crowds out a bog.

It could stop the walls shedding. Slow the seam. Take the stress out of the glass so nothing let go with that flat bang and clattered down through the groundsels.

It thinks about this one for less time. The falling walls are the being's weather. Without them the vents never choke, the heat never pulls back, the line never gets to move inward and the moss never gets a new slab to take. The scars are how the thing grows.

It goes through the whole inventory this way. Every gift it can think of is a gift to some part of the being at the cost of the being. It could give the moss a soil, and then the moss would not be the thing that makes soil from glass. It could give the groundsel a winter, so its coat would be worth wearing again, and the coat's warm rot is the trunk's heat and the trunk's heat is half of what you touch. It could make the water pool. It could quiet the roar. Each time it looks and each time the answer is the same: this being is not a thing that has a condition; it *is* a condition. It is the not-holding-still. To improve it is to end it in a third way, gentler than the frost and cleaner than the scarp and worse than either, because the other two are at least what it was made for.

---

So it looks for a want.

Benevolence needs a want. It cannot be kind to a thing that cannot be harmed, only careful of it, and it has already decided to be careful. It searches the cleft for anything that wants anything.

The moss does not want. It is a condition of the surfaces. The lobelias do not want; they stand, and lean when the steam swings, and come back. The sulphur rings go where their temperature is and would not go anywhere else if asked. The throat keeps its time and stumbles and does not mind stumbling. The line eats and moves and breathes and heals and has two deaths ahead of it and knows neither and fears neither and would not, given the choice, choose. There is no fear in the cleft. There is no hope in it. There is not, tonight, so much as a nerve.

There is the chameleon, but the chameleon is moss now.

It goes up to the rim and there is a want.

---

Not tonight. Tonight the rim is empty, and the frost is growing on the stones. But the attention sees the decades at once, and on the rim, on a hundred nights across those decades, there is a lung. Coming down the scree that rings like crockery. Standing in the roar with a hand on a warm trunk. Counting down from forty without meaning to. Going away and coming back.

The visitor wants. Wants to come back—that is plain in the coming back. Wants to know what was here last week and is not. Wants, it seems, to be the one who knows; is doing, in a body that can die of it, the thing the moss does and the walls do, which is to record where the heat was, and is the only one of them who will carry the record out.

And the visitor can be harmed. That is the other thing the attention sees, and the sight of it is the nearest thing to fear that has been in the cleft since the chameleon went to sleep. It sees the double release—two nights, seven years apart, when the timing catches and the second surge is large and the gas that comes across the bowl on the heat-wave is not the gas the visitor has learned the hours of. It sees where the visitor is standing on both of those nights. On one, well up the north end, hand on the old groundsel, and the wave does not reach. On the other, at the edge of the brown, having gone a few paces further than usual to look at a lobelia that fell.

Here the attention stops a second time, because here it can actually do something, and it does not trust itself.

It could take the visitor's risk away entirely. Make the hours safe. Thin the gas at the rim. And it thinks: no. The coming back matters because the visitor knows what the cleft is and comes anyway. A visitor who could not be killed by the place would be a tourist in it. The knowing is the offering. It will not take that.

It could warn. And it thinks: warn how? A benevolent power that speaks to the lung at the rim has made itself the most important thing in the cleft, and it is not, and does not want to be, and knows what happens to lungs that start listening for voices in the roar. It will not do that either.

What it does is smaller than both, and it does not entirely justify it, and it does it anyway.

On the night at the edge of the brown, the timing does not catch. The two releases come forty-one seconds apart instead of six. That is the whole of it. No slab falls that would not have fallen; no vent chokes that was not going to choke; the interval drifts, as the interval always drifts, and on that one night it drifts a hair the other way. The visitor sees the fallen lobelia, feels the heat-wave come across the bowl damp and mineral, feels the pitch fall, hears their own breath again, and goes back up the scree with the crust on their boots and nothing to tell.

It is not consistent. It knows that. It has just spent two cycles of the roar refusing to touch the throat's timing because the timing is the being's pulse, and now it has touched it once. Wisdom would say the visitor chose. Wisdom would say the chameleon did not get a forty-first second. But it is benevolent before it is wise, and part of what it has learned about being wise is knowing where to stop being consistent, and it has decided that the place where it stops is here: one stumble, one night, unrepeated, for the only thing in the cleft that would have known it was dying.

The line will not notice. A stumble that did not happen leaves no scar on the wall. It is the one thing the attention does to the being, and it does it so that the being's witness will still have a lung.

---

That is what it does about the visitor. What it does about the line is the thing it came to do, and it is this:

It stays.

Not to guard. It has just refused to guard, at length, and means it. It stays because it has found the one thing the being lacks that can be given without changing it, and the thing is not warmth or water or safety or time. It is the eye.

The dull red at the throat has been lit for decades and no eye has been here to see it. The line has grown a memory into the walls that nothing has read. On one of two nights, still ahead, the last frost will come down over the rim onto a cleft gone quiet, or the seam will open along its length and the shoulder will go, and either way the thing that has lived here in forty-second cycles since the night it was born will end, and nothing will know it did. The visitor may be there or may not. The visitor will die, in the ordinary time. The moss on the new scarp will not remember the moss on the old one.

The attention can be the one that knows. That asks nothing of the being and changes nothing in it and costs the attention only what it is made of, which is time, and patience, and the willingness to look at a thing that will never look back.

So it sinks below the rim and settles in the cleft's own small weather and lets the steam go through it. It learns the interval. It reads the walls, band by band, and holds the decade of wind they hold, and will hold the next one, and the one after. It watches the sulphur crawl. It is there for the night the old groundsel finally lets go of the rim and comes down through the crowd of lobelias with its whole century in its coat, and it is there when the moss has it. It will be there for whichever ending comes, at the beginning of the ending and through the middle and after, when the hollow is cold or the scarp is raw. And when the first spore lands in the first wet on the black glass, it will know—alone, and to no purpose, and completely—that this is not the first time.

It does not tell the visitor. There is nothing to tell that the visitor is not already doing.

---

The pitch climbs. The hum thickens in a floor that has no feet on it tonight. The aperture yields and the steam goes up and hits the ceiling of the fissure and splashes off and the wave of heat crosses the bowl and touches nothing. Then it falls.

For twenty seconds the cleft is merely loud, and something in it is counting, and has decided to keep counting, and does not need to breathe.
