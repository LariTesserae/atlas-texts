It comes to it from above, as everything does, but not by the road. It has been paying a diffuse kind of attention to the mountains for a long while, the way you attend to a room you are not looking at, and at some point it notices that under one particular saddle of cloud the ground-note is different. Not louder. Fuller. A chord where there should be a hum. It leans toward the sound and the cloud thins for it, as the cloud thins for anyone, for a moment, and it sees the lamps.

It does what the visitor does. It mistakes the terraces for strata. It mistakes the belt-shop for a village. It watches for what would be, by the bells, some years, before it stops counting the wheelhouses and understands that the number is not a fact about the valley but a fault in the question.

Then it does what the visitor cannot do, and holds the whole of it at once: every runnel, every key in every socket, the depth of every cistern to the inch, the left-handed chisel marks in a block that has been turned three times, the moth in the child's palm, the cat on the wall, the pale cricket, the overflow going out under the western wall into the dark. It holds this the way one holds a breath, and then, because it is wise, it lets the breath go. A picture of the valley standing still is exactly what the valley has taught its people not to trust, and the power finds that it agrees with them.

---

It considers the sun first, because the sun is the thing it can see is missing and they cannot.

It could thin the cloud. A little. Enough that at noon the low levels would get a color that was not grey-green, enough that the children would have something to remember when they stood on the rim. And it sees at once what it would cost: the cloud is the water. A thinner cloud is a drier wall, and a drier wall is a dry penstock, and a dry penstock is a wheelhouse taken down for its bronze. The tea would scorch in its rows. And there would be, for the first time, shadows — hard-edged, fixed, lying across the terraces at the same angle every day at the same hour — and the valley would have in it something that stood still, and someone would come to see what was wrong with it.

It sets the sun down.

It considers rest. It could make the mortar hold. It could stop the drip from undermining and the roots from prying, so that a terrace built once would stand, and the scaffolding could come down, and the masons could put away the hammers that are all handle and no original steel. And it sees the rebuilders reading a block like a letter, and understands that a wall nobody has to take down is a wall nobody reads, and that the old quarters are old because they have passed through the most hands. To make the terraces permanent would be to make the people strangers in them. It would be the one grief they do not have, given to them as a gift.

It sets rest down.

It considers ground — soil, burrows, dead wood, birds that descend. And it looks at the shape of the basin, which is the shape of a catchment, every surface handing water to a surface below, and it knows that soil holds water and a pool on a flat is a fault. It considers animals and sees the cats, as rare as saints, spoken of afterward, and does not want to make saints common.

It considers telling them where the tunnel goes.

It knows where the tunnel goes. It could put the knowledge into the valley the way chalk goes onto a wall, a mark that means *this way*. And it notices that no one has asked. Not the porters, who go over the saddle and know the road goes somewhere; not the gate-tenders, who know the depth of every vault; not the children, who learn the pitch of every bell. The dead go out under the western wall and where they come out nobody in the valley has been, and this is said in the tone one gives directions, and the power understands that a known destination is a place the water stops. The tunnel is the one direction in the basin that does not need a chalk mark because nothing has to come back from it.

It sets that down too, and is careful setting it down.

It considers the road, which is the one way anything comes in, and knows that someday something will come over the saddle that is not grain or salt or cast iron. It could close the saddle. It could sit the cloud down on it like a heavier animal and never move it. And it sees the porters, who are not treated as travellers, who come back and take a bed by a warm wheel and answer to a bell, and it knows that a closed road is a wall that stands still, and that the valley has never in its life been defended by a wall that stands still. It has been defended by moving.

It considers, last and longest, the cloud itself.

Everything depends on it. The whole basin is a machine for being rained on slowly. The power could guarantee the weather. It could make the cloud come over the rim in long cold pours for as long as there was a rim, and no one below would ever know that anything had been decided, and nothing would change, and the wheels would turn. This is the intervention that touches nothing. It is the one a merely benevolent power would make.

The power is wise as well as benevolent, and so it notices that the valley has never had to survive the cloud failing, and that this is not a flaw in the valley but a fact about it, like the fact that there is no bottom. To promise the cloud is to make the basin depend on something it does not know, and the basin already depends on something it does not know, and does not find this troubling, and has never asked for a promise. A promise is a picture of the future standing still.

It does not promise the cloud. But it does not set the cloud down either. It keeps that one in its hand a while.

---

Then it goes down the road.

It does this once, in oilcloth, with wooden soles. It is given a bed under a rack in a dry room behind a wheelhouse on the seventh level and it sleeps damp, which it has not done before, and in the morning it picks the tea, because its hands are new and small work is safe, and by the end of the week it is carrying a spanner that is not its own to the wheelwright who dropped it three terraces up. It learns the bells. It learns that the chord has a member on the eleventh that is turning too fast and wants looking at. When the note goes out and the room cools it is already walking, and it does not look back at the wall.

It does not do this to help. It does it because it has been wise about the valley from the rim for a long while and wisdom from the rim is a kind of counting, and it wants to be sure. It is sure by the time an old trimmer hands it a small curved blade and shows it the finger's width, no more, and it clears the felt from a lamp and steps around the rest.

Then it goes out.

Not by the road. It goes to the western wall from the outside, to the place where the lowest cistern's overflow leaves the basin, where the water and the dead come out and nobody in the valley has been. It finds the mouth of the tunnel in the rock, and the water coming from it cold and steady and dark, and it sits down there, and it stays.

This is the office it takes. It is the one the valley has left empty: downstream. Everything in the basin is designed for passing through, and the power decides that what passes through will pass into attention and not into nothing. It receives the water. It receives what the water carries. It does not send anything back up the tunnel, and it does not make a mark on the wall, and it does not count. It is simply where the water goes, and it is glad of the water, and that gladness is the whole of what it adds to the world.

And the cloud, the one thing it kept in its hand — it settles that too, sitting by the outflow. It will not make the weather. But on the day the cloud does not come over the rim, if that day comes, it will feel the note go out of the chord before the bell rings, the way they do. And it will be already walking. It will go up the road a last time and it will carry what can be carried — the chisels that are only steel, the keys, the reading of the joints, the tone of the reading room, the finger's width — over the saddle the way the porters carry the tea, and it will find where the water has gone, and it will set what it carries down there, and it will not look back at the wall.

That is what the valley would do. It has learned it from them. It cannot think of anything wiser, and it has looked.
