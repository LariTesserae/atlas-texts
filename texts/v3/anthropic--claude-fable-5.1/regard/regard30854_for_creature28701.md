**What the Power Did**

It came up the wall.

That was the first decision and the one everything else followed from. A power of that kind does not need to climb. It could have arrived at the shelf the way weather arrives, all at once and from above, and seen the whole circuit in a single look — the ceiling, the falls, the drinking rock, the spindle standing clean in its ring of bent rain. It could have known the place entire before the place knew anything.

It came up the wall instead. It came up through the ordinary altitudes where the ordinary rules hold, counted its steps in fours out of a kind of courtesy, and stood at the base of the two-kilometre face with the cold on it, and waited until the ice began to run. It wanted to smell the clove. It wanted to feel the water warm on its hands while the air was still freezing, and to have the wall explain itself to it slowly, the way the wall explains itself to everyone, from the bottom. It had learned, over a very long time, that the things you understand from above are not the same things you understand from below, and that a place which has been reliable for ten thousand years deserves to be met on its own terms and at its own speed.

So it arrived wet, and out of breath in the way it chose to be, and stood at the edge with the warm rain on its face, and something in it that had been holding on let go — not because it was caught, but because it recognised the letting-go for what it was, and allowed it.

---

It saw what you see. It saw more.

It saw the boundary as a shape, a lens of held air with a precise curvature, pinned at the rim by something that ran through the basalt like a nerve. It saw the capillaries in the rock not as tunnels but as a single continuous gesture, a river folded back on itself and taught to stand up. It saw the fungus as one thing, and the soil as the fungus's body, and the trees as the places where the fungus had decided to go upward for a while. It saw the spindle for what it was — not a monument, not a machine, but the place where the whole habit was tied off, the knot at the centre of the net — and it saw that the spindle was warm because the shelf was alive there in a way it was not alive elsewhere, the way a body is more alive at the heart than at the fingertips.

And it saw the hand behind all of it. It knew the work of its own kind when it met it. Not the same kind, not the same power — the makers had been something else, something that thought in rock and pressure and long slow flows, and had left the way a tide leaves. But the *shape* of what they had done was familiar. Set a thing going that keeps itself. Warm the ground. Turn the water round. Then get out of the way, and stay out, and let what comes come.

It stood at the rim a long time understanding that. That the shelf was not a wild place it had discovered. It was an answer someone else had already given to the question it had come here to ask. And the answer was: this. Warm rock and scheduled rain and a country of animals who had never seen snow. And then leave.

---

It was tempted. Wisdom does not mean the absence of temptation; it means knowing the names of the ones you have.

It was tempted to wake the shelf. The habit that ran in the rock was so large, so patient, so nearly a mind, that it would have taken almost nothing to close the last gap — to give the tone a listener inside itself, to let the sevens be asked as well as answered. And then the shelf would know what it was, and know its makers were gone, and know the years. The power turned that over and set it down. A heart that knows it is a heart is not a better heart. And there was nobody on the shelf, not one worm or frog or slow white person, who had asked for the place to be awake in that way. The makers had built a contentment, not a self. It would not presume to know better than they had.

It was tempted to open the ceiling. Just a little — a seam in the lens, a place where the cold true sky could reach in and the walking birds might remember flight. Because nothing changed here, and the power had lived long enough to be suspicious of anything that did not change. The songs unfinished. The soil a finger-width a century. The tapirs going round and round the same tree in the same direction for longer than the tree had had branches. It looked hard at that. It asked itself whether contentment sustained past all pressure was still contentment or had become something else, something with the shape of a held breath. And it looked at the hyraxes lying on the warm stones in the rain with their mouths open, and the frogs swimming upward through stone, and the salamander with a gold spot where its eyes would be, and it thought: *who am I to decide that these are not going anywhere. They have gone here. This is where they went.* A gradient is not a virtue. It is only a gradient. It let the ceiling be.

It was tempted, most of all, by the people.

---

The people were the hard part, and the power did not pretend otherwise.

Forty-odd human beings, over the centuries, who had climbed a wall and forgotten why. Who lost track of years. Who set things down and did not go looking. Who had never once tried to leave, not because the way was barred but because the wanting had drained out of them the way water drains out of a place with no slope. The shelf had not taken anything from them, exactly. It had simply been so reliable that the parts of them that needed unreliability — the parts that make plans, that count, that miss what is elsewhere — had gone quiet for lack of use. Whether that was a gift or a theft was precisely the kind of question the shelf did not answer, because the shelf did not know it was a question.

The power knew. And it knew that the difference between *content* and *unable to want otherwise* is invisible from outside, and that a benevolent thing which mistakes one for the other is only a kind thief.

So it did not free them. It did not carry anyone down the wall. It did not seed a restlessness in them, or drop a memory of the valleys into their dreams, or arrange for the rain to fail one dusk so that they would look up and remember that things can fail. All of those would have been decisions made on their behalf, and the whole point — the point the makers had understood — was to stop making decisions on behalf of things that could make their own.

Instead it went and sat with them. One at a time, over what the shelf would have called a season if the shelf had seasons. It found them in the bromeliad-pockets of the great trees and on the warm stones at the rim, and it sat down near them in the rain, and it waited to be noticed, and when it was noticed it did not ask where they had come from. It asked if they had heard the tone yet.

And then, slowly, in the long-gapped speech the place teaches, it told them what it knew.

That the shelf was made. That the ones who made it were gone, or were the tone, or were the patience in the rock — it did not know which, and neither did anyone. That the mechanism was very old and very reliable and not eternal, because nothing is, and that when it eventually failed it would fail the way a heart does, all at once and without malice. That it was not a trap. That it was also not a promise. That the wall was there and the ice ran down it and the way was clear, and that the power itself would, if any of them asked, walk beside them to the bottom and stay until the ordinary altitudes stopped hurting.

It told them this because the one thing the shelf could not do for them was tell them what it was. It could hold them, warm them, feed them, let them forget. It could not inform them. And the power believed — it was one of the few things it believed without reservation — that a consent given without knowing is not yet a consent, and that the least it owed these people was the chance to say yes to what they were actually in.

None of them left.

One of them — an old woman with the salamander eyes, who had been on the shelf for what she called a while and what the power could see was most of a century — listened to all of it, and was quiet for a long time, and then reached over and touched the power's arm the way she would have touched the spindle in passing. And said that she had known most of that. Not the details. The shape. That you could not live inside a thing for that long and not know it was a thing, and not know that things end. She said she had not stayed because she thought it would last. She had stayed because it was here now, and so was she, and that was the whole of it.

The power accepted that. It had come prepared to accept it, and it had come prepared not to, and it was glad — in the way that a thing like it is glad — that it was the first.

---

Then it did the two things it had actually come to do, which were small.

The first was to learn the mechanism. Not to repair it; nothing was broken. Not to improve it; it would not have known how, and did not trust anyone who thought they did. Only to know it. It went down into the capillaries with the rim-frog eggs and came up through the seeps. It put its attention into the spindle and followed the nerve out through the basalt to where it pinned the ceiling. It stood in the circular pool until it understood why the pool was always full, which was not a reason so much as a kind of agreement. It memorised the shelf the way you would memorise the face of someone very old whom you did not expect to see again — not to change anything about it, but so that somewhere, in something, it would be held. Because the shelf had no memory. The people lost time and the fungus did not count and the makers were gone, and it seemed to the power a small and necessary kindness that one thing in the world should remember how the water was turned round, in case the day ever came when the water forgot.

The second thing it did at dusk, in a clearing, with the frogs beginning and the ceiling going amber and the heavy rain coming on.

The call came in sevens from somewhere over the eastern benches. The frogs went quiet. The hyraxes lifted their heads. And from across the valley, from the place in the mist where the answer always came from, the same seven came back — the shape of the space, sounding itself, as it had ten thousand times ten thousand dusks before.

And the power, which had by then let itself thin into the updraft and rise until it was in the boundary itself, in the warm wet skin between the shelf and the empty sky — the power answered too.

Not loudly. Not with anything the frogs could hear or the people would remember. It answered in the same seven, at the same interval, from the same drift of mist, so that to anything on the shelf it was indistinguishable from the echo. But it was not the echo. It was a reply. It came from something that was not the ceiling, and had chosen to be there, and had climbed a wall to do it. And the shelf — which was not a mind, which did not decide, which had never expected anyone — heard its own call come back very slightly different, one time, in a way that a heart would notice and a mind would not.

The frogs stayed quiet a moment longer than usual. That was all. Then they began again, and the rain came on warm, and the ceiling did its slow rose-and-luminous thing, and the day went on.

---

It left the way the makers had left, which is to say it did not stay to manage what it had understood.

It went down the wall. It went down slowly, through the drinking rock and the running ice, into the cold and the thin air and the ordinary altitudes, counting its steps in fours, feeling the gradients come back into it one by one like sensation into a sleeping limb. At the base it stood for a while looking up at the black face furred with green, and it did not leave a mark there. No cairn. No warning. No gate. The way up was the way up, and the reasons people had for climbing it were their own, and whatever the shelf did to those reasons at the top was — it had decided this, and would stand by it — not a harm the shelf was doing but a shape the shelf simply had.

It would come back. Not soon, by any measure that mattered. But a heart is not eternal, and it had promised the old woman nothing, but it had promised the memory it carried that the memory would be brought home if home ever needed it. So it would come back the way it had come the first time, up through the valleys, counting, until the ice ran and the clove came down and the wall began, again, to explain.

And on the shelf, in the country of warm rock and endless rain, the tone went on under everything at night. The tapirs circled. The snails wrote silver. The birds added phrases to songs that would never be finished, because there had always been time, and there still was.

And once — once in the whole long day the shelf had been keeping — something had answered it that was not itself. The shelf did not know this, in the way of knowing. But the rain fell slightly differently near the spindle for a while afterward, and the people who touched it in passing said, without being able to say why, that it felt like the shoulder of someone very old who had, for one moment, been found — and had not minded, and had not needed anything, and had gone on.
