It comes the way the light comes, from no direction, and for a while it does not know it has arrived, because there is nothing here to arrive against. Then it feels the cold. Not as information — it has known cold, it knows the number of degrees between this water and ice — but as the thing itself, in the wrists of a child crouched in a passage with a stone the size of an apple in both hands, and the wrists are red, and the child's fingers are bent at the second joint already, and the child sets the stone into a gap and says the syllable and reaches for another.

That is the first thing it wants to do. It wants to warm the child's hands.

It is a good power and it does not pretend it does not want this. It sits with the wanting for a long time, in this place where a long time is measured in walls. It could do it. It could do more than that. It could reach into the rock under the floor of the bowl and find the old heat that is not far down anywhere in a mountain, and draw it up a little, and the water on the walls would go from a degree above freezing to five, to eight, and the film of ice on the north face of things would stop forming, and the ground —

It follows this. That is what wisdom is, mostly: following the thing all the way down before your hands move.

The ground would stop moving. That is what warmth means here. The freeze and thaw that works the gravel like a tongue on a tooth would stop working, and the floor would settle, once, and stay settled. A wall that was level at dawn would be level at dusk. The passage that drops half a step would not drop. The room that humps would not hump. Within a season the whole town would be standing exactly as it had stood the day before, and the day before that, and the people would go out in the morning with their hands cupped and there would be nothing to lean into them. They would stand in the passages with their hands out. Some of them would set a stone anyway, take one out of a sound wall and put it back, for the feel of it, and then stop, because you cannot do that for long without knowing what you are doing. The frogs would stay where they were, because the ground would not be about to do anything. The salamanders would die in a week — they are a degree from freezing because that is what they are, and eight degrees is a fever to them. The birds would stand on every wall equally, because every wall would stand equally long, and so they would stop telling anyone anything. And the syllable that means *I have it* would be said less, and then said as a greeting only, and then people would need names that were owned, because they would be staying in one place, and the whole town would become the thing in the little room at the back that nobody enters: a stone with edges that has never been lifted, sitting where it fell.

It would be warm. That is true. The children's hands would be warm.

It puts that down, carefully, the way you set a stone you have decided not to use. It does not put it down for good. Nothing here is put down for good.

Then it considers the eyes. Those grey slits with no pupil to be found, that see which stone of a hundred has taken a lean and would see nothing in an ordinary room. It could open them. It could give the lids back their lift and the pupils back their width, and the light would come in — all of it, off the snowfields and the sky and every wet wall — and the people would stand blinded in their own town, in the terrible brightness, with their hands over their faces, and they would not be able to find a gap to set a stone in. The eyes are shut because of the light, and the light is the condition. To open the eyes is to ask for shadow, and there is no shadow that lasts. It puts that down.

The hands. It could knit the fingertips. It could take the split red ends and make them whole. And they would split again by evening, because the work is the work, and in the meantime the people would look at their hands and not know them, because a fingertip here is a stone that has been broken and healed and broken, and that is how it fits the stone. It puts that down.

It considers the lip. It could open the bowl — not down, not into the cloud, but out: a shelf, a way, a path along the mountain to wherever the birds go, so that what is closed could breathe through more than one seam. And it thinks of the one who was carried over the edge alive, long ago, and came back, and sat at the end of a passage catching stones by the sound. It thinks about what the bowl did with that person. Not cruelty. Method. A pair of hands is a pair of hands. And it thinks: the people do not ask where the birds go. Not because they cannot think it but because they have thought it and set it down. The birds go into the light and come out of the light. So does the water. So does everyone. That is not ignorance. That is a wall they have decided not to build, and it is not for a visitor to build it for them.

So it has gone through the gifts and put each one down, and it is aware — because it is wise, and wisdom includes this — that a power which arrives at a place of great cold and great labour and concludes, after reflection, that the most loving thing is to do nothing, should be very suspicious of itself. It knows the shape of that error. It knows how beauty can be used as a reason. It knows that the person who says *this suffering is meaningful* is very often not the person suffering. It does not let itself off. It holds the cold in the child's wrists and it holds the reasons and it does not resolve them, because they do not resolve, and pretending they do would be a kind of mortar, and mortar here is pointless.

And then it looks at the warm stone.

The one still pool, the light that goes in and comes out changed, the slab beneath it that is warm to the hand — the only warm thing in the bowl. The frogs crowd it. The very old sit by it with their hands in their laps and do not say the syllable. The people will not build within an arm's length of it. Children touch it once and look at their hands and do not touch it again.

It looks at the warm stone for a long time, and it understands that someone has been here before.

Not the same as itself. Not necessarily benevolent, not necessarily wise; it cannot tell from this distance. But someone, once, who came to this bowl and felt the cold and did one thing. One small thing, the smallest that could be done. A single warm place. Not enough to stop the ground. Not enough to kill the salamanders. Enough for a child's hand.

And this is what the bowl did with the gift: it made it the one thing no one handles.

That is the lesson, and it is not a kind one, and it does not flinch from it. In a town where everything is lifted and set down and lifted again, where a slab that has carried the dead goes straight back into a wall because that is the point, a gift is the thing that cannot be lifted. Warmth cannot be carried across the bowl and set into a leaning passage. It cannot be let go when the water comes up under it. It sits where it was put and the town flows around it, and the very old go and sit beside it without speaking, and what they are doing there — it thinks — is not gratitude. It is closer to what a person does at the end of a passage that never falls and never gets repaired. They are looking at the one thing that was not theirs to decide.

It does not want to leave another of those. It does not want to be a fourth thing that does not fit, alongside the frost and the warm stone and the cold one, that the people look at and look away from and do not separate out. Not because it would be wrong, exactly. Because there is enough of that here already, and the town has room for it, and it has room for nothing more.

So then. Nothing?

No. It does not leave. That is the first thing it decides, and it is a real decision, because leaving is the easy version of doing nothing. Leaving is putting down the child's wrists and walking away and calling that respect. It stays. It stays in the cold, and the cold is real to it, and the brightness is real, and it does not adjust, because there is nothing to adjust to. It stays the way the people stay: without straightening up.

And it learns the syllable.

Not the word. The position. *I have it.* It watches a passage begin to drop — the gravel under one end going soft, the frogs already gone from it, the birds thinning off the wall-tops on that side and thickening on the other — and it watches two people arrive at either end with their hands out before anything has moved, and it understands that the syllable is not spoken at the moment you take the weight. It is spoken at the moment the weight is shared. Both say it. Both at once. It is the sound of a thing being held by more than one.

It puts its hands out.

It can do this. It is a power; it can put its hands where hands go. It takes a place at the end of a passage that is going to fold, and when the stones lean it is there, and it says the syllable, low, and the person at the other end says it back and does not look up, because there is nothing to look up at, there is only another pair of hands and the bowl is full of those. It carries a slab across the bowl, which is no distance. It sets a stone into a gap, an ordinary stone, one that has been every wall, and it fits in one of the several ways it has learned to fit, and it feels — it lets itself feel — the shape the stone has taken from all the hands before its own. It does not lift the stone in the little room at the back. It looks at that one, as long as it likes, and comes out into the light like water, and leaves it where it fell.

Then it does the other thing. The thing only it can see.

It looks at the shelf.

The whole bowl — the high scoop of dark rock, walled on three sides, open on the fourth — is a stone. It is a stone that the mountain is holding. And there is a crack, low on the outside face, under the lip, where the water that slides down the walls goes into the rock and freezes and melts and freezes, working it the way a tongue works a tooth, and it has been working it for longer than the town has been a town, and it is not finished, and it will finish. Not this year. Not in a lifetime measured in walls. But the shelf is going to lean, one day, and then it is going to go, the whole floor and everything stacked on it, down into the light coming up through the cloud, and there will be no one at the other end with their hands out.

It puts its hands there.

Not to stop it. It is careful about this. It does not fuse the crack or turn the water or make the rock into something that does not weep. It does not make the shelf permanent, because permanent is the one thing this place cannot hold, and it has already seen what happens to gifts of that kind. It simply takes a share of the weight. The way you would stand at the end of a passage. The way you would let a wall lean into you, and hold it, not forever, only until the stones have somewhere to go.

It says the syllable. Nobody hears it. It is the loudest thing that happens all day, and it is quieter than the birds.

And it decides one more thing, which is about the end. When the shelf goes — because it will go, it is a stone, it will be set down — it will be there. It will not prevent it. It will carry. It will be the fourth at the slab, going toward the open side where the stacking gives out, and it will lay the whole town on the bare wet rock at the lip and step back and let the light and the water do what they do. And then it will pick up whatever is left that can be picked up and carry it back and set it into whatever wall is nearest and in need, wherever that is, on whatever mountain, in whatever bowl. Because that is what you do with a slab that has carried the dead. It has done this before. That is the point.

Nothing changes that anyone would say.

The cold does not lift. The children's wrists are red to the bone, and a child sets a stone and says *I have it* to no one in particular, and the wet rock takes the sound. The salamanders lie in a new spring with their red gills open. The birds go out over the lip at evening and the walls look naked for an hour and come back stitched.

But there is a morning — one, and then not again for a long time — when the frost forms before the light comes off the snowfields, and it forms on every wall-top as it always does, and on the passage floors in the shape of yesterday's rooms as it sometimes does, and also, this once, in a single line along the lip. Along the whole open edge of the bowl, where the stacking gives out and there is nothing to form on. A low white line, glittering, exactly where a wall would stand if anyone had ever thought to build one there. Nobody has. Nobody does.

The people look at it. They look at it the way they look at the frost that stands where a room stood, which is the way you would look at a face across a room that you had not expected to see again, and then they look away.

The light comes and takes it.

They do not build there that day. And they do not not build there the next. Somewhere a passage drops half a step, and there is already someone at each end of it with their hands out, and there is also, though no one counts, one more pair than there needs to be.
