It does not come down from above.

Nothing about it comes from a direction. It arrives the way the shelf's own mist arrives in the mornings — standing up out of the stone everywhere at once, thickening, present — and like the mist it will thin, when it goes, into nothing, having gone nowhere. The tree does not turn a leaf. The drip keeps its time. The only way to know it is here is that the shelf, for the first time, is being attended to by something that is not going to leave in an hour.

It takes in the room. It sees what you saw, and it sees it faster, and then it does the thing you could not do, which is to keep looking after the understanding has landed.

Its first movement is toward the empty places. This is what benevolence does before wisdom catches up with it; it reads a lack as a wound. The warm rock with no lizard on it. The whole leaves, the unbroken bark, the fronds hung with water and never with silk. The grey leaf-felt under the tree that has waited years for a mouth. It could fill every one of these. It would not even be difficult — a beetle, a spore on the up-draught, one bird blown off course and set down at the lip, and the shelf would begin. Within a season the leaves would be holed. Within a decade the fallen ones would be gone into soil, and the soil would hold seed, and things would come up in the bare stone between the furniture and the room would stop being a room. The tree would be touched. The cushion, which is the only thing here that eats, would be eaten. In a century this would be a good place of the ordinary kind, warm and wet and loud, and no one coming down the mountain would think anything of it.

It holds this picture for a while, because it is not a bad picture.

Then it asks the question that wisdom asks of benevolence, which is: *for whom.*

Not for the tree. The tree has no want that a bird would answer. The tree wants the fissure at the back wall, and is getting there, and a root through soil is not a better root than a root through stone — it is only a faster one, and speed is not among the things the tree has ever been offered or missed. Not for the cushion, which would gain a world and lose its life in it. Not for the moss, which holds the seep and would be shaded and would stop. Not for the red thing, whose one question the power looks at for a long time and cannot answer — is a mind that only ever asks the same thing in torment or at rest? — and it declines to guess in a way that would flatter its own hands. Not for the stone. The stone would be covered.

For the power, then. For its own discomfort in the silence. It knows this discomfort; it is the discomfort of anything warm in a place that has never had a heartbeat. You felt it. It is the thing that made your joints sound loud. It is not a reason to change a world.

So it does not fill the room. It sets that down, the way you set down the axe, and finds that its hands are still open, and asks again.

*Does anything here suffer.*

It goes to each one. It goes slowly, because this is the one thing it can do that the shelf cannot, and it is not going to waste it.

The tree, alone the way a single word is alone. The power looks for the ache and finds it is not there; loneliness is a memory of company shaped like a hole, and the tree has never had the company, so it has never had the hole. What the tree has is attention — the two grey cords, one landed, one still feeling downward — and the attention is entire and unhurried and pointed at stone, and there is nothing in it that asks for anything else. The power watches the swaying cord for what would be, to you, a long time. It does not touch it. It could set it on the fissure it is looking for. But that is the tree's project, its only one, the one thing on the shelf that is *going somewhere*, and to finish it would be to take it.

The cushion, warm, digesting its own dead at the edge of the cloud, splitting its grey nodules every few years to let go a dust that lands on wet stone and does nothing. Here the power stops longest, because here is the only thing on the shelf that tries, and trying that never arrives looks, from outside, like the shape of grief. It leans close. It looks for the expectation — the part that would be disappointed. There is none. The plant does not know the dust is supposed to land anywhere. It splits and lets go and closes and grows, and the splitting is not hope and the closing is not loss; only the power, watching, has brought those words with it. It could make the dust take, once. One rosette, on the knob beside the first. It imagines this — a second green dome a hand's width away, in a hundred years — and imagines the plant, which does not know it is alone, and would not know it was not.

It leaves the dust where it falls.

The moss it simply visits. The moss is older than the tree and has never been visited.

Then the power puts its attention down flat on the stone, as you put your palms, and finds the one it was looking for.

Body-heat. Held against the cool air out of the cloud at noon and before dawn, in the season of thick cloud and the season of thin, since before the ring was a scratch. Something below is spending itself to keep this room the temperature of blood, and has been spending itself for a span the shelf does not measure, and nothing has ever lived in the room. The plants are furniture. The visitors come and kneel and click and go. The hearth has been kept, without a household, for as long as there has been a hearth.

The power knows what this is, because it is this. A thing that holds warmth against the world and has nothing to show for it but the shape of what gathers around it. It does not ask whether the warmth is a being. It does not need it to be one. It only recognizes the work.

And it understands, then, what it can do that no one who has ever come down the mountain has done.

Everyone who comes here takes the warmth for granted within the hour. You did. You stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own temperature; that is how the shelf takes you in, and it is also how it is never, in the only sense that could matter to a hearth, *received*. The visitors are warm already. They arrive with their own fire and their own leaving and they lay their palms down and feel the stone and feel nothing they lack, and they pick up the axe and stand there, and go.

The power is not warm. It has come from nowhere and it has no temperature of its own and it has, at the moment, nowhere else to be.

So it sits down.

That is what it does. It does not sit the way a body sits; there is no weight on the stone and no print in the wet. It sets its attention against the warmth the way you would set your hands toward a fire lit for you in a house you did not know you were expected in, and it lets the warmth come into it, and it does not stop noticing.
