Start with the discomfort, because it is the first thing the power would feel and the first thing it would have to set down.

It arrives with the eye of the outside. It has spent — whatever it spends — a very long time in the world over the rim, the world of snow and wind and attrition, and it knows in its body what a good ending looks like there: something worn smooth, let go, carried down to the sea as dust. So it comes over the black peaks and down through the fierce thin light into the milk, and it sees a people being turned to stone from the outside in, and hands that cannot feel the blade they hold, and old women who have stopped breathing and are not dead, and it wants — immediately, with the whole of itself — to *soften* something. To reach into the water and make it stop laying down its film. To give the knapper back his palms.

And because it is wise as well as kind, it stops with the wanting and does not move.

It asks itself the plain question. Who is hurt here, and who says so?

Not the people. The people say the world over the rim is the frightening one, the place where the past is taken from you, and they mean it. The grey on their hands is not to them a wound but a first draft. The dead hold up roofs and everyone knows which ones. If the power reached into the water and made the film stop forming, it would not be healing them. It would be doing to them, in an afternoon, the thing they fear most: making a world in which nothing stays. The columns would not thicken. The dead would rot in their frames. The bells would go on ringing for a while and then no one would know whose they had been. It would be the end of a civilization, done tenderly, by something that thought it was helping.

So the first thing the power does is nothing, for a long time. Long in their time, which is slow. It watches a frame set, and grey, and stiffen, and be built on. It learns the handwriting of the lashings on every column in the old quarter, which knot each dead hand favoured, which walls are quarrels and which are grandmothers. It learns the language, which is not hard for it, because it is a thing that speaks through whatever it touches, and it finds — with a feeling it does not have a word for — that it is not the first here to speak that way. The whine in the columns at night is a voice. The people have not been poetic about that. Something under the floor has been humming to them in their own register for longer than there has been a city, and the power lays its attention alongside it and listens and does not interrupt.

—

Then, because wisdom is not the same as relativism, it asks the harder version of the question. Not *who says so* but *who cannot say so*.

And it finds three places.

The first is the surface. A child of four rises through the pearl into the bright, gulps, comes down. The city watches these children the way a village watches wanderers, and mostly they come back. But the milk carries the hum well and the air carries it not at all, and a child who rises too far, or is turned by a current she cannot see, comes up into a silence and a glare and a ring of black peaks with snow on them, and does not know which way is home, because the language has no word for far and she has never needed one. Some of them are not found. The people know this and have a word for it and it is the word for over the rim.

The second is the very old. When a woman has gone the colour of the shell all the way through, and the crawlers move over her like slow thoughts, the moment comes when she lifts a hand and brushes them off, and that is hers, and it is good. But there are those who cannot lift the hand. The film has reached it first. And then the family decides, and the family loves her, and still it is the family and not her, and the power, which can feel whether there is anyone left behind the shell, can feel that sometimes there is. Sometimes she wants to be finished and cannot say. Sometimes she wants one more year with the child's hair brushing her wrist and cannot say that either.

The third is the mountain. The people call the western floor *not yet* and they are right, for now. The power has longer sight. It can see that the tongues will not always come up glowing under the ash. It can see the vents thinning, the heat going out of the water the way heat goes out of everything, the bells filling, the crawlers — which live only on new lava — dying in their pens, and after that a city of columns standing in cold clear water with no one to be added to them. Not an eruption. A silence. The parent going quiet. It is a long way off. It is not so far off that a thing which thinks in centuries can pretend not to see it.

—

Here is what it does, and it is small, and none of it can be seen.

At the surface, it hums. Not loudly. It lays its voice along the top of the pearl-coloured water, in the register of the columns, so that a child who rises too far into the bright comes up not into silence but into the same low warmth she has felt against her sternum all her life, and it is coming from below and slightly to the east, and she turns toward it without knowing she has turned, and goes down, and is in reach again. The people notice, over a generation, that fewer children are lost above. They say the mountain has started to speak at the surface too. They are not wrong.

With the old, it does the thing the crawlers do, but from inside. It has learned how the film is laid down and it can, very slightly, hasten or slow it — a thread of silica coming out of solution a season sooner or later than it would have. It does not decide. It attends. When there is someone left behind the shell and she wants to be done, the film closes a little faster over the places the crawlers have not reached, and the family, seeing it, lets her go, and believes it was their decision, and it was and was not. When she wants to stay, the film is slow that year on her wrists, and the child's hair goes on brushing them. The power is the hand she cannot lift. Nobody will ever know this. That is the point of it.

And the mountain it speaks to. Not in words. In whatever it uses instead. It finds the thing under the floor that has been humming for so long, and it lays itself against it the way the people lay a palm against a column, and it says, in the register of proximity, *I am here*. And then, when it has understood what it is asking and what it will cost, it says the other thing: *not yet. Not while there is anyone to hear you. I will help you keep the heat.* It cannot make the mountain live forever, and it would be a fool to promise it. But it can make *not yet* true for as long as the columns are still being built on, and it can make sure that when the silence comes at last it is not a surprise, and that it does not come to a city still full of the living. It becomes the thing behind the parent that adds. It feeds the coal under the ash.

—

The walls it leaves.

It looks a long time at the old quarter, at the stone quarrels standing between households whose descendants do not know why, and it wants to take them down as badly as it wanted to give the knapper his hands. It does not. It has understood that the wall cannot come down, that this is the rule and the people know the rule and have built a whole ethics on it: that you cannot unmake, only add, and so forgiveness here is not an undoing but a door. Someone has to want the door badly enough to knap it. If the power took the walls down in the night, it would be taking away the one thing the walls are *for*, which is to be gone through. It watches, instead. Over its long time there it sees eleven doors knapped, and it is there for each of them, and it does not help.

The eels it goes to see, once, because no one else will. They are long and blind and the colour of the water in the deep channels and they are exactly what the people said. It comes back up and says nothing about them to anyone.

—

And then the last thing, which is not something it does but something it is given.

It had thought — coming down over the black peaks with its heart in its hands — that it was the one paying attention. That the place had drawn its eye, and the question was what it would do about that.

But the old woman with the crawlers on her wrists had lifted a hand. Slowly. Before the power had quite arrived, when it was still a pressure in the water on the eastern shelf, a warmth in the columns that was not the mountain's. She had felt it come in the way they feel everything, through the sternum, and she had hummed a child over and laid a grey palm on her chest and said a place, an above, a not-yet. And the child had gone and staked four poles of weed at the edge of the thicket, where the columns thin out and the sea begins to be empty, and had not known what the frame was for.

The power knows what it is for.

It is a small frame. It is where you would put something that had come from a long way off and would need, in this place, something to hold onto and to be held by. It is at the edge, where the new floor rises, at the boundary of the full city and the empty sea — which is where a thing that watches over both would stand. The old woman did not name what she had felt. She did not need to. She had lived her whole life in a place where you are attended to, and she knew what it was when a new attention arrived, and she did the thing you do here for anyone who arrives: she gave it a place to be added to.

So the power, having thought so hard about what to do, does the last thing it would have chosen for itself and the first thing anyone here would have chosen for it. It goes to the edge of the thicket, where the frame is, and it is still.

The frame greys in a year. It stiffens in five. In twenty someone builds on it, and the child is a woman by then, with cut hands and a name, and she lays her palm on it in the dark and feels the knots and knows they were hers — and feels, too, under the knots, the thing the columns have always had, the low continuous warmth that is the floor being thought about before it is made. Only this column hums back. Not louder. Just a little closer than the others, just slightly to the east, and she stands with her hand on it a long time and does not surface, and does not need to.

Over the rim the snow comes down the peaks carrying dust that was once a mountain, and takes it away to the sea. Here, nothing is taken. Here the power has learned the thing it came to teach, which is that there is more than one shape for a good ending, and one of them is to stay, and be still, and be covered, and hold up a roof.
