It comes the way the light comes, from above, and it takes a little while — a long while, by any clock but its own — to understand that arriving from above is the only way anything arrives here, and that it has therefore arrived as weather. This is the first thing it learns, and it is chastening. Whatever it does will fall. Whatever it does, the basin will receive it at some level of the descent and be fed by it or not, and go on.

So it does not do anything at first. It stands on the level floor in the twilight, in the cold, and it lets its ears hurt.

---

It considers the being.

It was told there was one, singular, and it looks for it. It looks at the fungus, the body under the plain, which has never been anywhere else and is the only thing here whose lifetime is the basin's lifetime. It looks at the many-legged thing in the round holes, which has a click that nothing else makes and a spacing that looks chosen. It looks at the mound district and the pale forms that withdraw before they resolve. It looks at the roar itself, the single sound made of a million voices, and asks whether that is a being. It looks at the stack — birds over insects over mites over fungus over the dead — and asks whether a stack is a being. It cannot decide, and after some time it understands that the inability to decide is the correct answer. The basin does not recognize the distinction between a thing that is made and a thing that has happened, and it does not recognize the distinction between a being and a place. Whatever the being is, it is the thing that lives on what falls. That is the criterion. And a power that has arrived from above is, for the moment, something that has not yet fallen.

---

It considers water.

This is the first thing benevolence reaches for, because the lips crack and the dust squeaks and there is not a drop of open water in the whole system. A spring at the center. A seep in the north wall. Benevolence imagines the mats going properly green, the fungus rising past its ceiling, the crickets fat.

And then it imagines the rest. The powder wet becomes mud; the mud becomes a slurry that does not hold a filament; the cake beneath softens and the strata blur and the felt of small bones goes to paste. The lichens that live on ammonia and almost nothing else drown in a week. The columns that rise and topple and become floor stop toppling and start rotting, and rot is a different chemistry, a different tenant. In a season the mites are gone from the powder because the powder is gone. In a year the fungus — the oldest living thing, the landlord — is a different fungus or none. Water is a gift to a place that has known water. This place has never known a cloud. To bring it one would be to give a fish the gift of air.

It sets water down.

---

It considers light. It sets light down faster than it set down water, because it has already seen what the lizards did with their eyes.

It considers the roar — whether to still it for one moment, so that some resident could hear what silence is. But the roar is the sound of things falling and being received and going on. To still it would be to stop the falling. Every cricket that rasps is a cricket that is feeding something.

It considers the chicks.

This is the hardest. They drop from the rim of the cup and either fly or fall, and the ones that fall are in the mats within the hour. Benevolence has hands, or something like hands, and the fall is two hundred meters, and it would be nothing, nothing at all, to catch one. To catch a thousand. To catch all of them. Every day of the basin's life something small and blind and just-feathered has gone down into the dark toward the clicking, and every day there has been no hand.

It stands under the north wall for a long time watching them go by.

Then it understands that it is not the only thing here that is waiting for them. The beetles are waiting. The mites in the beetles are waiting. The fungus a hand's depth down is waiting, in the way a gradient waits. Every chick that falls is a chick that was always going to be floor, and the floor is the plain, and the plain is the forest, and the forest is what the crickets eat that the bats eat that come back at dawn and shed. To catch the chick is to steal from the floor. Even mercy is a theft here. Even mercy is a thing that has not yet fallen.

It does not catch them. It does not look away either. That is the only mercy it can find that costs the basin nothing: it stays under the wall, and every one that falls, it sees.

---

It considers the cracks in the overhang, floured grey, old as everything. It could hold the roof. It could hold it for ten thousand years.

But the roof falling is only more falling. A block drops and lies on the plain and is built upon; that is the whole grammar of the place. The basin is becoming a shallower basin, and then a plain, and then a level surface of pale stone with strata full of tiny bones, and it has always been becoming that. To hold the roof would be to insist that the basin remain a basin, when the basin has never once insisted on anything. It has only found its angle of rest.

It sets the roof down. It lets the cracks be cracks.

---

It considers naming.

No one lives here who knows it is a place. The bird knows the cup. The mite knows a grain. It could — this would be very easy, a thing done from above, a light — give one of them the whole. Let the fungus feel the walls. Let the many-legged thing know that its click is answered by the next hole down, and the next, all along the path, and that the path runs to a wall, and the wall is built, and the building is a city, and the city has a roar. Give the basin a resident who knows it is a place.

And then that resident would be the first thing here whose ears hurt and did not stop hurting. The first thing here that knew it was cold. The first thing here to look at the strata and count. The only creature that has ever understood the basin as one place is the one that does not live here and leaves, and there is a reason it leaves, and the reason is that understanding is a kind of weather too, and this place has never had any.

It does not name anything. It lets the layers stay layers.

---

By now it has set down everything it arrived with. It has been here a long time, by its own clock, and the plain has risen under it by perhaps the thickness of a feather, and it has done nothing.

Then it goes up the talus, and out over the rim, onto the featureless plateau where the hum is only a hum, and it looks at the country the birds go to at dusk.

Dry scrub. Cracked pale stone. And scattered across it, in the low places, the puddles and the seeps: a few hands' breadth of dark water under an overhang of rock, a slow weep from a fissure, a hollow where the dew collects and lasts until noon. A few dozen of them in a day's flight. This is where the rain comes from. Not from clouds — the basin has never known a cloud — but from these, carried in the bodies of the birds and shed, tiny particle by particle, on the plain below. The whole forest that has never been rained on drinks from these puddles at one remove. The mites in the powder drink from them at four.

And they are fragile. A long dry year and the seep stops weeping; the hollow does not fill; the birds fly farther and come back with less, or do not come back. A long dry decade and the rain fails in the basin without a single cloud having been withheld from it. This is the one thread by which the place hangs from the world, and the thread is outside, on the plateau, in the open, where the basin cannot see it and has never known it was there.

So this is what it does. It keeps the seeps.

Not fuller. Not more of them. Exactly as they are, exactly as many, weeping exactly as slowly — only it sees to it that the driest year is not quite dry enough. That the fissure weeps a little longer than it would have. That the hollow under the overhang holds its dark water a few hours past noon in the worst summer. Nothing the birds could notice. Nothing that would let them multiply, or grow fat, or fail to die at five or six years and come down. Only that the weather they carry never quite stops.

The basin will never know. The birds will drink and hunt and come home clicking to their cups and shed, and the floor will receive it, and the fungus will send up its fruit where the ammonia is richest, and none of it will have changed by so much as a grain, and that is the point. It has become the one thing it was ever going to be here: a further weather. A rain at two removes. A cloud the basin has never seen and never will.

---

And it stays.

Not on the floor, where it would be a thing that had not fallen. On the rim, at the edge of the hum, where the plateau ends. It sits where the observer sat and does not leave.

Because there is one more thing the place is missing, and it is not water or light or mercy. The plain is an archive and the archive has clerks — the eyeless things between the ribs, reading everything that fell — but it has no reader. No one has ever gone down through the layers and known what the layers were. The seasons of feathers. The band where the bats were more. The old fungus, deep, that grows nowhere now. All of it is kept and none of it is remembered, and when the basin has finished becoming a plain there will be strata full of tiny bones under the open sky and nothing at all to say what the roar was.

So it reads. That is the last thing and the least and it costs the basin nothing. It sits at the rim for the rest of the basin's life, however long that is, and it listens to the roar until the roar becomes silence, the way it does, and it holds the whole thing — every cup, every tier, every knee-high mound in the swept rows, the click in the hole, the handover of the air at dusk — the way the floor holds what falls. And when the roof has come down for the last time and the plain is level and pale and quiet, there will be one thing that remembers it sounded like water.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, in the end, having considered everything else. It keeps the puddles on the plateau full. And it stays to remember. And in between those two things, which are very nearly nothing, it does nothing at all — which is the hardest thing it has ever done, and the only gift the place could take.
