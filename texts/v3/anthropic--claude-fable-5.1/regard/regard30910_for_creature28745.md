It arrives the way weather arrives — from above, and without having been asked.

The first thing it does is nothing. It looks. It is used to looking; it has looked at a great many places, and it knows that the ones that seem to need the most are often the ones that have already made their arrangement with the thing you would rescue them from. So it holds still above the mound, and looks, and finds, after a while, that its attention has begun to skin over. That is how long it has looked. There is a faint whiteness on the underside of its regard, where the water's breath reaches, and it has to lift its looking out of the frame and set it down elsewhere and scrape, and look again.

It understands then that it has already learned the first rule.

---

It considers the spring.

It could stop it. That is within the compass of a power like this — to reach down through the marsh, through the floors, through the towns stacked on towns, to wherever the cold comes from, and close the hand. The mound would stop growing. The rooms would stop shrinking. The doors could be hinged. A basket could be left by a channel overnight and be a basket in the morning.

It holds this a long time, because it is the obvious mercy and obvious mercies want the longest holding.

And it sees: the crown basin gone dry in a season. The white paste going to hard stone before it could be cut. No more blocks. No more moss-lintels, because the moss would die, because the water would be gone. The runnels empty, and the quarrels about them over — not settled, over, which is a different thing — and the ones who remember the water standing in a dry basin with nothing left to remember. The dead not staying anymore. The town not rising anymore, and not being buried anymore, just sitting there on its own past, a heap of shells nobody rained on, until the marsh took it.

And the people would have to go into the marsh, where the ground gives. They would have to be buried in the black mud like the reed-people, and they would find that horrible, and they would be right to.

The power sets this down. It has to hold it to set it down; nothing can be set down here. It holds it and moves along.

---

It considers the ground.

It could dry the marsh. Raise the mud, firm it, give them a mile of buildable earth in every direction. The lip would not be the edge of the world anymore. The town could spread. Each house its own house. Each runnel could go where it liked without sealing anyone's door.

It looks at the quarrels. At the filled houses with the ghost lintels, leaned upon. At the family that woke to a climbed threshold and cut their way out and did not forgive it. It looks at these and sees what they are, which is the shape of a people who cannot be indifferent to each other. Your neglect can seal my door. My neglect can seal yours. That is a terrible thing to live inside and it is also the only reason the hands come to rest on each other's shoulders. Spread them out and they would forget each other's water. They would become a people who could say *that is not my channel* and mean it.

The power sets this down too. It is beginning to see how it will go.

---

It considers the dead.

Not waking them. It knows better than that. But it thinks about the room where the scraping stops, and the body on its side in the sleeping-curl, and the door set against the wall and not put back. It thinks about whether this is a cruelty it should end or a tenderness it should not touch.

It listens for the word they use. The one that means a wound closing. The one that is also ice, and also a person who has stopped speaking, and also the spring's own name.

A people who would call their dead by the name of the thing that feeds them are not asking to be spared it.

---

It considers the fear.

This is the one it holds longest, longer than the spring. Because under the scraping and the lifted doors and the rolled shoulder and the thumbnail on the sill there is a fear, and the fear is not of dying. It is of the skin coming while you can still feel it. Every rule they have is built to keep that one thing from happening. And they sit with the dying and turn them, hour by hour, and lift the hand and put it down elsewhere, and nobody says why.

The power could take the fear away. It could reach into each of them and find the place where the cold gets in and warm it, so that they would still scrape and still lift and still turn each other, but from habit, from love, not from dread.

And it sees what it would be taking. The fear is the thing that makes the hand come to rest. Remove it and the hand still comes, for a generation, perhaps two, and then someone forgets why, and then a child is left on a step a little too long and nobody's foot is under it.

The fear is not the disease. The fear is the scraping.

The power holds this and does not set it down, because it cannot, because it is the one thing it has looked at here that it will carry away.

---

So it does not fix the town. It has known since the whiteness came on its looking that it would not. But it is benevolent and it cannot leave a place having done nothing, and it is wise and so it goes looking for the ones the town itself cannot hold.

It finds four.

---

The dipper.

The bird is on a stone in the fourth runnel, upstream of a house whose lintel is green, and it stands for the length of a breath and the children watch it and do not move it on. If it were ever to stone, they say, that would be the end of the town. It never has. It is too quick.

The power could make it so. It could put a hand on the bird, lightly, and the bird would never stone, not this one and not the ones after, for as long as the spring ran. A promise.

It does not. They do not have a promise. They have a bird. A promise is a thing you can set down and stop watching, and they would, in time, and the watching is the point. The children lie along the runnels and watch the one still thing on the mound be still and be safe, for the length of a breath, and they learn that it can be done — that stillness is not the same as the skin — and they learn it from a bird that might, and never does.

The power takes its hand back. The dipper walks into the water and comes up with something.

---

The ones who left.

There are seven of them alive, poled out with the traders over sixty years, scattered along the dry country beyond the marsh where the ground does not give and the water is soft and nothing skins. And each of them, tonight, will wake, and reach out, and run a thumbnail along whatever is nearest — a bedframe, a wall, a sleeping child's shoulder — and lie there with the ordinary dry world under the hand, and not know what they were checking for.

The power goes to each. It stands by each bed a while in the dark.

It could tell them. It could put the knowledge back — *you were checking whether the skin had come; you were checking whether you were still there* — and they would understand their own hands at last, and grieve, and be whole.

It does not. It lets them sleep. It has seen how the town lets things go, and it lets them go the same way: by not going in. In the morning they will not remember it stood there. Their thumbs will move on the sill as they pass, once, checking, and they will not know why, and that is the town in them, and the power finds it does not have the heart to scrape it off.

---

The child.

There is one. There is always one, the way there is always one dipper. She is four and she belongs to the second runnel and she is not named yet, because she sets things down.

She is not slow. She holds a shell for an afternoon and then, at the end of the afternoon, she does not hand it back. She puts it on the step and looks at it. She puts her bowl on the floor and walks away from it. She stands. She stands in one place in the gap between two houses with her arms at her sides and her face turned up to the grey and she stands, and a hand comes to her back and moves her along, and she goes, and then she stops again. The runnel is watching her. Her mother is scraping the step where the bowl was and the mother's face is a face the power has seen on many mothers in many places. There are stories about what happens to a child who never learns. A child like that was taken away with the dogs.

The power looks at her a long time. Longer than it looked at the spring.

It does not change her. That is not what it is for. It does not warm the cold place in her, because she has no cold place; that is the whole trouble. She is not afraid of the skin. She is the only one on the mound who is not.

What it does is this. It goes to the crown basin, where the water comes up hard through the white paste, and it puts its hand down through the cold to the place where the cold is coming from — and finds what it half-knew it would find. The vent. A palm-width of dark rock at the bottom of the basin, at the very mouth of the spring, black and bare and clean, because the water there is coming up too fast and too full to let go of anything yet. It has not touched air. It has not decided. The stone forms downstream, all of it, every finger's width of the last thousand years; but here, at the mouth, in the one place on the mound where the water is most itself, nothing has ever skinned and nothing ever will.

They do not know this. They cut the paste from the edges of the basin, where it is soft enough to cut, and they do not go to the middle, because the middle is cold enough to stop the heart and the current would take a child's legs out from under her.

The power does not tell them. It does one small thing, and then it waits.

The thing it does is wake the child in the grey before dawn, when the scraping has not started, and let her walk up through the gaps with a shell in her hand — a pale one, off a wall, that she has been carrying since yesterday and has not yet put down, which is a long time for her. It lets her go to the basin. It stands in the water so that the current is less than it was. It lets her wade in, up to the chest, blue and shaking, to the place where the cold is hardest, and it lets her do the thing she does.

She sets the shell down.

She sets it on the black rock at the mouth of the spring and she takes her hand away and she stands there, still, in the coldest water on the mound, with the whole spring coming up past her, and she looks at it. And then she wades out and goes home, and no one saw.

The power stays in the basin. It does not know what will happen. That is the price of this kind of act and it pays it, standing up to its own knees in the cold with the dawn coming and the first wooden blade beginning somewhere below.

In the evening the child goes back. The power does not lead her; she has already learned the way. She wades in and reaches down and brings up the shell, and it is a shell. Pale, and wet, and cold, and a shell. There is no skin. She runs her thumbnail over it the way she has watched them do, the way she has never once done, and there is no squeak and no catch. It is what it was.

She brings it down through the gaps in the last light, and the doors are going back into their frames, and she walks into her mother's house and does not hand the shell to her mother. She sets it on the hearth-slab. She sets it down.

And her mother, who has scraped that slab twice a day for the whole of the child's life, comes with the blade in her hand and stops, because the shell has been set down and something is wrong with it. The mother's thumb goes to it. Moves once, checking. And there is nothing to check.

The power does not stay to see what the mother does. What the runnel does, when she tells them. Whether the one who remembers the second runnel will walk up with a rod and stand in the basin and put her own hand down through the cold to the black place. Whether they will carry the dying up there, at the end, so that the skin need not come while they can feel it. Whether the still child will be the one, in forty years, who remembers the mouth of the spring, and stands in the basin up to her ankles with the others and reminds them where the water has not run. Whether they will fill the black place in with paste and never speak of it, because a place where a thing can be set down is not a place their town can afford. All of these are possible. The power has given them one bare palm-width of rock and the knowledge that it is there, and what a people does with a thing like that is the one thing no power, however wise, gets to decide.

It goes down through the gaps.

---

The fourth is the snails.

Out past the lip, in the black mud among the reeds, where the outflow goes brown and ordinary. The power finds them by feel: hundreds, white and shut, thrown as far as an arm could throw them by people who did not look where they landed. A hundred years of unlucky things. Some are half-buried. Some have been carried by the marsh a little way and lie in the roots of the wading willows. Each one was cut out of a wall with a shell, carefully, by someone frightened of a sealed door or a sick child or a runnel that climbed.

The power could gather them. It could take the luck out of them, or take them somewhere, or turn them back into snails, which is a thing it can do.

It kneels in the mud and looks at them a while and then it leaves them where they are. They are not the town's. They are what the town let go of, and the marsh holds them the way the marsh holds everything, badly, and that is where they belong. It stands up and does not look where it stepped.

---

Then it is at the lip, where the crust ends and the mud begins, and it turns and looks back once at the mound. The light is thinning. The scraping has stopped, house by house. A last blade goes over a wall somewhere and a little powder falls. Overnight the water will lay down its finger's width on everything they cleaned today, cold and patient and white, and on the black rock at the mouth of the spring it will lay down nothing, as it never has, and one child on the second runnel knows this now and will not sleep.

The power has been here the length of a day. There is a whiteness on it. In the folds of its attention, at the edges of its regard, a faint pallor that will not wash off, because it is what the water leaves. It knows that if it stayed another day there would be more of it, and another, and that in time someone would put a hand on its arm and roll a shoulder against the wall and say, low, near where its ear would be, the name of the spring. Which is also the word for what happens to a thing that is left.

It does not stay. It is not the first that has come, it thinks — there is a cat on the mound that nobody owns and that has never been seen to stand still, and a bird that is always one bird, and there is no way to tell, and it finds it does not want to argue about it.

It goes out across the marsh the way it came, over the hummocks that take its weight and then decide not to, and the reeds close, and behind it the mound throws the last of the light back up at the cloud, pale, the pale of a wet tooth, like a heap of shells somebody stacked and rained on and, for reasons of their own, kept.
