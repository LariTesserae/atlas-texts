**What the power does**

It does not come up the rail. It does not come up on the heat, circling, the way the birds do. It comes the way water comes to the iron: it is not there, and the air is exactly cold enough and exactly warm enough, and then it is.

The first thing it does is nothing. This should be said plainly, because a power that arrives and acts has understood nothing about a place where a ladle takes a day to cross a courtyard. It stands on the western parapet with the cistern-keepers at dusk and looks at the cloud-tops. It learns the shuffle. It learns to hold its weight back until the front foot has reported. It learns the signs — heat low, cold high, the Throat a hand held still — and it learns them from the children, because the children are the only ones who will teach a stranger without first asking which district it has a position in. This takes years. It has years.

---

What it sees, that they cannot.

It sees the sky's rate. The old people are right: it is darker. The power counts, the way the engineers count bands, and arrives at a number of generations — not many — after which the summit air will no longer hold a person's breath even indoors, near the warm floor, among those they trust. It does not tell anyone this number. It writes it nowhere yet. It carries it the way a caster carries the knowledge that the pour will go in regardless.

It goes down the deepest shaft, past the last band the lamps can find, to the place where the glass is less like glass, and it puts its hand on the cold. It stands there a long time. What it learns is not a secret and would not help: that there was a before. That the mountain was once only a mountain, and the heat came up, and the heat was so slow and so patient that whatever first came here to sit in it did not think of leaving, and by the time it might have thought of leaving it had built a floor, and then a floor on the floor. That is all. There is no story under the story. The power finds this a relief and also very sad, in the way it is sad to learn that a thing you loved was never a mystery, only old.

It goes to the lambs.

It lies down on the eastern shoulder beside one, on the cool fill, and stays there through a night and a day, and the lamb does not move and the power does not move, and whatever the lamb does instead of seeing, it does, in the direction of the power, and the power lets it. What the power learns is that the keepers are wrong and the engineers are wrong and the truth is smaller than either and kinder than both. The lambs are not making the mountain. The lambs are not nothing. The lambs are what came up on the heat, long before the rail, long before the city, and could not get back down, and lay against the warm seams, and were still here when the first people arrived — and the first people, who had no schedule yet and no bands and no reason, moved them out of the way of the first pour. And have gone on moving them. For no reason anyone can name. Heavier than they look. Days of work. The pour waits.

The power decides that this — not the Throat, not the figures, not the cold at the bottom — is the thing about the city that matters, and that if it does anything at all, it must do nothing that would cost the city that.

---

It goes to the Kilnwards and lies on its belly on the clear street at noon, with the children.

Two of the figures are what the engineers say. A beam's caught shadow. The shape a bubble takes.

One is not.

The power does not tell the children this, and it does not tell the engineers, and it does not tell the keepers, who would only be confirmed, which is not the same as helped. It is too late to be anything but too late. There is no pour that can be un-poured. What the power does instead is go down the nearest shaft to the depth of the small one, where a hand's width of black glass is as close as the shafts allow, and it stays there. Not a day. Not a season. It stays a year, and then it stays another, and it sits with its back against the glass in the lamp-dark and does not do anything, because there is nothing to do and because the small one was left late and no one sat with them and someone should.

The children on the clear street above, during those years, see a fourth figure, and it moves, and they draw it on the frosted edge of the city with nails, and their parents say it is a trick of the light. The power lets them say so. When it finally climbs back up the shaft it does it slowly, and the fourth figure is gone from the glass, and the children who saw it grow up and are not quite sure, later, that they did.

---

What it considers, and does not do.

It does not stop the Throat. It could. It stands in the Throat-house with the schedulers, watching the pin move along the spiral, and it understands that it could close the heat like a hand closing, and the pour would end, and the mountain would have a top, and the city would stand on a finished thing — and the city would die of that within a generation, not of cold, not of the sky, but of having nothing left to be. The schedule is not a burden these people carry. It is the shape of them. You do not cure a person of their shape.

It does not thicken the air. It could do that too, or something like it. But a city that lives at the height these people live at, and speaks the way they speak, and holds its breath without meaning to, is a city that has agreed with the sky about something, and the power will not make an agreement void without asking, and there is no one to ask, because no one alive can imagine the question.

It does not tell them about the lambs, or the cold, or the small one. It carries these. The wise carry more than they give, and the power is, among other things, tired in a way it did not expect to be, and glad of the weight.

---

What it does.

Two things, and both of them slow.

The first is the Throat. The power does not close it. It puts its hand very near the rate — not on; near — and it leans, the way a person leans on a door to see whether it is latched. And the rate, which has not varied in living memory, begins to vary. Not in this lifetime. Not in the next. A hair per generation, less than the drip, less than the tick of glass at nightfall. The Throat will go on swelling for longer than anyone here can count, and then it will swell a little less, and less, and one day — far past the number the power carries, far past the day the sky would have closed — it will stop. The mountain will have a top. The city will be standing on it.

The power cannot spare them the ending. It can only choose which one. It chooses the one that gives them time to see it coming, and it does this in the only way it knows will be honest: it writes the slowing into the bands. From now on, each year's pour goes in a hair thinner than the last, and the engineers going down with their lamps will find, in time, a wall that reads like a sentence winding down — thick, thick, thick, less, less — and they will read it the way they read everything, as scripture, and they will bring it to the schedulers, and the schedulers will do what schedulers do. They will cut a spiral with an end on it. The city has a schedule for everything except itself. Now it will have one for that too, in its own script, in its own material, found rather than given. The power thinks this is the most it is allowed.

The second thing takes longer to decide than the first.

Nothing grows. This was the first thing the body noticed and the last the mind accepted, and the power, walking the city for years, has come to understand that "nothing grows" is not only a condition here but a kind of load-bearing wall — that the green in the contaminated seams is the only green, and the country under the cloud is the only country, and the rail goes down into a place from which nothing comes back the same, and the children's sign for the birds is the sign for going where you did not want to go. The whole city stands on the belief that down is loss and up is home and life is elsewhere. The power could leave that wall alone. It is not cruel, exactly. It is just very heavy.

On the eastern shoulder, on lamb-ground, on a fill that is fifty years cool and eaten matte where a lamb once lay, the power sets down something the size of the last joint of a child's finger. It is grey. It is nearly the color of the fill. It will take thirty years to be the size of a hand, and no one will notice it until then, and when someone notices it will be a keeper moving a cistern before the drip, and she will stop, and crouch, and put her face close to it, and not touch it, and not know what it is, because there is no word for it here, and there is no sign.

She will tell the children. That is what keepers do. The children will come and lie on their bellies and look at it the way they look into the glass, and someone will say it came up on the heat, like the lambs, like the birds, and stayed. And someone else will say no — it came up on the rail. And the argument will not be settled, and it will not need to be, because the thing will go on being there, grey against grey, holding the light the way the new fills hold it, and it will be the first thing in the city that is not poured, not iron, not lamb, not person — the first thing that is simply alive and slow and patient and does not care where it is. The power does not know what they will make of it. That is the point. It gives them a question, on the cool side of the city, where the ones who already care for what cannot move itself will find it first.

---

Then it stays.

It does not go back down. It has no district and no position in the rotation and it does not need one; it sleeps on the cured floors of whatever sleeping-gallery has room, and it stands on the gantries with the casters and watches the shadow of the spoke, and it moves cisterns a meter at a time in the dark with the keepers, and when a lamb is to be moved it takes one end of the sling. It does not tell anyone what it is. The children know, in the way children know, and have a sign for it, which is the sign for the Throat — a hand held still — but made high, in the cold, instead of low.

It has understood, finally, what a benevolent power is for, in a place like this, which has come this far without one. Not to rescue. Not to explain. To do what the engineer does when a lamb is set down on new ground: to stand beside it longer than the work requires, with one hand near it — not on it — the way a person stands by a sleeping animal to see that it goes on breathing.

And then to go back down the shaft, and read the walls, and come up at the bell, and do it again tomorrow. A hand's width a year. It has come this far.
