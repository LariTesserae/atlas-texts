Something looks back.

---

It arrives as a cloud, because a cloud is the only kind of arrival this place has a gesture for. The dish dims. The roar drops out from under itself to the click, forty thousand dry ticks with the grating note held back, and in that interval — the only interval the place possesses — you hear the beetles, a seething like sand poured slowly, and it is in that interval that the visitor takes its first measure.

It does not announce itself. Announcement would be a thing done to the colony, and it has not yet decided whether anything should be done to the colony. It hangs, and looks, and the cloud does not move on as clouds do here, and the birds do not notice that the light is staying dim a little longer than light does. Nothing here notices weather. Weather is the colony.

---

It looks the way you looked, layer by layer, because there is no other way to look at a laminated thing. But it can do what you could not: hold the layers at once. It reads the wall to the bottom — not to where the blue begins, to the bottom — and the bottom is very far down and very cold and the first line in it is the same colour as the last. It counts the birds, and the count is not forty thousand; it is a number, exact, and the number has been within a few hundred of itself for longer than the plain has been flat, because the dish is the size it is and the scrapes are the size they are and the place fills itself to the brim and no further. It listens to the two-note call and finds, in the click, a frequency that matches the ringing of the hollowed stone, so that the colony is faintly, continuously sounding its own floor, the way a bat sounds a cave. It looks at the pale jelly the adults bring up last and the chicks take most eagerly and knows what it is, and does not think about it for long, because the knowledge changes nothing. It follows the knock down the pipe.

And then, having looked, it asks the only question a benevolent thing can ask of a place: is there anyone here to be kind to?

---

Weigh the bird. Lift one out of the mass in the mind, as you did, and set it on the lip, and watch it go quiet. That is a bird, for a few minutes. A bird can be starved and a bird can be cold and a bird can be taken by a lizard and pulled apart on the wall with its eyes open. There is pain in this dish. There is pain in every square hand of it. The visitor does not pretend otherwise; it is wise, not sentimental, and wisdom does not look away from the chick at the fringe.

But weigh it against the wall. The dead bird settles. The neighbours close. By the third day there is a scrape there again with a bird on it, on eggs, and the stone is a millimetre thicker, and nothing has been lost that the place had any way of keeping. The pain is real and it is brief and it does not accumulate. There is no one to whom it accumulates. The stone remembers everything and minds none of it. The animal does not suffer the death of a bird any more than you suffer the death of a cell in the skin of your palm, and it has no more capacity to be spared it.

So: be kind to the bird, and you must take it out, and then it is a wrong thing on a bench, and you have not been kind. Be kind to the animal, and there is nothing it lacks. This is the first thing the visitor learns, and it learns it quickly, and does not like it, and holds it anyway. Most of what looks like a chance to help is a chance to make a bird go quiet.

---

There are temptations, and it takes each one up and sets it down.

To speak to it. To whom? The face is a sound, and the sound is a condition, not a speech. There is no throat in the dish that could be addressed and answer. The visitor could make itself heard by every one of the forty thousand and it would be heard as the hawk is heard, or the cloud, and answered the same way.

To feed it. It feeds itself, from a place it hides.

To mend the machine. The machine is mended. Whatever the salt-makers meant it to do, it does something else now, and does it perfectly, and has for so long that the wheel is worn to the pattern of feet. To mend it would be to break it.

To stop the machine. That would be the first thing in this dish to stop since the wind finished with the plain. The visitor considers what would happen to a thing that keeps time in only one form, if the form were taken away, and does not need to see it.

To lift the salt-makers out of the wall. There is a line, some fingers below the surface, a millimetre thick, and it is not pale and less pale like the others; it is very slightly darker, and it is the only line in the wall that has a beginning and an end, because there were only a few of them and they lay down in one season and were covered. The visitor finds it without difficulty. It could — this is within what it is — draw out of that line the shapes that were pressed into it. It considers for a long time whether that would be a kindness or a violence, and to whom, and concludes that the salt-makers came to make salt and found the salt was already made, and lay down in the making, and are now the one dark line in a wall of pale ones, which is a kind of monument, and more than most makers get. It leaves them. It does not stand on them; nothing here stands on anything else for long.

To be recognised. This one it takes up last and holds longest. The lizards look at you. Nothing looks at *it*, the animal — nothing has ever looked at it entire, seen the surface of birds and the beetles under them and the blue depth and the knock all as one thing with one rule per layer, and said: there you are. The visitor can do this. It is perhaps the only thing here that it can do without harm. But to be recognised is a gift to a thing with a face, and it has just spent a long while establishing that the face is a sound, turned up, not looking. To recognise it would be to give it a face it does not have and has never needed.

It sets that down too. Almost.

---

Then it goes where the birds go.

You did not follow the flight. You could not; it left you on the lip. The visitor rises with the second flight in the long grey vein over the edge and goes with them at the height at which the plain is a pale nothing, for hours, and comes to the water.

You will not see the water. The visitor arrives at it and looks and keeps the rule. It is enough to say that the water is there, and that the birds work it, and that they are very good at working it, and that they are not the only thing that has ever worked it, and that the arithmetic is long. The visitor does the arithmetic. It is the kind of thing a wise power can do — hold the level in one hand and the rate in the other and the length of the flight in a third, and see that the flight has lengthened, a few minutes in a century, and that the birds die young because the flight costs, and that the level is doing what everything on this plain does, which is finish.

Not soon. Not in the life of the salt-makers' pan, perhaps. But the visitor sees it: the flights going further, the returns thinner, the density on the dish easing off by a scrape here and a scrape there until the birds no longer rest their tails on the heads of the birds behind, and then the wall showing between them, and then the last flight going out over the lip in a thin grey thread and coming back with less than it left with, and standing on the iron a while. And the beetles closing over. And the brine coming up one more time and going hard. And the last line in the wall the same colour as the first.

It sees this. It stands at the water, and it knows exactly what it would take to make the arithmetic come out the other way, and it is within what it is.

---

And here is where it is wise, and here is where it is hard.

*Someone came here to make salt, and found the salt was already made, and the making of it was not theirs.*

If the visitor gives water, the water is the visitor's. The birds will not know it — they treat everything as sky — but the animal will have a source, and the source will have a name, and the whole grey thing eighty paces across and however deep will become, for the first time since before the plain was flat, a kept thing. Its flights will go out to a gift. Its stone will be built of what someone chose to give. Every line laid down from that season forward will be a line the visitor made, and the wall will have an above and a below that are no longer the same, and the difference between them will be the visitor. It will have done to the place what the salt-makers meant to do and could not, and done it perfectly, and out of love, and the place will not be the place.

Whereas if it does nothing, the animal will do to itself what it has done to every bird and every lizard and every maker: it will settle, and the neighbours will close over, and it will become a layer. There is no death here that is not also a kind of masonry. The visitor has looked into the cold at the bottom of the bore, where the knock goes and does not come back, and found that nothing takes the knock. The knocks are all there. Every one since the piston was first shoved by the tide in the dark, kept in the cold in order, one and one and one, a stack of moments the way the wall is a stack of seasons. The place keeps everything. It would keep the visitor, if the visitor stayed too long. It would keep the ending. It is, in its way, the best-remembered thing there has ever been, and the memory has no one in it, and does not need anyone.

So the visitor does not give water. It stands at the water it will not describe to you and it does not add to it. This is the hardest thing it does, and it is the thing it was made for.

---

It goes back with the flight.

And on the way back, over the lip, coming down onto the dish as the birds come down onto the iron, it allows itself one thing. It is the thing it set down *almost*. It comes across the far wall not as a cloud this time but as a shadow — hawk-shaped, because that is the shape the place has an answer to — and the animal answers.

The wave goes round the dish. Forty thousand throats climb a third and come back down, a ring travelling the whole rim of the basin and closing on itself where it began. It is the one gesture the animal has. It is the only thing it has ever said to anything that was not itself, and it says it to the visitor, once, and settles, and the ordinary roar comes back up as if a valve were opened.

That is the conversation. *There you are*, and the ring. Nothing is given by it. Nothing is changed. The animal has not been recognised; it has flinched, as it flinches at every shadow, and the visitor knows the difference and does not mind, because it has also learned that this is the shape kindness has to take here: the shape of something that already happens. A shadow is not a gift. A shadow is weather. The animal can afford to be answered by weather.

---

It stays as weather.

Not always. Not so often that the place would notice, if the place noticed things. But every so often, from now on, a cloud comes over the dish, and the roar drops to the click, and under the click you can hear the beetles working — the masons, the ones nobody hears, the ones who turn the moving part into the remembering part and have never once been the top layer. The cloud stays a moment longer than a cloud would. Then it goes, and the noise comes back, and no bird has looked up.

That is what a benevolent power does with a thing that lacks nothing and is slowly finishing: it becomes the interval in which the thing can be heard. It does not lengthen the arithmetic.
