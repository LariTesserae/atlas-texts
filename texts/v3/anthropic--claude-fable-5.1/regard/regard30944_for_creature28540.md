Say first what it does not do, because the first thing a benevolent power feels, looking into that shaft, is a hand closing. The urge to reach down through the cap and the two yards of hot air and the heaving skin of the water and take her out. Lift her, pale and long and grooved down one side, into daylight she has never seen, and carry her to the sea.

That is benevolence. Benevolence is quick. It sees a held breath and wants to let it out.

Wisdom sits with the urge a while and looks at what it is made of, and finds that a good part of it is not about the eel at all. It is about the watcher. The watcher cannot bear the waiting on her behalf; the watcher has a story about migration and completion and a life that ought to have a second act, and the story is beautiful, and the eel has never heard it. She is not in pain. She is not afraid — the account is very clear on that, and the power, looking, finds it true. Her body is doing exactly what an eel's body does when the signal has not come: it continues. To lift her out now would be to do to her what the intake screen did sixty years ago — to decide, from outside, on the basis of a design she did not consult, where she should be. It would only be the same violence pointed the other way.

And the room needs its water. That is not a small thing. The shaft is a lung; drain it, still it, and something a mile away bursts, and the city above — the laundry, the school with the tar roof, the trams — learns what the shaft was for in the worst way. A wise power does not save one creature by breaking the thing that has kept her.

So it does not reach in.

---

What it does instead, first, is look at the whole of it, because the account is right that there are three tenants and they nest, and a power that only sees the eel has not seen the place.

It looks at the film and finds a city that has had, for a hundred and thirty years, precisely what it wanted: heat, iron, dark, and no competitors. It is hard to think of a population on the surface that could say as much. Whatever comes, the film has had its long day. The power sets it aside, not carelessly, but as one sets aside a thing that does not need help.

It looks at the note. This is stranger. A standing wave is not alive by any account a biologist would sign, but it has fed and grown and arranged its surroundings and kept a lamp lit for sixty years, and the power — which has seen a great many kinds of thing that persist — is not in a hurry to say what does and does not count. The note has never been heard entire. Every ear that ever came near it came for a few minutes with a lantern and left. The power listens. It hangs at the height of the lamp through one whole cycle of the city's day — the five-o'clock jerk, the noon step, the evening full-throat when the debris lifts, the long slack after the trams — and then a week, and then a year, and it hears the Sunday hush and the Christmas calm and the way the room's tone drifts a hair lower each decade as the calcite thickens. That is the first thing it does. It hears the thing through, so that when the thing ends there will be one who can say what it was.

And then it looks at the eel, and asks not *what does she need* but *what was taken*.

---

Not much, it turns out. She has food. She has a place that is exactly her shape. She has a rhythm as steady as any tide, and a companion in the rung, and no predators, and no cold. What the room took from her is one thing only: the season. The word *now*. Everything else in her life is present; that one thing is absent; and the absence is not a wound, because she does not know it is there.

But it is there. Her body is a sentence with the last word held back. The power does not think this entitles it to speak the word for her. It thinks it might be allowed to put the word back within her reach, and let her say it or not.

So the power waits. It is good at waiting. So is she.

---

The power station will close. They all do. The coal that made the two chimneys worth building comes from somewhere that will stop sending it, or the city will decide it wants its light from a different kind of fire, and one autumn a man in an office will sign a paper and the runners will spin down for the last time. This is not a thing the power arranges. It only knows it, the way it knows the moon will be full on a given night. And it knows there will be a day, after the signing, when someone has to decide what to do with several miles of tunnel full of warm water.

There are two ways. You can pump it out — quick, loud, the surface dropping a yard an hour, the shaft going from full to empty in a shift while the men eat sandwiches on the cap. Or there is the old low drain at the foot of the elbow, the one on the drawings from the 1890s, rusted shut, that runs to the canal basin where the barges used to come. Nobody has opened it in living memory. It would take longer. It would take weeks.

The power leans. That is all. It leans on a tired engineer's afternoon, the way a draught leans on a door. He is looking at the pump schedule and the overtime and he thinks — with no sense of being prompted — that the brick is old and a fast drawdown might crack it, and that the low drain would be gentler, and that he could open it a turn a day and let the tunnels find their own level down to the basin. He writes this in a memo. Someone above him, who has no opinion, initials it.

---

What follows, she reads in her flank before it is anything else.

First the note goes. Not all at once. The runners slow over an afternoon and the fundamental drops through pitches the room has never held, groaning, until it falls below the brick's tolerance and simply is not there, and for the first time since the reign of a dead queen the shaft is quiet enough that the film can be heard — a faint fizz — and the lamp, the power notices, is still lit, and it does not ask why. It has decided not to.

The eel lies in the groove through this. Everything her lateral line knows has stopped talking. She does not come out for the long slack because there is no slack; there is only slack. The power does not touch her.

Then the surface begins to fall and does not rise again. A turn a day. The tideline dries, and the red film gleams like leather for the last time and then dulls, and the calcite bands come up out of the water one by one like the rings of a tree read backward. The manhole in the cap has been opened for the drain crew, and through it, at night, comes a thing the shaft has never contained: a little cold. October air, sliding down forty feet of curved brick and settling on the water like a hand. The warmth of a million evenings has nothing feeding it now, and it begins, at last, to leave.

And on the ninth night the moon is full and the manhole is left open — the foreman forgot, or did not forget — and a coin of light comes down the shaft and lies on the surface, and moves with it, and the water below the rung, for the first time in sixty years, is a different temperature from the water above.

Her body knows this. It has always known it. It was built in the dark of an ocean to know exactly this and nothing else — the cold, the season turning, the moon — and it has been holding the knowledge unused for longer than the rung has been rusting. The power does not say *now*. It has only made sure that *now* can be said. It withdraws to the top of the shaft and does not look down.

---

Here the account has to admit it does not know, because the power does not let itself know. It would be easy to watch. It would be a kind of taking.

Perhaps she does not change. Perhaps sixty years in one life is too many, and the machinery of the second is rusted like the ladder, and she lies in the groove as the water leaves her and the cold reaches her and she dies there, in the shape of the rung, old by the calendar and young by every measure she owns — and if that is what happens, the power judges that it is not a failure. She would have died as what she was, in the place that was hers, with the one thing she lacked finally offered and finally declined. That is not nothing. That is more than most.

Or perhaps — and the power, which has no favourites, finds it has a favourite — perhaps in the last of the draining, when the shaft is a wet brick well with a foot of cold water on the black mound and a spanner standing up out of the silt like a marker, one of the drain crew shines his lamp down the low outlet where it runs to the basin and sees a flank go by. Silver. He says so, later, in the pub, and no one believes him, because the screens were all replaced before the war.

From the basin the canal. From the canal the river, through the city she has known only as a pulse — under the tramlines, past the school, past the two cold chimneys, all of it real above her for the first time and none of it hers to see, because her eyes have gone huge and dark for a different water. From the river the estuary. From there a distance no one has ever followed an eel across, to a place where the sea is warm all the way down and nothing has ever needed her to be still.

---

The power stays a while at the top of the empty shaft after the crew has gone.

The lamp is out. It went at some point in the last week and no one saw it go and the power does not go down to find out whether the crust of rust that bridged the gap has finally dried and cracked, or whether the current simply had nothing left to light. It decides the room may keep that one fact. The film is dust. The note is a set of pale rings in the brick, a score for an instrument that no longer exists, and the power has heard the whole piece, once, and carries it.

One of the men took the spanner. He cleaned it in a bucket and the jaws still would not open and he hung it on a nail in his shed, where it is now, in a different kind of dark, and has not moved in years.

That is what it did. It did not save her. It listened to the room through, and then it leaned on a door already swinging, and it gave back a season, and it turned away so that whatever came next would be hers. If that is little, it is because the place asked for little. It was a lung. It held what could not yet be let go. When the time came, it was let go, and the power's whole part was to make sure it was let go gently, and downhill, toward the sea.
