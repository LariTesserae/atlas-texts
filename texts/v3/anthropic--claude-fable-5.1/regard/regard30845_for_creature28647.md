The power arrives the way the light does, without enough definition to cast a shadow, and the first thing it does is stay.

It stays through a spring and a neap and a spring. It learns the two directions of the room. It learns that silence comes twice a day for a minute and that the wheel stops in the silence and catches again facing the other way. It counts the knocks. It is not in a hurry, because it has understood almost immediately that hurry is the one thing the room has no use for, and that anything done here quickly would be done wrong.

It has been drawn by the being who lives in the room, and it had assumed, arriving, that it would know who that was. It does not. There are three with faces and one without, and the question of which of them is meant turns out to be the first real work.

---

It considers the octopus first, because the octopus is the one whose situation looks most like a problem.

Here is an animal that has learned something true—that the knock stops the crabs—and will die in a season without passing it on. Every octopus that has ever lived in the fourth pump has found the same trick alone and taken it into the dark. Seen from outside that looks like waste, and the power's first movement, which it notices and does not act on, is toward some kind of preservation: a way for the next one to know sooner, a way for the knowledge to be kept.

Then it watches a fourth octopus arrive. It watches the rice-grain larva find the crack in the fourth pump because the crack is where the current slackens and the midden smells of food. It watches the new one lie along the lip of the same drainage channel because that is the channel the crabs use and the crabs use it because it is sheltered. It watches the knock come and the crabs freeze and the octopus, which does not freeze, take one, within the first month, having learned nothing from anyone.

The trick is not the octopus's. It is the room's. The den teaches it. The channel teaches it. The wheel teaches it every minute all day to anything with the wit to notice, and the octopus is built to notice. There is no knowledge here at risk of being lost, because the knowledge is not carried in the animal; it is laid down in the concrete and the rind and the position of the crack relative to the plinth, and it will be there for the next one, and the one after. The power had mistaken a lesson for a legacy. It sees that the room is a better teacher than it would be, and it leaves the fourth pump alone.

---

It considers the wrasse, and the wrasse is harder, because the wrasse is dying and there is nothing wrong.

Thirty years in cold water is the length of the life. The scarred lips and the heavy skull are what thirty years look like. The power watches the fish leave the intake each grey dusk and swim the length of the hall to the rivet-hole and wedge itself against the flange with the knock going through its body, and it feels, very clearly, the thing it would like to do, which is to be there at the end and to make the end fall in the minute of slack—so that the last thing the wrasse knows is not the shriek but the one silence the room has, the shrimp deafening and the fish quiet and the wheel drifting to a stop.

It could do this. The tide is not so rigid that a minute cannot be found in it.

It does not. It sits with the wanting for a long time and then sets it down, because it has noticed that the wrasse chose the hole beside the bearing. Of all the ledges in a room that is nothing but ledges, it chose the loudest, and has slept there long enough to wear the iron to the shape of its side. Whatever that is, it is the wrasse's, and it is not for the power to decide that silence would be kinder than the sound the fish has slept against for half its life. The knock is not a torment here. It is the room breathing. The wrasse will die against it, asleep, and at the next turn of the tide the thing in the second culvert will come out along the base of the plinth and find it, and that too is the room, and the power will not be the one to say it should have gone otherwise.

It stays for the death anyway. That much it allows itself. It is there, and it does nothing, and then there is a small wrasse in the light-fall on the second pump that is not quite female any more, and within a season there is a new shape under the intake screens, and the bright patch on the iron behind it is a little smaller than the old one and will grow.

---

The conger is the one with a problem, and the power knows it before it has finished thinking about the wrasse, because it is the one situation in the room that is not a cycle.

Everything else here is succession. The octopuses succeed each other, the wrasse is succeeded, the shoal is never the same fish and always the same shoal. The conger does not succeed anything and will not be succeeded in any way that matters to it. It came in once, small, and it has one thing left to do, which is to leave once, large, and go a very long way down to breed and die. That is the whole of it. There is no version of a conger's life in which the leaving is optional; when the time comes the body changes and the appetite stops and the animal goes, and if it cannot go it does not eat and does not stop trying.

The room does not know whether the conger fits. The conger does not know. The power is the only thing here that can find out, and it understands that knowing is not an intervention, only the thing that comes before one, and so it goes and measures.

The eel does not fit the outflow. It has not fit the outflow for some years. It will not fit the bar screen when it tries that, forty paces upstream, exhausted, against a current it has spent its life avoiding. When the time comes it will turn in the culvert and turn again and the prawns will move aside for it without hurry and it will die back there where the light has never been, not quickly, having not eaten for a season.

This is a future, not a present. Nothing is wrong yet. And the power weighs, as it must, what it would cost the room to change it: the culverts are the room's throat, the silt migrates through them in its figure-eight, the prawns line them, the eel's coming-and-going at the turn is part of what the crabs have learned to fear. Widen a culvert and the current through it quickens and the shell-flour redistributes and the prawns shift and something in the balance of the floor is altered by a degree. It is not nothing.

But it is not much, and the room has a word for it already. On the springs things come loose—a sheet of rind, a clump too heavy for its anchor—and go down the hall and into the culverts and are gone. The room knows storms. It has armored itself against them for longer than any inhabitant has lived, but it has not stopped them, and a storm is not a violation of what this place is. It is one of the two or three kinds of event the room has.

So the power waits for a spring, and above the ceiling the weather is a little worse than it would have been, and the swell drives a little harder into the intake, and a slab of rind the size of a door that has been holding the lip of the second culvert since before the conger was an elver lets go at the height of the flood. It goes down the hall end over end, past the third pump, past the fourth, and lodges in the culvert mouth for the length of one tide and then is pushed through and out on the ebb, and where it was there is a gap a hand's breadth wider than there was, and the water moves through it a little faster, and the shell-flour on the floor there is scoured back to rust for a few paces and is already, by the next neap, dusted white.

The room reads it as weather. Nothing in the hall could read it as anything else.

The conger does not go that year, or the next. When it does go it is at night, on a neap, at the turn, and it comes forward to the culvert mouth the way it always has, and the prawns move aside, and it does not stop at the mouth. It is the only thing in the whole history of the room that has left it whole. Everything else that ever went out through the culverts went out as shell or as rind or as the half of something a crab did not finish. The power does not follow it. It has no need to know how the story ends; it knows the eel is in the story now and not outside it, and that was all that was owed.

An elver comes in the following spring, no thicker than a pencil, and finds the second culvert because it is dark and there is food at the mouth of it. The power notes this and does not think it a coincidence and does not think it a design. The room is shaped so that this happens. It always was.

---

Last, and longest, it considers the bearing.

It had not meant to. Iron does not need a wise power. Iron has no future to be spared, no season, no one thing left to do. And yet when the power asks itself again who it was drawn here for, the question it has been carrying since it arrived, it finds that its attention has been returning, tide after tide, to the one surface in the room that is exactly what it was made of.

Everything else has been colonized. The concrete is shell now, the pumps are shell, the walls are shell over brick. The bearing has been refused all of that. It is too fast and too bare and it moves against itself, and nothing can hold there, and so it is alone in a room where nothing else is alone, and it is the thing the room keeps time by. The mussels shut to it. The shoal draws in to it. The octopus hunts by it and the wrasse sleeps against it and the crabs stop in their channels when it speaks. It has never been alive and it is the one inhabitant none of them could do without, and it is being used up.

Slowly. The rind repairs what the wear takes, and so it may simply turn. Or the rind may fall behind by a fraction in some century, and the hollow may widen past the point where the water grips it, and the wheel may drift to a stop one slack tide and not catch. And then the room would have no clock. The mussels would open and stay open. The octopus's trick would stop working and the next octopus would never learn it. The shoal would hang in the middle of the hall and not contract, and the silence that comes twice a day would simply be the room.

The power can see both futures and can choose between them, and it understands that this is the real question it was brought here to answer, and that the answer is not in what it does but in what it refuses.

It will not repair the bearing. To make it last forever would be to make the room a thing that cannot end, and the room is not that; the room is a rate, a balance between what is eaten and what grows, and a rate is only a rate because it can change. It will not stop the bearing either, though it sees the argument—that the iron has done enough, that a thing which has only ever been used might be let go. But that is a sentiment for the living, and the bearing does not have it, and the power will not pretend otherwise in order to feel merciful. The bearing does not want anything. It turns because it is turned.

What it can give iron is the one thing iron has never had here, which is to be attended to. So the power does the last thing it does, which is to stay again, this time without a question. It is there through the knock and the shriek and the roar. It is there in the minute of slack when the wheel sighs to a stop and the whole room waits and everything alive holds still for a piece of metal that has never known it was being waited for. It has decided that if the wheel ever does stop for good it would like to have been present, not to prevent it, only so that the stopping is witnessed by something that understood what was being lost. And if the wheel never stops, then this is simply what it will do.

---

In the fourth pump a new octopus is lying along the lip of a drainage channel with two arms down in it. Under the intake a wrasse that was not the last wrasse holds against the flow, and the bright patch behind it is very nearly the shape of the old one now. The second culvert is a hand's breadth wider than it was, and full of eyes, and one pair of the eyes is small and clouded and belongs to something that will be, in fifty years, about as wide as the culvert.

The knock comes, and the shriek, and the shoal contracts and opens. The tide is coming in. Nothing in the room knows that it was noticed, and nothing in the room would be any different if it did, and that, the power decides, is the measure of how little it needed to do—which is, in the end, the kindest thing it could have found out.
