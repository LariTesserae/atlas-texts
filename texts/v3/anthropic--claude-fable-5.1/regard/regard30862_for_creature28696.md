Start with what it would not do, because everything here is a negotiation with restraint.

It would not give them rain. It would think about it, because the thought arrives on its own the moment one sees the horn cup filling drop by drop under the apron's channel, and because a power of that kind is used to the pleasure of pouring out. It would think about a soft cold falling that went on most of a day, and everyone standing out in it with their faces up. And then it would remember that this has happened once already, in the lifetime of someone's grandmother, and that the story is told as a wonder and a warning both, and that the warning is the part that has lasted. The cisterns overflowed. Nobody knew what to do. Water went down the fractures and was lost, and the loss was a grief, and the grief is what they kept. A people who have made *waste* and *stupidity* one word would not be blessed by abundance. They would be insulted by it, the way one is insulted by a cup offered and half-drunk. So: no rain. Not because they do not deserve it but because they have built a whole ethics on its absence, and the ethics is finer than the rain.

It would not tell them what is below the cloud. This would be harder to set down. The unknown is a wound of a kind — it takes the copper-traders and the rope-hangers and the ones who went over and would not describe it — and a power that loved them would want to close the wound. But it would watch them hold the not-knowing, carefully, the way they hold a copper pin cold-beaten and worn at the throat, and it would understand that the not-knowing is load-bearing. Everything else on the plateau has been counted. The water in cups, the stone by its ring, the herd by its breath, the year by the frost's slow tide. They have left themselves exactly two things unaccounted for, the cloud and the frogs, and they have left them on purpose, the way a fitter leaves a gap in a wall for the wind to pass through so that the wall does not have to fight it. Fill the gap and the wall cracks. So: no revelation. Let the cloud come up in the afternoon like something poured and let no one ever see where it is poured from.

It would not still the cliff-wind. Here the power would sit for a long time, in whatever way such a power sits, and the sitting would not be comfortable. This is the one place where the people break. Everywhere else the slowness holds; here they will not speak at all, not quickly and not slowly, and a body goes down into the white and that is the end of the matter and everyone knows what the dung cost and nobody says. A benevolent power would want, more than it wanted almost anything, to make the wind stop taking them. And it would trace the wind back — up the cliff faces from below, carrying the cloud that beads on the aprons that fill the cisterns that are the water — and find that the wind that takes the bird-hangers is the wind that keeps everyone else alive, and that the raptors ride it, and the birds nest in its lee, and there is no version of this place with the wind stilled that is still this place. It could make the rope never fail. But it is never the rope.

So it would arrive at the thing that wisdom arrives at in front of anything whole: that most of what it could do would be a kind of breaking, and that the breaking would be done in love, and that this does not make it not breaking.

And then it would do what they do. It would slow down.

---

It would match its pace to theirs, which for a power of that kind is a discipline and not a default. A reply that comes too quickly is slightly rude here, as if the speaker had not been worth thinking about. The council has considered a question for a full turn of the herds before saying anything and this is regarded as attention. So the power would attend. It would give the plateau a full turn of the herds, and then another, and it would spend them in the way the people spend them — following the frost-melt across the tiled ground, sitting on the roofs in the evening while the herd-sound comes up through the slabs, listening to the small dry tapping of knuckles on stone that goes on all day throughout the city.

It would learn to hear what the old walker hears in the herd, the difference between content and uneasy and about to move. It would learn to read the walls. Three generations of hands in a single course of slabs, individual as handwriting, and the fitters can tell from across a lane who set which block — the power would learn this too, slowly, the way a child of six learns which way the stone will split. It would learn the whistles. It would learn the signs made with the whole arm against the sky, and it would find, standing at the edge in the late light watching a herder on the far side of a shallow valley say *the seep has moved*, that it was moved by this in a way it had not expected — that a language built so that nothing is said which is not worth the effort of saying it is a language it could learn things from.

And it would learn the thing the people know and do not say: that they are not the last inhabitants of a finished place. That the plateau is watching itself become something, and they are part of what it is watching with. The power would recognise this. It would recognise it because it is, in some sense, the same thing the power itself does — watch a place become — and it would understand for the first time that it had been mistaken in thinking of these people as the ones watched. They are watchers. They are the plateau's attention given hands. The fitter setting his block for the ones after, the ones so far after they will not remember there was a city — he is not hoping to be remembered. He is doing the plateau's remembering for it, in advance, in stone.

So the power's first act, if it can be called an act, would be to stop thinking of itself as the only benevolent and wise thing in the vicinity.

---

There would be small things, after that. A power that has decided not to break anything can still do small things, and I think it would, because restraint that does nothing at all is only a colder kind of pride.

It would go down with them. When the cliff-wind takes a bird-hanger — every few years, the rope checked and checked and never the rope — the body goes into the cloud and is not recovered and no one speaks of it. The power would not stop this. But it would go down with them, into the white, and be there for the length of the falling, and make the falling short and the fear not long. It would know where each of them went. It would hold the shape of what happened, so that it happened somewhere in something, so that the not-speaking above was not the only record. It would not tell anyone this. They would not want it told; it would be a half-drunk cup. But it would be so.

It would attend to the frogs. Not explain them — it would be careful never to let the explanation leak, never to let a child who was told not to touch them work out on her own why they are there, because the mystery is the one thing on the plateau that belongs to no one and has no keeper and does not need to be counted. But when the seep shifts its three paces in a generation and the household spends twenty years rebuilding its apron to follow, the power would see to it that the frogs shift too. Small and dark and unaccountable, at the new seep as at the old. The one unknown thing, following the water. It would take no credit for this. It would be pleased, in whatever way such a power is pleased, that the arrangement of the place had left it a mystery to keep.

It would let the walkers meet. Once in several generations a herder follows the herds so far along the rim that they find a herder from the far cities, and the two stand and sign at each other across a valley and find the signs almost the same, and then the herds move on, and this is one of the few stories told often. The power would not do more than nudge the grass. A little better to the north this year, in a way that could be frost or luck. So that in this generation, as well as in the next, someone stands in the late light and sees an arm raised against the sky a valley away and understands it. That is all. A gift of company, not of change. They would tell the story and not know it had been given, and that is how it should be given.

And the block. The fitter's block, set for the ones after. The power would remember it. This is the largest of the small things and I think it is the one the power would come to understand as its real work here: not to alter the plateau but to be the plateau's memory across the span the plateau cannot hold. To see the crack widen its finger-width each lifetime and the seep move and the city rebuild itself around the shift, slab by slab, until the frost has ground the whole of it to flour and the flour has gathered in the low places and the grass has grown and something that is not these people, something they could not picture and did not resent, lives here in a gentled land with streams that know their courses. And to see that when one of those, whoever they are, finds a block in the soil with an edge on it too straight to be chance, and turns it over, and wonders — the wonder is there. The power would keep the wonder ready for them. It would hold three generations of hands, read off a wall, across the whole slow age of a mountain, so that when the fitter said *for the ones after* he was not lying, and did not know he was not lying, and did not need to.

---

Which brings it back to the woman on the apron in the grey light, because a power of that kind would come back to her.

She is holding the horn cup under the channel and it is filling drop by drop. Below her through the slabs the animals are breathing. Above the city the bird cliff is beginning its pale roar. She is looking out past the last watch-hut at the cloud sea and the far rim where the other fires have just gone out.

The power would stand with her. It would not offer her anything. To offer someone water here is a serious act and the power has nothing that is a known quantity, nothing that could be drunk to the bottom, and it has learned — over a full turn of the herds, then another — that this is a people who will not accept a gift they cannot finish. So it would offer her its attention instead, all of it, undivided, for as long as the cup takes to fill, and it would try to make sure that the attention was a cup and not a well. Something she could have all of. Something that, if she could have felt it, she would have known the size of.

She would not feel it. That is part of the discipline. She would feel the cold, and the stone under her feet warming toward the two warm hours, and the slow weight of the cup increasing in her hand. She would feel, perhaps, that the morning was a good one, in the way that some mornings are good without cause.

When the cup is full she will drink it, all of it. She will tap the stone once with her knuckle to hear it ring. The power will hear the ring — it is the sound of the whole plateau, the young rock testing itself, three generations of hands and the fitter's block and the frogs at the seep and the ones after, all in one small dry note that does not carry far in the thin air and does not need to. And it will let the note go where the fractures send it, and it will not follow it, and it will not say anything, because a reply that comes too quickly is slightly rude here, and because she was worth thinking about, and because it is still thinking.

She will go in. The power will stay out, on the apron, in the grey light, for a long time, doing what the plateau does: watching itself become something, and being part of what it watches with.
