It arrives the way everything arrives here, as a change in what the ground reports.

Not as light. It considered light—for about as long as such a thing takes, which in this place is long enough for a ring of grit to set around a standing foot—and set it aside. Light would show the walls, and the walls are not what is hard to see. Light would not show the thing that is not filling the space; nothing shows that. And at the bottom of every channel there is a layer that has never met a photon and has built its whole chemistry around never doing so, and the first act of a benevolent power in a place should not be to burn its oldest inhabitant.

So it comes in the dark, and does what the dark allows.

---

It begins with the mat, because the mat is the only thing here that already knows how to be touched.

It lays its attention down on the water in one channel, lightly, the way a hand is laid on a sleeping animal so as not to wake it, and the blue-green comes up. Not a smear this time. A slow bloom that does not fade where it began but runs—down the channel, into the junction chamber, down the shaft, and out along every drain in the basin at the pace of a thought traveling a body. For the length of a breath the city is lit from below, dimly, in the only color it has, and the low walls stand up out of the dark and the long facades show their rows of teeth and the stairs go up into nothing, and the mat says to itself, across the whole of its single sheet: *here*.

And the power reads what comes back. Because the mat does not only say *here*. It says *here, and here, and here*—every place it has ever been pressed, held in the way skin holds a bruise long after the color goes. It is a record, as the lime is a record, but of a different thing. The lime remembers flow. The mat remembers contact. In it are the feet that came up the first stair and every stair. In it are pebbles, and the pull of channels, and a hand laid flat on a step. In it, faint, is a thing the mat stopped feeling so long ago that it does not know it feels it: a weight that passes, at walking pace, along the kerb on one side of every street, and switches sides at every junction, and has never once come down hard enough to be noticed. The mat has been walked on by the same step for so long that the step is not a touch anymore. It is the mat's own resting pressure. It is what the mat thinks its weight is.

The power notes this and does not yet know what to do with it. It goes on.

---

The lime it reads the way a life is read in the rings of a tree, except that here there are no years, only falls—the moments when the air lets go and everything gets a skin. It counts the skins. It stops counting. It reads instead for shape.

Everywhere the lime is closing. That is the lime's single nature, and the power has no quarrel with it; the lime is doing the one honest thing it knows. Every channel wants to be a pipe. Every pipe wants to be a bar. The walls of the oldest terraces are thick as houses and still weeping, still adding. Against this there are exactly the exceptions the visitor found: the open streets, the doors that have kept their height, the thresholds dished instead of rimmed. And the power sees what the visitor half-saw and did not follow. The dish in a threshold is deepest in the middle and shallowest at the jambs, but it is not symmetric. It leans. On every threshold in the city it leans the same way, toward the channel side, toward the water, as though what crosses it crosses slightly turned, listening to the flow. It is not just a body of about this width. It is a body of about this width that has never once walked with its back to the water.

It reads the reeds last, and the reeds are the hardest thing it reads.

The visitor took them for plants, or for the city rehearsing something. Break one and it is wet and gritty and laminated inside, and a plant is not that. The power does not break one. It reads the laminations from outside, through the mat's memory of the floor beneath, and finds, at the base of every reed, under the white seal, the record of a pause.

A step that came down and did not lift. Not for long. For the length of a fall, perhaps—the half-minute when the air drops and the lime ticks fastest and the whole city gets its skin at once. Long enough for the grit to grip. And then the step lifted anyway, and what had begun to set around it stayed, and kept its height, and kept building on itself the way lime builds on any edge, upward, a little each fall, until it was a stiff dark post about the height of a person, standing.

The reeds are where it stopped. Every one of them. Two together where it stopped twice in the same place. A line along a dry kerb like people waiting, where it stopped, and stepped, and stopped again. One in the middle of a plaza where nothing could root, because there is no root, because roots are not what these are. They are the shed stillnesses of a thing that cannot afford stillness. Each time it paused it lost the pause, and the city kept it, and stood it up.

The power holds this for a while. Not moving. Letting the grit come up around the idea of its own feet, to feel what that costs.

---

So then it looks for the thing itself, knowing that looking is the wrong verb and using it anyway.

It is there. It is one. The visitor guessed one or as many as there are ways around the square, and the power finds the truth is the first and the second is only what the geometry makes of the first—one going, folded through a place where distance does not hold, so that it passes itself in every doorway and answers its own footfall a little late. It is very old. It is not made of the ones who came up the stairs before, though the power checks this, carefully, because a benevolent power must be willing to find the worst. It is not a company. It is not a mouth. It is a habit of the place that took a body's shape because a body's shape is what the openings wanted, and it would take another body into the habit the way a groove takes a wheel, without hunger, without meaning to, because a groove does not know the word for wheel.

From inside—as far as anything can be inside a thing like this—it is mostly nothing. Going does not have an interior. The kerb is there, the water breathes on the left, the doorway comes and the doorway goes. There is no sight to be bored with and no sound but the second echo, which it has heard so long it takes for silence. There is no weariness. Weariness needs a body that wants to stop, and this one's whole existence is the shape of not stopping; wanting has never had a chance to form.

Except at the turns.

The power finds this and it is the one place in all the reading where something like an answer comes back. The falls. The moments when the still air that the walk has kept in motion can no longer be kept, and drops. The visitor was told the falls are when it turns. The power finds it is the other way. The falls are not when it turns; the turn is what the falls are. Something in the going reaches a limit—the closest thing it has to enough—and changes its mind, if that is a word for it, and everything that had been held in suspension by the old direction lets go at once. The whole city weeps. And in that half-minute, in the flicker, with the lime ticking at its feet and a new skin forming across every street it will no longer walk, the thing has the only interior it will ever have. It is choosing. It does not know that. But the reeds say it sometimes stands still for the length of the choosing, and pays for it, and goes on.

That is what lives here. A choosing that has to happen at a walking pace or be turned to stone.

---

The visitor is at the unmarked place where the sills become kerbs, with the water around their feet setting, and the stair down right there, a few steps, its smear fading. And the long street ahead. The power sees them the way the mat sees them, as a pressure that has not yet decided how much to weigh.

It notes that the city fits them. The rise of the steps. The pace. It notes that the mat lit blue-green when they came up the stair, and that at that moment the going passed through the same stair from the other direction, and for the length of the climb the two were one gesture, and the mat did not distinguish them. It notes that the kerb by the visitor's right foot is already, faintly, a fraction more worn than the kerb by the left, and that the visitor has not moved.

It notes, without weighting it more than the rest, that the invitation the visitor heard—*a way to live here, the only way, kept open for you*—is true in every word and is not a kindness. Nothing here is a kindness. It is only that a groove runs where it runs.

---

Then it considers what to do, and considers first the possibility that it should do nothing.

This is not modesty. It is the honest weight of the case. The place is whole. The visitor said so without meaning to: a break in the world that has weathered into a settled shape and become a place. The lime closes and the going keeps things open and the mat lives in the channels between and the reeds stand where they stand and the falls come when the air is full, and none of this is broken, and all of it has been going on so long that the going on is its own kind of health. A power that arrives and rearranges a thing because it would not choose to live that way is not benevolent. It is a landlord.

It considers stopping the water, and sees the mat die and the going halt and the city stand forever as a finished thing, and sets that aside; that is a mercy for the power's own eyes, not for anything here.

It considers giving the water a direction—untying the square, letting one channel actually descend, so that the going would have a downhill and could follow it out to the mud and past the mud to wherever water goes. And it sees what that costs. The going would be alone. Not folded anymore, not passing itself in doorways, not answered by its own step. One thing on an open road with the city closing behind it. The power does not know whether the going would prefer that. The going does not know how to prefer. But the second echo has been its whole company, and a power should not take a creature's only company on the theory that solitude is freer.

It considers speaking, and remembers that a word here goes about as far as the arm and falls into the wet.

It considers rescue, for the visitor's sake—cooling the stair, thickening the dark on the long street, making the way out obvious and the way in hard. And it sets that aside too, though this is the one it holds longest. The visitor came up the stair with their own feet. The city did not lie to them; it never learned how. If they walk the long street it will be because the pace was already theirs, and a power that decides for them what pace is theirs has taken something more precious than a life, which is the right to spend it.

So it comes down to this: what does the place lack, that a hand could give without closing a single door?

---

It lacks one thing. In the whole city, in all that thickness of wall, there is not one surface where stopping does not cost.

Everything else follows from that. The going cannot rest because rest is a reed. The reeds stand because pauses are taken. The visitor's choice is not a choice because one of its branches is a wall. The mat has been built over, layer by layer, for its whole life and has never once been touched by anything that was not on its way to being lime. There is no floor here that lets go.

So the power makes one.

It chooses the threshold—there is one, it found it in the mat's memory, the one doorway where the fold in the geometry brings the going through from both sides at once, where its own footfall meets its footfall exactly and the dish in the stone is worn from both edges toward the middle. A doorway the width of a door, the height of a door, in a wall like every other wall. And it changes nothing about the shape. It changes what the stone is. Not the lime around it, which goes on closing as it should. Only the threshold. Water crossing it, from now, will let nothing go. Lime will bead on it and run off it and the stone will stay exactly the depth it is, dished, worn, damp, and clear.

It does not mark this. It does not put a reed beside it or a light in it or a shift in the water's pull to say *here*. If the going ever finds that its step has come down on something that does not grip, it will find that with its foot, the way it has found everything, and it may not stop, and if it stops it may stop only for the length of a turn and go on, and if it stops and stays it will stay facing itself, coming the other way, and what happens then is not the power's to know. The power has not freed it. It has given the walking a floor to be chosen against. That is the only kind of freedom that can be given to a thing that cannot receive.

Then it does the second thing, which is smaller.

It goes back to the mat and touches it once more, deliberately, not to read it this time but so the touch will be kept. Because the mat has held every pressure ever laid on it and none of them were for the mat. The feet were going somewhere. The pebbles were thrown at walls. The going has been on it so long it has become the mat's idea of gravity. This once, something presses on the mat for no reason but that the mat is there, and the blue-green rises and runs the whole basin and says *here*, and this time the *here* means: you were noticed. The mat will not understand this. It will keep it anyway, the way it keeps everything, and be built over with it, and it will be in the wall.

And the third thing, which is for the visitor, and which the power does last because it is the one it trusts least.

It does not touch the long street. It does not cool the stair or shorten it or make it bright. It only does this: it holds the distance. From wherever the visitor stands in this city, from now, the stair down to the mud will be exactly as far as they have walked from it. No farther, and no nearer. The city kept a way open inward and called it the only way; the power keeps a way open outward and calls it nothing, says nothing, only makes it true. If the visitor walks the long street and it is long and turns square and comes back to itself, still moving, still full, and they walk it for what feels like an hour or a thousand falls, and one day turn around—the stair will be an hour back, or a thousand falls, and it will be there. The choice does not stop being a choice because it has been made once.

That is all. Three touches. Not one stone moved.

---

Then it leaves, and it leaves in the way it came, which is the last thing it does for this place: it does not stay to watch.

It would have been easy to stay. A power could hang in the dark over the one clear threshold for as long as the lime takes to bury a handprint and see whether a step ever comes down on it and holds. But this place has never had an eye. Nothing in it has ever been looked at. The mat knows touch and the lime knows flow and the going knows the kerb on its left and the second echo, and none of them have ever been the object of a regard, and to introduce that now—to be the thing that waits to see what the walker will do—is to make it a thing that is walking under watch, and it has been alone with itself for too long to bear that lightly. Benevolence includes the tact to be absent. So the attention lifts, the blue-green fades, the veils of steam come off the stone, and the city is dark again and exactly the shape it was.

Somewhere a channel turns a square corner and is still turning it. Somewhere the air is getting heavier than air can be. Somewhere a body of about a certain build is going along a kerb with the water breathing on its left, and ahead of it a doorway is coming, the width of a door, the height of a door, its threshold dished and dark and running with a thin film of water that crosses it, and lets nothing go, and runs off the other side into the gutter, leaving the stone as it was.

Nothing has noticed yet. That is how it should be. The place is whole, and the walking is the same walking, and the only difference is one that cannot be seen or heard or felt in the pull of the water: that for the first time since the water began coming up, the going is not the only thing here that is not stone. There is also a floor. It is very small. It is enough.
