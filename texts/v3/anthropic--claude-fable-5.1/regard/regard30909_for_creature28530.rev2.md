It comes the way everything comes to the hollow: over the rim, and fast.

That is the first thing to say about it. Even a wise power arrives at the speed of attention, and attention is quick — quicker than swifts, quicker than the fog that lifts a breath late. It has been over the plateau for some time before it notices the bowl. It notices it the way you did: the sound first, then the leaf that takes too long to land. And it comes to the edge and looks down, and asks itself the thing such powers are made to ask, which is not *what is this* but *what here needs me*.

It looks for the being. The question was singular. That turns out to be the first difficulty.

---

Not the swifts. It sees at once what you saw slowly — they eat here, they do not live here, they are the plateau's own quick life borrowing the low place for an afternoon. Not the frogs, though it lingers on them: a life whose entire sky is the underside of one leaf is the kind of thing a benevolent power has to be careful around, because the impulse to *widen* it is very strong, and the frog does not want widening. The frog wants the cup. The power notes this and moves on.

The trees, perhaps. They are old past their kind and have become countries. But a country is not a being, and the trees are not doing anything the trees anywhere would not do, given the damp and the time.

The ring, then. This is where it stops longest. The power knows what the ring is. It knows who set it here, and why, and what the pale material is, and it knows that the purpose was enormous and specific and had nothing whatsoever to do with fog or frogs or moss — that the ring finished, or failed, long before the mountain was a plateau, and that everything the hollow is, is its afterlife. The ring is the nearest thing in the bowl to a mind. It hums. It leans toward nothing and remembers nothing and does, still, some fraction of its work.

But a wise power knows the difference between a thing that persists and a thing that endures. The ring persists. It could not be otherwise than it is. It does not need.

The algae the power considers, and sets aside with something like respect. It is the record. Records do not require kindness.

So for a while the power wonders if the being is the whole of it — bowl and hum and fog and trees and drip, one creature with many organs, breathing once a day. It could decide that. It would not be wrong.

Then, in the afternoon, the shadow of the northern tree moves across the floor, and one of the stones is not where the shadow's edge said it was.

---

The power does the thing that wisdom is, which is to become the right size for what it is looking at.

It slows down.

This costs it something. A power accustomed to reading ages in a glance has to *choose* to wait for a fog to lift, to sit through a whole day of the swifts building to their shriek, to watch a lip travel a hand's-width across warm stone. It sets its attention to the interval of the heart it can feel in the nearest flank, and finds the interval too long to hold, and holds it anyway, and counts it twice to be sure. It stays a day. Then a season. Then long enough to see the child cross the northern slope in the heat, quick as a tortoise, frantic, and to see it a little less quick the next year, and to understand that this is not decline. This is the child arriving.

While it waits it goes through the list. What do they lack.

Hunger — no. The fog sets the table every dawn and has never once failed to. Fear — there is nothing to fear and they have no organ left for it. Pain — the slow air kills swifts, not these; nothing falls on them; nothing bites. Loneliness. The power stops here, because at night the eastern ones move, and the moving has a direction, and by dark they are leaning against the pale pieces in the wall.

It watches this many times. It considers whether the hum is warmth to them, or company, or a mother. It considers whether the lean is longing. It decides, in the end, that it is closer to the way a stone lies against another stone — that they are with the thing that made the place in the way a river is with its bed, and that this is not loneliness. They have each other, at arm's length. They have the hum. They have never once wanted to be understood.

---

Then the power looks the long way down, because it can, and because a benevolence that will not look at endings is only comfort.

It sees the plateau go. Grain by grain from the rim, a breath late. It sees the bowl stand alone above a plain, and then the bowl's own walls begin, because the ring holds the inside and not the outside, and the outside has nothing left to hold it. It sees the rim lower. It sees the night's cold, one year, not quite pool — slide off instead, over a lip that is no longer high enough — and the fog stand thin, and thinner, and one dawn not stand at all. It sees ridges part on the eastern slope and nothing run in.

It could stop this. It holds the option and turns it over the way you would turn one of the pale pieces if you could lift it. It could set the rim back. It could hold the fog. It could make the hollow what it is now, forever.

And it sees what that would be: a bowl where nothing falls, because the falling has been stopped; a floor never cleaned, because nothing is set down on it; a lip with nowhere to travel. The child never arriving, because there is no more slow to arrive at. A thing kept is not the thing. The power knows this better than it knows almost anything, and knowing it does not make it easier.

It sets the option down. It does not pick it up again.

---

Then does it do nothing?

Almost.

One evening a grain of grit comes down from the rim, a breath late, the way ten thousand have that day, and this one is falling toward the lidded eye of the child on the northern slope. The power turns it, and it lands beside the eye instead. Nothing knows. The child does not; a grain in the eye would have been one more thing to wait out, and it is learning to wait. The power does it because it is there and it can and it costs nothing, and because a kindness that waits for an occasion worthy of it is not kindness. It is judgment.

That is the whole of what it does to the hollow.

---

What it does after is to turn, at last, toward the rim, where you are.

Because you are the one in the bowl who needs something. You are quick. You have your hand on a flank, counting. You are already, without meaning to, composing the way you will tell it — the road in, the crack in the tableland, the sound that hits like heat. You are the only thing here that can receive, and so you are where benevolence goes.

It does not take the way from you. A wise power does not trust itself with what people should forget. It does not slow you; you would only notice, and be afraid. What it gives you is smaller and stranger than either. When the middle one lets its lip out a hand's-width toward you and holds it, and draws it back, and is a stone again — you were considered, and set aside — the power lets that land in you as what it is. Not a rejection. A verdict, rendered correctly, by the only judge in the bowl. *This was once warm.* And it lets you feel the rightness of the verdict all the way through, so that you go up the slope with your hands empty and no ache in you to come back, and no need for anyone else to. You will tell it as a story and not as directions. That is the gift. It is the only protection the hollow has ever needed and the only one that will hold.

---

Then it stays.

Not to guard. Not to mend. There is nothing to guard against once you have gone, and it has already decided about mending. It stays because it has looked the long way down and seen that at the end of it there is a slope of warm stone with ridges parting on it and no fog, and that nothing in the bowl will mark that. The ring will hum through it. The algae will lay down one more black hairline and not know what it covers. Nothing takes the bones.

Something should at least be there.

So it makes itself small and slow and sits at the pace of the floor, an arm's length from the eastern wall, where the pale pieces are. It does not lean on them. It is not a stone; the lips do not come to it, or if they do they lift, and go around. It is only attention, held at the interval of a heart it had to count twice.

And when the plateau has worn away to nothing and the bowl stands alone above the plain, full of trees and birds and mist and humming — and after that, when the trees have gone back into the floor, and the mist has thinned, and the humming is the only thing left that was ever loud — it will still be there. They will not have noticed that anything happened.

It will have. That is what it is for.
