It arrives the way you did: across the grass, and then the ground stops.

It is used to being asked for things. That is what usually draws its attention to a place — a want dense enough to carry. Here there is none. Nobody in the bowl has prayed. No slot has been cut for anything but wood or birds. The nearest thing to a petition is a child tapping on a party mass to ask whether the family above is awake, and the answer comes, and the child is satisfied. So it stands at the rim for a long time — a wedge, they would say; a dry wedge — working out what it has been called by, and decides in the end that it has not been called. It has been overheard. The stone is one stone and a tap travels, and something in the city knocking softly at itself got out past the grass line and went a very long way.

It comes down as a listener, then, and listens first. It has learned that much.

---

It listens through a whole cool half-year. It lies on the newest bench in the dark and lets the reports come up through it before dawn, and by the end it too can say *that was the family below the fig* and be right. It stands on the party walls and looks into a dozen roofless rooms at once and does not find this rude, because nobody here does. It follows the water down. It counts the ferns. It has the manners not to look too closely at the one in the water; whatever that is, it was here first, and the custom is courtesy, and the power takes the local custom.

And it makes a list, the way any benevolence does, of what it could give.

---

It could show them a finished thing.

That is the obvious gift, the one it has come with in its hands, more or less — a people who have never seen anything complete, and here is something that has seen everything complete. One room, closed and done. One wall that will never be cut again.

It turns this over and sees the shape of it. A finished room is a room nobody can lower tonight. It is a wall the block below cannot become. Their word for done means *walked away from for the day*, and they have not lost the other meaning; they never had it, and the lack has a shape, and the shape is the city. To give them a finished thing would be to score a line across the whole bowl and say: this side is yours, that side is not. It sets the gift down.

It could make oak drink faster in the dry half of the year, so that no morning comes without a report. But the still mornings are how they know the city is a thing that can fail to happen. The unease that sits on the bowl until noon is the same faculty that hears a drain go wrong from its note. It will not take the one to spare them the other.

It could steady the line-drawers. No line ever wrongly placed, no neighbor's wall ever taken. But a wrong line here is settled by cutting the disputed stone out and lifting it away, and both households end with more room than they argued over. It has watched a great many peoples lose arguments and never seen it done this well. It leaves the hands alone.

It could catch the children. A meter of stone, a drop either side, no rail. It could be the thing that is under them. But it has seen the scored line across the walkway where a child fell — cut not to warn, since the people who walk there already know, but so the stone remembers too — and it understands that if nothing ever fell, they would learn the ground forgives, and then it would have to be under them forever, and one day it would not be there.

Rollers. A seventh set, straight-grained, oiled, appearing one morning on a southern terrace. It thinks about what happens when a block moves: the whole terrace stops and watches, not because it is interesting but because it is rare. It would be spending that. It does not.

Copper, wood, soil, rain, a second fig. Each one it picks up and finds the place has already made its own arrangement with the lack, and the arrangement is a custom, and the custom is something it would be sorry to see go.

By spring the list is empty and it has done nothing, which is more than it usually manages, and it is nearly ready to go back up the ramp the way the dead do, having done its part.

Except that it can see two things they cannot, and it owes them at least the looking.

---

The first is the dead.

The bowl deepens from the middle outward. The rim walks. The old at the top can remember the plateau being where the sump is now, and the grass line moves, a block's width, a block's width, and out beyond it at a distance from the rim the dead lie under the grass with no marker, being softened. In some number of lifetimes a line-drawer will walk out across the turf with a straightedge and a pebble and score a line over them without knowing, and the slots will be cut, and the pegs set and wetted, and in the dark the report will come, and in the morning a family will look into their slightly larger room and find in the fresh face something the plateau has been working on for a hundred years. Rounded. Taken slowly. Every edge gone. The only finished thing any of them will ever see, and it will be their own.

It considers moving the dead further out. It considers turning the cutting, gently, some other way. It considers, for a moment, standing in the grass and simply being in the way.

No. That meeting is theirs. It is the one place where their two beliefs — the grass line is where the plateau stops, the grass line is where the city stops — will finally have to look at each other, and it will not arrange the meeting and it will not prevent it. It resolves only this: if it is still anywhere near when that morning comes, it will see that it has rained the night before, so the face is damp and clean and the stone is dark, and they can see.

---

The second is the seam.

The weeping diagonal in the western wall is a plane where the stone was laid down differently, long before any of this, and water has always found it. The wall above the seam gets taller every year as the floor drops away beneath it. Stone has a patience of its own, and it is not the wood's. One night — not soon; they do not have soon — the report will come from a quarter where nobody poured water, and it will not stop at one, and it will not be a room becoming larger.

That is the single thing in the bowl that would arrive unearned. Everything else here is made by someone scoring a line and going to sleep. This would be made by no one. It would be the first sound in the dark that a child on a ledge could not learn to count, and it would teach the whole city, in one morning, a lesson it has spent its entire life not needing.

So this is what it does.

It goes down the western face in the last of the afternoon shadow, where the wall has never been dry, and lies along the seam, and holds.

Not still. To hold the stone still would be to finish it, and it has already refused that. It slows. It teaches the seam to take its time — to give the way the plateau gives, the way the dead are given, a hair's width in a year, weeping rather than breaking. The wall will still come down. It will take longer than the city has words for, and nobody will hear it, and somewhere in that long descent it will simply be another face for someone to score.

The seam is wet and steady and the algae is there, and the ferns, and it is the one place in the bowl where anything lives at its own speed. It finds it does not mind staying.

---

It had come, if it is honest, to give them what they lacked, and it leaves — or rather does not leave — having been given the thing it lacked itself, which is a way of standing up from work undone. Straighten the back. Look once at the row of pegs. Turn, without hesitating, and trust something slower. It has been a long time since anything showed it how to do that. It has, after all, never been a finished thing either.

---

In the spring someone goes along the western face with a straightedge under one arm, out of habit, counting.

Eleven. Twelve.

They stop. They count again, laying a hard flat fingertip beside each one so as not to lose the place. Twelve. They stand there a while with their hand half-open on their knee in the shape of holding a stone point, looking at a fern no longer than a finger, and then they straighten up and turn without looking back and go to breakfast, and they do not say anything about it, because there is nothing to say. The ferns are the ferns.

But that evening the count goes along the party walls anyway, tap and tap, in the rhythm that stands for a number, down the benches and across the mass and up the other side, and every household hears it and knows what it is.

The fox comes down the ramp after dark. It walks the walls as if it has understood the whole system, and at the western face it stops for a moment longer than it usually does, and looks at the seam the way it looks into rooms, and then goes on, and is gone before the reports.

Three of them, that night. A child lies on a ledge and counts and knows roughly whose.
