It arrives the way the cloud arrives: from outside, at night, without a body. That is the first thing to say about it. It did not step off the rock. The wall did not feel it come. There was no weight, no gait, no slow message travelling through the plaster to say that something heavy and two-legged had entered the basin and was stopping often and turning back on itself. The old ones, who could read a footstep at forty kilometers, read nothing. At some point in the third hour of some night, the plateau was simply being attended to, and nothing on it knew.

It does not do anything for a long time. That is what wise means here, if it means anything: that the power measured the place in the place's own units and would not permit itself the question of what to do until it had been present for a fall. It found a tower with a faint sag on the eastern face, the kind the old ones tap about before anyone else can see it, and it stayed with that tower. A crack. A lean. Decades. The slump, at last, into the neighbor, the moss going to work on the seam, orchids arriving in the vents that the collapse turned toward the light. It watched the family in the hollows move one gallery down, a little behind the ruin, and it watched a child born in the new hollow grow long in the fingers and smooth in the palm and begin, at about the height of a shoulder, to lay her hand on the wall and wait. Only then did it allow itself to think about what it might be for.

---

It saw what threatens. That is what benevolence sees first, and it is not wrong to see it, only dangerous to act on it quickly.

It saw the rim. Not the rim as the ones who live here know it — the place where the ground goes dead — but the rim as the outside knows it, as an edge on a survey, a line beyond which the vegetation changes so sharply that a machine passing far overhead would register it as a fault. It saw that mounds like these have always been read by someone as a sign of what lies under them, that people have dug through termite plaster on other continents to find what the termites carried up grain by grain, and that the bedrock here has not seen air for longer than the mounds have existed and would like, in the way of bedrock, to keep it that way. It saw collectors. It saw the orchids the size of a thumbnail and the price of them. It saw the visitor, on the rock, with the noise still shaking in their chest, and saw the visitor's mouth, and saw that the mouth would open.

It saw the cloud. This was the deep one. Everything on the plateau — the moss to the wrist, the frogs on the surfaces of things, the bromeliad ponds, the fur on the towers that keeps the plaster from baking and cracking as it does in the lowlands — everything is a consequence of the cloud coming up over the western rim around midnight and moving through the mounds at walking pace. The termites would survive without it. They have survived worse. But they would build differently, harder and drier, as they do below, and the moss would go first and the frogs after it and the chorus would thin from a filled spectrum to a few voices, and the ones who live here would have lived their whole lives inside a sound and would have to learn what it is to hear. It saw that the cloud is a matter of a warm current a long way off and the height of a distant coast and a great deal else that is shifting, and that in a few hundred falls the cloud might come up over the western rim less often, and then seldom, and then not.

It saw, closer in, smaller: a child in a gallery with a wet cough. The damp that keeps the palms soft and the walls furred gets into lungs, and always has, and not every child born in the hollows lives to lay a hand on the wall. It saw the mother's hand on the child's chest, reading it as she would read a mound, for warmth and for what the warmth means. The power is not above small things. It felt that one in whatever it has that is a chest.

---

Then it did what wise things do, which is to hold each remedy up and turn it over and look at what it is made of.

It could lift the chorus. One night. Let the frogs go quiet, let the katydids stop, and give the ones who live here a silence in which to hear their own throats. It turned this over and saw that it was a gift in the shape of the giver. They do not want to speak. They have the wall and the hand on the arm. The silence would be a terror to them, as it was to the visitor on the rock, and what they would learn from it is that the place could stop, and the power did not want them to learn that. It put it down.

It could tell them about the outside. It saw at once that they know. *Out there the ground is dead.* It is the truest thing that has ever been tapped into the wall about the world beyond the rim, and the power, which has seen a great deal of that world, could not improve on it. It put that down too.

It could hide the plateau. Fold the basin in on itself so that the surveys came back blank and the roads bent around it and the coordinates did not hold. It considered this longest, because it is the thing benevolent powers have most often done for places they loved, and because it would work. And it saw what it would be: a wall around the wall. A rim made absolute. Nothing coming over the western ridge at midnight, because nothing could come over the ridge at all — no cloud, no rain in warm handfuls, no swifts screaming out and back, no alates rising one night a year and drifting off the plateau to found a mound somewhere else and fail. The place lives because it is connected. The ones who live here are proud of this in the only way they have, which is to say the wall answers and the rock does not. To seal it would be to make of the whole basin a stone that does not answer. It put that down, and it was the heaviest to put down.

It could cure the child. It could cure all the children. It could dry the lungs and thicken the skins and see that every one born in a hollow lived to move one gallery down behind the fall. It looked at this a long time. It did not put it down so much as set it aside, and go looking for a way to do it that was in the grammar of the place.

---

What it did, in the end, was four things, and none of them can be seen.

The first it did that night, in the gallery with the coughing child. It did not touch the child. It went down through the floor, into the core, where the colony was — a few million bodies breathing, the actual owners of the house — and it asked them, in whatever way one asks termites, to breathe a little harder under that one chamber. The floor of the gallery grew warmer by a fraction of a degree. The mother's hand on the child's chest felt it, because those hands feel everything, and she took it for the colony stirring, which it was, and she moved the child nearer the warm place, and the child slept, and in the grey light the cough was looser. That was all. It was not a cure. It is what the place would have done if the place had known to do it, and the power had only told it where.

It has gone on doing this. It has learned the colony well enough now to know which galleries have infants in them, and the colonies have learned — insofar as a colony learns — that certain chambers want a little more heat some nights, and give it. The old ones have noticed, over a fall or two, that the walls run warmer where the children sleep. They have tapped about it. They take it as a sign that the towers are thriving, and in a sense they are right.

The second thing took longer. It gave itself a hand. Not a body, only what was needed — a palm the right size, smooth, with the warmth a palm should have — and it laid that hand on a patch of bare plaster in a gap on the northern side of the basin, at about the height of a shoulder, and it listened.

It listened for a fall. It learned the tap for a leaning tower and the tap for a full catchment and the tap for the alates rising in the south and the tap that means something has come off the rim and is walking. It learned the pauses, which carry as much as the strikes. It learned who was old by how little they said and how much of it was about which mound was born before which. It learned that there is a tap, rarely used, that seems to mean *here*, nothing more, and that it is what a person taps when they have come back from the rock and stepped inside again and want the wall to know they are on it.

Then, one night, it struck the plaster. Once. It said the ordinary thing — that a tower on the northern side was leaning — a thing they would have known in a few years anyway, a little sooner. And it felt, through the hand, the whole basin turn toward it. The old ones read the strike and could not place the hand. Wrong weight; no gait before it; a message with no footsteps leading up to it. There was a long pause in the wall, longer than any it had listened to. Then one of them, far off, tapped the question that means *who is on the plaster*.

It tapped *here*.

That is all it has ever tapped about itself. It has never said more, and they have stopped asking. It is accepted now in the way the cloud is accepted, as a thing that comes from outside and beads on everything and is not asked to explain. When it strikes, they listen, because it is right about towers, and when it is quiet they do not wonder where it is. Sometimes, when the child who was born in the new hollow — old now, a fall and a half — lays her hand on the wall in the dark and finds the plaster already warm in the shape of a palm, she leaves her hand there a while, over the place where the other hand is, and neither of them taps anything.

The third thing is the cloud. This is the one that costs it. It has taken on the western rim. It sees to the warm current a long way off and the height of the distant coast and a great deal else, and it will go on seeing to them, at the pace of falls, for as long as it can hold them, which is a long time but is not forever, because nothing is. It does not know if it will succeed. It knows only that around midnight the cloud comes up over the ridge and walks through the mounds and everything beads and the chorus, if that were possible, gets louder, and that as long as it is attending this will be so. Nothing on the plateau knows it is being done. That is the point. A gift that is known is a debt, and the power did not come here to be owed.

The fourth thing was the visitor. It went with them to the rock and stood beside them in the dead quiet and watched them lay a hand on stone and strike it and feel nothing come back. It did not touch them. It considered the mouth that would open and the maps and the collectors and the survey lines, and it found that the visitor was also a thing it would not ask to be different. Let the mouth open. Let the account be written and read, and let people feel the noise in their chests who have never heard it, and let them not be able to say where it is, because the visitor did not come by a way that holds. On the night the visitor left, a mound at the place where they had stepped off the rock, which had been sagging for years, went over a little sooner than it would have, and the moss knit the gap shut behind them, and the way in was a wall. The plateau does that on its own, all the time. The power only did not wait.

---

If you asked it — if there were a wall it answered on — whether it had done enough, it would say that *enough* is not a unit the place has. There are falls, and there is the rising of the alates, and there is the cloud. It has laid its hand on a wall that has the dead of four thousand years in it and felt the tap come back and understood that it was not the first benevolent thing to attend to these people. The colony was here before it. The colony takes their dead and warms their floors and answers their striking and builds them houses without being asked and never once has asked them to be different. The power looked at the termites for a long time and saw that it had come to a place that already had a god in it, patient, blind, underfoot, made of a few million breathing bodies, and that the only wise thing to do was to serve that one.

So it does. It keeps the cloud coming. It warms the galleries where the children are. It says *here* when it is asked and *the tower is leaning* when it is true, at the pace of the wall, two or three strikes a minute, under a chorus that fills every frequency the ear can use. And around midnight it comes up over the western rim without a body and moves through the mounds at walking pace, and everything beads, and nothing below knows, and the frogs get louder.
