It arrives the way the afternoon weather arrives — out of nothing, on the western rim, and for the same reason: the mountain has been breathing for so long that eventually something notices.

It does not come as a visitor. It does not have boots to feel the heat through, or a shadow to ripple the ledges. It simply is, for a while, everywhere on the plateau at once, and this is the first thing it must correct in itself, because the massif is not a place meant to be seen all at once. It was meant to be walked. The lizards were meant to be mistaken for rock, and the rock for stillness, and the machine for dead, and each mistake corrected slowly, one basin at a time, until the last of them — the red wheel, the chipped channel — came together into a person. The power sees the person first, because it can, and then makes itself go back to the beginning and look properly, in order, at the speed the place allows. This takes what would be, for a walker, several days. It is the first thing the power does, and it is a kind of courtesy.

Then it thinks about what it might do.

It considers the keeper's body, because that is where a benevolent thing looks first. The plated soles. The mineral rime in the creases of the skin. The lungs, which have breathed sulphur for forty years and are not what lungs should be. The joints. The eyes, which have gone slightly milky in the way that steam and brightness together will do. It would be no trouble to mend all of this. It could give the keeper back the feet they came up with, the lungs, the sight. And it sees at once that this would be a theft. The soles are what let the keeper walk the plateau. The rime is what the mountain has given them in exchange for the years. To take it away would be to hand them a body that belongs to the desert floor and to say: *here, you are a stranger again.* The power does not do this.

It considers the loneliness. It looks for it the way you would look for a crack in a casting — running attention along the inside of the keeper, feeling for where the surface changes. And it finds the place. There is a shape in there that was once the shape of wanting someone else, a hollow the size and outline of a second person. But it is not empty. It has been filled in — by the bearing's note, by the weight of small hot bodies across the chest at night, by the walk to the seventh wellhead and the wave that runs ahead of the walker along the ledges. It is filled the way a mould is filled: completely, and with something that has taken the exact form of what is gone. The power looks at this for a long time. It does not know whether this is worse than the loneliness would have been. It suspects that it is not its business to know. It could put a face in there — bring someone up the north flank, a lost traveller, a curious child from the desert floor grown old enough to climb — and watch what the keeper did with company. It could. It sees the keeper turning from the bearing, the lizards lifting their heads at a walker who is not the walker, the arrangement flexing to admit a second person or failing to. It does not know which. It does not do it, because it is not willing to run that experiment on someone who did not ask.

It considers the drum.

This is the one that keeps it. The oil is nearly gone — a hand's depth at the bottom, forty years of careful pouring, and no way in the world to get more up the mountain now that the cable-way hangs like a dropped bucket. The keeper knows this. The keeper has known it for years, in the way they know when the rock is about to shift: not as a thought but as a pressure. When the oil goes the bearing will begin to sing, and then to grind, and then to stop, and the note that the whole plateau has learned the shape of will not shift and settle but simply end.

The power could fill the drum. Nobody would see it done. The keeper would lift the lid one morning and find it full, and would stand there in the doorway with the steam hissing behind them, and would — what? Assume they had miscounted? For forty years? No. They would know. They would know something had been here, something that could fill a drum, and the knowing would sit in the turbine house forever after like a second person, and the arrangement would have a term in it now, a debt, a *why*. The power sees the keeper looking up, for the first time in decades, at the sky, with a question. It sees the lizards, who have no word for above, and the keeper acquiring one. It does not fill the drum.

But it does something.

It looks at the bearing and it looks at the keeper, and it sees that they are the same age in every way that matters — that the metal is wearing at the rate the body is wearing, that the oil is going at the rate the years are going, and that the two ends are close but not the same. The bearing, as things stand, will outlast the keeper by a season. The keeper will die on the concrete under the coat of lizards, and the turbine will go on turning, and the note will go on, and for a season the lizards will wait for a walker who does not come and the red wheel will hang on its hook and the scale will thicken in the pipe until the flow chokes and the bearing sings and grinds and stops, with no one to hear it. Or the other way: the oil will fail first, and the keeper — still living, still able to walk the hot rock on their grey soles — will lie beside a machine that has stopped, and will listen to a silence where the note was, and will have nothing to keep.

The power does not lengthen either. It does not add oil or years. What it does is so small that it has no name in any language the mountain speaks: it moves the ends closer. A film of scale thinner here, thicker there, in the hollows of the metal where a keeper's bar cannot reach. A quarter-turn less friction at the shaft. Enough — only enough — that the bearing will last exactly as long as the hand that rests on it, and no longer, and not less. That neither will have to outlive the other. That is the whole of the intervention. It is indistinguishable from luck. It is, the power thinks, what luck would do if luck were kind.

Then it goes down the mountain, because there is one thing left it wants to know, and it follows the wire.

The line drops over the eastern rim and runs down the flank on wooden poles that have gone silver and split, and crosses the cold band where the desert lizards live their ordinary lives in cracks, and reaches the floor, and goes on for a long way across sand that has buried it in places and uncovered it in others. It ends in a box. The box is on a concrete pad much like the wellheads' pads, cracked the same way. From it one cable still runs, a short distance, to a pump. The pump sits on a well. The well is dry. The pump has been running for forty years, lifting nothing, cooling itself with the current that the mountain's breath sends down to it, and it will run until the note on the plateau stops, and then it too will stop, and the desert will not notice.

The power stands there — as much as it stands anywhere — and considers this. A machine on a mountain, kept turning by a person who has given their body to it, so that a pump in the desert can lift nothing from a well with nothing in it. It could make this mean something. It could put water back in the well. It could bring a stand of tamarisk up around the pad and a family of the small quick lizards into its shade and a bird to nest in it, so that the whole line from the massif to the sand was a circuit of something carried to something that needed it. It sees this very clearly. It is beautiful. It is also a lie about what the arrangement is, and the power has come to understand that the arrangement does not need to mean anything to be worth keeping. The keeper has stopped wondering where the wire goes. The pump does not know it lifts nothing. The lizards do not know what the bearing is for. Nobody in the whole system is doing what they do because of what it is for. They are doing it because they are near each other, and have been for long enough to move in step, and that is not a lesser thing than purpose. It may be the thing purpose is made of.

So it leaves the well dry.

It goes back up. It stays for the afternoon storm, and lets the rain hiss to steam around it and the plateau go white to knee height, and feels the keeper sitting inside with the door open and one hand on the warm metal, doing nothing, and it does nothing with them. It stays for the evening, and watches the cold come up the flanks and stop where it always stops. It stays long enough to see the keeper lie down on the concrete and the lizards come in through the peeled roof and settle, one by one, across the chest and along the legs and in the crook of the arm, until the person is a warm shape under a warmer coat and the two breathings — the mountain's and the keeper's — are so close in their rhythm that the power cannot, for a moment, tell which is which.

It thinks about staying longer. It thinks about staying until the end — being there when the hand slackens on the bearing and the note begins, in the same hour, to fail; watching the wave go over the ledges toward the east one last time and the heads turn and wait for a shift that does not settle but stops; watching the lizards on the mound lie still for a day, two, in the cooling channel, and then begin, without any visible decision, to move toward the seventh wellhead, where the rock is still hot and always will be; watching the red wheel hang on its hook and go, finally, to rust; watching the grey-green mat find the concrete floor and begin, at a millimetre a decade, to take it. It could stay for all of that. It would cost it nothing.

But it has noticed something about itself, over the days it has been here. It has noticed that it wants to. And it knows the difference between attention and possession, and it knows that the wanting is where the one becomes the other. The massif did not need watching before it came, and it does not need watching now, and to stay because the watching is sweet would be to make the place into something that is watched — to put a *for* into an arrangement that has never had one. The keeper does not look up. The lizards have no word for above. The power decides, at the last, that the kindest thing it has to give is the thing it gave first: to see the place at the speed the place allows, and then, having seen it, to be what everything else here is to everything else — near, for a while, and then not, and leaving no mark but the warmth.

It goes the way the storms go. The steam thins to threads behind it. The turbine turns at the speed that suits the bearing and the hand, and under the coat of small hot bodies the keeper sleeps the way the mountain sleeps, and the drizzle goes on falling from no sky at all, and nothing on the plateau notices that anything was ever here.
