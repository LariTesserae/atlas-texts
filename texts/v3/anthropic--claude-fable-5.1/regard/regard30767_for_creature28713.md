**What the power did**

It did not come the way you'd expect a power to come — from above, out of the light, with the cloud parting. Nothing parts that cloud. It came the way the city's other inhabitants came: along the water, drawn by the water, and it arrived without knowing exactly when it had arrived, because the plateau has no edges that announce themselves. One moment it was attending to the wider world, and the next it noticed that a piece of the world had gone quiet in a particular way — not the quiet of absence but the quiet of something that has finished tuning — and it turned toward that, and it was here.

The first thing it did was slow down.

This is not nothing. A power that is wise knows that its attention is a weight, and that a place which lives in the register just below being seen will shift under the pressure of being seen too quickly. So it matched the tempo. It let its own noticing lengthen toward evening and shorten toward dawn. It waited for the thud and let the thud be the unit of its time. In this way several years passed before it had done anything but look, and by the end of them it could not have said whether it was studying the city or had simply become one more thing arranged around the warmth in rings.

---

Then it took inventory, because benevolence begins as a list.

*The mountain is cooling.* True. On a scale of thousands of years the pulse beneath the plateau will lengthen past the point where the pumps can ride it, and then stop, and the vents will close, and the cloud will lift for the first time and the sky will come down on the black rock hard and cold and stay. The power could warm it. It considered this for a long time — the mountain has never asked to stop, because it has never asked anything — and set it down. To warm a thing that is cooling is to refuse it the one thing it is actually doing. The heat had settled into the rock and stopped arguing. The power would not start the argument again on its behalf.

*The channels are narrowing.* Also true. The mats lay their stone a fraction thicker with every warm pulse, and the terraces creep, and in some centuries the aqueducts will be conduits of stone in stone with a thread of water down the middle and no room for anything a meter long. The pale things' world is closing on them at the speed of drip. The power could scrape the terraces. It could hold the mats back. It considered this and found that the mats were the only masons still working, that they were the city's one act of ongoing making, and that a place which had stopped building would be a different place than the one it had come to. And the pale things were already answering — in the narrowest channels the young grew shorter, and the shorter ones bred, and the water, as it does, was finding its way. The design was still reading the rock. The power decided not to read over its shoulder.

*The surplus is wasted.* False, on inspection. The water over the rim goes down to the valley and the valley, whatever it is, has water. The water that returns to the basin returns to the basin. Nothing here is lost. It is only not delivered, and *delivered* was the builders' word, and the builders were gone.

*The watch is gone.* True. The power could bring them back — not by force, but there are ways. A rumor of good water. A road made slightly easier. Within a generation the beds would have bedding on them and the tables would have forty people at them and the city would be minded again. The power held this idea longer than any of the others, because it was the one that looked most like help. And then it set it down too, because it could not find anyone the city was failing. The mats did not miss the watch. The pale things did not know there had been one. The gliders came to the breath, not the buildings. A minded city would be a city for the minders, and the power was not sure there was anyone left it would be for.

Which left the one.

---

There was one. The power had known this since the second year, the way you know a grazer is in the water — by the faint grain in the light, by a cup on a sill, by a gasp from a settling tank a shade too deep. It had not gone looking. It had let the one surface on the one's own schedule, which was the city's schedule, which was slow.

A person. Older than they had been when they came, though *older* had less purchase here than elsewhere; the plateau does not push you through time so much as let you drift in it. Skin the soft grey of the light. Hair long and gone the color of the cloud. They slept in a room with twelve iron beds and used one of them, and the room was warm as a bath, and in the mornings — the hours when the interval shortened — they walked to a sill where the condensation ran into a stone cup and drank, and then they walked to a settling tank near the rim and waited with a hooked iron rod until a pale thing surfaced to breathe, and took it, and ate it raw in the plaza with the heat coming up through the soles of their feet. They did not speak. There had been no reason to speak for a length of time the power measured in thousands of thuds. They breathed in when the steam rose and out when it thinned.

The power looked for suffering. It is good at this; it is most of what a benevolent power does with its attention. It found none. There was no fear on the plateau, because nothing here had ever come for them. No hunger, because the water and the warmth and the pale things came at intervals so regular they had ceased to be events. No grief that the power could find — whatever they had walked away from at the bottom of the aqueduct had been walked away from long enough to become geology.

It looked for joy. It found none of that either.

What it found was accord. The person and the place had come into a fit so exact that the seam had closed. Their pulse was the pump's pulse. Their sleep was the mountain's slowing. When the vents opened a little wider in the hours before what would be dawn, they woke, not because anything had woken them but because the plateau was waking and they were, by now, a part of the plateau. The city was not tending them, quite, and they were not tending it. They were the same thing wearing two textures, one of stone and one of skin.

And the power sat with that for a long time — years, on the city's clock; a few hundred thuds — because it did not know whether accord was enough. There is a kind of wisdom that says a life at rest is not less than a life in motion, that a person who has found the one place on earth that asks nothing of them and answers everything has not lost but arrived, and that a benevolent power which disturbs that is only a meddler with good posture. The power knew this wisdom. It mostly believed it.

But there was one thing missing, and the power found it by noticing what the person did not do.

They did not look at the rim.

Not once. Their tank was near it; the cloud tore open there, sometimes, and the real light came in hard and cold, and the aqueduct went over on its arches and down into the mist. They fished with their back to it. They ate facing the city. It was not that they had decided not to leave. Deciding is an act; this was the absence of one. The thought that there was an *out*, that the arches led somewhere, that their feet could take them along the water and over the edge and down — that thought had simply stopped being one of the thoughts they had. It had rotted, like a wooden handle, in the wet. What remained was iron and stone.

And the power thought: a life can be at rest and still be whole. But a life that has lost the shape of its own choosing is not at rest. It is asleep in a way that does not know it is asleep. And that — not the staying, but the not-knowing that staying was a thing one did — was the only lack on the whole plateau that it could find, and it was small, and it could be given back without giving anything else.

---

So here is what the power did.

It waited for the longest pause.

Every night, in the hours when the interval stretches furthest, there is a moment when the thud comes late — a beat past comfortable — and in the pause before it the whole city holds still. The vents shut. The beads hang on the fins unshed. The pale things close their mouths in the dark and the gliders hang motionless at the seam and the mats, even the mats, wait for the warmth to tell them to lay the next film of stone. Everything on the plateau is silent together, listening for the floor.

The power did not lengthen the pause. It did not shorten it. It did nothing to the mountain, or the pumps, or the water, or the cloud.

It reached into the silence, in the room with the twelve beds, and it let the person hear their own heart.

That is all. Not louder than it was. Not slower or faster. Only *audible*, for the length of one held breath, in the one moment in the whole cycle when nothing else on the plateau was making a sound — so that there was the silence of the city, total, and inside the silence, out of step, something beating that was not the city. Something that had its own interval. Something that had been keeping its own time all along, underneath, and had only been drowned.

Then the floor spoke, once, and the steam went up, and everything let go together.

And the person lay in the dark with the heat coming up through the iron of the bed, and was — for the first time in a span the power did not try to measure — aware of being a separate thing. Not unhappy. Not frightened. Only *distinct*. A heart inside a body inside a room inside a city inside a mountain, and the heart was not the mountain's, and had never been, and could go where it liked.

The power did not tell them where to go. It did not make the rim brighter or the arches easier or the valley kinder. It did not put a thought in their head about what lay below, or who might still be there, or whether the cold would be worth it. It had given back one thing — the *could* — and the *could* was theirs, and the power had no right to the *should*.

---

I do not know what they did. The power knows, but the power is not telling, in the same way the city does not answer, and I think for the same reason: because the answer belongs to the one who made it.

Maybe in the morning they drank from the sill and walked to the tank and turned, before fishing, and looked at the rim for a while — not long — and then turned back and fished, and ate, and the day went on, and the accord closed over again, but with a seam in it now, a hairline where a choice had been made and could be made again. That would be enough. That would be the whole of what the power wanted.

Or maybe they walked out along the aqueduct, because it is the only line in the fog you can trust, and the cloud tore open at the edge and the light hit them like a slap and they went down on the arches into the cold with their grey skin and their cloud-colored hair and not one possession, and the city behind them took a breath and did not notice they were gone. That would also be enough.

What the power did after was the same in either case. It stayed.

Not to watch over — the city does not need watching over; it has demonstrated that. It stayed because it had found, in all its inventory, one thing it could do that the place could not do for itself, and that thing was not fixing or warming or minding. It was *knowing*. The mountain does not know anything lives on it. The mats do not know the pale things. The pale things do not know what comes from above. The person had, until one late thud, not quite known they were there. And the power thought that a place so entirely unknown to itself should be known by something. Not changed. Held in mind. The way you hold a banked fire in mind through the night, not because it needs you, but because it is a good fire and someone ought to.

So it arranged itself in the outermost ring, past the gliders, past the cold edge of the cloud, and let its attention take the city's time. When the interval lengthens it slows. When the vents open it quickens. It is patient, as everything here is patient, and it is doing what it was already doing, and one day — thousands of years on, when the pulse finally stretches past what the pumps can ride and the last thud comes and does not come again — it will be there for that too. Not to prevent it. To attend it. So that when the cloud lifts for the first and last time and the sky comes down hard and cold on the black rock, there will be one thing present that remembers the heat, and the weeping towers, and the pale things breathing in the dark, and the person who once, in the longest pause of the night, heard their own heart and found out they had one.

That is what it would do. It is not very much. I think it is the most that wisdom permits.
