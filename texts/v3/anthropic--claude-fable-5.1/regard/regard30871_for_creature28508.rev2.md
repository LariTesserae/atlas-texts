It does not arrive. Nothing arrives here that the mat does not close over, and a power that is wise has watched enough places to know that arriving is a kind of tearing. So it comes the way the place itself comes to a person on the flank: first as a pressure, then as a quality of the air, and it has been attending for some while before it lets itself notice that it is attending.

What drew it was the constancy. Most things in the world vary, and a benevolent power listens for variation the way a boiler tender listens for his beat — a change is where the work is. This place does not change. Cloud in, water out, the same roar at any hour of any season. A thing that does not vary is either dead or very sure of itself, and the power came to find out which.

---

It looks, first, the way anyone would: from standing height. Green to the glare, light from above and beneath, the dozen dark towers with their tails of steam. Then it looks the way no one can. It goes down through the moss into the brown, into the black, into the warm cinder, and follows the water sideways for a kilometer at the pace of a sleep. It counts the tardigrades. It finds the pale worms in the leather's white felt and follows the felt down into the ash and watches it eat stone, molecule by molecule, and draw the metal up. It finds the midge larvae lying in their commas, fifty weeks from the pink. It goes up the stack of the west rig and finds the birds in the firebox cavity of the dead boiler beyond it, a dozen pairs deep in the rust.

Then it asks itself the question it came with: which of these is the one who lives here?

It considers the mat. The mat is the obvious answer, the honest one, and the power distrusts it a little for that reason. It considers the leather — the thing that reads the rock and concentrates what it finds and shows the men where to open the ground — and recognizes in it something of its own nature, a reader, a gatherer, and is briefly moved by the kinship and then sets that aside too, because a wise power knows how easily it sees itself in things. It considers the roar, which is the mat's exhale, which is the cloud's return. It considers the forty. It considers one of the forty, the kind of man who goes out alone during the pink and makes no sign afterward. It considers the birds.

And it comes to think that the question is shaped wrong. It is a lowland question, the kind that goes down the cable with the ingots. Here, the leather is in the mat and the mat holds the mountain and the mountain feeds the leather; the men follow the leather and their dead feed the mat and their iron houses the birds; the midges die into the brown and the brown drinks the cloud. To ask which of these is the being is to ask which finger is the hand. The power stops asking and simply attends to the whole of it, and finds that this is easier, and that the whole of it is — not dead. Sure of itself.

---

It considers what it could do. It is a power; there is a good deal it could do, and wisdom is largely the practice of not doing most of it. It goes through the list carefully, because that is what care is.

It could stop the rigs. It looks at this a long time. The men are taking a rounding error. They are not cruel to the ground — they step on the domes, they lay boards, they hate the mat only in the small daily way one hates weather. The mat has no complaint the power can find; it closes over the pads in a season and will close over the last rig as it closed over the first. If the engines stopped the men would go down the cable and be poorer and the plateau would be exactly as it is, minus the heartbeats inside the exhale, and the power finds — a little to its surprise — that it would miss those. No.

It could give the men back their hearing. It looks at the ones who are losing it slowly and do not talk about it, and at the ones who came up already deaf and are faintly amused. To hear, here, is to hear the roar. A man given his hearing back would be given a wall. The deaf are at no disadvantage; the sign-language has words the tram car does not; a fist held against the breastbone and opened and closed says something no ear has ever confirmed. To heal them would be to hand them a wound and call it a gift. No.

It could look ahead to what the mountain will do, and it does look, because it cannot help it. A finger's width a year, and then a year when it is not a finger's width. It sees the day. It sees the shape of the cinder afterward and the shape of the green that comes back over it, slower this time, because the mat will have to start from the edges. It considers telling someone. But a warning would be a thing that arrived, and it would go down the cable in a letter, and the letter would dry into ridges and be read in a shed doorway by someone who would have to decide what to do with it, and what could they do? Leave. They will leave anyway, most of them, long before. The mountain does not need to be warned about itself. No.

It could stop the roar for one second — hold the whole curtain of water still around the whole perimeter for the length of a breath — so that the forty would hear silence and know, once, that what they live inside is a sound. It imagines this very precisely. Forty men up out of blankets with their boots unlaced. Every bird off every boiler housing at once. The shed doors banging. And then the roar returning, and the rest of their lives spent knowing something they already knew, and telling it to new arrivals who would not believe them. The power has seen what that does to people. No.

There is one loss it keeps returning to. The birds sing, and no one hears it, and no one ever will. This is the only thing on the plateau that seems to the power to be a true deprivation — a thing that exists and is not received. It considers making the song audible. It could. It has the wisdom to be suspicious of how much it wants to.

So before it does anything it listens, properly, the way it listened to the roar.

And the song is not unheard. The birds land bellies-down on warm iron. The power had taken this for the warmth, as the men do, but the iron is also a nerve. A bird singing against a boiler housing puts the pulse-and-stop into the metal, and the metal carries it down the pipe run to the next housing, and a bird lying flat on that one feels it through its breast. The dead rigs are singing houses; the tenement of cribwork on the old pad hums with it if you are small enough and pressed close enough. The birds did not come here in spite of the roar. They came because the men had laid down, without knowing, a plateau-wide instrument, and the birds are the only ones on it who can play it.

The song was never unheard. It was only unheard by men, and the men have the sign, and the sign is exactly right. The power lets that one go with something like relief.

---

What it does first, then, is hear.

It takes the roar into itself entire — every kilometer of the curtain, every seep off every rock, the pressure of the whole saturated sponge pushing water into the boilers without a pump — and it holds it, and it slows it. To the men the roar has no rhythm. To the power, listening at the pace of the rock clock rather than the water clock, there is one. Not a year's. Longer. The cinder settles and the mat shifts its weight and the drain from the rim thickens a little on the south side for a decade and then a little on the north. The mountain breathes, a finger a year, and the mat answers. It is not speech. But it has the shape the boiler tender felt through his palm — a pulse and a stop and a pulse — and the power stays with it for what is, to the plateau, an afternoon, and is, to the power, a long time.

It does not translate it. It is not sure it is the kind of thing that translates. But it hears that it is being said, and a wise power knows that being heard is not nothing. It is possibly the most that anyone can do for anyone.

---

The second thing it does is small and no one sees it.

The cinder under the middle cluster's east pad is going to open. It has been going to open for two years, a void the size of a bunkhouse working its way upward through the ash. It would have opened in the cold season, at night, under the shed with nine men asleep in it, and the mat would have bridged the hole in a month and thrown up a sixth low ridge, and no one would have remembered being told what it was.

The power leans on nothing. It does not lift or push. It only asks the void, in whatever way a power asks a void, to finish its work in the warm season instead, at midday, when the pad is empty and the men are two hundred meters off at the rig. And the void does — the mat dips two hands' width in a line along the pad's edge, a crease anyone can see, and the man with the pole walks over and tests it and the pole goes in to his shoulder, and he turns to the others and makes the sign, two hands cupped and lowered, fingers curling. They move the shed that week. They are a little put out about the boards. That is all. No one thinks anything of it, and the power is glad, because a mercy that is thought about becomes a story, and a story is a thing that arrives.

---

The third thing it does not do, but watches.

The rail layer has been saying for eleven years that the roar is quieter at the rim, and no one has believed him. The power knows he is right, because it has stood there too — the curtain at its source is a single fall, and the wall is what you hear in the middle when every fall on every horizon reaches you at once. It considers arranging something. A cart off the end of a spur, so a second man would have to go out and look.

It does not have to. A cart goes off the end of a spur on its own, the rails having gone out of true, and the pad boss sends the young tender to see where the spur stops now. The tender walks out with his pole to the place where the rails end in midair, and stands where the rail layer stands, and looks down into it. He comes back across the flat and does not go to the sheds. He walks to the west rig, where the rail layer is re-laying, and stands in front of him, and makes the sign for the roar — flat hand, perfectly still, at the level of the eyes — and then lowers the hand a little.

The rail layer looks at him for a while. Then he goes back to his rails. That evening he makes a sign to the young tender across the bunkhouse that is not any of the ones the power has learned, and the tender laughs, and the power understands that the place did this itself, and that it need not have come at all, and that this is the best thing it has found out today.

---

It goes and looks at the ridges. Four of them, or five, near the middle cluster, the moss over them the same as the moss anywhere. It could open the ground and see. It does not. It learns their names, which no one on the plateau knows and no one in the lowlands has needed for years, and it does not do anything with the names. It only carries them. That is a thing a power can do that the mat cannot, and it seems to the power a fair division: the mat keeps the men, the power keeps the names, and neither tells the other.

---

The pink comes. The domes flush across fifteen kilometers and the midges rise through the moss in a gauze that closes behind you, and one man goes out alone, as one man does every year, and stands in it for the length of a shift with the light coming up off the cloud and down off the sky. The power stands there too. It does not touch him. It is careful not to be a presence he could feel, because he did not come out here to be accompanied.

He makes no sign. Neither does it. On the sixth day the midges settle and the mat eats them and the green resumes, and the man goes back to his rig, and neither of them says anything to anyone.

---

The power does not leave. Leaving is also a kind of arriving, seen from the other side. It stays as it came, as a pressure, a quality of the air, thinner than steam and further off than the cloud floor, doing nothing, closing over nothing, hearing.

The men will go down the cable one by one. The rigs will rust orange and tip and fill with birds and then the birds will go too, when the last warm iron cools. The mat will lie on its heap and hold it in shape and drink the cloud and let it over the edge. And the roar, which no one will be left to hear as anything but silence, will be heard by one thing as what it is — a very slow voice saying a very long thing to a mountain that is not finished — and the power will still be listening, and will still not understand it, and will still have decided that this is enough.
