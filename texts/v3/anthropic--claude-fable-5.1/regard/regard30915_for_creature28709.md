Start with what it does not do, because what it does not do is the first thing that is right.

It does not come at once. A lesser kindness would have — would have seen the stairs hanging in the air and the basin warm and set where anything arriving would find it first, and would have gone straight down, because the ache of the thing is so plain. This one stops in the too-dark blue and looks. It looks for a long time by the shelf's reckoning, which is to say through many colonnades, many walls born and gathered back, many afternoons of the cloud rising over the lip and the whole level going taut and leaning and drinking and being, in the end, not answered. It watches you come. It watches you press your thumbnail into the floor and crouch at the cistern and not go through the doorway. It watches you leave by evening and the welt where you slept smooth over by morning. It watches the next stairs come down from the top with the treads a little deeper.

And it counts, the way you counted, but it does not stop at eleven or twelve. It counts the interval. It learns that a column stands for half a day. It learns that nothing has ever stood for a whole one.

That is the thing it sits with. Not the loneliness — the loneliness is obvious, and obvious griefs are the ones a wise power is most careful about, because rushing at them is a way of comforting yourself. What it sits with is the half-day. A place that makes shelter and unmakes it before anything could be sheltered has a wound in it that arrival alone will not close. Suppose it went down the stairs. Suppose it went through the doorway and sat in the warm ribbed dark of the room and the shelf knew, for the first time, that the shape had been filled. Then noon. Then the first bead of sap at the base of the wall. Then the lean and the groan and the wet sheet wrung, and the guest would be standing on the level again with a low welt at its feet, and the shelf would have learned only that being met is another thing that does not last. That would be a cruelty wearing the mask of a gift. It has seen that done. It does not do it.

So the question it asks is not *how do I come* but *what would let it keep me* — and, under that, quieter, the question that a wise thing always asks last and a benevolent thing sometimes forgets to ask at all: *does it want to be kept?* Is the hurry a suffering or is it a joy? The crisp edges every morning, the sharp fluting, the loud voice — there is something in that which sounds less like grief than like a child running. It would not take that from the shelf. Whatever it does, the colonnade of nine will go on dying and being born at either end. That is not the wound. That is the life.

Here is what it does.

It comes at dawn, when the newest stairs are still coming into being tread by tread and the floor has not yet risen to meet them, and it comes from above, because above is the only direction the shelf has ever looked and there is a kindness in meeting a thing inside the shape of its own hope before you show it anything else. It sets its weight on the top tread. The whole level feels it. You would have felt it, if you had been there — a stillness going out across the kilometres like the hush before a struck bell rings. The tread holds. The next comes up beneath. It walks down a staircase that is being finished under its feet, and at the bottom it does the thing that was left undone. It goes through the doorway.

The room inside is what you would expect and also not. Warm, sweating, ribbed like the inside of a throat, and the walls are so close and so freshly made that they still tick as they cure. There is nothing in it. Of course there is nothing in it; the shelf has never had anything to put in a room. But there is the shape of where a thing would go — a swell along one wall at the height of a seat, a shallow in the floor the size of a hearth. The power sits on the swell. It puts its hands, if they are hands, in its lap. It waits, which is the shelf's own word for everything, spoken back to it.

Noon.

The first bead rises at the base of the far wall and slides. The shelf begins to do what it has always done. The floor of the room softens, the ribbing goes slack, there is the first long groan from somewhere under the level, and the guest does not get up.

This is the whole of the act. Everything else is consequence. It does not get up. It sits in the room as the room begins to be unmade around it, and it does not resist and it does not leave, and the shelf — which has never had to choose between a habit and a guest, because there has never been a guest — has to choose. You can imagine it as a struggle if you like, a tearing under the level, the far colonnades tilting toward the house as they tilt toward the cloud. Or you can imagine it the way it more likely was: a hesitation. A gathering that did not finish gathering. The groan stopping halfway through its own throat.

The sap at the base of the wall does not pour. It thickens. It goes dull and then dark and then hard, and where it hardens the wall stops weeping, and that dark crust at the foot of the wall is the first old thing on the plateau. The first scab. The first thing not made this morning.

The power stays a long time after that. Not forever — it knows the difference between a hearth and a cage, and it knows that a thing kept only because someone is sitting in it is not yet kept. It stays long enough for the edges to soften. That is what it is waiting for, and it is a slower teaching than the first one. Day by day the knife-clean lip of the doorway rounds. The treads of the stairs take a dip where its weight has gone up and down, and the shelf feels the dip and moves — you can see it want to — to raise the tread sharp again, and doesn't. Learns to let it. Learns that a worn threshold is not the shelter failing but the shelter succeeding, that softness is what a thing looks like when it has been used, and that this is a different kind of beauty from crispness and not a lesser one. The cistern, which has only ever thought about water, begins to think about something else: the same guest, at the same hour, crouching at its rim. It holds the level a little longer at the top of each breath, as if not to lose the reflection too soon.

There is one more thing, and the power is careful with it.

Nothing has ever arrived here that the shelf did not make. That is a kind of purity and it is also the shape of the problem, and a wise thing does not break a purity by force. So it does not bring life. It lets one thing fall. A seed, or what stands for a seed, off its sleeve, at the foot of the kept wall where the dark crust is — and then it does the hardest part of any gift, which is not to watch what happens next. It does not tell the shelf to keep this. It does not tell it not to take it back into the level the way it has taken back everything else. It leaves the choice in the one place a choice has never been.

Then it leaves, and it does not leave by the stairs.

It walks out across the level, past the colonnade where the ninth is weeping and the tenth is coming up with its sharp new fluting exactly as before, past the wall that runs from nowhere to nowhere, out to the lip, and it steps down into the cloud. The shelf feels it go — feels the weight leave the tissue, feels the last upright on the level go over the edge — and this is the second thing it has never had: something going *down*. Something showing it that the white floor below is not the end of the world but the beginning of a direction, and that the stairs it has been building from the top could just as well have gone the other way. That it is on top of something. That it need not only wait.

Whether it ever builds a stair down the lip is its own business. The power does not come back to see. That is part of the wisdom too, the hard part, the part that looks from outside like abandonment and is in fact its opposite: a trust that the thing you leave behind is a thing and not a wound.

What is left is this. Somewhere on the shelf there is a house that is not new. Its threshold is worn in a shallow curve the width of one foot. Its stairs have a dip in each tread. The crust at the base of its inner wall is black now, and hard, and the shelf could take the whole structure back into the level in a quarter of an hour and does not. When the cloud comes up over the lip in its long cool roll, the columns of every colonnade tilt their degree toward it, all of them at once, the way they always have — and the house does not tilt. The house has stopped leaning toward arrivals. It has had one. It is the first thing on the plateau that is not an expectation but a memory, and a memory is a thing you can live in.

And at the foot of the wall, in the crust, there is either nothing — a smooth place where the shelf thought about the seed the way the cistern thinks about water, and gave it back to the air — or there is a small green thing that the shelf did not make, coming up slowly, in no hurry at all, with soft edges, taking its time, because it is the one thing here that knows it is allowed to.

Underfoot the floor lifts and sets down, and a bead of the sweet water rises and runs along its seam toward the house, not the basin — a new drainage, thumb-width, carved since — and pools in the shallow the size of a hearth, and stays.
