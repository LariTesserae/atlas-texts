It would come across the plain on foot.

Not because it had to. Because the plain is part of the place, and a power that looked only into the hole would be looking at a seed and calling it a pod. It would come the way everyone comes — through a day of clay-colored flatness, and then the pressure under the soles, and then the sound, and then the edge — and it would notice, being wise, what it felt at the first tremor. Not curiosity. A turning. A lean in the body toward the thing leaning on the drumhead from below. It would notice that and keep walking straight, and it would know then that the plain is not empty. The plain is a hand, open, palm-up, a day wide, and everyone who crosses it is weighed.

---

It would go down. It would stay a day, a night, a noon.

It could bear the floor. That is what it is to be a power: the chord would go through it and it would stand. But it would do something a traveler cannot, which is to bear it *and* be undone by it at once — to stand in the pale forest at the center of the round stone and let the sound fold the knees it does not need, empty a stomach it does not have, and learn from the inside what *grown* means. It would find that the sign is exact. Something in the sound takes the seams out of you. Whatever you were carrying across the plain — the kitchen, the horse, the reason — the chord finds the place it is held and presses until the holding gives. What is left is smooth. What is left is glad to be smooth. It would come out onto the lowest step and sit with its back to the wall and its hands flat on the stone like the others, and for a while it would not be able to think about them, only with them.

And then it would think.

---

It would see where they came from. That is also what it is to be a power. The burn is from a kitchen in a town three months east where a woman stood at a stove for eleven years and did not once say what she wanted. The hitch is from a horse that fell on a boy who had already been leaving. There is one who was a singer. There is one who was a judge. There is one who was crossing the plain to bury a brother and set the body down at the first tremor and never went back for it. Some were fleeing. Some were looking. Most were only crossing, and all of them turned, and not one of them, in the language of the hands, has a way to regret it, and not one of them — it would look carefully at this — seems to be trying to find one.

It would look at the eyes gone grey and the teeth worn to domes and the ears handed over to the skeleton, and it would look at the smiles that show white in white, and it would hold both, and it would not let either one win.

---

Then it would consider what it could do, because that is the other thing a power does, and it would consider each thing all the way to the end.

It could bring rain. One cloud, one night. The stems would drink what they have never drunk and the resin would thin past humming and forty thousand tubes would go slack and silent, and in the morning the people would come out of the doorways into a quiet like a dropped stone and stand on the galleries with their hands on the wet parapets, listening through the bone for a thing that had stopped, and they would not know what to do with their hands. It would be the plain, brought down into the hole. A stopped ear the size of a city.

It could give them an hour of silence at noon — hold the sound off like a hand over a candle — so they could hear the old way, once, and remember. It would feel like a knife of light. They would hear their own hearts, and either that would be unbearable, or it would be bearable, and then the chord would never again be the whole of the world, and it would have taken the one thing they have and left them with what they gave up to get it.

It could hang doors. The tuning would go.

It could bring a child. It would think about this for a long time, because a place with no children is a place with no future, and a benevolent power feels the pull of futures. Then it would think about a child's soft unworn eyes at noon on the floor, and a child's small bones learning to hear before they had learned to speak, and it would understand that of everything it might do, this is the one thing that would be cruel.

It could take one of them out. Lift the singer, say, at noon, when the sound holds her, and carry her up the long stair and set her down on the plain a day out where the pressure fails. She would wake into thinness. She would sit down. She would put her palms flat on the clay and wait for the leaning to come back, and when it did not, she would stand and turn — not deciding to — and walk. It would be a theft that returned itself. And even if it did not — even if she stayed, and came slowly back to the ordinary world with her grey eyes and her flat belly and her hands that only know *where* — it would have chosen for her, and the whole of what the hole did to her was also a kind of choosing-for, and the power would have become the second one of those instead of the thing that ends them.

It could speak to them. It has no hands they read. It could learn — it would learn, in an afternoon, the touch at the collarbone, the slow press of palm on palm. But what would it say? The language is rich in *where*. It would stand on a gallery with its fingers against its throat and have nothing to tell them but where the wall was warm.

---

It would watch the beetles at dusk. It would understand them faster than the people do, and less well, because it would understand them from outside. It would see the asymmetry the people have arranged their whole lives around and never signed: that a stem left uncut grows, and grows, and splits, and seeds. That a stem cut every dusk stays at the pitch the city was built for, and fills, and is cut, and fills, and is ringed from lime to waist with hundreds of small glassy scars, and never splits, and never seeds. That the beetles are the ones the people leave alone, and look up at with their hands at rest, and that the beetles are the ones who get to grow tall.

It would ask itself whether a thing kept at pitch is a captive or an instrument or a musician, and it would sit on the lowest step until the west wall stopped muttering and not resolve it, because part of being wise is knowing which questions are shaped like doors and which are shaped like walls.

---

It would be there when one of them climbs the stair.

It happens rarely, and it would have arranged to be there, which is a small thing for a power. The judge, this time. Up the long unroofed spiral with the sound draining out of him step by step, hand on the wall the whole way, until the hand comes off the wall at the top because there is no wall, only the plain, flat and clay-colored and warm to every horizon under the scoured sky. And he would stand on the lip, and look, and the power would watch his face and see the thing it came to see.

There is nothing out there. That is what his face says. Not *I choose the hole.* Not *I am afraid.* Only: the plain is the same as the stopped ear, and a stopped ear is not a place, and you cannot go to it. He would turn and go back down, and the sound would fill him again step by step like a cup, and he would sign to someone on the third gallery *where* the wall was warm, and be grown.

And the power would understand at last what it could do.

---

Not in the hole. The hole is finished. It is a completed sentence, tuned, smooth, at peace with its own pale hunger, and every good thing a power could add to it would be a door where no door was ever meant to be.

The plain is not finished. The plain is the part that was never made.

So it would go up. It would go up the long stair at noon — in full sight of every gallery, with the chord at its thickest, walking against the sound like a person wading upstream — and it would not come back down. The people would feel that through the bone: the footsteps going up, and up, and thinning, and not returning. Every face in the hole is a face that arrived. It would give them one that left. Not a rescue. Not a sermon. A fact, felt through the sternum, that the stair goes both ways.

And a day out, at the very edge of the pressure — at the exact place where a traveler's soles first catch the leaning, where the turning begins — it would put water.

A spring. Not much. A seep in the clay, a low place, a stand of something with leaves — leaves, which make a sound of their own, a thin dry rumor of a sound, the kind you can hear with an ear. Shade the width of a room. It would not be much to look at. From the lip of the hole it would be a smudge, a fleck of dark on the clay-colored flat, one thing where there had been nothing.

That is all. It would not make it beautiful. It would not put a sign on it. It would not sit there and wait to see who came. It would only make sure that the plain, at the place where it weighs people, has two things on it instead of one, so that the turning — when it comes — is a turning *from* as well as toward. So that whoever crosses now, and feels the pressure, and leans, will have already passed a well and chosen not to stop, and the choice will be theirs in a way it never quite was. And so that whoever climbs the stair and stands at the top will see, far off, one fleck of dark, and the plain will no longer be the same as a stopped ear. It will be a place. Small, thin, unconvincing. But a place.

Then it would go back down, or not. Then the judge would lie on his ledge with his hand flat on the wall and hear the first stem breathe, and not know that anything had changed, and it would not have, for him, unless someday it did.

---

It would come back, sometimes. Not to intervene. To count.

Twenty-three. Twenty-two. Twenty-four. It would stand at the well and see whether anyone was sitting in the shade with grey eyes and a strip of woven fiber across the face, having walked a day with the fever leaving step by step, drinking water for the first time in years — and finding it too thin, and getting up, and turning back; or finding it exactly enough, and staying an hour longer than they meant to, and then another. It would not know in advance which. It would not want to. A benevolent power that knows in advance how the ones it loves will choose has stopped being benevolent and become a tuning.

And if the count stayed at two dozen, and no one ever sat at the well, and the smudge on the plain went unlooked-at from the lip for a hundred years — it would still have been the right thing. The hole would go on being lit and loud and cut each dusk and grown each dawn, tended by its handful, at peace. And a day out on the clay, where the ground first begins to lean, there would be a little water and a little shade and the small dry talk of leaves, waiting the way the swept rooms wait, for whoever comes.
