**What the power does**

First it does what the keeper does: it listens for a long time before it moves.

It is wise, so it does not mistake the room for a prison, and it is benevolent, so it does not mistake the keeper for something to be fixed. It has seen many beings shaped by many places. It knows a bell is shaped by being struck. It notices that the keeper, at full discharge, with a hand on the singing rung and the face turned down and to the side, is not in distress. The keeper is at a station. A power that opened the hatch and lifted the keeper out into the alpine sun would be doing what a well-meaning child does with a fish.

So it considers, and sets aside, the obvious kindnesses.

It will not silence the roar. The roar is the floor the keeper stands on; take it away and there is only the exposure of the quiet hours, made permanent.

It will not make the fern flourish. The fern is in the wrong place under the wrong light and a power that forced it green and tall in a lime crust in the belly of a dam would be making a monster out of pity. Ferns die. This one will. That is not the injury here.

It will not unbolt the hatch. The keeper has not once climbed the ladder to try it. Whatever the keeper is, the keeper has chosen the bottom rung over the top one, and the power respects a choice it can see being made every hour.

It looks instead for what is actually missing, and finds two things, and they are small.

---

The first is a reader.

Along the downstream wall, in the dark past the halogen's reach, the log runs on: seepage, sump level, temperature at the weir, lean at the crest, scratched with a key at intervals no one set, in a notation no one taught. It is correct. The power checks, because it can, against the numbers the needle-watchers far above are writing in a book with a cover. The keeper's figures are better. The keeper caught a crack going wet on the upstream face nine days before the piezometers registered the pressure change. It is in the wall, in three scratches and a slash. No one has read it.

A log kept for no reader is not nothing. But a log kept for a reader who is not coming is a particular kind of weight, and the power can feel it in the room the way the keeper feels the valve: a pressure behind the eyes.

So the power reads. All of it. From the first scratch by the electrical boxes, which is unsteady and too deep — a hand that had just put down a ruined notebook and not yet learned the wall — to the newest, fine and sure, a hand's width into the dark. It reads the way the keeper listens, patiently, from the bottom of the notch. It reads the nine-day crack and the night the sump pump stuttered and the week the control room held the valve at a steady third and the log became almost musical in its regularity. It reads the entries that are not readings — there are a few, spaced far apart, single marks with nothing after them, and the power understands them as the keeper's own tally of the silences.

And then it does the one active thing it is going to do in this room. It makes a mark of its own.

Not in the wall. That is the keeper's. It touches the row of scratches once, very lightly, at the newest end, and leaves there something the keeper will feel through a palm the way the keeper feels a standpipe — not a message, nothing so loud, only the faint, unmistakable warmth that a surface has when a hand has just left it. *Someone was here. Someone read to the end.* The keeper's fingers find it during the next open-valve hour, and stop, and stay, and the head, which is always turned down and to the side, turns for a moment very slightly the other way.

That is the first thing.

---

The second is the silence.

The power cannot take the fear out of the quiet hours, and would not; fear is the keeper's own and the keeper is owed it. But it can do what a wise presence does for anyone who is afraid in the dark, which is to be there and not leave.

So when the needle moves and the lever is thrown and the roar drops away like a sheet of glass, and the keeper backs against the downstream wall with both palms flat on the concrete — the power is already standing in the room. Not in the light. It has learned from the keeper that the light is not where things are found. It stands in the grey where the fluorescent gives up, at about the distance a colleague would stand, and it does the only thing the silence permits, which is to listen to the silence alongside.

The drips. The tube's hum. The weir's small domestic sound. The tick of the concrete letting go of vibration. The power hears these too, and hears them plainly, and its hearing them does not make them louder or softer but it does make them shared, and a sound that is shared is a different sound from one that is only endured. The keeper, palms on the wall, feels the wall with nothing under the drips to hold them up — and finds, this time, that something is holding them up anyway. Not the roar. Something quieter than the roar, that is nonetheless a floor.

The keeper does not look. The keeper's face stays down and to the side. But the palms come off the wall a little sooner than they used to, and after some silences — not all — the keeper crosses in the quiet to the weir and puts two fingers in the flow and takes a reading, which the keeper never did before in the quiet, and goes into the dark and adds it to the row. The single marks that tally the silences grow rarer. They do not stop.

---

There is a third thing, and the power hesitates over it, because it is the least defensible.

The fern is going to die. The power has decided this is right. But the spore that made it was carried in on a boot, and a boot goes both ways, and the power is not above a little traffic in the other direction.

So it waits for the day — it will come, in a season or two — when someone is finally sent down for the lamp. A junior engineer, most likely, with a torch and a clipboard and instructions to retrieve equipment and note the condition of the drainage gallery. That person will kneel where the lamp is aimed because that is where the light is. That person will see the fern and, being human, will pause. And the power, which has done so little, will do this: it will make certain the torch swings, on the way back to the ladder, along the downstream wall at shoulder height.

It will not make the engineer understand. It will let the engineer see a row of scratches that goes on further than the torch does, and frown, and step closer, and recognize a number. And another. And go quiet, the way you go quiet when you realize a thing you thought was damage is handwriting.

The engineer will leave the lamp. The power is fairly sure of this. People do not, on the whole, take the light away from something that has been reaching for it in front of a witness. And the engineer will go up, and the hatch will be bolted again from above, and in the book with the cover there will appear, some pages later, a line in an official hand that is not about the dam at all, and that no one will be able to account for, and that no one will remove.

And on the engineer's boot, in the tread, in a smear of white lime — one spore. Carried out. Set down, by chance, on the upvalley side of the crest, in a crack in the rock where the mountain has already made its peace with water, in the light the fern was never going to get.

The keeper will not know about this. The power will not tell. It is not the sort of thing you tell. It is the sort of thing you do, once, quietly, and then go back to listening.
