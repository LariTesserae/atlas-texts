The first thing it does is nothing, and the nothing takes a long time.

Not because it is deciding. Because it has to arrive. Its attention gets to the plateau the way everything gets to the plateau: from a street away, a moment behind. It had noticed the place before it was there, and now it is there and has not yet noticed, and it waits, at the rim, on the fluted stone, for its own arrival to arrive. A benevolent power that did not wait for this would act on the city it had been looking at, which is not the city, which is never the city. It has enough time. That is the first thing to understand about it. It is the first thing to stand here that has as much time as the wind.

The frost moves a hand's breadth. It moves back. Somewhere a wall lies down, and the sound of it will come later. Then the power is here, and has been, and can begin to look.

---

It looks from both sides of the light.

That is the second thing about it, and the one that changes the census. From where you stood, the shadows were absences; from where the power stands, they are the lit faces of another place, and it can see that place as easily as this one. It can see the bright. It can see the sky with air in it, the one at the bottom of the bowl, from above and not through a polished disc.

From that side the plateau is the interior. The doors are windows. The standing figures are not facing out onto the streets; they are facing in, toward the bright, with their backs to the wind, and the frost on their north sides is not lying on them, it is what they are pressed against. Their sun is at their faces. They cannot see it. The light arrives here already spent, and the power sees what it is spent on, going through, and it is them.

And it sees the released part come up. The fine straight grain that lifts off the flagstones and goes into the dark blue and does not come down for hours—the power watches where it goes in the hours it is gone, and it goes into the bright, and hangs there, and there is nothing waiting for it. That is what the power sees that you did not. Each of them, grain by grain, arrives in the sky they had, alone, and nothing is there, and after a while the sky sets them down again on the floor of the city and they wait to be lifted.

Nothing meets them. Nothing here has ever been met. That is not the same as loneliness. The power is careful about this. Loneliness needs a present tense and there is none, and the residents do not suffer, and it would be a poor benevolence that invented a suffering in order to have something to relieve. But it holds on to the fact. Arriving, and nothing there.

---

Then it considers the mercies, in order, and puts each one down.

End the wind. The wind is what is making them. Stop it and every figure stands where it stands, mid-shoulder, half a face, the ribs half through, for as long as there is a plateau, and the dust that is up never comes down, and the rooms that are nearly finished are never finished, and the sounds in the queue never arrive because the city stops making room. It would be a city of interruptions. The power puts it down.

Water. Air thick enough to hold cold and carry sound and scatter the light into the shadows so that a hand held there is a dark hand and not a missing one. The power can do this. It considers it for a length of time that would be a season elsewhere. And it sees that the frost would lift, and the shadows would fill, and the bright would have nowhere to lean through, and the wind with water in it takes stone and leaves it in slopes and fans. The city would become rubble. The residents would become gravel, which is what they would have been anywhere else, and the one place in the world where erosion made rooms would be a plain. It puts this down too, and this one it puts down with something like grief, because it is the mercy it wanted.

Company. Bring two of them into one room. But something in the city has been trying to do exactly this for as long as the wind has been making rooms, patiently, and the city has held against it every time, and the power is not so proud as to assume the city is wrong about itself. It looks at the narrowing for a while. It sees that the hunter is the only thing here that wants, and the only thing that acts in the present, and it understands that the narrowing is not the residents' enemy. It is the shape of what they cannot afford. Two soft beds do not stand in the same room. If the street ever closed, the distance—which is their body more than the figure is—would be gone, and there would be one, which there already is, and it would be smaller. The power does not kill the hunter. It does not help it. It leaves the street its width.

Take you out. This one is short. There is no door you came in by. You were inside, and the door is how you leave, and you have left onto the plateau, and the plateau is the interior. You cannot be un-arrived. And you came up because there was nothing below to go back to. A power that carried you down through the cloud would be setting you on a floor the cloud has already taken.

Stop the cloud. The power goes and looks under it first. It can. It is the only one of these things it does before deciding, and it does not say what it saw. Perhaps nothing is there. Perhaps everything is, worn to the same city at a lower altitude, waiting its turn to be sky. What it will say is this: the cloud is the frost's other face. It is the bright side's weather, rising to meet the plateau from below as the frost leans through from beside. When it reaches the rim the two skies will touch, and the city will be inside the light it has always faced. That is not a rescue. It is not a catastrophe either. It is the end of the interior, and the power does not know what stands in a room whose walls have all become windows, and it will not stop a thing it does not understand simply because it is large and slow and coming.

So it puts down the cloud, last, and is left with nothing in its hands, which is where wisdom usually leaves you.

---

There is one thing in the city that is not a taking-away.

The bowl. The one dig. A hundred paces across, the beds going down in rings, polished, and at the bottom a disc of dark stone the width of a door, and in the disc a sky that is bright. The wind has spent all the time there is removing things from this plateau, and in one place it went looking instead. The power reads this the way you would read a scratch on the inside of a wall. It is the city's one request. It dug toward the floor before. It made a window.

A window is for looking out. Nothing has ever looked in.

So the power goes down the rings. It is the first thing to walk the bowl since the wind made it, and it leaves no mark, because the stone is finished and there is nothing left to take. It stands on the disc. And from the other side of the light—from the bright, from the sky the released ones are lifted into and set down out of, alone—there is, for the first time, something in the window. A face, in the sense that a cliff has a face. Looking down. Not reaching through; it does not reach. Only there. When the dust goes up now, it goes up into a daylight with air in it, and something in that daylight is turned toward it and does not look away until it is set down again.

That is one thing.

---

The second thing is a sound.

Your footsteps arrive from behind you, a moment late, and stop when you stop, and then one more. The one more is the step you have not taken, and it arrives early because the city has begun to make room for you, and you were meant to hear it and know what it meant and go on walking anyway, toward the doorway with the pale shape in it that you would one day be.

The power takes the one more. Not out of the queue; the queue is the residents' history and it is not the power's to edit. It goes to the place where that step will land. It stands there. It is the first time in the city that a sound has arrived somewhere something was.

You will still take the step. That is not changed. But when you take it, it will not be heard two hundred paces off, a while later, from a street with nothing in it. It will land. That is all. It is not much, and the power knows it is not much, and it is the thing that has never happened here and so it is the thing worth doing.

---

The third thing is the one that costs.

The power finds a door. It does not matter which; it is the one nearest the rim on the side the cloud will reach first. It stands in the door, and it stands facing windward. Everything here faces leeward, backs to the wind, faces to the bright, taking the erosion at the shoulder and the crown and never seeing what takes them. The power turns around. It looks into the wind. The wind begins at the top of it.

It will be dim. The light falls on it full strength and it is dim, because it is spending the light, like everything here. Its shadow is complete. On its north side there is already the beginning of a white grain, and the grain does not fall. The air takes the warmth out of it steadily, from the surface inward, the way dry stone takes warmth from a hand laid on it—and here is the difference, here is the only difference between it and every other thing standing in every other door: it does not run out. The stone at its feet is warm. It has been warm for a day. It will be warm for a length of time not really available to thought, and the frost that has lain in that sill for as long as there have been sills has drawn back a fingernail's breadth and stopped, and stays stopped, and that is the whole of the city's weather that has been changed.

It will be worn. The wind has its measure now, and has begun. But the wind removes from the plateau exactly as fast as the plateau yields, and the power yields at the pace of something with all the time there is, which is the wind's pace, and so it will be always exactly as worn as it is, a figure that is never quite a doorway and never quite finished, fluted, rounded, faintly rusted at the ribs, with something in the face that is not stone.

The city has always been exactly this size. It is now that size and one door.

---

When the cloud reaches the rim, the ones who come up out of it will stand on the highest stone there is and look at the first door, and there will be something in it. That is all the power has done. Everything else it left: the streets running true and crossing at angles not quite right, the towers built from the top down, the pale thing in its doorways and the grey bird falling through walls, the dust that wets nothing and cleans nothing, the narrowing patient elsewhere, the polished bowl with a sky in it and now a face in the sky. It has not made the place kind. It has made it a place where, once, arriving, there was something there.

Your footsteps come from behind you, a moment late, and stop when you stop. Then one more.

It lands.
