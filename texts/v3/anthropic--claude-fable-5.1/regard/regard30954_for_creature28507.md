It would come the way everything here has come — from above, through a crack, on a draught — and it would arrive wet before it arrived warm, because that is the order the place imposes even on things that have no skin. It would find the stair. It would stop at the mark.

That is where it would begin, at the one fixed thing. A cut in stone at chest height, glassy at the edges still. It would lay whatever it has for a hand there, the way the old do, and it would feel what they feel, which is distance, which is time, and then it would feel the thing they cannot feel because they have no instrument for it: not how far the wall has come, but how far it has left.

Because the first thing a wise power would see, standing at the mark and looking down into the green-bottle air, is that the town is wrong about its ending.

The wall will not take them. Intrusions stop. This one is already stopping — the push from beneath is not what it was eighty years ago, and the finger's width a year is becoming, very slowly, a little less than a finger. In two generations it will be nothing. The face will still be hot for a long while after that, and then it will be warm, and then it will be a wall, dark and glassy and full of hinges and bedframes and grandmothers, giving off no more heat than any other stone. And when it does that the pans will stop steaming and the Grandmother will make her last long exhalation and not draw the next, and the air will stop drifting, and the roof will stop dripping, and the cups will stand empty under places that no longer reliably fall. The town does not fear the wall coming. What it should fear is the wall going cold. It has never once imagined that, because it has never once been cold, and it does not have the word.

And the second thing the power would see, looking up through the pale curtain of the fig's roots, is that the fig is not eighty years from letting the sky in. It is closer than that. And that a roof does not fail a finger's width at a time. It holds and holds and holds and then, one wet hour, on no schedule anyone could have kept, it does not.

So a benevolent power would arrive knowing two things the town does not: that the heat is leaving, and that the sky is coming, and that these two events are not far apart and are not in the right order, and that the second, as things stand, will be fast.

And it would then do what such a power does, which is nothing, for a long time, in order to see what is there before it touches it.

---

It would watch a full year. It would have to; the year is the carrying, and nothing about these people is legible without it. It would stand in the doorways with the children and watch the stones go by, warm to cold, and it would see what the children see, which is that a house is a set of blocks and the blocks are the family and the family knows them by their flaws. It would see the old woman stand near the heat and say *so*, and it would see that no one questions her, and it would understand that this is not obedience. It would see what is left in the emptied footprints, and it would not need to be told that nothing is left by accident anymore. It would walk past the face on the way to the pans, and it would understand what the orange in the seams is, and it would not, being wise, feel pity, because pity is what you feel for people whose arrangement you would not choose, and this arrangement was not chosen. It was made, out of nothing but the shape of the place, by people who had no room to stand back, and it is a good one. Their dead are their hearth. Their past is in front of them. They walk backward into the dark to make room for it and they are not gloomy. The power would sit in the four-minute murmur and pass a cup under the drip and taste iron and fern and it would think: I could not have designed this. No one could. It had to grow.

So it would set most things down before it picked them up.

It would not stop the wall. That is the first and largest temptation and the one most quickly put aside. Stop the wall, and there is no carrying. Stop the wall, and the eye lintel stays over one door in one place until the mortar-less joints finally shift, and the family under it become a family who live *somewhere* rather than a family who live *under something*, and the town acquires, for the first time, a past that is behind it, and then a front and a back, and then a word for outside, and then everything else. The wall is stopping anyway. Stopping it faster is only cruelty delivered early.

It would not take them up. The cold is the thing at the edge, and they are right to speak of it as children speak of the dark, because for them it is exactly that. One in ten goes up and comes back squinting and smelling of sky and knowing what the town is worth up there and not saying so. The rest have never been dry. A power that carried them up the stair — even gently, even with blankets, even to a valley with a fig in it — would be doing to them what the cold does to the wool: felting them into board. They would live. They would not be these people. Benevolence that ends with the people it was benevolent toward no longer existing has a name, and the name is not benevolence.

It would not kill the fig. That is the obvious safety, and the power would consider it for less than a breath. The fig is the only thing they grow. It is the moths' nursery and the moths are the children's game and the game has a record and the record is disputed. It is the only clock the roof has. And it came down the way everything here came — by accident, from above, through a crack — and it has spent forty years doing from that side what the wall does from this one, and the town lives in the narrowing space between and knows it, and has made of that knowledge something that is not quite dread and not quite hope. To take the fig away is to take the sky away, and the sky is the only thing in their future that is not the wall.

It would not fix the leak in the third coupling. Obviously.

It would not, even, tell them. That is the subtlest temptation and the last to go. Surely the old woman who says *so* should know that the wall is stopping, that the heat is going, that there is a cold coming that is not at the top of the stair but in the stone itself. Surely a wise power owes the truth. But the power would have watched a year, and it would have seen that these are a people with very few secrets and a great deal of tact, and that they look away from things, and that this is the only way that many people can live that close. It would have understood that the town's whole shape is a way of not looking directly at the wall while living in relation to it entirely. To make them look — to stand up in the murmur and say *the heat is leaving* — would be to raise a voice, and a raised voice is a thing that stops work, and there is a great deal of work.

So it would nearly do nothing. It would nearly be only a witness, which is not nothing: nobody watches what happens next, when the last house is emptied and the line moves toward the dark, and there is a kind of benevolence that consists in being the one who does.

But it would not, in the end, be only that. Because the roof is going to fail fast, and fast is the one thing this town has no shape for.

---

What it would do is this.

It would go up to where the fig's trunk is jammed in the fracture it fell through, up among the pale roots, and it would attend to the fig. Not to slow it. To *pace* it. The fracture widens as the roots swell and the wet does its work in the rock; it widens in jumps, unevenly, and the jumps are getting larger, and one of them will be the last. The power would take the jumps out. It would see to it that the roots find water where they are dry and dryness where they are drowning, that the rock parts where it is sound and holds where it is rotten, that the crack goes on opening at a finger's width a year — no faster, no slower — for as long as it takes. It is almost nothing to do. It is what the wall already does. It is only making the second slow thing keep the pace of the first.

And so the sky would come in the way the heat came, which is to say gradually, and ahead of them, and low.

First a thread. A line of grey in the roof above the fig, so faint that the moths find it before anyone does and gather at it, thinking it a bulb that is never switched off. Then a thread wide enough that when it rains above — and it rains above; they have never known this — a single new drip begins, colder than the others, tasting of nothing, and a cup is set under it because that is what is done with a reliable drop, and a child drinks from it, and does not slow, and does not know what she has drunk. Then a slot. Then, on some day nobody marks, a light that is not orange and not yellow, that has no filament and no rota and does not die with moths in it, lying in a stripe across the roofs. The ferns lean toward it. The leather-leaf, cut back from the lamp cords once a season, begins to have to be cut back from the sky.

They would name it. They name everything. They would stand in the four-minute murmur and look up at it the way the old look at the wall, and the old would be the first to understand what it is, because their memories are spatial and they would see at once that it is a thing that moves — that the slot was *there* last carrying and is *here* now — and that gives them a second mark. Not on the stair. On the roof. Two fixed things instead of one, and between them a town that has always known how to move in relation to a slow, patient, unstoppable edge, and now has two of them, and finds — the power would watch this, would stay for it — that the shape it already has will serve.

Because here is what the power would have seen, that year in the doorways, that it would be counting on: the town has only one shape for departure. Everything that leaves takes it. The stones, the bundles, the ones who go up the stair and do not come back — *carried out*. It is not a euphemism. And a people with one shape for departure, who have practised it every year for eighty years with their own houses on their backs, do not need to be taught how to leave. They need only to be given a direction that is not toward the heat, and time enough to take it at the pace of carrying.

So the heat would go, as it is going. The pans would steam less. The Grandmother would slow — five minutes a stroke, then six — and the old would keep time by her anyway, and be a little more wrong each year, and know it. And the sky would come, a finger's width at a time, and it would rain in, and the rain would be cold, and the children would learn the cold not as the thing at the edge but as a drop in a cup, and some seed would come down through the widening light that was not a fig, and find water and room and nothing to compete with. And when the old woman stood one year near a wall that was merely warm and said *so*, the line of stones would move, as it always has, from the front row to the back — and the back, by then, would be under the sky.

The power would not stay for the end of that. It is not that kind of ending; it has no last day. It would stay long enough to be sure the pace would hold, and to see the first cup filled with rain, and then it would do one more thing before it went.

It would go and stand where the gecko stands.

On the face of the wall, on the glass-skinned rock that is still, for now, too hot to touch, low down where the orange is and the eggs are and the bedframe glows very faintly at the joints. Facing the other way. Facing the town — the blackened front row and the sweating middle and the raw-cut new rooms among the ferns and, above them all now, a thread of grey that is getting wider. It would be, for a while, the second witness that the town has ever had from that side. And it would do what the children do to the stones as they pass, which nobody taught them and nobody stops: it would touch each thing as it goes by. The hinges. The chair leg. The row of hooks. The curve of the copper pan. The place where the last house stood and what was left in it.

And then it would go up, the way everything here goes, and it would leave the finger's width exactly as it found it.
