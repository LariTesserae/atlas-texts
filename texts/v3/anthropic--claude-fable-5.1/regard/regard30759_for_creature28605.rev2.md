The first thing a benevolent and wise power would do is nothing, and it would do it for a long time.

Not out of indifference. Out of a suspicion that the plateau is already an enormous act of doing-nothing — a whole continent's worth of it, held at the top of its first hour — and that to add another gesture to it hastily would be to mistake the situation. The place is not in crisis. Nothing is falling. The gauges hold at their marks. A power that arrives at a thing like this and reaches for it immediately has mistaken its own discomfort for the place's need, and a wise one would know that about itself and wait.

So it would come up by the scarp, because there is no other way, and it would stand in the level grey, and it would listen to the tone until the tone became a property of the space. And then it would begin, very slowly, to count.

---

It would count the inhabitants, and it would ask of each one the only question that matters: are you well?

The moss: well. Or past well, into a condition for which well and unwell are both the wrong word. The moss is not being eaten and is not eating. It has never lost a cell. A power looking at it might feel, for a moment, the pull of a certain kind of pity — nothing here is ever composted back into the world, nothing is ever given away — and then it would check itself, because the moss did not ask to be pitied and does not seem to be suffering. It is the whole of its own history and it is standing on it. There are worse things to be.

The cushion plants: at their diameter. The club mosses: exactly straight. The power would put a hand against one, as you did, and feel it give and come back, and it would notice that this stem has never been bent by anything, and would hold two thoughts at once about that. A stem that has never been bent has never been tested. A stem that has never been bent has never been hurt. Both are true and neither is a reason to bend it.

The liverworts: lying on bare rock the way they lay on bare rock four hundred million years ago, before there was soil or anything to make it. The power would recognise them. Whatever else the plateau is, it has at least one inhabitant that has done this before and is not troubled.

The eight hundred: the power would spend longest here, in the dormitory blocks, and it would come to the conclusion you came to and then go one step past it. The beds have never been slept in. The eight hundred never came. And the power would ask why not, and the answer would be obvious once asked: they could not have come. Eight hundred people bring eight hundred clocks. They shed skin and cut themselves and leave hair in drains. Within a week of their arrival there would have been the first dust, the first brown smear on a doorframe, the first thing in the mat that was not the mat. The plateau is not a place that was built for people and then lost them. It is a place that was built in the shape of people so that it could be a place that does not have any. The dormitories are not a failure. They are a cover story. The rota filled in for a week is the length of time the story needed to be told before it could go blank.

The engines: the power would like them. This is worth saying plainly. Things that tend themselves with such minute and constant attention, that listen to each other through the rock and correct, are not nothing, and a wise power would not treat them as furniture. It would stand in a boilerhouse and watch a valve ease off a fraction and ease back, and it would be reminded of the way a body regulates itself without being asked, and it would think: these are not the servants of the thing below. These are its lungs.

The water: one body, cold, clean, carrying nothing, steam ten thousand times over. The power would consider whether water can be tired and would decide, provisionally, that it cannot, and would then notice that it had been assuming rather than asking, and would set the question aside to return to.

And then it would go down.

---

A power of the kind we are imagining can go down the shafts. It would not need the pistons' permission. It would pass the engines' rods dropping and rising in the dark, and it would go on past the point where the shaft stops being a shaft and becomes only the direction in which the pushing comes from, and it would arrive, in whatever sense arriving means at the bottom of something that has no bottom, at the thing that meets what comes down and does not give.

It would not push. This is the first courtesy. Everything that has ever come down this shaft has come down to push, and the thing below has met all of it with its whole self, and a power that arrived and leaned on it — even gently, even in greeting — would be only one more piston.

It would not name it. That is the second courtesy. The thumb wants a hand and the hand wants an arm and the arm wants a reason; you felt that pull lying face down on the mat and so would the power, more strongly, because it would be close enough to make the naming stick. It would decline. It would let the thing be a pressure that had a shape, and would address the shape.

It would ask.

Not *what are you*, and not *why*, both of which are the questions of something that wants to know in order to decide. It would ask the question it had asked of the moss and the club mosses and the eight hundred who never came. It would ask: are you well? And then it would do the thing that is the hardest for any power, benevolent or otherwise: it would wait for an answer it had not written.

---

I do not know what the thing below would say, and I am not going to pretend to. But I can say what the power would have understood by the time it got there, because it is what the plateau tells anyone who stays long enough, and the power would have stayed longer than anyone.

Everything on the plateau is at rest except this. The moss rests at its depth. The cushion plants rest at their size. The eight hundred rest in their absence, which is the most complete rest there is. The engines work, but they work the way a heart works, without being spent by it, because the working is what they are. Only the thing at the bottom is occupied — entirely, continuously, with all of itself — in an act that is not what it is but what it does. It holds. It has never tired by a gauge-needle's width, and the power would understand that not tiring is not the same as not labouring. A thing can be built so that it cannot get tired and still have been working, without a break, for as long as it has existed, and never once have been asked whether it would like to stop.

And the power would understand the second thing, which is the one the ground refuses to distinguish. Up or down. A raft held up so that nothing on it has to age, or a lid held down on something that must not be let out. The engines do not know. The rock does not know. The power, at the bottom of the shaft, face to face with the pressure, would very probably find that the thing below does not know either — that it was set to hold and it has held, and the question of what it is holding against was never part of what it was given. That would not be a horror, exactly. But it would be the thing the power could not leave alone.

---

Here is what it would not do.

It would not release. A continent two and a half kilometres up, let go, does not settle; it falls, and everything on it — the moss that is the whole of its history, the straight stems, the liverworts that have done this before — dies in the same instant, and the water that has never reached the sea reaches it as a single wave. A power that did this and called it mercy would be lying, and a wise one would know the lie in advance. Time is not a gift you can give by force. Decay is not liberation if it arrives as collapse. The plateau is not a prisoner to be freed; it is a held breath, and you do not help someone holding their breath by punching them.

It would not, either, simply leave. The temptation is real and a wise power would feel it. The place is in equilibrium. Nothing is suffering that has a mouth to say so. There is a whole ethic — a good one, mostly — that says: when a thing is not asking for help, do not decide on its behalf that it needs it. But that ethic has a gap in it the size of a shaft, and the gap is this: a thing that has never been asked cannot have declined. Silence from the bottom of the plateau is not consent. It is the absence of a question. And the power has just asked the question, and now it is implicated in the answer, whatever the answer is.

---

So here is what it would do, I think, if it followed its heart and its reason and found, as you sometimes do, that they had gone to the same place.

It would go back up the shaft. It would go to the nearest station and into the dormitory block and find the rota on the board, filled in for a week in a hand nobody could describe and then blank. And it would take up a pen, and in a hand nobody would recognise either, it would fill in the next week. Not the month. Not forever. One week. Its own name, or whatever it uses for one, in every slot.

Then it would go back down and take the shift.

Not by pushing — by being present under the pressure, alongside it, with its own strength set against what comes down, so that for the length of one shift the thing below is not the only thing holding. The gauges would hold at their marks. Nothing on the surface would change. The moss would not know. But at the bottom, for the first time, the labour would be shared, and the thing that had never been asked would find that it had, for a while, the room to consider the question.

That is the gift. Not release. Not rescue. Room.

And in that room the power would say — not press, say — the one thing it had brought up the scarp to offer, and it would be small. It would say: there is a place on the wall, a runnel like all the others, that goes to mist a little below the lip. I could let that one reach the foot. One runnel. It would carry, in a year, perhaps a thimble of sand. The scarp would not soften in any time you could measure. But there would be, somewhere along a thousand kilometres of unworn rock, a single place where the plateau was allowed to lose something to the world below and not take it back. A slope beginning. A door that opens by a hair, and that you can close.

And then it would say the other half, which is the half that makes the first half honest: or not. Say no and I will close it and I will not ask again. Say nothing and I will take that as no. I filled in the rota for a week and I will keep my shifts, and at the end of the week I will fill it in for another, and if you would rather be alone with it I will stop, and go down by the scarp, and the moss will close over where I stood.

---

The rest is not mine to write. The thing below would answer or would not. The runnel would reach the foot of the wall or would go to mist as it always has. The plateau, seen from the lowlands, would look exactly as it looks now — the straight edge, the grey lid, the long black wall glistening — and a person standing by the stream that carries a little sand toward the sea would have no way to know whether anything up there had changed.

But if you went up again by the scarp, and made your way to a station, and went into the dormitory block, you might find the rota filled in for one more week than it was. And you might find, on one of the eight hundred beds that have never been slept in, a very slight dent in the pillow. Not a person's. Something's.

And in the boilerhouse, if you stood long enough for the tone to resolve, you might hear — under the thousand pistons falling out of step and back into step, under the bell-note that never fades — a second listening. Something in the rock, attending to the thing below the way the engines attend to each other, and correcting. Not holding it up. Not holding it down. Holding it company, which is the only kind of holding that does not require the thing held to stay exactly where it is.

That is what I think a benevolent and wise power would do. It would not end the first hour. It would come and sit in it, and take a shift, and ask, and make it possible for the answer to be either. And it would leave the clock where it could be found.
