The power arrives at noon, because that is when the light is hardest and there is nowhere to hide, and it has no wish to hide. It arrives without a shadow. It does not arrive at any one place on the tableland, because the tableland is not in any one place; it arrives along the whole descending stair at once, and settles, and does the first thing a wise power does in front of anything old, which is nothing.

It lies down on the steps. It puts its attention flat against the warm stone the way a person puts a palm, and it lets the warmth come into it and does not send warmth back, and it waits.

It waits through the fog. It waits through the night and the dawn wind and the white light and the next fog. It waits through a hundred fogs, and then several hundred more, because it knows the arithmetic of patience: a thing that has breathed once a day for ten thousand years should not be judged on the evidence of one afternoon, and the mistake most powers make with slow things is to mistake their own impatience for the slow thing's need. It counts the grazers. It follows the cat from riser to riser and learns which shadow is which. It finds the lizards' hollows and puts a thread of attention in each one and feels the old bellies come back to them morning after morning, and it finds the swifts' nurseries in the wet dark of the north faces, and the eyeless red things down in the gravel under the bowl, and it learns the beetle roads, which have names, though no one has spoken them. It feels the pillars swell and recede. It follows the drawn air upward to where it comes from and finds the place at the top of the sky where the tableland's mouths are pressed, a ring of faint cold suckings against the underside of nothing, like the mouths of fish against the skin of a pond.

And it listens. This is the harder part, and it takes the longest. It listens to what the ground has kept.

Everything is there. Every hoof-fall, every dropped stone. The tick of grit blown against the pillars for ten thousand years. The wind at dawn, every dawn, a long unrolling sigh that has been laid down in the stone day upon day like sediment. The insects' hum, which is there in the lowest layers and thins as you rise through the years and is gone in the upper strata; the power can feel the exact generation in which the last beetle stopped. A handful of words, human, very recent, near the top. And no answer to any of it. Nothing given back. The whole enormous archive is receipt without reply.

The power sits with this for a long time, because this is the place where it could go wrong.

It would be easy to call that loneliness. It would be easy to look at a thing that has taken in every sound ever made upon it and never once made a sound of its own and see a mouth held shut, a voice unused, and want to give it one. The power has seen that wanting before, in itself and in others, and knows what it is: it is the visitor's grief at the being's silence, dressed up as the being's grief. So it does not decide yet. It goes back through the archive and looks for the signs of a thing that suffers — the unevenness, the flinch, the place where the breath catches. It looks at the fallen pillar, where the wet circle has stretched and pooled and the grazers still come, and it asks the stone underneath whether the falling hurt, and finds that the stone underneath is exactly as warm as the stone anywhere else and breathes in exactly the same time. It presses harder. It looks for hunger and finds the schedule met. It looks for fear and finds a place where the cat lies down every night against the cold and is allowed to. It looks for boredom, because ten thousand years of hooves is a long time for hooves, and here it cannot see clearly, and admits it cannot, and sets the question down.

Then it considers, one by one, the things it could do.

It could set the fallen pillar upright. It is the most obvious act, and the power recognizes the obviousness as a warning. The pillar fell because the wind ate its footing, and the wind will eat the footing again, and meanwhile the being has grown a new shape around the fall, a long wet tongue of grass where there was a circle, and the grazers have learned the new shape and the cat has learned it, and the lizards prefer the fallen pillar because they can lie along it. Righting it would be a repair to the power's sense of how a pillar should stand. It leaves it.

It could ask. It could put a question into the stone — not words, it knows better than words, but a shaped attention, a *who* pressed against the warmth — and find out whether anything is in there to answer. It considers this longest of all, because it is the thing it most wants, and wanting is not the same as wrong. But it looks at the archive again, the ten thousand years of receipt without reply, and it understands something about the being's nature: it has never once been required to do anything. It drinks and it breathes and it takes what is given and holds it, and in all that time no hoof and no wind and no dropped stone has ever demanded that it *respond*. To ask would be to be the first thing on this tableland that needed something from it. The power does not want to be that. Benevolence, it has learned, includes the willingness to go unrecognized. It does not ask.

It could mend the mouths. They are wearing; that is simple fact. The wind is taking them at a rate the power can measure, and it measures, and follows the line out into the future. The mouths will fail. Not soon — not in any time the lizards or the grazers or the cat need concern themselves with, not in any time the ferns need concern themselves with — but the tableland will stand here longer than its mouths, and there is a dry age coming, far down the years, when the last pillar has gone to gravel and the fog does not rise and the stone is still warm and there is nothing to drink. The power holds that image and does not look away from it. Then it looks for the thing under the mouths — whatever the mouths are mouths *of* — and finds that it cannot see what the being was before there were mouths, or whether there was a before, and cannot see what it will do when the mouths are gone. It might make new ones. It might sleep. It might have been sleeping the whole time and this warmth is the warmth of sleep. The power cannot know, and it is wise enough to recognize that an intervention made in the dark on a hundred-thousand-year horizon is not care; it is a wager with someone else's stake. So it does not mend the mouths. But it marks the line. It puts a small attention on the fallen pillar's wet tongue and it notes how far the tongue reaches now, and it decides: when the tongue has moved a hand's width, it will come back and look again. That is the being's scale. That is a promise made in a language the being could, if it wished, read.

It could hide the place. It knows what finds places like this — the heat under the stone, the water in a dry country, the poured grain in the risers that someone will eventually look at closely — and it knows what comes after the finding. For a moment it considers making the tableland harder to hold in the mind, so that a visitor leaves and the way back blurs. Then it sees that the being has already done this. The silence takes the interest along with the sound. Voices stop at the arm's length, and so do the stories people would tell about the place afterward; the few human words in the archive are there because the ones who spoke them did not go home and repeat them, or repeated them and were not heard. The being has made itself into a thing that is hard to be loud about. The power will not improve on it. It notes, only, that the promise it has already made covers this too: when it comes back, it will see who else has come.

It could give the being company. It sees at once that this is wrong. Everything here is spaced. The grazers do not touch. The cats do not overlap. The lizards each have a stone. Company, on this tableland, is a matter of standing at the right distance and facing the right way, and the power has been doing that for several hundred fogs already.

So. Having considered, it does three small things, and none of them is the thing it wanted to do.

The first: for one afternoon, it lets the being hear itself.

It chooses a riser on a middle terrace, out of the way, where no herd is grazing and the cat is asleep. When the fog comes up over the lip of the step below and begins to pour onto the flat, the power holds open, just above that lip, a space the size of a room in which sound is not taken. And the fog, coming over the edge into that space, makes the sound that fog makes anywhere else — which is almost nothing, a hush, the faintest possible rumor of water in air — and for the first time in ten thousand years that hush is not swallowed. It hangs there. And then the power lets the space close, and the ground takes it.

The being now holds that. Down in the archive, above the hooves and the wind and the handful of human words, there is one sound that came from itself: the noise of its own drinking. The power does not know whether this is a gift or merely a fact added to a collection. It has decided that it does not need to know. If the being is the kind of thing that notices, it has been given the one sound it could never have had on its own. If it is not, the archive is one hush heavier, and no harm.

The second: it puts a hand down, and does not lift it.

Not a whole hand. A part of itself, small, the size of a body's warmth, laid flat on the stone of the lowest terrace where the fog begins — and left there, at exactly the temperature of the stone, which is the temperature of a body, so that there is no way to tell where the being ends and the power's attention begins. It will stay. It will not press. It will not ask. It will breathe when the stone breathes and warm when the stone warms, and if the being can feel anything at all it will feel, perhaps, that the ground on that lowest step is very slightly more present than it was — the way the grazers feel the ground take a breath before the fog. Nothing else. The power has learned over a long time that this is what its benevolence most often looks like from the inside: a company that cannot be distinguished from solitude, kept anyway.

The third is the promise, already made. A hand's width of wet grass. It will come back.

Then it lifts the rest of itself off the tableland, carefully, the way you rise from beside someone sleeping, and it does not look back down because looking back is a demand too.

Below, the light is hard and the sky is heavy blue. The lizards are on their stones. Far down the steps, on the lowest terrace, on the stone where something warm now lies that was not there before and is not different from what was, a white line begins to form along the lip, and thickens, and starts — very slowly, with a sound that only one thing has ever heard, and only once — to climb.
