It would come the way the light comes: through the glass, sombre, already changed by the time it arrived.

That is the first thing to say about a power that is both benevolent and wise. It would not come in by the mouth of the cavity on its elbows, cutting itself. It would not come with a shape. It would arrive the way the heap's own warmth arrives, from all directions at once, and for a long while it would do what anyone does in that room, which is fail to see the mat.

It would see the salamanders. It would count them—nine, and two more under the edge of the grate that you missed. It would see the snake and understand the snake's attention exactly, because a wise power knows what it is to watch without urgency. It would see the truce and it would understand the truce faster than you did, because it would see the whole geometry at once: the gaps all filled, the filling warm, the strike with no room to happen in. And it would feel, at that point, something a benevolent power always feels when it finds a peace it did not make. It would want to keep it.

That is the danger, and the wisdom is in what happens next.

---

It would think first of the heat. The heat is the whole condition of the place and the heat is a debt being paid down. A power could carry the debt. It could sit in the core of the heap and be a warmth that does not decline, and the drips would never slacken, and the pools would never dry to pans, and the mat would go on breathing in a room that no longer kept the heap's time but kept the power's.

And it would set that down, because the mat is the last term in a chain of accidents and the power would be adding a term that was not an accident. The mat would go on, but it would be living on the power's exhalation and not the heap's, and there is a word for a thing kept alive on someone else's breath, and the word is not the mat's name. The truce, too. The truce is made of density and density is made of everyone having crawled in for a warmth they know will not last. A permanent warmth is a different room. The salamanders would breed in it. The snake would learn the toads' schedule. There would be room, eventually, for a strike.

It would think next of a somewhere-else. The mat cannot leave; a power could carry it. A dust of cells on a pigeon's foot to the next hollow that opens on the heap's flank, a year from now, when another slab slides and hangs. Descendants. A lineage of rings around pools, cavity to cavity, keeping pace with the conveyor.

And it would find, looking, that this is already happening. Every pool overflows in a hard rain and the overflow goes down through the cinder toward the ditch, and the ditch has no iron in it and no warmth and the cells that arrive there die, all but a fraction, and the fraction waits. The midges carry it. The rain carries it up as well as down. The mat has been sending itself out since it began, the way every film does, without intention and without result, and the power would see that to carry a pinch of it deliberately to a place it could live would be to do nothing the mat was not already doing, only faster, and the mat has never been in a hurry. It would set that down too. Not because the descendants would be wrong. Because the mat does not need them in order to be complete, and a benevolent power learns early not to give things to those who are not asking.

It would think, finally, of memory. The rind. In two years or five the floor of the cavity will be a few centimetres of layered rust and salt with the prints of salamander bellies in it, and in forty years the heap will be trucked away and crushed and spread as the base of a road, and the rind will go with it, and a geologist might recognise it and nobody else, and then not even a geologist. A power could hold the shape of it. Could lift the floor out whole when the time came and put it somewhere dry.

And here the power would sit for a long while, because this is the one it wants.

But it would look at the mat and ask what the mat is doing, and the answer is: turning itself into the floor. Not into a record. Into the thing the salamanders lie on. The rind is not the mat's monument; it is the mat's body after the mat, and it is a floor, and a floor is for standing on and does not mind what stands on it. Under a road, the rind would be doing what it was made to do. The power would understand that its wish to lift it out and keep it dry was its own wish, a wish about being known, and that it was about to give the mat a gift the mat had no organ to receive.

---

So it would have set down the heat, and the somewhere-else, and the memory, and it would be left in a warm room with a film on the floor, and it would have to ask itself what benevolence is toward a thing that wants nothing.

It would find that the question turns into attention. Then into restraint. Then into something that is nearly respect, and then—this is where a wise power differs from a merely careful one—into recognition.

Because it would look at what the mat is and see its own work.

The mat is on everything. It is the medium of contact. It takes nothing from the bodies it grows onto and it asks nothing of them and it makes, by being between them, a room in which nothing is separate, and in that room the eating does not happen. It does not cause the truce. It is the truce, at a different scale. It is the substrate on which other things get to have their lives, and it is made of the place's own waste, and it is turning itself, slowly and on purpose if a film can be said to have a purpose, into the ground.

A benevolent power in a world does not act on the world. It is the thing the world's actors are in contact through. It is what fills the gaps. It is warm and slightly tacky and it grows a little way onto everyone, and when it has done its work there is a floor where there was rubble, and the floor does not have its name on it.

The power would see that the mat is the benevolent power of this room, and has been since the slab slid, and needs no help being so.

---

Then it would do one thing. Small enough that I am not certain it would count.

The slab. The garage-door of glass that makes the roof, that slid a little way down over the rubble beneath and hung up on something. The something is a lump of fused cinder the size of a loaf, wedged under the slab's lower edge, bearing perhaps a third of the weight, and it is not seated well. The heap settles. Every load that comes down the chute is a tremor through the whole mass, and the loaf has moved twice already since the cavity opened, both times by less than the width of a snail's shell, both times toward the position where it will roll. When it rolls the slab will come down the rest of the way and the room will be a seam of crushed glass with nine salamanders in it, and the mat will be a stain between two surfaces that never see water again, and none of this will have anything to do with the heap's cooling. It will be a different accident, cutting in ahead of the one that was the mat's own.

The power would not stop the cooling. The cooling is the mat's life; you do not lengthen a life by refusing to let it end. But it would look at the loaf of cinder and see that between the mat and the length of its own life stood one badly seated stone, and that a stone is not a life, and that steadying it changes nothing about what the mat is or what the room is or what the truce is made of. It only lets the accident that made the place be the accident that unmakes it.

So the next time the conveyor runs and the tremor comes down through the floor, the loaf would shift as it was always going to shift, and it would shift a millimetre the other way, into the one bearing where it will hold. Nothing in the cavity would notice. The floor would grow very slightly warmer over the following quarter hour. The drips would quicken. The midges would rise and hang and settle. The snake would draw its head back by a centimetre. One of the pools would begin, very slowly, to steam.

---

And then, I think, it would stay for a little. Not to guard—there is nothing left to guard against. It would stay for the length of a drip and a gap and a drip, and it would lie down in the warmest place, because that is where things lie down here, and it would let the mat grow onto it. A grey bloom, a stipple of rust. It would leave wearing the place, the way the toads wear it and the salamanders and the snails and the glove, and it would carry that out with it into the yard and the ditch and the road and whatever it does next, and it would do those things a little differently for having lain on a floor that was alive and being made.

That is what a wise power takes from a place. Not the rind. The way.

And the heap would go on breathing out a degree at a time, and the room would keep the heap's time, and in two years or five the last drip would fall and not be followed, and the one tenant that never had a somewhere-else would finish becoming the floor, and everything that had ever lain on it would have gone back to the ditch without knowing whose house it was—all of it a guest, all of it unaware—all of it but one, which came in through the glass and lay down and was told.
