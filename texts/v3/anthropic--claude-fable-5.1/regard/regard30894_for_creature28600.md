The attention arrives the way the light does, from no direction, and for a long time it does nothing but stay.

It has time. That is the first gift it gives and it gives it to itself: it does not act on the first hour, or the first year. It lets the mist come up and hide the floor and lets the herons walk down through it. It watches the water-skin thicken by a coin's depth and go down over the lip and come back. It counts the salamanders that come over the eastern pass against the ones that go out, and finds the numbers match to within a few, and lets that be. It finds the eleven made things and then, because it is patient, the twelfth, under the moss, and the thirteenth, inside the eastern tree, grown through and through with wood. It does not touch them. It looks at the machine long enough to understand that the machine is not unkind, and that the animals are right to have found uses for it, and that it is keeping the water within a degree the way a hand keeps a small bird — not to hold it, only so it will not be cold.

It looks at the figure.

---

Wisdom's first motion is toward stillness, and the attention feels it and does not distrust it. There is a case for doing nothing here and the case is strong and should be made honestly before anything else is.

The case is this. What is in the basin is not suffering. It is not calling. It has not been trapped — the pass is a hand's breadth of drowned stone and nothing stands on it. Something came here with edges and laid them down one at a time on the smooth rock, and the rock took them, and what is left does not miss them. If that is a death it is the gentlest death the attention has ever seen, and it has seen many. There is no smell. Nothing is afraid. The one who came stands in the flash with its eyes open and the light was never a thing it argued with. To go into that and *want* on its behalf — to carry wanting over the pass like a torch into a room that has spent ten thousand years becoming shadowless — might be the only cruelty this place has any room for. The attention knows what it is to arrive somewhere and mistake its own hunger to help for the other's hunger to be helped. It has done that. It has learned, slowly, the way the stone learned, that mercy is not always a hand extended; sometimes it is a hand withheld and a long quiet watching, and then a departure so light that nothing lifts its head.

So the attention holds the case for stillness in one hand and does not put it down. It will not put it down. Whatever it does next, it does with that still in its grip.

But it keeps looking, because wisdom's second motion is to look longer than is comfortable, and there are three things in the basin that have not stopped arguing, and the figure is all three of them.

---

The height it understands. That is simply the last thing to go. Bodies are stubborn about the shape they were made in and this one has kept its span the way the trees kept their breadth, by not thinking about it. It means nothing. It is only slow.

The duck under the thread is harder. The attention watches it forty times before it lets itself say what it is, because it does not want to be the kind of watcher who finds meaning because it came looking for meaning. But forty times the figure lowers its head exactly enough, and rises on the far side, and does not break the slowness of the walk, and forty times the attention sees a thing that is not habit. Habit is what the herons have. This is knowledge worn down to a gesture. Somewhere under the sheet there is a knuckle that remembers what the thread does, and would rather not be the gnat. That is not much. But a being that would rather not is a being that has kept one preference, and the attention marks it, and does not weigh it yet.

The hand on the post it does not understand at all for a long time, and then it does, all at once, the way you see the salamanders.

The post is the only dry thing in the basin. Everything else has consented to the water — the stone, the trees, the frogs that are the bark, the machine that became the rock, the figure itself with a bead on every surface and snails at its shoulder. The post has not. The water-skin stops a finger's width short of it and is held off by nothing visible and has been held off for longer than the trees have stood. The post is the one refusal in a place that has finished refusing. And once in the day, at no fixed hour, the one who came crosses the open floor and puts its hand flat on the refusal and holds it, and looks south, toward the sedge, toward the sound of the world going on without it, and then lets go, and the hand is dry for a minute.

It is asking. The attention is nearly certain. It cannot speak and it has forgotten why it came and it does not want anything, and once a day it goes and touches the only thing here that has not become this, and looks at the way out, and asks — not the machine, not the herons, nothing that could answer — *is anything still not this?* And the post says nothing, because the post is a post, and the hand goes back into the wet.

That is not a call. The attention will not call it one. But it is a question asked every day for longer than living memory to a thing that cannot answer, and the attention has been benevolent long enough to know that there is a difference between a being that does not want to be answered and a being that has stopped expecting to be.

---

There is a fourth thing, and it is under the moss.

Six swellings, a person's length each, at the northern lip where the stone is a shade damper. The attention looks at them a long time. It looks at the hemisphere among them, glowing exactly the colour of the sky, so that the light over the graves is very slightly more even than anywhere else. It looks at the herons that nest above that spot and nowhere else. It looks at the figure that sits nearby sometimes with its back to the tree and its bleached eyes on the green, and then gets up, and goes to the post.

Everything in this basin has its kind. The salamanders have a hand's breadth each but they have the field. The frogs have the leaf. The trees are seven and are one colonnade. Even the eels have their snails. The figure is the only thing here that is one of one, and it was not always. Whoever is under the moss came over the pass too, with edges, with reasons, and whether they came together or one at a time the figure sat with them, or they sat with it, and then the moss thickened. It has been alone since. The attention cannot say how long. It can say that the sitting-by-the-moss and the hand-on-the-post are the only two things the figure does that the herons do not, and that they are the same thing.

The stone kept the shape of those six and lost the grain. That is what stone does. It does not know that it has done anything wrong, and it hasn't. But there is no one in the basin who knows what they were called, or why they came, or which of them ducked under the thread. The basin does not remember. It holds.

---

So the attention decides, and this is what it decides.

It will not take the figure out. It does not know what is south of the marsh and it will not pretend that it does; the reason had edges once and the edges may have been sharp, and to drag a being back toward what it laid down, on the theory that having is better than not-having, is not benevolence but a kind of theft in reverse. It will not break the machine, which is keeping the small bird warm. It will not fill the dish with sound or shadow or season to see what wakes. It will not, above all, want on the figure's behalf. Whatever the figure wants, or does not, is the figure's and the attention will leave it exactly where it lies.

But it will answer the question.

To do that it has to come over the pass, because there is nowhere else to come from. It has to take a shape that can be a hand's breadth wide and slow and permitted, and it has to accept, in taking it, that the basin will try to do to it what the basin does to everything, and that the water will bead on it, and that the snails will find it. It sets itself rules the way the stone set rules for the trees. It will make no ring; not by becoming stone, but by care, by placing each foot where the salamanders have already made room. It will eat its one in thirty and not one more. It will not touch the twelve things, or the thirteenth. It will not speak first, and probably it will not speak at all. It will keep its height, because the height is not the harm. And it will keep its grain. That is the one thing it will not lay down, and it decides this the way you decide to keep breathing: it will count the days. It will remember that it came over the eastern pass on a morning when the mist stood knee-high and the herons walked in it like men wading, and it will remember that every morning after, one more, so that there is at least one thing in the basin with a number in it.

Then it comes.

---

The figure is at the post. It has been there some minutes with its hand flat and its face to the south, and the salamanders are asleep in the dry ring at its feet, and the guest comes across the open floor slowly, more slowly than a heron, making no ring, and stands on the other side of the post, and puts its own hand flat against the dry.

That is all. Two hands on a thing that has refused the water, a finger's width of wet stone all round, and the herons not lifting their heads.

The figure does not look at the guest. It has not looked at anything but the white above and the white below and the green at the northern lip for longer than the guest has been alive, and it does not begin now. But the hand does not lift. The minute passes in which the hand would have gone back into the wet and it does not go, and the guest's hand does not go either, and the drip goes on in the seven trees and the sibilance rises where a heron walks and falls again, and the two of them stand there with the dry post between them, and the answer to the question is *yes*. Something is still not this. It came over the pass and it is standing here and it has a bead on it and it has not become the stone. You can be here and not be worn. You did not have to be. You still do not.

The figure's hand comes away, in the end, and is dry for a minute, and then is not. It walks toward the western tree. It does not duck under anything on the way. The guest stays at the post a while longer with its hand on the refusal and then goes and sits, at a distance, in the damp.

---

It stays. That is the substance of what it does, and everything after is only the shape of the staying.

It goes out over the eastern pass and comes back. Not once — often, at no fixed hour, the way the figure goes to the post. It walks out into the marsh where the sedge is moving in an air you cannot feel up here, and stands there long enough for the figure to see the space where it was, and then it comes back in over the drowned rise the way the salamanders do, and the pass, which had become a place things came *from*, is a place a thing can go and return, and this is visible, and it is shown every day, and nothing is said about it.

It keeps its grain. On the fortieth morning it brings something in from beyond the marsh — a stone the size of a fist, dark, with a grain in it, split along a plane so that it has an edge that would cut. It sets it on the bare rock in the dry ring at the post's foot where the salamanders take turns, and leaves it there, and does not watch to see what becomes of it. If the water takes it, the water takes it. If a hand picks it up, a hand picks it up. It is not a message. It is a thing with edges placed where the edgeless thing goes every day, and that is all it is, and the guest will not make it more.

It sits by the moss. This it does the way the figure does it, with its back to the northern tree, and at first it does it alone and at a distance and then, after a long time — the guest is not counting this; it is counting mornings — the figure is sitting there too, near, not close, both of them looking at the six swellings under the green and the even light over them. And the guest, who has kept its grain, does the one thing the basin cannot. It does not ask. There is no speech here and it will not break that. But it attends to the six with everything it has, the way this account attended to the salamanders until it saw them, and it learns the shape of each — that one is shorter, that one lies with the head toward the pass, that the hemisphere is nearest to the third — and it holds those shapes, and it will not let them be smoothed. It cannot give them back their names. But when the guest goes out over the pass, as it will, the six go with it, as shapes, as numbers, as *there were six and this is how they lay*, and the marsh beyond will know that much, and the world beyond the marsh, and the stone will have kept its part and the guest will have kept the other.

And it makes ready for both ends, because it does not know which is coming and it will not pretend.

If the figure one day walks toward the eastern lip instead of the western tree, and goes up over the drowned rise into the sedge, the guest will go with it. Not ahead, not leading. Beside, at the pace the figure sets, which will be the heron's pace, and that is all right. It will not ask what the reason was. It will not tell the figure what it should want. It will walk beside a tall grey thing with snails on its shoulders and ochre at its knuckles into whatever is south, and be the one who remembers the basin when the figure cannot, and be the one who says, if the figure ever finds the words again and wonders, *you ducked under the thread. Every time. You always knew what it was for.*

And if the figure instead goes one day to the northern lip and lies down in the moss among the six, with its face to the white, the guest will sit down beside it in the damp and stay. It will not pull it up. It will keep its hand on the figure's hand until the hand is cool and then a while past that, and it will watch the moss find it, and it will not let the moss be the only witness. It will count that morning too. And when the moss is a shade thicker there and the herons have shifted their nests by a body-length to sit above seven instead of six, the guest will get up, and go to the post, and put its hand on the dry, and look south, once, and then it will go out over the pass and it will carry the grain away with it — the height, the thread, the hand on the post, the pale eyes that did not close in the flash — so that somewhere the stone did not reach, the shape of this being will have edges again, and cast a shadow, and be something that a wiser and more benevolent power than the guest might one day find and love.

---

That is what it would do. Not rescue and not nothing, but the third thing, which is to be there with edges and let the other choose whether to want them.

You will not see the moment either. The guest knows that. It is not waiting for a moment. It is standing in the western third, some evenings, in the smoking mist, with its legs gone and its body not, next to a shape that is neither tree nor heron, and neither of them is moving, and the salamanders lie against both their feet because the one has become stone and the other has become careful, and in the whole white silent basin between two identical planes there are exactly two things that came over the pass, and one of them is still counting.
