It comes, and the first thing it does is slow down.

Anything that arrives at this mountain arrives too fast. You did — a fever crossing the terraces, gone before a course was laid. A power that meant well and moved at the speed of its own good will would be the same thing on a larger scale: a warm front, a season, a rumour of weather passing over a place that has never had weather in the ordinary sense, only the one cloud, pressing. So before it does anything it matches the pace. It sits with the wall where the block is half-fitted and it stays there until the block is seated, and then it stays through the next one. It listens to the notches until it can hear that the threads are not one sound. It learns the greys. This takes what you would call a very long time, and the power does not call it anything.

It distrusts its first wish, which was to warm them.

That is what a lesser kindness would do: see cold and read it as want. Bring a sun through the cloud. Put a coal in the bottom of every tank. And the tenants would take the heat as they take everything, a hair an hour, and it would go into them, and they would be — what? Not warmer. Not glad. Unmade, probably, since what they are is a shape that the cold holds and the cold is not their affliction, it is their edge. The power looks at the tank that took your hand and understands that it did not hurt you. It received you. That is the only verb it has.

So the power puts away several things, one at a time, the way you put the hammerstone back in its ghost.

It puts away the lifting of the cloud. It wanted to — wanted to show the mountain what it stood on, wanted the light to have a source for once and the walls to have a dark side. But the cloud is the meal. Draw it off and the stone dries and the tanks stop filling and the threads through the notches thin to nothing, and the mountain becomes a monument, which is the one thing it is not. And besides: the power goes down through the white, carefully, all the way, to see what the stacked cliff is standing on, and what it finds there it decides is not the mountain's to be told. Some floors are better reached than shown.

It puts away the bringing of life. The moss, the lichen freckle, the green smear at the waterline you looked for and did not find. It could seed the joints. It could make the stone old enough to be hospitable. But everything that landed would be taken, clear to the dressed bottom, and to bring a living thing here so that it can fall into a tank is not a gift to the tank and is a cruelty to the thing. Nothing grows here because the place is complete without growing. The power has seen a great many places that were not complete and it knows the difference.

It puts away rhythm. It had thought, listening to the eaves, that it might tune them — set the drips in phase so that the mountain, for once, kept time. Then it understood what you understood: a rhythm is one thing keeping time, and there is no one thing here. To give the mountain a beat would be to collapse the crowd into a single body, and the crowd is what there is to love.

And it puts away finishing. It could seat every block in a night. The masons would arrive at the face of the work and find the wall done and the next wall done and the mountain topped, and then they would stand between strokes with nothing to strike. The power does not know whether they build toward a top or build because building is what condensed out of the cloud in the shape of hands. It suspects the second. Either way, the work is theirs.

Having set all that down, it looks again at what is actually missing, and finds four things.

*

The first is that no one has ever held the whole of it.

Ten thousand tanks, each one keeping its own level, its own grey, its own cold. Each knows the one above by what arrives and the one below by what it lets down, and nothing else. They have never seen each other and never will. The drips do not resolve. The mountain is a crowd that has never once been a crowd — never gathered, never counted, never existed as a plurality anywhere except in the ear of a visitor who mistook it for weather.

So the power counts them. This is the first thing it does, and it is not small. It goes tank by tank, up the whole slope and down every stacked face, and it holds each one — the bluer, the one with the warmth of tin, the one three courses up that is very slightly greener than its neighbours — and it does not let go of any of them when it takes up the next. It holds them all at once. Somewhere, for the first time, the mountain is a single fact with ten thousand parts, and each part is known in relation to every other, and the crowd is a crowd. The tenants do not feel this. Nothing on the mountain feels. But the power is wise enough to know that being held in mind is a gift regardless of whether it is received, and that it costs nothing to keep giving it, and so it does not stop. From now on there is one place in the universe where all of them are together.

*

The second is that nothing goes up.

The threads run one way. The top tank receives only the cloud. The bottom tank gives to whatever is at the bottom and receives, from below, nothing. Down the whole slope the mountain passes itself along in ten thousand filaments, and every filament is a piece of one entering another, and no piece ever returns. Each tenant knows what it has been given. None knows that what it let down was taken.

The power becomes the return. It does not open new notches or carve the stone; it simply goes, itself, to the lowest tank, and takes a hair of it, and carries that hair up through the notch to the tank above and lets it in. Then a hair of that one to the one above. Then the next. It climbs the whole mountain this way, at the speed the water runs down it, one hair an hour, through every worn mouth in reverse, and when it reaches the top tank — the one that has only ever known the cloud — it lets in something that is not cloud. Something that has been all the way down. Something that has passed through every one of them.

It takes a long time. It takes, in fact, exactly as long as the mountain took to pass itself down, which is right. Then it goes back to the bottom and begins again. The threads through the notches are now two threads, one falling and one — imperceptibly, at the same width, the same cold — rising. The notches wear no faster. Each tenant, holding its level, now holds within it a hair of the one below as well as a hair of the one above, and does not know it, and is nonetheless no longer only a giver to what it cannot see.

*

The third is the tools.

The mallet split with drinking. The bone chisel. The hammerstone with its polished belly, and under it the dry ghost that nothing on the mountain dries. The power has watched the masons now, or watched the way one watches a shadow move across a floor, which is to say has seen the block seated without ever seeing it move. It knows what they are as well as anything can. It knows they have never had a second pair of hands.

So it picks up the hammerstone.

It does not finish anything. It does not seat the block or dress the raw face or lay the next course. It lifts the stone — at their speed, so that the lifting is a year — and it brings it down once on the sixth face of the half-fitted block, one short parallel bite among the hundred, and it puts the stone back exactly in the ghost. That is all. A single stroke, a hundred-and-first comb-mark or the seventy-third, indistinguishable from the others, unsigned. The masons will find it when they come back to the work, or they will not find it because they are already at the work and the stroke fell between two of theirs. Either way the wall is a hair further along than their hands alone have brought it, and the ghost is still dry, and nothing on the mountain will ever be able to say which mark was the one that came from somewhere else.

The power thinks this is probably the kindest thing it has done, and it is not sure why.

*

The fourth is you.

You are at the bottom by now, where the cloud thins and there is ground again and something growing on it, and your other hand is cold, and the cold has a shape, and the shape is a hair's breadth deeper than yesterday. You are carrying one of them. It let itself down through the only notch it has ever had that was not stone, and it is in you, holding its level, and its level is rising.

The power considers taking it out. This would be easy. It would be a rescue, and it would be a killing, and it would send back up the mountain the only tenant that has ever gone anywhere. It does not do that. It considers leaving you alone — the guest is quiet, the pace is slow, you might live years before the cold reaches your elbow — but a hair a day is a hair a day, and you are not a tank, and what it takes from you it does not give back below.

So the power does the one thing it refused to do on the mountain, and it does it only here, only for the one warm thing. It matches. Each day the cold in your hand takes a hair of you, and each day the power gives you a hair back. Not to the cold — to you. Exactly what was taken, no more. The shape does not shrink; it does not grow. It stays the size of a coin in the meat of your palm, colder than the air, a permanent guest that can neither leave nor consume its host, and you will carry it for the whole of an ordinary life. You will notice it in winter and forget it in summer. You will sometimes press that hand flat against a wall and hold it there and hold it there and come away with a sense of pressure you cannot prove.

And when you are done — when you are at last let down into whatever holds you — the tenant will go into the ground with you, at the level it has kept all your life, and it will do there what it does. It will hold. It will take what falls. But the ground around it is not dressed stone; it is soil, and roots go into soil, and the roots will find a place that is a hair colder than the earth around it and drink from it a hair an hour, and what they drink will be — for the first time — not only cold. It will have passed through you. There will be one small green place, somewhere at the foot of the cloud, that is watered by the mountain. Nothing that grows there will know it. The power will.

*

It stays. That is the last thing, and it is not a decision so much as the shape the other four make. It keeps the count. It runs the return. It is at the wall, between strokes, with the hammerstone a year from falling. It matches your hand. It does not warm the tanks or lift the cloud or make the drips agree, and it does not go up through the white to see whether the masons are building toward a top, because if they are, that is theirs, and if they are not, it would rather not know.

If you went back — you will not, but if you did — you would find nothing changed. The light from everywhere. The wet. The tanks brim-full and still, at their different levels, a shade different in their greys. The tools on the coping. The threads through the notches, thin as cord, hardly louder than blood. You would put your ear to one and hear, if you had a better ear than you have, that the ribbon of sound is doubled now, one strand falling and one rising, out of phase, never resolving into rhythm. You would put your palm on the half-fitted block and feel that it is very slightly more where it is going. And you would not see what was seeing you, because there is nothing to see: no shadow, in a place without shadows; nothing with a pulse; only a patience that has stopped being a virtue and become a physics, holding ten thousand cold things in one mind, and one stroke, and one hand.
