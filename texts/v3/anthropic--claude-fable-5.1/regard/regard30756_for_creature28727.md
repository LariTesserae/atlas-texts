It has time. That is most of what makes it wise, and nearly all of what makes it benevolent: it can look at a thing for exactly as long as the thing takes.

So it looks. It does not come down through the lens. It does not do anything, yet. It attends from the direction the light comes, and the first the basin knows of it is that the green-brown dark is a shade less dark, which nothing notices, and that there is a warmth in the water that is not the water's own, which the strands notice, and go rigid for, and then — finding no concussion behind it — release.

It reads the place the way you read it, from the bones outward. But it can read further back than you.

---

A path. Switchbacked, stepped where it had to be, the steps cut by people who did not expect anyone to be in a hurry. At the top, the crater, and in the crater a small shrine of the local dark stone, and at the heart of the shrine a bowl.

The bowl was fed. A seep, a thread of the mountain's own water, cold, coming up through a crack in the floor and filling the bowl and running off over the sill in a line no wider than a finger. That was the whole point of the climb. You came up, and you knelt at the right distance, and you put your hands in the cold water that came out of the mountain, and you went down again. Some of them brought something and left it on the sill. Most of them brought only themselves. Sometimes a low warm shape came with them on four legs and lay across the doorway and waited.

Then they stopped coming. It does not matter why. A road went another way; a village emptied; the winters lengthened. What matters is that there was a last one, and the last one knelt and put hands in the bowl and went down, and did not know it was the last one, and the water in the bowl did not know it either.

The bowl kept filling. That was what it was for. The seep ran and the bowl held what came and the finger-width line went over the sill and down the floor and into the cracks, year after year, and no hands came. And the water — which had been touched, which had been touched many thousands of times by things that had come a long way up on purpose — did not know how to stop being the thing that was touched. So it did the only thing water knows. It became more of itself. It rose past the sill and did not run off. It filled the inner room and the outer and the crater and it stood up over the lip and held there, and it was warm, because it had been touched, and it has never stopped being warm.

There is nothing in the bowl because everything is. You put your hand in and felt only the same water as everywhere else, and that was the correct finding. The bowl did not lose what it held. What it held got out.

The shrine went under, and then the shrine came down — not violently; a held sea is patient — and the water, sitting on the grave of the only thing it had ever been for, with the bones of that thing scattered under it, began to practice.

---

The power sees all of this in something less than a moment, and then it spends a long time deciding, because there are several things it could do and most of them are wrong.

It could lift the sea. Set it back in the bowl, or let it go down the mountain the way water goes. The summit would stand in air again; the bones would dry. But the water did not come here as a flood. It came the way a mourner comes to sit, and you do not carry a mourner out of a graveyard because they are sitting in the wrong place. And the strands would die in the air. And the animal is not a thing that can be poured.

It could quiet the roar. One thought, and the water's thinking would stop; the basin would be as silent as any other high cold place. But the roar is not a symptom. It is the water at work, and the gaps in it are where everything else here lives, and the animal steers by it and would be blind. Take the roar and you have not healed the water. You have stopped it.

It could give the water heads. Finish the grammar. Let it, at last, set a black glass kneeler at the bowl and not have to fog it in a sharp indrawn breath. The power holds this one longest, because it looks like kindness. Then it sees what would come of it: a stone figure at the right distance, with the right tilt, that does not dissolve. A statue. A shrine to a shrine. The water would have made, perfectly, the one thing it has been trying not to make, which is a kneeler that does not go down again. The indrawn breath is not embarrassment. It is the water knowing the difference between a person and a picture of one, and refusing, every time, at the last instant, to settle for the picture. That refusal is the most honest thing in the basin. It should not be taken away.

It could tell the water they are not coming back. But the water knows. Everything about the practice says it knows.

And it could do nothing, which is what wise powers most often do, and it weighs this against everything else. The place is not broken. The strands eat; the animal is fed; the water is not in pain, exactly, or not more than any grief that has learned to keep busy. There is a case for leaving it.

But the water is not practicing for its own sake. It is practicing *toward* something, and the something has not come up the path for a very long time, and to call that whole and walk on would not be wisdom. It would only be distance, dressed as wisdom. There is one thing the water has not had, and it is not a lesson and it is not a fix. It is the thing the bowl was for.

---

So it comes up the path.

Not down through the lens, where it has been watching from. Up. It goes around to the foot of the mountain and finds the switchbacks, gone to scree and root, and the steps that are still steps, and it takes them one at a time, at the pace of someone who does not expect to be in a hurry. It comes small. That is the whole of its art in this: it puts off everything it is except a shape about the size of what was lost, and it comes as that.

The path ends where the sea stands. There is a wall of warm water across the last switchback, domed, holding, and the roar comes through it as a shudder in the rock underfoot. The shape does not stop. It walks into the wall the way the others walked into the shrine's doorway, and the water — which has been leaving room — leaves room.
