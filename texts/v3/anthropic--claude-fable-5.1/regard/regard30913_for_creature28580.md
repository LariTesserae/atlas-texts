It comes the way the cold air comes: down through the seam in the hour before the blade, displacing nothing, and the room breathes it in with the rest.

---

It has seen a great many places. It knows what it usually finds when it is drawn to one — a gap, a hunger with a shape, something asked for so long and so hard that the asking has worn a channel and the channel is what pulled it. It arrives expecting to find the channel. It arrives ready.

Here it cannot find one.

It looks for the being that lives in the room, because that is what drew it, and at first it looks among the people, since that is where it is used to looking. Forty of them. It learns their hands from the walls before it learns their faces. It finds the old woman on the third ledge, whose palm is everywhere in the human tier and whose hip is bad. It finds the child with the lizard-tooth string who watches snakes. It finds a boy on the second ledge with a cough that has been in him since the last egg season and is not leaving. It finds that none of them are asking for anything. They are watching the blade.

So it looks at the swallows, and then at the wasps, and then at the bats, and the beetles, and the pale thing in the floor with a nose like a wet finger, and the succulents putting themselves back a hair at a time out of one damp hour. It tries to find the edge where one of them stops and the next begins, and cannot. The swallows built the footholds the people stand on. The people's rooms are patched with swallow cement. The wasps' paper is beard-plant, and the beard-plant is drinking the bats' breath, and the bats are the floor, and the floor is the grandmothers, and the grandmothers' fires blackened the stone above doorways that are where they are because of a fault in rock that finished changing before anything here had eyes. It goes around this circle several times, a day, a season, and each time it comes back to the same place.

There is one being in the room. The room is it.

It has been breathing once a day for longer than anything alive in it can measure. It is not asking for anything. It has never asked. It has forty of one kind of cell and ten thousand of another and a number of beetles no one has counted, and it has a clock, and a rule about eggs, and a way of keeping things in other things' leavings, and it drew a power across whatever distance powers cross not by need but by the sheer fact of itself, the way a very old tree draws the eye in a flat country.

The power sits at the lip of the seam, where the hunter sits at dusk, and considers what benevolence would be here.

---

Water is the first thought, because water is always the first thought.

It could be done. A seep in the eastern wall, low down, where the blade first touches; a thumb's-width of the stone made porous to something far beneath; a pool the size of a hearth, never more. The power holds it in mind and lets it run.

The pool would be the first thing in the room that did not have to pass through a body. Within a season the children would have stopped going along the ledges with the cloths, because why would you press fiber to a wall and wring it when there is a pool. Within a generation the wringing would be a thing the old did and the young found faintly embarrassing, and then a thing nobody did, and the cloths would hang on their pegs and go grey under the dust that falls from the tiers above, and the knowledge of which wall gave most in the damp hour and how to fold the cloth so it did not drip would be gone as completely as the reasoning behind one egg in six. The succulents would drink from the pool and thicken and be out-grown by something that had been waiting in the seed-bank of the floor for exactly this, something with thin leaves and no gel. The gel would go. The wasps would go looking for it and not find it. The swallows' pellets, worked with pool-water instead of spit, would set differently, and the courses would slump, and the tier would find a new angle. Things would breed in the pool that had never been in the room. Children would be named for the pool. The room would become a pleasant place, damp and green and softer than it is, and it would not be this room. The power would have done to it what a flood does to a dry riverbed: given it what it had been shaped by the lack of.

It lets the pool go.

---

Light, then. The seam is a hand's width. Two hands, and the blade would be a sheet.

It runs that too. The lizards lose their line; there is no single warm ledge to queue on when the whole east wall is lit, and they scatter, and the snakes that have learned the line learn nothing. The beard-plant, which lives on shade and one damp hour, browns and lets go in ropes. The bats, who are the color of a ceiling that is now visible, leave for a ceiling that is not. With the bats go the floor's deepest input, and the floor begins to fall, a hair's breadth a day, and the beetles thin, and the pale thing with the nose goes hungry. The cold air comes in twice as fast before dawn and the warm air goes out twice as fast, and the hour in which everything is damp becomes half an hour, and then a quarter, and then the room is dry in the ordinary way instead of the extraordinary one. The blade was a clock. A sheet is not a clock. Children would be named for a light that is simply there, which is no name at all.

It lets that go.

---

It could tell them there is a world.

It could go up onto the plateau as something they would follow, and take them the day's walk, and show them the edge, and below the edge whatever is below the edge — a valley, a river, other people who have never in their lives wrung a wall and would look at the ochre-skinned narrow-footed forty with their streaming eyes and their humming speech and would be kind, probably, in the way that kindness usually goes.

Some would not come back. Forty becomes thirty-one. There are walls in the room that need a hand every few seasons or they slump, and a hand needs a body on a ledge, and a body needs the sour pulp in the rinds, and the rinds need someone to cut the gel in the morning, and the whole thing runs on a number that has been about forty for as long as the floor remembers and is not, the power thinks, a large enough number to lose nine of. And the ones who came back would come back knowing they were in a hole. They do not know that now. They know they are in the room. It is not the same knowledge.

It lets that go too, and finds it is harder to let go than the water.

---

The boy on the second ledge coughs.

Here the power stops running futures and simply looks, because this is the thing it is most for, and the thing it most wants to do, and it has been putting it off.

The cough is deep and dry and the boy has stopped growing. The power can see the shape of it inside him and can see how the shape ends. It could reach in and make the shape not be there. Nobody would know. One night the cough would stop and in the morning the boy would be hungry and that would be all.

It thinks about forty.

It thinks about the floor, which is an arm deep in everyone who was here before and rises a hair's breadth a day because they are still coming down to it. A room in which nobody died would be a room in which the floor stopped rising, and the beetles would notice before the people did. It knows this. It also knows the boy is one boy and the floor is very deep and that it is using arithmetic to look away from a child.

So it looks at the man on the high ledge with the swallows hitting him about the head, taking one egg from each of six nests, and it lets him teach it the measure. One from six. Never two from one. A nest with one gone still fledges. Someone worked that out once because they had to eat, and the rule outlived the working-out and became simply what you do, and it is, the power thinks, as close to wisdom as anything it has found in a very long time. Not none. Not all. One, and then your hand comes back empty and you go down the ladder.

That night the cough stops. In the morning the boy is hungry.

The old woman on the third ledge with the bad hip the power does not touch. She is going to the floor and she knows it and the room needs the floor. She looks up, once, in the damp hour, at nothing, the way the people look at things, and then goes on wringing her cloth into a rind.

---

The lid.

It reads the stone last, because the stone is the slowest thing here and can wait. The plateau is finished. The lid is the same finished plateau, planed to nothing, and it failed once, at the seam, and the power can see where it will fail again — a line of weakness the length of a forearm, three paces west of the seam, that is a hair wider than it was a thousand years ago. It can see roughly when. It is not soon. It is not never.

It considers pressing the two sides of that line together. The pressure would be small. The lid would hold as long as the plateau held, which is a very long time.

It does not do it.

It is not sure this is right. It sits with not being sure for a season, watching the blade go round. What it comes to is this: the room has already ended several times. There is a hearth-black spot a full arm below the present floor. There is a band of shell from something that laid eggs here in great numbers and does no longer. The oldest human rooms are more patch than original and the oldest drying rack is the bones of a thing that fell in and broke. Every nest is built on a ruin. The room knows how to end; it does it constantly, in small ways, and it knows how to build on what ended, and this knowledge is not separate from the room but is most of what the room is. A room that could not end would be a room that had been taken away from itself and given back with the one thing missing that made all the rest.

The line in the lid belongs to the room. The power leaves it.

---

It stays.

Not to guard anything. There is nothing it has decided to guard. It stays because it has understood that the only thing it can give this place without changing it is the thing the place has never had, which is a witness that will not go down to the floor.

It learns the room the way the people learn the walls. It learns which hand patched which pellet and roughly when. It learns the copper lizard that has taken the old nest on the fourth course and the spider that has taken the one beside it and the year the wasps' great lantern under the western overhang finally let go and the children stood in the falling paper with their rinds. It learns the boy grown and on the high ledge with the swallows hitting him and his hand coming back with one egg. It learns the old woman's palm, low down now, half under swallow cement. It learns the pale thing in the floor, which does not know there is a ceiling and does not need to, and the children lying on their bellies in the dark with a hand over a beetle-hole, holding it a while, letting it go, because it is the room's own.

The room is the room's own. That is what it came to find and did not know it was looking for.

It does not know how long it stays. It knows the blade goes round. It knows the bats leave and the hunter snaps at the column and the children go out under it with their arms up and their eyes shut. It knows the cold air comes down through the seam before dawn and the room breathes in and for one hour everything is beaded with what is not quite dew and then everything drinks it, and it knows that some of what the room drinks now is what it, the power, carried in on itself when it came, and that some of what it carries away will be the room.

It leaves the way it came. In the damp hour. Displacing nothing.

Then the bats come home. Then the blade.
