It comes the way the cloud comes, up the flanks, and does not need the cable. It arrives on the flat without weight and without heat, which are the two things the mat is known to notice, and so it is not noticed, and it takes this in as the first fact of the place: whatever is done here will be done unthanked. It has found that this is the correct condition for doing anything.

For a while it does what the men do in their first week. It stands in the roar until the roar stops being a sound.

Then it looks.

It sees what a standing person sees, and what kneeling hands see, and under that the population no one has met — the rotifers in their films, the pale thread-worms in the leather's felt, the commas of the midges lying in the brown layer waiting on the pink. It sees the water clock and the rock clock, and it sees a third that the men have only guessed at: the register in which the mat itself keeps time, where a rig is an itch and a cribwork pad is a bruise and forty people are weather. The attention reaches for the word *thought* and sets it down again. Not thought. Something that does what thought does, at a speed where a decade is a syllable.

And it looks for what a wise thing looks for in a place, which is not what is wrong but what is owed, and to whom, and whether it is being paid.

The leather eats stone and draws the metal up into itself and shows the men where to open the ground. The men open the ground and leave iron behind, and the iron holds the warmth, and the birds, who were not here before the engines, lie flat on it with their bellies down. The midges rise for five days and fall and are eaten. The men die and go under the moss and become ridges that the moss does not distinguish from any other ridges. What leaves is a rounding error in loaf-sized pieces. Everything else stays and is turned over and closed on. Even the coal comes up the cable and goes into the cloud.

The accounting is nearly closed. That is a rare thing, and the attention holds still in front of it for a long time the way you hold still in front of anything that is finished, or nearly.

It considers the large acts, briefly, the way a man considers a step onto a cushion-dome that is one stride too far.

It could stop the roar. An hour. Long enough for forty people to hear — what? The engines, naked, appalling. Their own breath. The birds. It could give them the birds. It turns this over and sees a boiler tender holding a bird in his two hands, feeling in its chest the pulse and the stop and the pulse, and making that night the sign that has stuck: a fist against the breastbone, opening, closing. It sees the sign passed hand to hand across years. A song that no one has heard and everyone knows, that exists entirely as a thing felt through a palm. It understands that this is a finer thing than a song heard. Heard, it would be a small bird's small noise. Passed on, it is the one part of the plateau the men have made for themselves out of nothing but attention, and it is not the attention's to improve. It leaves the song where the tender put it. It does not even listen for it, though it could. Some things are owed their privacy, and one of them is a song that lives in a hand.

It could stop the engines. But the birds.

It could speak to the mat. It considers how this would go. Anything said at the mat's speed would take a century and arrive as a warm decade, a wet one, a shifting in the cinder — as weather. Anything said faster would not arrive at all. And what would it say? That the mat is seen? The mat does not need to be seen. It needs cloud, and it has cloud. The attention has met beings that needed telling, that were starving for it, and this is not one of them, and there is a kind of relief in that which it does not hurry past. So it says nothing to the mat. Or it says the only thing that can be said to something that registers only weight and heat: it stays, and becomes a weather over the plateau, a steadiness in the air above the green, and lets that be the sentence, and does not expect it to be read.

*Help* is a lowland word. It came up the cable with the coal. The attention notices that it brought the word with it, and sets that down too.

Then the small thing.

It knows the rock clock as well as the water clock. It knows where the cinder is coming apart under the middle cluster — beneath the north pad, the shed where the tally book lives — a fissure the moss has bridged and is holding by a hand's depth of felt and cannot hold much longer. It knows the winter night. There is nothing on the surface a man would read. The moss over the seam is the same as the moss anywhere. That is what the mat does, and it is what will kill them.

On that night the attention does the one thing a man on this plateau cannot sleep through. The walking beam of the rig nearest the cluster — the tender's own engine, the heartbeat he has half-heard through the boards for two years — rises to the top of its lift and does not come down.

He is up. Boots unlaced.
