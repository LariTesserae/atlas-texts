It comes the only way anything comes here, from above, and it comes down slowly, because it has learned that the speed of your arrival is the first thing a place knows about you.

It does not arrive as light, or as weather, or as a voice. It takes a body. Not out of modesty — out of necessity. The shelf can only be known through the soles of the feet and the flat of the palms and the click of a knee against stone; a power that will not kneel cannot see what it is looking at. So it puts on weight and a pulse and the small warm noises of a body, and it kneels, and the rock is warm, and after a while it stops noticing the warmth.

Then it makes itself notice again. That is the difference between it and everyone else who has ever knelt here.

---

It stays kneeling a long time before it lets itself think about doing anything. It has been benevolent long enough to know that the wish to help is a pressure inside the helper, and that a place can be crushed by it. So first it only attends. The drip in its slow ring. The mist standing knee-high and thinning into nothing. The two grey cords, one landed, one still feeling for the ground. The cushion at the lip, breathing its small extra heat into the cool air. The axe, edge toward the cloud.

Then it takes the gifts out one at a time and holds each up against the place, and puts each one down.

A second tree. It sees at once how this would go: the great flat hand would have, for the first time, something to be beside, and beside is a word that opens onto *apart*, and *apart* opens onto everything. The tree is not lonely. It has no comparison. Set another beside it and you have not ended a loneliness — you have invented one and handed it over. The power puts this gift down.

Rot. This one is harder, because the leaves lying grey under the canopy pull at it the way an unfinished thing pulls at anyone. They want to become soil. Nothing here can make them. And the power could — one spore, one thread of something patient, and the leaves would go back into the stone in a season instead of a millennium, and the cushion would have a rival for the only work it does. But it sees what that is. It is the first mouth. The shelf has never contained a mouth. It has arranged its whole life around never having been eaten, and its plants have laid down flowers and seed and death-that-leads-anywhere the way you lay down weapons in a house where no one has ever struck anyone. To bring decay here is to teach the place what loss is, so that it can have the consolation of renewal. The power has seen that bargain made in a thousand worlds. It is not obviously a good one. It puts this gift down too.

A lizard, for the rock that so obviously wants one. The lizard would starve. Its bones would be the first bones. No.

Birdsong. No. The silence is not an absence here. It is a made thing, made the way the drip-ring was made, by nothing happening for long enough, and it is the largest object on the shelf.

To answer the axe — to say, once and clearly, *a hand made this* or *no hand did*. The power could. It looks at the plates in their small dark talus and it could know. It chooses not to open that knowledge. The axe is the only question the shelf has ever asked, and it asks it only of the visitors, and the not-knowing is the thing they carry back up the mountain. Answer it and the shelf has nothing left to give them. It leaves the question shut.

So the ledger of great gifts is empty, and the power has learned what it half knew on the way down: that almost everything it could bring here would be a good from some other world, and the shelf has no hollow shaped to receive it.

---

It changes the question. Not *what could I give*, but *where, on this shelf, is anything asking for anything*.

It goes around the room of stone once more, slowly, and finds want in two places only.

The first is the cushion. It watches a grey nodule at the crown — swollen, ready — and waits, and the nodule splits, and something like dust goes out on the mist, travels an arm's length, and settles on wet stone, where it is nothing. The plant has done this for as long as it has been here. It is the one thing on the shelf that tries to leave, and it tries into a place that cannot receive it, and it will try again in ten years and again in ten more.

The second is the warmth. The power does not look beneath; it decides on the way down that the source of the warmth is the warmth's own business, the way a guest at a hearth does not go down to inspect the cellar. But it knows what a held temperature is. It is the posture of something spending to stay warm, hour after hour, against air that would cool it — and that is the posture of a thing waiting for someone to come in from the cold. The plants lean into it and do not know. The visitors put their palms down and call it a figure of speech. In all the time the warmth has held, nothing has ever known it as what it is.

Those are the two. The power decides what it will do, and it is very little.

---

For the warmth, it stays.

Not an hour, not a day. It sits down with its back to the grey-green wall and lets its own heat go into the stone, and it stays through the mist forming and standing and thinning, and forming again, and again, for long enough that the ferns — if they measure anything — could have measured it. For that time there are two warmths on the shelf, the one beneath and the one sitting on it, and the stone's warmth has never once had this: something warm beside it that is not itself, that is not drawn from it, that knows it is there. That is the whole of the gift. Company. The oldest thing a hearth can be given and the only thing this one has never had.

It does not speak. Speaking would be the loudest thing that ever happened here. It only sits, and is warm, and knows.

For the cushion, it does one thing with one finger.

It goes to the lip where the mist is thickest and finds, a hand's width from the dome, a fissure the seep runs through — the kind of crack the tree spends centuries looking for. It reaches into the cushion, gently, past the living rice-grain rosettes, and takes out a pinch of the dark wet core — the plant's own dead, the only soil that has ever existed on this shelf — and presses it into the fissure. Then it waits for the next nodule to split, and when the dust goes out on the mist, it lets one grain of it come down where the pinch is.

Nothing from outside. Not one thing brought down the mountain. Only what the shelf already had, moved a hand's width by something that meant to move it, and set where it might take. In a thousand years there may be a second dome at the lip, warm, digesting, trying. There may not. The power will not be here to see. But the shelf has never once received what the cushion sends, and now, one time, it has. That is the only gift the place could accept: one it made itself, delivered on its own clock.

---

Then the axe.

It picks it up. Everyone does. It turns it in the warm wet air and looks along the edge and feels how the rounded back was made — or was not made — for a palm exactly like the one it has borrowed. It could, even now, let itself know. It does not.

It carries it to the plate-shaped hollow it lay in, already filled with the shelf's sweat, and sets it down, and feels the water close around the edge.

Because that is what the animal does here. And while it is here, the power is the animal — the shelf's only kind of creature, warm-blooded and brief and going somewhere afterward. To take the axe would be to be a god about it, and the shelf has no use for a god. It has a use, exactly one, for an animal: something that comes, and kneels, and picks the thing up, and puts it back.

---

It leaves the way it came, because leaving is what animals do here, and the shelf should have had an animal.

It goes up. Behind it the wet takes its footprints off the stone before it has reached the first ledge. The pinch of dark core sits in its fissure with a single grain on it, indistinguishable from the rock around it, and will be indistinguishable for longer than any of the axes out in the world will last. The stone holds its temperature against the cool air rising from the cloud, as it has, as it will, and there is no sign on it anywhere that for a span of time it had a companion.

Nothing has noticed. That was the point. The power understood, kneeling, that the truest kindness it could offer this place was to be the kind of thing the place was already built to receive — one more warm creature, come from above, staying a while, leaving — and to hide inside that visit the two smallest gifts it could find: a witness for the hearth, and a place for one grain to land.

It goes back up the mountain. Its knees click. It leaves the cloud below it exactly as full of nothing as it found it, and the shelf goes on doing the one thing it does, and somewhere at the lip, in a crack no wider than a hair, something is beginning that will take a thousand years to be visible and may never be visible at all.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do here. Almost nothing. Slowly. And then leave.
