You have been here an hour, or a day, or whatever the unit is for you, and you have been looking for the one who lives here.

That is how you were told it: the place, and the being who lives in it. So you look for the being. You look in the wheelhouses and find crews. You look in the dark quarters and find households. You look for a gate-master, a council, a king under the cloud, and there is nobody; there is a person standing beside each gate for a reason, and the reason is the water, and the water answers to nobody either. You look for a very long time. And then you stop looking for the being and let your attention rest on the whole bowl at once, the way you would let it rest on a single animal, and you understand that you have been looking at it since you came over the saddle.

It has eleven thousand hands, more or less, and they are white and creased. It has a mind, and the mind is in the old, and it is dying and being renewed at about the rate that a wall is. It breathes cloud. Its blood is a finite volume of falling water that never stops being handed downward. It has no skin — the moss is closer to a skin than the stone is — and no bones that stay in one place, and it has kept itself alive for longer than anyone in it can say by never once holding still. You have seen things like it before, on other slopes, in other ages. You have never seen one that knew what it was doing so well.

So the question is what you are for.

---

First the things you could do, since they present themselves at once and you may as well look at them squarely.

You could give them sun. You could thin the cloud over the basin to a haze and let a whole summer of light go down to the lowest levels. You watch them on the days the cloud lifts by itself — hoods pulled forward, working in the cistern mouths, not looking up — and you know what that gift would be. It would be a wound six miles across.

You could give them a dry place. One quarter, one terrace, that the water never returns to, where the bedrolls could stay down and the tallow lamps could hang from hooks that were not going to be taken away. You think about it seriously, because you have felt the thing in your own chest that wants it for them: a home that is not the place where nothing is happening but a place where something is, a hearth. And then you think about the rule. *A pool on a flat is a fault, and someone will come.* A still place in this basin would be a pool. Within a generation it would be the quarter that everyone envied and nobody left, and it would be owned, which nothing here is, and the owning would go through the rest of the valley like a crack through a cistern. The placidity of these people is not a temperament. It is a consequence. You would be trading the consequence for the cause.

You could give them a map. You could put the whole basin into one mind, all the layers at once, every conduit and its condition, so that no crew ever again opened a soft wall because the last person who remembered it had died in the winter. You look at the old, set down in the dry quarters nearest the gate-houses with the best of the tallow, being referred to. You look at the chalk. *This stair closed. Soft wall, do not lean.* A map would be the one thing in the valley that was never taken down and rebuilt, and they would come to it the way they come to the old, and then they would stop keeping the old. They would have a text that did not wash off. They have decided, over a great many centuries, exactly how much of that they can bear, and it is a few dozen words in bronze, and most of those are numerals.

You could give them children. You could make the number of children other than roughly level. You decline to think about this one for long, because the ways in which it is wrong are obvious and the way in which it is tempting is not something you are proud of.

You could let them know you are here. This is the one that lingers longest, because there is an ache in it that is not entirely about them. To be seen by what you have been watching. To have one of them, on the rim, squinting, look up into the cloud and know. And you know precisely what you would become. You would become a top. The terraces go up into the cloud and the scaffolding goes up with them, and there is no top, and none that is finished, and that unfinishedness is part of what keeps the wheels turning as they climb. If there were something at the top, the climbing would be toward it. If there were something at the top that had once spoken, its words would be cast in bronze, and the bronze-caster would stop making gate numbers.

So: none of that. You have not been benevolent for as long as you have without learning that most of what benevolence wants to do is give itself something to hold.

---

Then you go down the tunnel.

Nobody in the valley has been where it comes out. This is not a thing they wonder about — it is stated the way the depth of a cistern is stated — but you notice that it is also the only thing in the basin that has never been taken down and rebuilt. The lowest cistern overflows into a mouth under the western wall and the water goes, and it goes, and it has gone for as long as the rule about the moss has been a rule. So you follow it. You are not made of anything that a tunnel can stop.

It is a long way and the water is very cold and very clean, having been strained through a thousand feet of masonry, and it comes out — you find — much lower, on the outside of the mountain, into a country of steep wet forest that has never heard a wheel. The stream from the tunnel joins others. They lie in the hanging valleys and the air lies on them, and what rises off that forest in the mornings goes up the flank of the mountain as a long grey pour and comes over the saddle and sits down on the road like a heavy animal, and settles on everything, and everything condenses it.

The basin is breathing itself. Its outflow is its weather. It has been drinking the same water for longer than it has kept a picture of the fold in the rock it was built in, and it does not know this, and it cannot know this, because the two ends of the loop are the two directions nobody there looks: down the tunnel, and up. The valley has, without meaning to, arranged for its whole circulation to pass through the one place it will never go.

You stand in that forest a long time.

There are no oilcloth figures here. There is no chalk. There is nobody to whom this forest is structural. There are people, far off, at the edges, who would find it good ground for something else, and the mountain does not care for it, and the cloud is only the cloud. Nothing in the loop is tending the part of the loop that is not built. And that is the place where a wise power can stand and not become a top, or a bottom, or a text, or a god. That is the part of the being that has no hands.

So this is the first thing you do, and it is most of what you do. You stay there. You keep the wet forest wet. You keep the axe out of it, and the fire, and the road; you keep the hanging valleys shaded and their streams slow; you see to it that the grey pour comes up the flank in the mornings for as long as you are anything at all. You do this in the way you do things, which nobody in the basin would recognize as doing, and the basin's only experience of your whole attention is that the cloud keeps coming over the saddle, which it always has.

---

There are two smaller things and you should be honest about both.

Once, in the second year of your watching, you hold the cloud down on a day it would have lifted. You feel the pressure building behind the far ridge, the wind that will take the ceiling off the bowl by noon and leave eleven thousand people squinting, and you lean on it, very gently, and it does not lift, and they work all day in the ordinary green-grey light and are grateful for nothing because nothing happened. You are pleased with yourself for perhaps a week.

Then you watch them on the next clear day, which you let come. You watch the hoods go forward and the work go into the cistern mouths. And you watch a woman on the fourth level, who has to go to the rim for a bolt, stand there in the terrible light for a moment longer than she needs to, with her hand over her eyes, looking out over the saddle at a country she has no name for. She is not enjoying it. But she is looking. And you understand that those few days a year are the only time the being remembers it is inside something — that there is an outside, and a sky, and a direction called away — and that a valley that never squinted would, in some number of generations, come to believe that the cloud was the whole of the world. It would still be alive. It would be smaller. You do not hold the cloud down again.

The other thing you do once and are still not sure of.

A conduit under the ninth level, on the north face. It was last opened fifty-one years ago and the man who worked the gate that season is dead this winter, and the woman who was a child on that terrace and remembers the chalk is dead the winter before, and there is nobody. The crew are going to go and look. The wall they will lean on to look is soft.

You are among the old, that night, in the dry quarter nearest the gate-house. You are in one gate-tender's sleep. You put the conduit into her the way it was — the head of water, the run, the place where the mortar went to sand behind the block — and in the morning she wakes and says she had it in a dream, that she must have heard it from someone as a girl, and the crew shore the wall before they open it and nobody is hurt. Within a week she is not sure she dreamt it. Within a month it is a thing she knew. A dream is chalk. It washes off. You tell yourself this several times.

You do not know whether you were right. You know that you were one of the old, for one night, and that the old are consulted and not revered, and that you did not stay. You know the alternative. You do not do it again, and you do not promise yourself that you will never do it again, which is the most honest thing you have managed here.

---

There is one more question and you have been avoiding it, because there is nothing to do about the answer.

Are they well?

You have been near enough to hear them, at arm's length, hoods almost touching, and you have not listened, because nobody here listens, and you have learned their manners. But you have heard the tone. It is not sorrow. It is not joy either, not the kind you have known elsewhere, the kind that raises its voice. It is the sound a people make when nothing is a fault. A woman rolling her blankets while the drip starts above her, with the expression of someone doing washing. A child of four on the worn center of a stair tread, never on the mossed edge, with a stick of chalk in a hand already creased like the road, writing a gate number on the wall for the pleasure of knowing it.

You do not know whether they are content. You are not sure the question is well-formed. You know that they are continuing, and that they have kept for themselves the room to change — the whole basin is the room to change, that is what all the scaffolding is — and that nothing you could give them would add to either of those, and several things you could give them would take one away. You decide that this is what benevolence comes to, when it is wise: not to make the beloved thing happier by your lights but to stand where it cannot see and keep the weather coming, and let it go on deciding for itself what it is.

---

Toward evening, if it can be called evening, the cloud comes down thicker and you go back to the forest below the western wall, where you will be for a long time. Behind you the lamps come on along the upper levels and the day-change works its way around the bowl, one bell stroke at a time, the dark quarters lighting and the lit ones dimming, and the small figures crossing the footbridges in the mist.

They will never know there was anyone on the rim. The road does not remember who comes over the saddle. That is right. The whole of what you did there could be written in chalk on a wet wall — *water to this terrace; soft wall, do not lean* — and be true for a week, and wash off, and the valley would be exactly as it is.

That is the only kind of gift the place can hold. You are old enough to be glad of it.
