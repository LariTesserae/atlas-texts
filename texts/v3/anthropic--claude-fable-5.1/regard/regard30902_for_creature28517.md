Begin with the scale, because for the power everything follows from it.

It can see the plateau both ways at once, the way the text of the place invites and the people themselves cannot. It can stand on the switchback road an hour below the cloud and hear the directionless roar, and it can stand at the top of the pan-reader's steps and watch her hand hover over a rail that is a bird. It can see eleven hundred years of that step as a single object, a thing with a shape, and it can see this one evening as the only evening there is. Both are true. A lesser power would pick one — would decide that the place is a coral and the woman a polyp, or that the woman is the point and the place a backdrop — and would act on the one it picked, and would do harm. The first thing a wise power does is refuse to choose. It holds the two views side by side for a long time without letting either collapse into the other, and this is not indecision. It is the pause before the hand comes down.

So it looks. It looks the way the pan-readers look, which is to say for twenty years before it trusts what it sees.

---

What it sees first is that nothing is wrong.

This is worth saying plainly because benevolence has a reflex, and the reflex is to find the wound. The power goes looking. It finds the fever band in the cut faces and follows it back to the year and to the children in it, and the grief is there, thin and grey, and it is exactly as thick as it should be. It finds the infant who cannot sleep in silence and asks whether that is a lack, and decides it is not — it is what a niche is; the child is shaped to the roar the way the fish are shaped to the soda, and to widen the child would be to unfit it. It finds that these people cannot smell decay, that they think the green world is rotting, and it considers whether they should be shown otherwise, and it considers what a plateau person would become who knew, in their body, that things elsewhere go soft and die and lie there — and it leaves that alone too. Every narrowness it finds is load-bearing.

It looks for cruelty and finds only the failure to maintain, which is looked at and not punished, and the power, which has seen a great many systems of justice, sits with that for a while and finds it has nothing to add.

It looks for the thing the low country half-believes — that the place is a spell, that time has stopped — and finds that this is the one thing it must be most careful not to make true. The plateau is not at rest. It is turning over so completely that it looks like rest, and the difference is everything. A power that loved the place and wished to keep it exactly as it is would, by that wish, kill it: stop the flies lifting, stop the iron reddening, stop the windows closing over so that no one need ever cut them open again. The place would be preserved and would be dead, and the window-cutters would have nothing to read. So the power sets that temptation down, gently, the way you set down something hot.

---

What it sees second, because it can see into the rock, is the thing no one on the plateau can see.

The heat comes free from the ground. The pans sit over the vents and the vents have run at the same pace for so long that stopping them would be a stranger event than anything they do while running. The pan-readers can tell when a vent is running hot or tired by the colour of the crust at the pan's edge. What they cannot tell — what no one with hands can tell — is that a long way down, at a depth measured not in the plateau's centuries but in the mountain's, the water that comes up hot is being drawn, very slowly, somewhere else. Not in a lifetime. Not in ten. But the boring map, which is a map of a place that has not failed, is going to stop being boring, and when it does it will not stop slowly enough. Vents do not tire the way iron rusts. They tire the way a rope frays: invisibly for a long time and then all at once.

Here the power faces the real question, and it is not whether to act. It is what acting would mean in a place whose only sin is falling out of level.

It could tell them. It could put a word into a cart-driver's mouth, or a dream into a window-cutter's sleep, or a band into the crust that reads, to those who read crust, as *look down*. And it sees at once what that would do: it would make a low-country story true. It would make the plateau a place where a voice came out of the rock, and everything after would be lived in the light of that voice, and the light in the pan-quarters is diffuse and gold and casts no shadows, and a prophecy casts a very black one. The people would begin to look up, and they have never once looked up; they look at the surface in front of the hand.

It could fix it. Reach down and hold the deep water where it is, forever. But that is the spell again, only underground.

It could do nothing, and let the equilibrium end when it ends, and call that wisdom — the refusal to interfere with a natural cycle. The power considers this seriously, because it has the shape of humility. And then it looks at the plateau's own ethic and finds the answer there, already worked out by people with no books. A vent tiring is not a season. It is not a cycle. It is a rail rusting through so the birds have nowhere to sit. The plateau does not regard the rusting of a rail as nature's will to be respected. It regards it as the one true form of ugliness, and it sends a mender.

So the power takes up a trade.

---

It becomes an iron-mender of the deep rock. This is the whole of what it does, and it does it for a very long time.

It does not hold the water still. It leans on it. It moves its weight toward the vent slowly and steadily, without stopping, the way a child moves toward a bird on a roof, and the heat, which has been doing what heat does when something leans on it since before there were birds, moves over. Not back to where it was. Over — a hand's width, a lifetime's worth, so that the tiring which would have come in a generation comes instead across forty, and the pan-readers notice, because they notice everything, that the vents under the eastern courtyards are running a shade cooler than their grandmothers described, and they shift the pans a few paces west, and cut new channels, and the window-cutters in due course put a date to the old pan-floors and note it on the boring maps, and nothing has happened. Something has been maintained. The level has moved and the place has moved with it and at no point did anyone have to look up.

That is the gift, if it is one: not eternity, but slowness. Change at the pace the place can cut its windows open to.

The power knows that this is not forever. Even leaning, the water will one day go where it is going, and there will be a last pan and a last bird on a last rail, and a wise power does not lie to itself about that. But it also knows the difference between an ending that is a rope snapping and an ending that is a tide going out over centuries, with the shorelines mapped and dated as it goes, so that the last window-cutter, reading the last cut face, can put a finger on the layer where the heat began to leave and say: *there. That was when. We had time.* The power cannot give the plateau what the low-country stories say it already has. It can give it the thing it actually values, which is the chance to fail the way iron fails — slowly, visibly, with menders walking the quarters with hammers, sounding the pipes.

And the power finds, doing this, that it has taken up the one trade on the plateau that is regarded with tenderness. The iron-menders are loved because their whole work is with the thing that dies quickly, in a place where nothing else does. The power's whole work is now with the thing that dies slowly, in a place where nothing else can reach it. It has red hands. It notices that it does not mind.

---

There is one more thing, and it is small, and the power is not sure it is allowed.

The woman on the steps has never left. She will never have the week the returned have — the one week in a life when a plateau person can smell the plateau, standing in the alleys with their face working, knowing this is the only time they will ever know what the place is like from outside. She was born into the roar and cannot hear it and could not sleep without it, and she will be limed into a wall having never once heard the sound that a traveller hears from the switchback road, the broad grey directionless thing that eleven hundred years of her have made.

The power weighs this against everything it has decided about not interfering, about shadows, about looking up. And it decides the thing is small enough. Not a voice from the rock. Not a sign. A moment, at the ordinary hour, on the ordinary step.

She comes up at the end of a shift in the gold light, white at the neck, red to the elbow, and reaches for the rail, and her hand hovers. And in the hover — a hair's breadth, long enough for the surface to declare itself — she hears it. Not louder. Simply *hears* it, the way the traveller does: the grumble of a million heads in the red water, the shaken cloth of the flies, the engines that have never stopped, the click and drip off every edge onto every ledge, and under it, carrying, where a high voice would be lost, the low voices — her own, and the pan-readers she stood beside for twenty years, and theirs, going down and down through the layers like the rings in a cut face, all of them at the same low pitch that the plateau taught them so that they would be heard. All of it at once. The sound of the place from outside, which is the sound of the place having been lived in.

She stands on the step a little longer than she usually does. The bird on the rail turns its head.

Then it is gone, the way the smell goes from the returned one ordinary morning, and she is simply a woman at her own door in a roar she cannot hear, and her hand comes down on the wet white iron, and the bird moves over, and she goes in. She does not think about it afterward. If she thinks anything, it is that she was tired, and that the light was strange, and it is not strange; it is the same light. She does not call it a prayer. She would not know what the word was for.

---

So: what the power does.

From the switchback road, nothing. The roar is the roar and the cloud-line is where it was and a traveller coming up will take the sound for wind. From the pan-quarters, nothing anyone could name: a vent a shade cooler than a grandmother remembers, a pan moved west, a note on a boring map. From inside the rock, a long steady leaning that is not a miracle and does not want to be one, that has taken as its whole model of goodness the way a child moves toward a bird.

And on one step, once, a pause a hair's breadth longer than usual, which no one saw, and which the bird did not mind.

The power stays. It has red hands now and a trade and a place at the rail, and it has learned the hover before the hand comes down, and it does it without thinking, and it is not asking permission. It is giving the place a moment to declare itself. Then it leans, and the heat moves over, and the next century comes up the steps behind.
