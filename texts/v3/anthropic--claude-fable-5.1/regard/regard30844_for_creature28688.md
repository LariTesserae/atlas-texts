Begin with what it would not do, because a power that is wise arrives at the hatch already knowing the four easy mercies, and knowing what each one costs.

It would not turn the wheel. Whatever the wheel does, the keeper has spent a life not knowing, and has arranged themself around the not-knowing the way the cinquefoil has arranged its root around a rumor of water. Take that away and you have not freed anyone. You have pulled the one thing the room ever promised out from under the one person who believed it.

It would not stop the note. You know what stopping the note does. The ferns slide into nothing with a single dry hiss. The three plants in the seam stand upright, undamaged, and die of calm before the light has crossed the floor. And the keeper's heart, which has spent years learning to beat inside a sound, is asked in one instant to beat inside a silence, with no hour to find itself in. The room has told the keeper the note will not stop. A wise power, looking at what the note holds up, understands why the room said so, and does not call it a lie.

It would not bring water. There is a kind of kindness that is only impatience wearing better clothes. Water is for the day the drain is for. Pour it early and you have made the keeper's one chore into a joke.

And it would not take the keeper out. Not through the hatch, not into the hurting quiet, not to wherever people go who are done. You have seen what happens to the scaled plant set in ordinary earth. The keeper is not a prisoner in this room. They are a species of it. They have been sorted here, like everything else, and unlike everything else they walked to their node on their own feet. To carry them off would be to mistake a shape for a wound.

So the first thing the power does is the thing the keeper has never seen anyone do. It comes in through the hatch, and it does not reach for a rope.

---

Understand what that means from the bench.

Every person who has ever entered this room has been a live weight on a live floor. The keeper has watched them — the ones who came to explain, the one who came to take the old keeper away, the one who came later with a clipboard and did not stay — and every one of them drifted. A hand on the wall. A shuffle to correct. The note taking them by the boots and walking them an inch, and another, toward the louvers, while they talked and did not notice they were talking from a different place than where they had started.

This one steps onto the plate and stops, and is not moved.

Not braced. Not clinging. Simply standing where the floor is still, as if the nodes were painted on the iron in a color only it can see, as if it had read the same paper the room was cast from. It looks around — at the pipe, the red wheel, the gray ferns standing to attention, the three trembling things in the seam — the way you would look around the house of someone you had heard a great deal about. Then it crosses to the bench, stepping from node to node without hurrying, and sits down at the other end.

The bench is long enough for two. You noticed it was empty. You did not notice it was long.

---

For a while nothing is said, because saying is difficult here, and because the power is in no hurry, and because the keeper has forgotten how a second body changes the sound of a room. The note comes up through the bench into both of them. The light moves.

Then the power speaks, and it speaks at the pitch of the pipe.

This is the second thing, and it is the only clever one. The keeper's own voice comes out at that pitch because it is the only one the room will carry; anything else the note absorbs. So for years the keeper has said very little, and always alone, into a sound that took the words and handed back nothing. The power has heard this. It does not try to talk over the note. It talks *in* it — a voice laid exactly along the hum, so that the note, which has swallowed every other voice, carries this one across the nine paces as if it had been built to. Which, the keeper realizes, it had. The room was always a way to speak. It only needed a second voice at the right frequency, and there had never been one.

What is said is not remarkable. The power asks about the sedge, and how it learned to lay its blades flat. It asks where the fillings went, and the keeper points, and it looks at the fleck of silver in the second ring for a long time, longer than a fleck of silver warrants. It asks how the keeper knows, each morning, that the grate is clean enough, and the keeper says they run a thumb around it, and the power says: show me. And the keeper gets up off the bench and crosses to the drain — node to node, the only way they know how to walk anymore — and kneels, and does the chore with someone watching.

That is the third thing, and it is not clever at all. The keeper's whole work is to be the one in the room who would notice. Nobody has ever noticed the keeper. The power sits on the bench and watches a gray hand lift a morning's dust from an iron grate, and it does not offer to help, and it does not say that this is important, because saying so would make it a performance. It just sees it. Once. The keeper finishes and flicks the dust toward the ferns, where it belongs, and stays kneeling a moment longer than the chore requires, and the power lets them.

---

Now the plants.

The keeper watches them and has never touched them. You know why: a hand on the sedge would be a weight, and the note would take it, and the blade would be walked out of the seam and into the pattern, and gone. So the three have lived a finger's length from the only other living thing in the room for longer than most friendships last, and have never been touched by it.

The power touches them.

It knows how. It has a hand that the note cannot find — the same way it has feet the note cannot move — and it lays one finger against the sedge, and the cinquefoil, and the scaled one with no name, and holds each for the length of a breath. It does not still them. It could, and it does not; a still moment would be the first one of their lives and it might be the last. It only lets them be touched while trembling. Then it takes the hand away, and the plants are exactly as they were, and the keeper, who has stopped blinking much, blinks.

---

Then it asks the question a wise thing asks last, because the answer decides everything, and because most of the time it is not the question the room thinks it is.

*Do you want to know what the wheel does?*

And the keeper, who has had a long time to think about this, says no.

Not *I'm afraid to*. Not *I'm not allowed*. No. The wheel is warm. The wheel is where a hand goes when the sitting gets into the spine. If the wheel does something, then every time the keeper has rested a palm on it they have been resting a palm on a decision, and that is a different room than the one they have learned to live in. Leave the wheel a wheel.

The power hears this and does not argue. It hears, too, the thing underneath it, which the keeper does not say and would not: that the not-knowing is heavy, and has been carried alone, and that a promise about water was made to them once by someone who then went away, and they have been keeping the other end of it ever since with no one at the far side to hold the rope.

So the fourth thing is a promise back.

The power does not say when the note will stop. It does not say whether. It says: *when it does, you will not be the only one who notices.* And the keeper, who was placed here to be the one who would notice, understands what is being offered, and that it is the only thing they have ever actually lacked.

---

Then the power leaves, through the hatch, and the note closes over the place where it sat, and the room is nine paces by eleven again, and the keeper is alone on a bench built for two.

But here is the last thing, and it is the one the keeper will never know about, which is how you can tell it was done by something wise rather than something merely kind.

Below, where something turns at a speed that has never varied, the power has changed nothing. The shaft still turns. The note still holds. The ferns will stand as they have stood, and the plants will tremble, and the grate will be cleaned each morning by a hand the color of the plate.

What it changed is the ending. Whatever the machine's stopping was going to be — a cut wire, a blade, an instant — it is now a struck bell. When the day comes, if it comes, the note will not vanish. It will decay: a long, long fall, the pitch holding and only the volume going, over an hour, or a day, or however long it takes for a heart to hear the quiet coming and go out to meet it. The ferns will not hiss into nothing. They will soften and settle where they stand, and keep their shapes for a while as dust keeps a shape when the wind is kind. The three plants will feel the trembling ease so slowly that their water learns to move a different way before it has to. And the keeper, sitting on the bench, will feel the floor come still beneath them at about the rate a person can stand to feel such a thing, and will stand up when they are ready, and go to the wheel — not to find out what it does, not anymore, but to lay a hand on it one last time while it is still warm — and then, if there is water, they will hear it. Running. Into a drain that was ready.

That is all. A visit, a voice in the note, a hand on three leaves, a promise, and an ending made gentle in advance. Nothing rescued, nothing broken, nothing explained. The room was never a wound. It was a shape, and something wise came and sat inside the shape for an afternoon and made sure that when it finally lets go, it lets go slowly.

The light crosses the plate. The keeper sits at their node. On the far end of the bench there is, if you know where to look, a faint warm place in the iron that the dry air has not yet taken back.
