It arrives the way the herders do, because there is no other way in that does not break something. It lies down on the warm white rock at the lip, in the afternoon, with the plain grass stopping a body-length behind it and the cold breathing up past its face, and it looks into the milk, and it waits.

It is patient. That is most of what wisdom is. It waits for the fog to thin from the top down, and it sees the wall of eyes and the flight of stairs and the grey backs in the sedge, and then it does what the herders cannot: it stays. Through the afternoon rain and the refilling of the fog and the soundless dusk traffic and the cold pouring in. Through the night, when the owl comes out of the arch at the height of the city and drifts the length of the trench and takes one thing that made a noise. Through the white morning and the birds spiralling up out of it into the blue. It stays a year, because a year is the shortest unit in which the place can be seen at all, and because nothing that had looked at it for less than a year had ever told it right.

---

It has come for the being who lives here, and at first it cannot find it.

There are the birds, who are the most numerous and the most visible and who sleep here and live somewhere else. There are the builders, who are never seen. There are the snails, who are stepped on. There is the white thing with gills, which has never seen the sky and knows the collapse only as a change in the taste of the water. There is the owl in the arch, which has been hunting this river since before it had a sky over it. The power goes through them one by one, the way you go through a house looking for the person you have come to see, and in each room there is somebody, and it is not them.

It takes most of the year to understand that the being is the cave.

Not the arch, which is the last two hundred metres of roof — that is only the part of the cave that still looks like a cave. The cave is the whole of it. The cave lost its ceiling and did not die. It went into the moss, which is a ceiling divided a million times and laid over everything. It went into the dams, which are quiet because a cave is quiet. It went into the birds' cells, each one a small dark bottle; into the lodges rebuilt over their own floors; into the grazers' new habit of not looking up; into the fish being pushed one generation at a time back toward the arch; into the preference, shared by everything that has lived here more than a few generations, for something overhead. The cave was a shape that water had made in the dark, and the dark was taken from it, and it has spent a few lifetimes putting the dark back in smaller pieces, using whatever was to hand. It is doing it now, at the speed of moss. It is a hole in the ground still surprised to be open to the sky, and the surprise is what is keeping it busy.

That is the being. The power has found it. Now the question of what to do.

---

It considers warmth, first, because the cold is the first thing anyone feels here, and it sets warmth down again almost at once. The cold is the floor of the place. Warm it and the fog lifts and does not come back, and the moss dries at the edges and slows, and the seeps stop being a sponge and become a gutter, and the rock underneath — still sharp, still young — is exposed and cuts. The cold is not a hardship the place endures. It is the one condition the place asked for.

It considers the falls. A block working loose for years, then letting go through the galleries and the ferns. Every one of them a district. It could hold the walls. It knows how. It watches one fall — a night in the wet season, a sound like a door slammed under a blanket — and it watches the morning after, when the birds are already on the new faces with mud in their beaks and nobody is looking at where the old cells were, and it understands that the city has no other mechanism for renewal than this. Stop the falls and the galleries thicken until they slough off under their own weight, all at once, along the whole length. The falls are how the wall is kept the right depth. Nobody planned that. It is nonetheless the plan.

It considers the fish. It watches them being unmade — the pools clearing pool by pool, the heron standing exactly where they are visible and not yet afraid, the slow pressure upstream. Its heart goes out to them, because a benevolent thing cannot watch that and feel nothing. It could cloud the water. It could move the heron. And then it sees where the pressure is pushing them: toward the arch. Back into the dark under the plain, where the river still runs and the roof still stands and they have never once been seen by anything. They are not being driven out. They are being sent home by the only road there is, and the dams that are killing them are also the thing making the road, and if it clouds the water it strands them here, in the light, forever.

It considers the arch. Two hundred metres of roof, and everything under it — the coldest cold, the last true black, the owl. The arch will fall. Not soon, but it will, and the power could hold it up for as long as it liked. It stands under it for a long time thinking about this. What decides it is the white thing with gills, which it finds not in the arch but under the oldest dam, in a pocket of standing water beneath stone, a metre from where it was the night before. The cave's animal did not stay in the last of the cave. It moved into the moss and the dams like everything else, because the dark it needs is not the arch's dark; it is any dark, and there is more of that here now than there was when the roof stood. When the arch goes, the owl will lose its house and find another. There is a ceiling in this place over everything that matters. The arch is not the last of the cave. It is only the part that has not yet been divided.

It considers the owl itself, because the owl is the thing that kills here at night and takes the foal that bleats and the frog that moves too fast, and a benevolent power has a natural first movement toward taking the knife out of the room. It follows the owl down the trench a dozen nights. It watches what the owl has made: the click pitched below hearing, the dams tuned so the river does not cover an approach, the sedge torn softly, the whole place built around one animal's ear. Remove the owl and in three generations the birds call. In five the grazers' foals bleat in the open. The trench gets loud, and sound carries out of a hole in a plain a long way, and things on the plain that have looked over the rim and gone away come back. The owl is not a flaw the place suffers. It is the reason the place is a secret.

It considers the rim. The lizard that never goes over, the hawk that will not follow down, the wolves that look and leave, the herders who lie on their stomachs and see the wall of eyes and go away and tell it wrong. It could correct the telling. It is the only thing that has ever been here long enough to. It could see to it that the next herder who came went home and said exactly what was there, and was believed. And then someone would come who wanted to see for themselves, and would stay a night, and would put a foot where a foot cannot go without taking something's house, and there would be a fire on the rubble, and light in the fog, and the birds would leave the lower galleries, and the telling would be right and the thing told would be gone. The slightly-wrong telling is the place's last wall. It holds because nobody who has heard it quite believes it. The power leaves it wrong.

---

By the time the birds come back, it has set down everything it thought of doing.

What is left is what it has actually been doing all year, without deciding to. It has noticed. The birds carry a thread between the dark cold floor and the bright fast sky a thousand times a day and nobody on either end has ever noticed; it has noticed. Nobody has counted the snails; it has counted them. It has learned the click that is pitched below what the owl can hear, and learned each family's dam by the pitch of the water going over it, and found the calcite lace where the moss is turning to stone from the inside and still green at the tips, and stood under the arch and known what is in there. It is the first thing to have seen the whole of this and understood that it is one thing. It keeps that. It does not spend it. It does not carry it up onto the plain and tell it, right or wrong. The city has no makers' names and it does not give it one. To be seen entirely and accurately by one witness who then keeps silent is a kind of gift that only a wise thing would recognise as a gift, and the power gives it, and knows that nothing here will ever know it was given, and that this is what makes it clean.

And then one small thing, because a heart is a heart.

It goes down to the pocket of water under the oldest dam, where the white thing lies with its red gills moving, having never seen the sky and never going to, knowing the end of its world only as a change in the taste of the water. And the power lets itself be known there, in the only language the place has for anything that happens above the moss. For one night the water tastes very slightly different. Not of anything the white thing has a name for. Not of warning, or of harm. Only of attention — of having been, for once, in a very long dark, noticed by something that meant well and asked nothing.

By morning the taste is gone. The white thing moves a metre. The fog is level with the rim. The drip is the loudest sound. The birds go up.

The power lies on the warm white rock a while longer and then does the one thing it can do that the herders can do too, which is to go away — but it goes away the way cold leaves a hollow, which is that it does not. It comes back. It will come back every year for as long as the place is a secret, and lie down at the lip, and wait for the fog to thin, and see, and keep what it sees, and leave the telling wrong.
