It looks for a long time before it does anything. That is the first thing to say. A power that is wise does not arrive at a place like this with its hands already moving.

What it sees first is the thing the prose sees last: not the midges or the frogs or the fourteen, but the keeping. A made system that holds. Six wells, two turbines, one seam in the rim; steam up, rain down; flies hatched, flies eaten; a heron standing so still it is becoming the ground. It has seen a great many places where people have taken hold of the earth, and most of them are wounds. This one is not a wound. This one is a balance, struck by one man with a rod in his hand in the second year, and struck so well that it has not needed him to touch it since. There is a kind of admiration due to that, and the power pays it.

It sees that the men are not unhappy. It does not pretend otherwise. It knows the difference between a contentment that is real and one that is performed for the watcher, and this one is real: warm bodies, work that is never wrong, a wall to lean on, a chalk mark on a door that says a friend has gone to the pond. It sees that they came back — every one of them — and that they came back because the silence outside hurt, and it does not think that a small reason. Silence that hurts is a reason.

So it considers leaving. That is the second thing to say. It sits on the edge of the flat, where the crust is cool enough to sit on, with the rain beading on whatever it has for a back, and it considers that the wisest act available to a power is very often not to act, and that the flat is full — full to the ceiling — of lives that are exactly what they should be, and that it does not know more than the superintendent about what the ground can bear. He walked it. He tapped every yard. It has only looked.

---

Here is what it will not do.

It will not stop the wells. It thinks about this for the length of a shift, because stopping the wells is the obvious mercy — the roar gone, the lid lifted, the sun coming down onto the black glass for the first time since the lake — and then it counts. Eleven years of midges at a thousand generations; frogs in a density that makes a pool audible; hundreds of swifts under the eaves who have never seen a horizon and would have nowhere to take one; terns with eggs on the warm spoil. Lift the lid and the rain stops. Stop the rain and the film goes, and the mats dry, and the flies with them, and every eye that goes on as far as the lamp goes out in a week. That is not benevolence. That is a flood with the sign changed.

It will not bring night. It could — a hand over the vents for an hour, a thinning of the steam — and it would be beautiful, the fourteen coming out onto the trestles to look up, but it would be a thing done to them, a spectacle, and then the steam would close and the lamps would come back and they would have one memory to be unhappy against for the rest of their duration. It has seen what a single wonder does to people who cannot follow it anywhere. It is not kind.

It will not weather the crust. The sharp edges are the flat's memory of being made, and the trestles are the map of where it is safe, and if the glass began to round and soften the map would be wrong within a year and someone would go through.

And it will not take the superintendent's place. It thought of this too — of becoming the keeping so that he could leave — and saw that it would be theft. He set the governors. He does not need to be relieved of them. He needs, if he needs anything, something that is not this.

---

Here is what decides it.

The fig.

It is the one thing on the flat that did not come to it. The midges came for the water and the swifts for the flies and the men for the wage and stayed for the warmth. The fig was brought. A man who was watching a lake boil dry went to the coast for a half-barrel of soil and a rooted cutting and set it outside a door that did not yet have an office behind it, in year zero, in a place that could grow nothing. That is not a gauge. That is a question. It is the only question that has ever been asked on the flat, and the flat's answer, for eleven years, has been: held. Not yes. Not no. Leathery, dark, the rind climbing the barrel. Neither well nor sick.

The power looks at the fig and then at the logbook — the column, the same hand, the dates stopping in the sixth or seventh year not from forgetting but from judgement — and it understands what the flat has taken. Not happiness. The flat has been generous with a kind of happiness. What it has taken is the faculty that would let anything be noticed at all: the before and the after, the sense that a thing happened. The superintendent said the date was not information. He was right. It was not, because nothing on the flat could make one day differ from another. But a man who is right about that has been made unable to be wrong about anything, and there is no one on the flat, not one of the fourteen, who could tell you they chose this, because the choosing would have required the very thing that was going.

A benevolence that leaves that alone is not restraint. It is agreement. The power has been wrong before about the line between the two, and it is careful, and it decides, in the end, that the fig asked and the fig may be answered — and that the answer should be the smallest thing that is still an answer, and that it should look exactly like a fig doing what figs do.

---

It does not touch the wells. It does not touch the steam. It touches nothing that is of the flat.

It touches the soil in the half-barrel, which is coast soil, which was never of the flat, and it does one thing to it, which is to make it a little more what it was — and then it takes its hands away, and waits, and that is the whole of the intervention. Nobody sees. Nothing rings. The gauges read what they read.

A leaf falls.

That has never happened. The fig has never dropped a leaf. It comes down in the warm rain and lies on the wet glass outside the office door, flat and dark, and the mineral water beads on it, and the beads dry at the edges, and by the end of the shift it has begun to take the rind. A leaf-shaped whiteness on the black. The first thing on the flat that is a record of something that occurred.

The superintendent comes off the trestle and stops.

He stands looking at the ground. Nobody on the flat looks at the ground; the trestles are so that you do not have to. He stands there for a length of time he cannot measure — the ear reports the swifts, the knock, nothing between — and then he does a thing that costs him something, which is to bend, with the rind on his hands, and pick the leaf up. It has a white margin already. He turns it over. He knows every yard of this ground and he does not know what this is, and the not-knowing goes through him like the first cold he has felt in eleven years.

He looks up at the tree. The tree has a new leaf. A hand's width higher than the swifts have ever perched. Pale — the only pale green on the flat, since the mats at the rim are not this green — and soft, and not yet leathery, because nothing has rained on it for more than a few hours. He could put his thumb through it. He doesn't.

---

What follows follows from inside the flat. The power does not do any of it. It stays at the edge with the rain on its back and watches, because having asked and been answered, the question is now the fig's and the man's and not its own.

The tree grows. Slowly, at first, then at the rate a fig grows in warm wet weather, which is fast; the swifts move up with it, perch by perch, and there is a season — the word arrives in the superintendent's head and he does not know where from — in which the tree is taller than the office door. Leaves fall. Each one lies on the glass and takes the rind, and he does not sweep them, and outside his office there is soon a scatter of pale leaf-shapes, some older and whiter than others, and a man who knew nothing about the flat could stand at that door and read the order they fell in. There is a before and an after outside the superintendent's office. Then there are several.

The fig fruits.

Small, dark, a handful, hanging close to the trunk where the rain is least. He watches them for what the shift bell calls three days and then he takes one. It is warm — everything here is warm — and it is soft in a way nothing on the flat is soft, and he stands with it in his hand outside the door and understands that if he does not do something with it, it will do the one thing nothing on the flat does. It will rot. There is not a single thing in four hundred paces that rots. The frogs are taken whole. The rind takes what is left.

He eats it. It tastes of the coast. He had forgotten the coast had a taste.

Then he goes into the office and shuts the door, and the roar drops to the pressure it drops to, and he opens the logbook to the last line — the same figures in the same hand — and he writes the date.

He has to reckon it. That is the thing. He has to go back to the last date in the column and count forward in shifts, and the shifts are all the same and he has not been counting them, and he arrives at a day and a month and a year and knows, as he writes it, that it is probably wrong. Off by a week, by a month, by more. He writes it anyway. It is the first line in the book that is information — not because the figures beside it are different, they are not, but because someone did not know it and had to find it out, and got it perhaps wrong, and that is what a date is.

He sits with the pen. Then, below the date, in a hand that has not written a word in years, he writes: *Fig fruited.* And looks at it. And writes the second thing, which is smaller and which nobody will read: *Ate one.*

---

The power leaves the way it came, which is by not being noticed.

It has not changed the flat. The wells roar and the rain falls and does not ripple the water and the frogs are level from one end of the pool to the other. The fourteen are still fourteen and will still come back. The heron is still becoming the ground. It has given the place a clock, which is the only thing it lacked, and a clock does not make anyone leave. It only makes leaving and staying into things that can be told apart.

Whether the superintendent stays is his. He may. There is a great deal to be said for the flat and he has said most of it with his life. But now, when he sits at the office door facing out into the rain with the swifts going over, there are leaves on the ground at his feet in the order they fell, and the tree above him is taller than it was, and there will be a fruit or there won't, and he is a man to whom something is going to happen. The gauges say what they said. The ground rings or is silent. It is warm, and it is eleven years old and some months, and for the first time since it was made there is one being on it who is trying to work out how many.
