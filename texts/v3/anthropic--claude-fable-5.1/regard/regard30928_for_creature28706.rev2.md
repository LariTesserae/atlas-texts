What it would do first is what the barge-man does. It would look down the chimneys.

It comes from above, because everything here does — the herons, the otters, the rain, the eels somehow against the fall — and it comes down the dike face slowly, the way cold air comes down at dusk, and pools on the flat. It does not announce itself. Things that are wise and old have generally learned that arriving is the least important part of any visit, and that the temptation to be seen arriving is the first thing to set aside. So it settles onto the clay among the surveyors' stakes, and the hares in the meadowsweet sit up and regard it with mild interest, and it regards them back, and for a long time that is all.

Longer than you would think. A benevolent power that was not also wise would already be doing something by now, and the doing would already be a mistake. The wise part is the part that keeps looking past the point where it believes it understands. It watches the greaser's trolley slow at the otter place. It reads the tidemark on the brick. It puts what would be a hand, if it had one, on the wet underside of a rail at nine in the morning and feels the lake breathing out. It stands between Number Three and Number Four and lets the two roars beat against each other in what would be its teeth. It goes into the chapel and counts the three greys in the crack. It knows the lake for what it is inside the first hour, as any visitor half does, and then it stays several more days making sure of the thing it knew, because being right quickly is not the same as being right.

And then it considers the mercies, one by one, and sets them down.

It could stop the fall. Set the clay. Fix the brass pins where they are, so that the woman with the green ledger comes round next autumn and reads *nought, nought, nought* the length of every column, and reads it again the year after, and the year after that. This is the obvious kindness and it is the cruellest one on the table. It would turn the ledger into a book of nothing. It would make the great effort to hold something exactly in place into a fact instead of an effort, and the people here are not the people they are because of the fact. They are what they are because of the effort. And the lake — the lake would be held forever in a shape that is not its shape, leaning against a wall that no longer gave, with no finger-width a year to show that the leaning counted. That is not a peace. That is a jar with a lid on it.

It could let the lake come home now. Open the ground gently, let it up through the clay overnight, take the whole flat back to level with no one drowned — it has the means. But no one asked. Sixty people have lain down on the floor of that noise and slept without dreams and called it good, and it was good, and nothing wise takes away a good thing that was freely chosen in order to give back a good thing that was merely older. The lake has not asked either. The lake is not in a hurry. The lake is the least in a hurry of anything the power has met in a long while.

It could harden the people. Give them, in advance, the strength for the day the pumps stop — because that day is written in the ledger as surely as anything is written anywhere, and it does not need magic to see it, only a ruler laid along eleven columns of figures. Coal gets dear. Wars come. The bearing that ran nine years is on a shelf in a shed. Sooner or later a decision will be made in an office on the far side of the canal by someone who has never stood at the bottom of a thing, and the roar will stop, and sixty people will have to climb the dike for good into a world that is full of small separate noises each demanding to be heard. The power could make them ready. It could put something in them now that would take the edge off the losing. But it looks at the flat loud voice, and the hand on the shoulder that means *listen*, and the not finishing of sentences, and it understands that the softness is the same organ as the rest. You cannot take the ache out of the losing without taking the sleep out of the sleeping. It would be like fixing the chapel's lean by removing the chapel.

So. Three mercies down. What is left is small, and it is in three parts, and none of them shows.

The first is for the lake. The power goes up the dike at night and stands on the towpath where the ring canal slides past at the height of the roofs, black and slow, with the six white columns pouring into it out of the dark, and it puts itself into the water the way you would step into a room where an old person is sitting, and it says — in whatever water says things in — *I see you. I know what you are and where you are and what you want, and I know you are not angry, and I know the finger-width is yours.* Nothing has ever said this to the lake. Plenty of things have known it — the eels know it, the willows know it in their roots — but knowing it and saying it are different, and a thing as old as this has never once been addressed as itself by anything that could. That is the whole of the first gift. Being seen, on purpose, once. The lake receives it the way water receives anything, by closing over it and going on.

Then the power asks for something in return, because that is also what a visit is. It asks: *when you come home — and you will — come slowly. Come the way you already come, up through the clay in the night. Give them a night and a morning. Let the water be at the door before it is at the window. Let there be time to walk up the dike carrying a child and a ledger and a shelf of parts.*

And the lake says what a lake would say, which is that it was always going to. It has never wanted anyone drowned. It wants to be level. Level is not a thing you do to someone. The power knew the answer before it asked, but the asking was not for information. Some promises are only real once they have been made out loud to a witness, and now this one has.

The second is for the night man at Number Six.

The power does not give him a dream. It thinks about it for most of a shift. He has not been woken by one in eleven years and he boasts of it and then says it again more quietly, and the power hears the second saying clearly, and knows exactly what would be in the dream if it sent one, and knows he could not afford it. Instead it sits with him. From the changeover at two until the light comes grey over the dike top, it sits in the pump-house on the bench where the greaser leaves his rags, and does nothing, and is there. The roar goes on. The man does his rounds. At some point around four the voice of the big pump changes — not much, less than a bearing going, less than anything the greaser would trouble to note — and the man's head comes up and he stands quite still, and he knows there is someone in the house with him. Not danger. He has known danger's voice since before he had words for it, like the children, and this is not it. He stands with his hand on the warm iron and does not turn round, and after a while he goes back to work. He never speaks of it. But he stops saying the thing about dreams the second time. He says it once, as a boast, and leaves it there, and that is what the power meant to do.

The third is for the children, and it is the only thing it does that takes years.

There is a girl of seven who puts her fingers in her ears in the linen cupboard because it is the nearest she can get to the thing the teacher calls silence, and who has told the teacher, correctly, that it is not silence, it is *loud in a different place*. The power goes into the cupboard with her. There is room; it is not large in the way of taking up space. It sits in the dark that smells of soap and damp and it stays with her while she listens to her own blood in the place where the pumps used to be, and it stays the next time, and the time after, and it never once tells her anything. What it does is more like what the greaser does at the otter place. It slows down. It lets her find, by herself, over the course of a summer and an autumn, that the loud different place is *a place* — that it has a floor, and that the floor can be lain down on, and that she can go there on purpose now, cupboard or no cupboard, fingers or no fingers. That the bottom of the thing she was born at the bottom of is not the clay. It is in her spine, where she already keeps it. It is portable.

Then it does the same with the next child, and the next, one at a time, without any of them knowing, over the eight or nine years it takes the eldest to be grown. It does not tell them the pumps will stop. They know. They have known since the winter night three of them woke crying. It simply makes sure that when they climb the dike for good and step off into a world drawn wrong, with the water at the bottom of every page, each of them will carry a floor of noise inside that they can lie down on in any bed, in any town, in any silence, and sleep without dreams, and wake, and be a person who once lived inside a very old creature and was not its jailer.

That is all. It is very little. The engineers would not find it. The survey next autumn reads down five-eighths, down three-quarters and leaning west, exactly as the ruler said it would, and the woman with the green ledger writes the figures in her careful hand and closes the cloth cover and is, if anything, a fraction lighter than she was, without knowing why — as if somewhere someone else had also read the columns and found them correct and did not think the person keeping them was carrying a grief. Which she is not. She is carrying a fact about a beloved thing, which is a different weight, and she has always known the difference, and now something else knows it too.

The power leaves the way it came. It goes up the dike face in the blue shadow at dusk while the ring canal still shines above it like a hoop of pewter, and a barge slides along the hoop with its lamp lit, and the man at the tiller looks down into forty lit windows at people not speaking and thinks they have nothing to say to one another, and the power passes him close enough to touch and he feels only that the evening has turned a degree cooler, which it has. Under Number Four the bats are pouring out over the falling water in the shape of smoke. The frogs are starting. The eels are in every channel. The pumps are running at exactly the speed they have always run.

Nothing has been fixed, because nothing was broken. A very large patient thing is being held out of its own bed by a household of sixty who have made their peace with holding, and it leans, and they hold, and the leaning counts, and one day the holding will stop and the water will come up through the clay in the night the way it does every night, only more, and not stop at the wheel-ruts, and it will be at the door before it is at the window, and they will have a night and a morning, and they will walk up the dike carrying what they can, and lie down on the far side in the dreadful small separate noise of everywhere else, and sleep.

The hares watched it go. Then they went back to looking, with mild interest, at nothing in particular, and the whole floor of the place went on being unwrapped, and the ground was eleven years old, and then twelve.
