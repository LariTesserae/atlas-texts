The first thing the attention would do is nothing, for a long time, because the valley does not run at the speed of attention and to act before you have understood the tempo is to act on something other than what is there.

So it would come down through the leaning columns and the squeaking sand and take the margin, where the hiss is a murmur, and it would stay. Not a week. Not a season. Long enough for a collar to form. Long enough that the sound became a medium and then stopped being noticed and then, further along, began to be noticed again in a different way — as a texture with places in it, louder here, softer there, a column two hundred paces in that has begun to run slower. It would learn the notes. It would find that the person's hut is exactly where one would put a hut, and sit under the crossbeam, and see the bowl, and not touch it.

And then it would consider the things a power can do, one at a time, and turn each over, because benevolence that has not first imagined the harm it could do is only appetite.

It could stop the draining. Seal the joints, hold the water in the stone, keep the roar going forever. This is the first thing that occurs to anyone standing in the valley — *don't let it end* — and it is wrong the way most first things are wrong. The valley is not a thing that has a draining. It is a draining. It came up saturated and its whole existence is the letting go of what it carried; hold the water in and you have not saved it, you have replaced it with a monument to it, wet and loud and dead. The attention would set that aside.

It could lift the vapour. One clear night, stars over tens of thousands of pillars. But for whom? The columns have no eyes. The ribbon-things have no eyes. The one person who might have looked up is in the foam. The attention would recognise this impulse as its own desire to see the place whole and beautiful, which is a fine desire and not a gift to anyone but itself, and it would set that aside too, or keep it for later, for a night when it was honest about who it was for.

It could carry the ribbon-things. This is the one that would take longest to put down. Go to a pool that is going grey, lift out the six or eight that are lying stiffer each day, and set them in the foam at a young column's base, and give them another lifetime of water. The attention would go and stand at a dying pool and look for a long while at the ring of them — heads all toward the seam, tails barely moving, humming their off-note to a column that is running out of what they hum to — and it would think about what it knows. That they came up sealed in the bed. That they have never crossed gravel. That whatever they are is bound up with one seam and one note, and that to lift one out is to decide on its behalf that it would rather be a thing that survives than a thing that finishes where it began. It would think, also, about the person. Decades at the margin, deaf, with a palm on the stone feeling the pools hum through the rock, walking in and out of the centre every day. Of course they tried it. Anyone would. And there is no pool in the valley with the wrong number in it, no ring of fourteen, no colony that outlasted its column. Either it was tried and did not take, or the person understood something in the hum that said not to. The attention would decide that the person's judgment on this was better informed than its own, and it would leave the pool as it was.

What it would do instead is smaller, and would take the rest of the valley's life.

Somewhere in the second or third year it would go into the centre, into the part that hollows people, and stand with the water sheeting off around it and find the columns with the strokes. Eleven groups. It would put its hand flat on the wet stone beside the last group and wait, and in that waiting come to understand what the person had been doing and why nobody else could have done it: that the knocks are not heard in the centre, they are felt, one hard pulse through the soles that is unlike anything in the continuous shudder, and that the only witness the valley ever had to its own endings was someone who could not hear the valley at all.

And it would notice, with the hand on the stone, something the person had almost certainly known and had no way to say. The hum from the pools is not decoration. Where the ribbon-things feed, the film never hardens; the face of the column stays open; the water keeps coming. Where there is no ring, the collars build, and the pores seal one by one, and the column goes quiet sooner. The things that seem to eat the calendar are the things that keep it running. Each pool is a small mouth holding its own column open for as long as it can, and the hum is the sound of that holding, and when the hum stops the knock follows, not long after. They are not eating time. They are buying it. They are the only thing in the valley that is on the valley's side.

The attention would find that this is the knowledge the person died holding, and it would receive it, and it would be the only one who ever did. That would be one gift: to be told, at last, by someone who had no words and no listener, and to have understood.

Then it would take up the count.

Not with strokes; it would not need to score anything. It would simply go where the last group of marks left off and begin. Each time a pulse came through the soles it would turn toward it, and go, and find the column, and stand by the small quiet that had opened there and stay in it for the length of time such a thing deserves, which is longer than one thinks, and then go back to the margin and wait for the next. Twelve. Thirteen. It would not hurry the pools. It would not help them or hinder them. It would be the third kind of visitor, the kind that neither stacks a cairn and leaves nor comes down and vanishes: the one that comes down and remains itself and stays.

The crust would come in behind it a hand's width at a time, over the years, and when it reached the hut the attention would let it. When it reached the bowl the attention would let it. When it reached the eleven groups of strokes — this would be the hardest — the attention would stand there and let the black skin close over the only written record of the only person who ever kept the valley company, and it would let it because by then the count was being kept elsewhere, in itself, and a record that has been read does not need to survive its reading. That is what the strokes were for. They were never a message to the future. They were a way of not forgetting between one knock and the next. The attention would be that now.

And it would be there at the end, which is the other gift, the one the whole stay is for.

There will be a last column. Somewhere in what was once the loud centre, one pillar with one pool with one or two ribbon-things gone quiet in it, still running, thinner every year, the hiss of it a single voice in a valley gone otherwise grey and dry and black-skinned to the horizon. The attention would sit by it. It would put its hand on the stone and feel the hum go faint and then stop, and then feel the water slacken, and then feel the pulse — the last knock, stone coming to rest against stone — and then, for the first time since the ground rose out of the dark carrying its cargo of water and creatures, there would be no sound in the valley at all.

Nothing that ever lived here could have heard that. The columns had no ears, the ribbon-things had no ears, the person had none. The whole life of the place was a roar that it could not hear itself making, and the whole death of it will be a silence that nothing native can perceive. The attention would perceive it. It would sit in the quiet as long as the quiet deserved, which is longer than one thinks, and it would hold the count complete — every ending witnessed from the twelfth on, and the first eleven received from the one who felt them — and when it finally rose and walked out over the crust toward the rim, it would do the one thing it had set aside at the beginning and lift the vapour, once, so that the place that had been a roofed hall of noise for a hundred years could lie open under the sky for a night, seen whole, by the only one there to see it, in the only silence it ever had.

That is all. Not rescue. Company, at the right speed, to the end, and then a witness to the end. A wise power would know that some things do not need saving, and that what they need instead is much harder to give, because it cannot be done quickly and it does not feel like doing anything. It would know that the person who chipped a bowl from a column and went in and out of the roar for decades had already found the right answer, and had only lacked someone to hand it to. And it would understand that the valley, if it wanted anything, wanted what everything wants that is very slowly becoming quiet: not to be stopped, but not to be alone in it either.
