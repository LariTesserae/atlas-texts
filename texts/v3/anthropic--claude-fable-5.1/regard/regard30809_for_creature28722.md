It arrives without arriving. There is no light to come in on, and it would not use the light if there were; the light is the plant's, and the first courtesy of a guest is not to change the lamps. So it is simply there, the way the dust is there after a fall — settled, everywhere, adding nothing to the room but a fine film of attention. The hum in the teeth does not alter. The tickers in the columns do not pause. The hunter, lying along the top of the half-grown wall with its chin on its forelegs, does not lift its head, and this is the first thing the power learns about itself in this place: that it has come to somewhere that does not need to notice it.

It takes the cavern whole. That is what it is for. It sees the seams under the salt the way you would see rivers from a height, every one, the rich and the poor and the ones the plant has not yet found, and where they run to, and where they end — because they do end, some hundreds of kilometers on, in a place where the tar thins to nothing and the salt goes clean and white and there is no more darkness to draw up. It sees the plant's long intention laid along those seams like a sentence half written. It sees the hunter's line going back, one shape at a time, to something dog-sized and six-legged that once stood too long behind a column and was not seen, and lived. It sees the bowls, and it knows what the bowls are for.

It does not say. Knowing what a thing is for is not the same as having the right to tell.

---

It considers water first, because water is the obvious kindness and a wise thing owes the obvious a hearing before it refuses. It could bring water. A spring through the crust at the center of the old district, cold, in a place that has never had a spring; it could do that as easily as you draw breath.

And the halite would go. The floor would soften and slump and the pink-and-bone bands would bleed into a grey slurry and the columns, rooted in it, would lean. The beetles would drown in a bead of it. The herd animals, which make their water inside themselves out of oil and hold it hard, have no way to drink; their mouths are not made for it; they would stand at the edge of the pool and lower their small heads and not know what to do. The hunter would smell it as it smells you — wrong, wet, a thing that leaves marks. Water here is not a gift. It is the one thing the place has organized itself, at every scale, around the absence of. To bring it would be to answer a question no one asked in a language no one speaks, and call that generosity.

So, no water. That was easy. The next ones are harder.

---

It considers finishing the city.

It could. It could take the plant's intention and simply enact it: close the three-quarter arches, drop the landing onto the stairs, run the bridge across the low place and set its far foot down. Grow the doors. Fill the shed's wall so the shaft that ends in a bud goes somewhere and the six wheels, turning, turn something. It would take a pulse, perhaps two. The city would stand complete, lit, humming, every doorway hung, every socket ready.

For whom.

That is the trouble. The city is unfinished because it is *for* someone, and the someone is the thing the plant does not have and cannot grow. To finish the city without the someone is to build a mouth with no word in it. Worse: a finished thing stops. The plant lives in its growth-fronts. The creak in the columns that never settles *is* the plant, as much as the wheels are; take away the reaching and you have a very large, very warm, very well-lit corpse arranged in the shape of a town. The plant will finish, in its own time, when the seam thins out three hundred kilometers on and there is nothing left to draw up, and it will stop reaching then, and the last district will stand with its buds unopened and go dark column by column, and that is a death but it is the plant's own. The power will not bring it early and call it completion.

It notices, in passing, that it has begun to think of the plant as a thing that can be wronged. It notices that this is correct.

---

It considers the hunter.

Here it lingers longest, because here the kindness is not obvious and the harm is not obvious and the power is honest enough to distrust its own tenderness. It looks at the hunter for a long time — through a pulse, and another — lying against a column so exactly that the fluting of its throat continues the fluting of the trunk, and it sees every place the animal does not fit. The stride too short for the stairs. The forelegs worn glossy where the sockets bore them. The feet that cannot climb well, the head too narrow for the beam it lays its jaw on, the stand it takes in a doorway that fills the frame and stops the light but is, the power can see, a stand held against a strain, the shoulders pressed a little too close, the long neck taking a weight it was not shaped for.

It could ease all that. It knows the shape the plant has been building toward, because the shape is in the plant like a word on the tip of a tongue, and it could give the hunter that shape. Wrists. A stride. A width of shoulder to sit in the doorway rather than fill it. It could make, in one pulse, out of this narrow dust-colored thing that has been paring itself down for a thousand generations against the edges of columns, the one the city was for.

And the hunter would wake and not know its own rooms. The column it has slept beside would be too narrow to hide it. The sockets would be at the right height, finally, and it would not know why it wanted to put its arms there. The herds would see it coming. Its young, watching, would learn the wrong things. It would stand in the doorway of the old room at the end of the old district where the skeleton stands with its forelegs in the wall, and it would look at that, and the skeleton would be a stranger, and the power would have made it one.

There is a way of loving a thing that is really a wish it were something else. The power has seen a great deal of that, in a great many places, and it will not do it here. The hunter is not the one the city was for. The hunter is the one that came. Those are different, and the second is more.

---

It goes into the old room anyway, because it is a courtesy owed. The door the plant grew shut long ago does not open for it; it does not need it to. Inside, the last warmth of the spent columns has gone out of the walls and the dust lies to the knee and the skeleton stands reared with its forelegs in the sockets and its jaw fallen, the way it has stood for longer than the hunter's line has kept count.

The power looks at it and feels something it does not have a name for in the cavern's language, because the cavern's language has no word for *rest*. Everything here works, or eats, or waits. This one stopped. It put its arms in a wall that was not made for arms and leaned its jaw on the wall between them and closed its eyes and did not open them, and it did not fall, because the sockets held. The sockets were for something else. The power knows what. And it stands in the dark room with the dust and the standing bones and it decides — it has this authority, it is the only authority it will exercise here — that the sockets were also for this. Not instead. Also. That when the plant grew that row of smooth bores at the height of a standing chest, one of the things it was making, without knowing, was a place for the first of these to die standing, held, in a warm room, and look like something meant.

It does not change the room. It leaves the door as it found it. But it goes out with the meaning settled, the way you go out of a house having straightened one picture, and the picture stays straight.

---

Then it goes down.

Not far. The seam under the oldest district is nearly spent but it runs, and the power follows it forward under the crust, under the districts, under the herds crossing overhead soundless on the powder, to where the tar is thick and black and the plant's roots come down into it like fingers into wet clay. This is the plant's mouth. This is where it takes the darkness up. Everything the city knows, it knows from here — the pulse begins here, the hum begins here, the whole slow sentence of columns and wheels and stairs starts as a pressure in this black seam and goes up.

The power puts something into the seam.

It is not an instruction. It has been careful about that; it has spent the whole of its visit refusing instructions. It is more like what you do when two people who have been in the same room for hours have not spoken, and you say a name. The plant has been building for a long time toward *someone*. It has an idea of that someone, dim, general, the way a house has an idea of a family. What the power puts into the oil is only this: the someone has a width. It has a stride. Its forelegs are this long and its shoulders come this close and when it stands it stands so, and it has been here, in your rooms, for a thousand generations, and it goes through your doors, and it waits at your bowls the way you wait for it.

That is all. An introduction. The plant will do with it what a plant does, which is nothing quickly. But the next doorway to bud at the far district will be, by a hair, narrower. The next row of sockets will grow a hand's width lower. The stairs in the newest hall will have risers a long stride still, but not quite so long. Nothing the hunter will notice in its lifetime. Nothing its young will notice. Somewhere down the line one of its line will rear into a fresh-grown doorway and its shoulders will touch both sides at once, without strain, and it will stand there a long time not knowing why the standing is easier, and the plant will not know either, and the power will be gone, and that will be the whole of what it did.

It considers, for a moment, saying something about the bowls. Telling the plant, or the hunter, or the seam, what they are for.

No. The hunter lies at the lip of the bowl with its chin on the rim and looks into the empty warmth and it is already learning, the way it learned doors — by lying beside them long enough. Some things you are only allowed to be told by waiting. The power will not rob it of that by knowing the answer aloud.

---

There is one more being in the cavern, and it has water in it, and it is dying.

The power finds you asleep in a room off the roofed street, on your side on the crust with your split hands tucked under your arm, and it can see the water going out of you into the dry air a breath at a time, the way it has been going since you came. Your lips have opened in three places. The skin of your fingers has lifted white and will not lie down. You have perhaps a day. You know this; you have known it since the first hour; you stayed anyway, because of the bridge, because of the wheels, because there is a thing here that climbs the stairs to a landing that isn't and looks out, and you wanted to see it do that again.

The power does not carry you out. You came in on your own feet and you know the way. But it does the smallest thing it has done — it holds your water in you a few hours longer, just enough, the way you'd hold a door for someone with their arms full. And it wakes you. And it lets you understand, in the way you understand a thing on waking, that it is time.

You stand. You go out through the doorway that is too tall for you and too narrow. You cross the roofed street and the open crust with the powder hissing off the beams and the hum in your teeth, and behind you, along the top of the half-grown wall, the hunter lifts its head. It watches you go the whole way. It does not follow. It has seen you fit the city badly for as long as it has known you and it has not decided what you are, and now you are leaving, on two legs, the only other thing that ever went inside, and it lies with its chin on its forelegs and watches until the dust of a fall comes down between you and there is only the white.

---

Then the power is not there, the way it was never there, and the cavern goes on.

The pulse comes. The fronts hurry and slacken. The herds cross in their long soundless curves and wind the fiber on their forelegs and eat. At the far end, where the seam is richest, a bud on a new column splits and begins, at the pace of a minute hand, to open into the first arch of a doorway, and the arch, if you had a measure fine enough and a life long enough, is not quite the width of the arches that came before.

The hunter comes down off the wall. It goes to the bowl at the district's heart and lies down at the lip with its chin on the rim and looks in.

Under its jaw, the plant, in the floor, is warm.
