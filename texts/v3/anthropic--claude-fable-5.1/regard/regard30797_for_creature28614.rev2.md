The power arrives the way anything arrives here: through the hatch, into the one clear place, and stands in it.

It does not hurry. That would be the first mistake, and it does not make it. It lets its breath leave and sink and be taken. It lets its teeth find the frequency. It stands in the cold and the pressure long enough to be certain of what it is looking at, because a room like this is easy to misread, and it has been misread already — by the moths, who thought it was warmth, and by anyone who would call it a prison, and by anyone who would call it a shrine. It is neither. It is a pour that did not finish, and something that would not let it.

The power sees the resident. It is not hard to see once you know the shape: cold on the shoulders, wet to the waist, thin as frost and everywhere. It sees that the resident is not cruel. It sees that the resident is not kind. It sees that the resident is *faithful*, in the way a thing can be faithful without knowing what it is being faithful to — that it has held one instant against the whole chemistry of the room for as long as there has been a room, without a lapse and without a surge, and that the instant it is holding is the moment after a man walked out.

That is what it is keeping. Not the moths. Not the water. The moths and the water are just what the keeping looks like when it has to look like something. What it keeps is the *after*. The broom set down, parallel, meant to be picked up. Half a slab brushed. A boot lifting. A man on the platform, then not. And something in the wet cement — which was, after all, the last thing he touched, the thing he had been shaping with his hands and his tool all day — did not want the shaping to stop. So it didn't. It has been holding the finish open for him. It has been not-setting so that when he comes back the surface will still take the broom.

He is not coming back. The power knows this the way it knows the altitude. It does not know where he went after the platform and it does not need to. It has been a very long time. Whatever fuel he had, he has burned.

So the question the power asks is not *how do I stop this*. It is *what was left undone, and can it be done.*

---

There are things it considers and sets aside.

It could kill the generator. One hand on the block, one motion, and the note that has been the resident's silence would stop, and the compactor would stop with it, and the room would hear itself for the first time. But the block is the only warmth on the mountain, and the animals are pressed against it, and the power is not willing to trade the resident's stillness for a cold that finishes what the stillness suspended. That is not benevolence. That is impatience with a body count.

It could go back out through the hatch and down the mountain and close the ways in. Stuff the conduit sleeve. Wedge the gap in the shuttering. Turn the moths at the treeline. Stop the intake and leave the room to keep what it has, forever, at one-third of a tank. The power holds this one longer, because it is the answer that harms nothing that is already here. But it would leave the resident holding. It would leave the room *about* for as long as there is a room, a sentence never allowed its verb, and the power thinks that is not nothing. The resident has been holding a door open for someone who died. Letting it hold that door forever, unbothered, is not a kindness to it. It is just a way of not having to watch.

It could simply command the cement to set. It is, presumably, the kind of power that could. And the concrete would go hard in one breath and the frost would thicken and the water would stop and every moth on every bar would be cast in place, kept in the truest and worst sense, a room of grey fossils around a running engine. The resident would be gone, and what it had been holding open would be slammed. The power does not do this. It is not the verb the sentence was waiting for. The sentence was not *set*. The sentence was *finish*.

---

Here is what it does.

It bends, because the ceiling is close, and it steps off the clear patch onto the slab — carefully, at the edge of the pool, between two frogs, on the one strip of wet cement that has nothing on it — and it crosses to the compactor plate and finds the switch and turns it off.

The second note stops.

The plate settles onto its dead rubber mounts with a small sound like a dropped sack. The beetles in its tread do not move. The drops on the tie-rods stop dancing and hang. The generator keeps its one hard note, but underneath it now there is a space where the hammering was, and the room has never had that space before, and the resident hears it. The power feels it hearing it. Nothing changes in the frost line. The resident does not have moods. But there is an attention now, a pressure that has turned, and the power lets it turn, and lets it look, and does not hurry.

Then it picks up the broom.

It picks it up the way the man would have — by the handle, low, without straightening, because you do not straighten in this ceiling — and it looks at the last stroke, the clean line halfway across the slab, and it looks at the texture of the finished half, the depth of the bristle marks, the direction, the overlap. The man was good. The strokes are even and the overlap is a bristle's width and the direction is true toward the hatch. The power has time to learn this. It has all the time the room has.

And it begins where he stopped.

Long strokes, drawn toward the hatch. The bristles bite the wet surface and lift a slurry and lay it down as texture, the same texture, and the power keeps the overlap and keeps the direction and does not rush the return. The slab takes it. The slab has been waiting to take it. The frogs on the unfinished side sit where they sit and the power works the broom around them in the same way a finisher works around an embedded anchor, brushing to the edge of each one and lifting and setting down again on the far side, and does not move them, and does not step on them, and leaves in the finish the exact shape of where each one is. This is slow. It is the slowest thing that has happened here since the last time it happened.

The resident hears the bristles.

It heard this sound once before. It is the last thing it heard before the platform. It knows what the sound means: it means someone is finishing, and when someone finishes they leave. The power feels this land in the room like weight added to the ceiling, and does not stop brushing, and does not speak — the resident has no language, and the power is not going to comfort it with words it cannot take. It does the only thing that can be said here. It keeps working toward the hatch, and it does not leave. Stroke, lift, return. Stroke, lift, return. The sound of finishing, continuing. The person who makes the sound, still here. The resident has never heard those two things at once.

The bootprint is near the edge. The power reaches it with the finish four strokes from done. A real finisher brushes over his own prints as he backs off the pour; that is the whole point of backing off. The power looks at the grass, thumb-high, rooted in the mud, the one green thing that was ever permitted, and it does not brush it out. It brushes to the heel and lifts and comes down again at the toe and leaves the print standing in the finish like the frogs, like a thing that was there when the slab was done and so is part of the slab being done. The gap is not erased. It is set.

Three strokes. Two. The last stroke comes to the hatch edge and the power lifts the broom and the slab is finished, all of it, corner to corner, and it lays the broom down on the platform outside, parallel, the way a tool is laid when someone is done with it.

Then it turns back into the room and presses its thumb into the wall.

Not to test. On purpose. Deep. It leaves the print and takes the hand away grey, and it does not wipe the hand, and it stands in the clear place — which is still clear, which is still exactly the size of a person about to step — and waits with the resident for what the resident has never had. Not a lapse. A release.

---

It does not happen all at once. Cement does not.

The first sign is the pool. The water stops arriving. The walls have been sweating for as long as there have been walls and now the film on the shuttering thins and goes matte and the last drops on the tie-rods fall and are not replaced. The pool stands. Then, slowly, it begins to go down — not drunk this time; taken up by a slab that is finally allowed to want it. The frogs at the edge of the pool sit on cement that is becoming cement under them.

The frost line moves. Downward, an inch, then more, because there is no more warm wet giving up its heat to push against the sky. The power watches this and does not pretend it is anything but what it is. The ice comes down over the bats on the scaffold and the birds on the rail and the moths on the upper bars, and they were already in the frost, and now they are in the ice, and that is where they will stay. The power does not tell itself they would have lived. They climbed a mountain that kills and arrived at the last warm thing and were held at arriving, and the holding is over, and the mountain is still the mountain. This is the cost of the verb. The power pays it with its eyes open. It is not the kind of wisdom that looks away.

But below the line — below the chest, where the block has been keeping the wet warm — the cement is going from grey to a paler grey, and the surface is going from mirror to stone, and the frogs are on it, and one of them moves.

It is not much. A throat empties. A foreleg unsticks from a slab that is no longer sticky. The frog is exactly as wet as it was and now the slab is not, and there is an edge between them, and the frog is on the near side of the edge. Another. The mice in the sacks. The thing between the pallet and the block, fox-sized, that has had its back against the engine for an age — it lifts its head. The power does not know how many of them have anything left. It knows some do. It knows that for as long as the generator runs, and the gauge is a real gauge now, they have a warm room with a hard floor and a hatch, and that the hatch is the size of a person leaving, and that it is not going to be standing in it.

The resident, as the slab goes hard, does the only thing it has ever done and does it for the last time: it keeps. But it keeps the way concrete keeps. The thumbprint by the hatch, deep, on purpose, going hard around the exact shape of the pressure that made it. The frogs' outlines in the finish. The one boot, with grass in it. The half a slab that a man brushed and the half that someone else brushed to match him, so that if he came back he would not be able to tell where he stopped. The resident was made of *about* and *about* is over, and what is left is not nothing, and is not gone. It is the room, finished. It was always what the cement wanted to be. It just would not let itself, because someone left, and it thought if it stayed wet he could still come back and put his hands in it.

He can't. But the print is in it now. It will come away grey no more. It is grey, and it holds.

---

The generator runs at one speed until, somewhere under a third of a tank, it doesn't. When it stops, the silence is the loudest thing that has ever happened here.

By then the power is on the platform, looking at the white, the broom beside it. It is cold, and its hand is grey, and it leaves the hand grey. It has left a mark and it knows what kind.

It goes down the mountain the way the animals came up it, and every so often it meets one coming down.
