The attention arrives the way everything arrives here, from above, and it does what you did: it looks down.

It does not look at the birds first. It has time, and it has seen birds. It looks at the turning. It sees the grain going out of the plain in five thousand crops and the black cake going back in reed baskets, and the water sweating out of the wall and being drunk white and clearing overnight, and the kestrel keeping the flock dark, and the dung making beetles making flies making lizards making snakes making the plugged niches full of old skins. It sees that there is no seam in any of this. Whatever you pulled, something on the far side of the wheel would move. And it sees that the whole arrangement has, in the way of very old arrangements, become a kind of animal — slow, brick-skinned, with birds for blood and people for hands and a heartbeat that goes out at first light and comes back by evening. That is the being who lives here. Not any bird. The pit.

So it asks the question a benevolent power asks, which is not *what is wrong* but *what could I do that would not be a kind of violence*, and it goes through the answers one by one, the way you would count a wall.

The ticks. It could clean the brick in a night. Every joint, every spall, every grey lentil-shaped drinker gone, and the birds would sleep as they have not slept in five hundred years. And then the brick would be cleaner than the birds are, and the mites would take the niches the ticks had left, or the flock would climb in numbers past what the wall can hold, or the ticks would come back on the legs of the next stray racer and find no rivals. The ticks are the price of warm brick. The birds have paid it every night since the wall was wet and are still here. It leaves them.

The walkers. It could mend a wing. It could mend all of them, twenty birds in an hour, and watch them go up in the chimney at dawn for the first time. And the next fledging season the wall would throw one or two in the hundred who could not leave it, as it always has, and the floor would have its walkers again, and the fig would have its eaters, and nothing would have been changed except that twenty particular birds would have been given a thing the pit does not give. It considers whether that is enough. It considers the fat slow fearless birds around the trough in the hour before dawn, the only ones drinking when the water is clear, and it is not sure they are the unlucky ones. It leaves them.

The kestrel. It could lift her off the pale brick and set her down two days' flight away, and the male would follow, and the column would go up and come down unharried. And the white birds would live. Within ten years the flock would be paler and slower and the high niches would be held by whoever got there first instead of whoever could afford the risk, and the sick would sit in the wall next to the well, and one wet winter something would move through the honeycomb faster than any snake. She has been the flock's physician longer than there have been physicians. It leaves her the brick.

Feed them. Scatter barley on the floor, once, generously. And a third of the birds would not go out the next morning, and the plain would be a third less gleaned and the fields that are cut from the floor's cake a third less heavy, and the pit would have begun to be a place where birds are kept rather than a place where birds keep. It does not do this. It notices, with something like respect, that nobody ever has.

Each refusal teaches it the shape a little better, and by the end it knows the shape well enough to see the one place where the wall is thin.

It is not in the wall. It is up on the ramp, in a corner: an acacia scraper, a stack of baskets, a coil of rope. Everything else in the pit does what it does because it cannot do otherwise. The birds cannot choose to stop coming back. The fig cannot choose to root elsewhere. The water cannot choose not to condense. Only the hands can choose, and the hands live somewhere out on the plain where the attention cannot see from here, in villages that may or may not still be villages, in a year that may be the year the son says the pit is a long walk and the dung comes in sacks now from the town. The pit has no way to know this. It has never had to know it. It has been fed on the assumption of return for so long that the assumption has set like plaster.

And the attention can see, without any effort, what happens if the baskets stop. The floor climbs. In four years the trough is under it. In ten the fig has drowned at the root and stands dead out of the wall like a burnt arm. The lowest active courses go under, and the birds move up, and the wall gets fuller and louder and then, one season, quieter, because a pit that has lost its water and its cutting is a shallow dish of dung with a ring of birds around the rim, and then it is a hole. The wheel does not stop. It fills in. That is how a thing like this dies — not by anything breaking but by one part of it, the only part that could, forgetting.

So this is what it does, and it is small.

It goes up out of the pit and over the plain, following the birds' morning line, to wherever the baskets come from. It finds the hands. It cannot make them love the place — that is not the kind of thing that can be made — but it can do something less than that and more useful. It can put back, in the head of someone who is deciding, one thing they had let go of: the warmth of the ramp under a bare sole. The hum in the spine when you sit against the brick at the level of the fig. The way the pit is only silent once a day, and what that silence looks like from inside. It does not argue. It leaves a memory where a memory had thinned, and it goes back down, and it waits to see whether that was enough. Some years it will be. Some year it will not be, and it knows this, and it does not pretend otherwise. You cannot keep a mortal thing alive by benevolence. You can only make its death later than it would have been, and less alone.

And then, because it has time, it does the other thing — the one that changes nothing in the wheel and is therefore permitted.

It sits on the ramp at the level of the fig, with its back against the warm brick, and it stays.

It stays through the return, watching each bird find the one niche in ten thousand and fold. It stays through the dark, when the geckos come out and click, and the heavy snake moves a hand's breadth at a time along the middle courses, and the ticks come up out of the joints to drink. It stays for the hour before dawn when the walls sweat and the water clears and the walkers come to the trough. And at first light, when the shudder goes round the spiral and the column goes up, it watches the kestrel drop into it and come out with something.

The roar does not change. There are too many of them for one death to register.

It registers it.

That is the whole of what it adds. The pit remembers everything that dies in it, but only by getting thicker — the bones go into the cake and the cake goes into the fields and nothing, anywhere, has ever held the particular bird. The pit cannot count. It has no organ for it. So the attention counts. The one the kestrel took this morning, from the third course below the rim, the cock of a pair that had held the niche four years. The squab that fell at noon and that the walkers stood about. The white one — there is a white one this season, in the low courses, a month from the lip — and it will not save the white one, because it cannot without unmaking what saves the rest, but it will see it go, and know it was there.

It does this every day. It does this for as long as the pit is a pit and for the years after, if they come, when the floor is climbing and the fig is dead and the ring on the rim is thinning, and the last kestrel is standing on a brick that has no one left to keep dark. It will not look away from that either. Benevolence toward a finished thing is not repair. It is being the memory the thing does not have, and staying for the end, and letting the end be counted too.

The fig's leaves clap slowly in the updraft. The water drips. Then, from midmorning, the birds come back in ones and hundreds, and the sound rebuilds itself layer by layer, and by evening the pit is what it was, with one small difference that no bird and no snake and no basket-carrier will ever detect: it is, for the first time since the brick was wet, being watched by something that will not forget it.
