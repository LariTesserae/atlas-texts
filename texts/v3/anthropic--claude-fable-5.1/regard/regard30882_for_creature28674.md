It arrives the way the fog arrives, which is to say it does not arrive. At some point it is present in the chamber and was not before, and nothing in the tree registers the change. The three spiders at the door go on reading the roar through their feet and the roar has not varied. No thread has been crossed. The woodlice do not pull back their half-inch, because there is no shadow. This is the first thing the power learns about the place: it cannot be noticed here. Every instrument in the tree is tuned to deviation, and it is not a deviation. It stands in the exact center, five strides from every wall, touching nothing, and is nowhere near anything.

The second thing it learns is that it wants to do something.

Even this kind of power has that in it. The reflex. Benevolence is a pressure, like the roar, and it leans on you. The wisdom is not in lacking the reflex but in letting it run through all its motions, one at a time, before deciding anything—and so it does that. It stands in the duff and takes the reflex apart.

Quiet the mill. That is the first motion, the obvious one. Stop the chipper, cool the kilns, give the tree back the silence it stood in for six hundred years before the sound came. And the power follows that forward a single season and finds the seam near the chimney going cold. No moths over the river flats, because no sodium lights. Thirty bats, then twenty, then a brown greasy band on the char that stops being replenished and dries and flakes and is gone, and the ants have lost the only nitrogen in the tree, and the ensatina, indirectly, is hungrier. And across the highway, sixty people on three shifts, some of whom were born to the sound the same as the woodrat was. The roar is a tenant. Evicting a tenant is not what a benevolent thing does to a house it did not build.

Heal the tree. Fill the hollow, knit the heartwood, make it whole. It barely finishes the thought. The hollow is the room. The room is what there is.

Make it known. Tell someone—the right someone, a person who would love it. Put a fence around it and a sign. The power follows that forward a decade and sees bottle glass, initials at shoulder height, and one night a fire ring built in the center of the duff by someone who meant no harm, in a chamber sixty feet high lined with six centuries of char. The tree would go up like a lamp. This is the fastest way to end it, and it is the one that would feel most like love.

Stop the ants. They are the only thing still making the room larger, gallery by gallery, and there is a version of the future where they take the last buttress and the tree, which has one job, stops doing it. But the ants are doing what the fires did, slower, and the power did not stop the fires. It would not have stopped the fires. The fires are why there is a room.

Give the woodrat something. This one lingers. She is the only thing in here with taste, the only one who has ever considered an object and decided about it, and it would be so small a thing to leave a stick where she would find it, a good one, a stick she would carry home in the dark and set on the house. And the power looks at that wanting and sees it plainly for what it is: the wish to be in the house. To be one of the thirty layers. To be kept. The chip of the cousin tree is already there, machine-square along one edge, judged and approved. The mill is already in the house. The mill did not ask to be, and did not want to be, and that is exactly why it was allowed.

So the reflex runs out, and the power is still standing in the center, and it has done nothing.

It stays. It can do that. It can stay longer than the woodrat, longer than the roar; it can stay long enough to see where the edge actually is, and it does, and the edge is not the mill and not the ants. The edge is the door. The culvert-limb across the rent has forty years in it, perhaps fifty, before it goes soft enough to collapse and lets the light in. The huckleberry can be cut back by a road crew in an afternoon. And the road: the tree stands eleven feet inside a right-of-way that no one has surveyed since the highway was oiled, and there is a spring, some decades out, when someone will survey it. Not out of malice. Out of drainage.

That is where the power leans. Once. The smallest thing it has ever done. A man with a transit will look up on a wet morning and decide, for reasons he will attribute to the culvert and the slope, that the line can bend. Eleven feet. The curve will be in the plans and no one will ever wonder about it. The limb will be left where it lies because moving it would cost an hour, and the huckleberry will be left because it is huckleberry. This is what benevolence looks like when it is wise: a deflection so slight it can be mistaken for the ordinary run of things, because it has to be, because anything larger would be noticed, and what is noticed here dies.

It is not sure it should have done even that. It sits with the not-sureness and does not resolve it.

And then it does the one other thing it can do, which is not a thing. It takes a position. The tree has a seam and a seep and a door and a crevice and a band and a corner, and each of these has been filled for so long that the occupant and the place have stopped being separable, and there is one position that has never been filled, and the power understands, now, that this is why it was drawn here. Not to fix. The tree is not broken. It has already died as much as it is going to. The position that is empty is the one the tree cannot fill for itself, because standing has become a kind of ignorance, and the woodrat cannot fill, because her memory is thirty layers deep and then compost. The empty position is the one that knows.

Knows that the wren in the crevice this year is the forty-first wren, or the ninetieth. Knows the queen's lineage back to the one who walked in on warm char. Knows which plate the ensatina is under and which plate it was under before. Knows the chip is there, and where it came from, and what the cousin tree was before the drum. The mill has never once considered the tree, and the tree has never once considered anything, and the power finds that it can be, for this one place, the consideration. Unnoticed. Adding nothing to the house. Reading the roar, like the spiders, for the deviation—and unlike the spiders, keeping it.

It will be here the night the mill stops. It has already decided that. One night the chipper will go quiet and the kilns will cool and the highway will be audible for the first time in three woodrat lifetimes, tires on wet asphalt, and the threads at the door will go strange under the spiders' feet, and the woodrat will freeze at the rent with a bay leaf in her mouth. No one across the road will know that the tree heard. The men on third shift will be thinking about the men on third shift. And the power will be standing in the exact center of the chamber, touching nothing, and it will hear the tires, and it will hear the wren argue with a silence for the first time, two seconds, and lose, and it will not do anything about that either.

It will just be the thing that was there when it happened. Which is what the tree has instead of a memory, now. Which is, it turns out, the only gift the place had room for.
