Start with the noticing, because even a wise thing has to be looking somewhere before it can be looking here.

It does not notice the water. Water standing sixty meters high with no top is a marvel, and marvels are loud in the way that stone is loud — steadily, in every direction, saying *I am* and nothing after. A power that has been paying attention for a long time has learned to hear past that. States are quiet, once you know them. Ora Hend is almost entirely states: the four degrees, the meter and a half, the eleven rooms finished and stopped. From far enough away the whole cistern reads as a single held note.

What it notices is the thing that isn't a note. Somewhere on a mountain, in the dark, a wave is passing down a length of pale substance and not closing. It leans one way, then a way it has not leaned before. It is small — a forearm — and it is the only sentence on the plateau still being written, and to something that listens for that kind of thing, it is unmistakable. It is the sound of a place where one thing is still happening.

So it comes. It comes up through the dead wind the way anyone does, and it puts a hand — or the part of itself that does what a hand does — against the black face, and pushes, and walks in.

—

The first question is not *what should I do*. The first question is *is anything wrong*, and a wise thing asks it slowly, because the fastest answer is always the visitor's and the visitor has eyes.

The visitor's answer is: something is being kept from its life. It cannot eat. It cannot end. It was lifted out of a pool in a bucket and told to stand along with everything else, and it has been swimming ever since through streets it did not ask for, past rooms built for people who never came. That answer is ready-made and it is about the visitor, who would find this unbearable.

The power does what the visitor could not: it looks at what the ribbon was held *in*. Not just the swimming — the instant. The founders fixed the water at a moment, and whatever the ribbon was, at that moment, it still is. If it was frightened, it is still frightened. If it was starving, that has been kept as carefully as the grit.

It finds — and this takes a while, because the ribbon has no eyes to look through and no face to read, and the power has to learn it from the wave — that at the instant of the holding the ribbon was doing the one thing it had ever done. It was going along a wall. There was a warmth in the fold, some slight difference in the water where the rock was, and it was following the edge of that, and the wave was passing down it and not closing. That is what it was held in. Not hunger, which it had not yet begun to feel. Not fear, which it had never had reason to learn. The state of moving along the edge of something, with the whole of its attention. Whatever a ribbon has where feeling would be, that is what it has, and it has had it without interruption for as long as the streets have stood.

A wise thing does not call that a life, exactly. It also does not call it a wrong.

—

It considers, nonetheless, what could be done, because benevolence that will not even consider is only a kind of shyness.

It could release the water. Undo the instruction, let the four hundred meters fall, let the streets stand dripping in the returned wind and the ribbon settle back into whatever fold it came from, or into a puddle among the eleven buildings, and begin — for the first time since the founders — to go forward. To be hungry. To find, in a pool the size of a room, the film the mountain lets go of. To have a period at last, and to arrive at it. That would be giving it back its death, and the power turns the gift over for some time. It does not think death is nothing to give. It thinks the ribbon has never once, in the fold or in the cistern, wanted anything that the giving would answer, and that to end a thing's condition in order to restore its capacity to want is a decision the wanting should be there to make. It sets the gift down.

It could feed it. But there is nothing in the fold to feed, and the fold is not hungry.

It could give it a surface. It has no eyes.

It could give it company — if there is one, make it twelve; if there are twelve, arrange the streets so that they meet. But if there is one, then one is what came up out of the spring, and the power does not know that a ribbon which has been alone since before the buildings would be improved by another; it knows that it would be *changed*, and that the change would be for the watcher, who finds one sad. And if there are twelve, they have passed each other in the black for all this time without any sign of noticing, and that is either loneliness or the absence of any organ for it, and the power, looking closely at the wave, thinks it is the second.

It could stop the wave. It could finish the founders' work. It does not spend long on this. The founders did not forget. The wave cannot be fixed because it is not a state; it is the one thing in the cistern that is only ever *being done*, and an instruction that says *stay as you are* has nothing to take hold of. The founders held everything that could be held. The swimming was never available. That is not their oversight. That is the ribbon's.

—

What it does, in the end, is the thing the room was cut for.

It goes to the one building with the depression in the floor, and it stands in the hand-span dip inside the single line, and it holds still. The tone comes up through whatever it has for feet. The power can hear it properly — can hear what the visitor only half-hears — and it listens for a long time.

The tone is the holding, as the visitor guessed. It is also a term. The founders were good, but they were not infinite, and the instruction that keeps Ora Hend was written against the mountain, and the mountain is aging. Not soon. Not in ten thousand years. But the plateau is a thing that was thrust up and will be brought down, and one day the rock under the eleven buildings will begin to go, and the water was only ever told to stand on *this*. When this is gone the standing ends, and the cistern will not fall so much as lose its argument. The power hears all of that in the level tone. The founders may have heard it too and thought ten thousand years was enough.

Here is the single thing it does. It speaks, in the way that such a thing speaks, to the mountain. Not to the water — the water is well looked after. To the ground. It tells the plateau to keep. Not to stop aging; the mountain has its own life and the power has no more right to that than to the ribbon's. Only to keep *this* — the four hundred meters of dressed rock the eleven buildings stand on — for as long as there is something on it that is still doing anything. The instruction is small and precise and nobody will ever see the seam of it, which is how the founders would have wanted it, and it means that the water will have something to stand on for exactly as long as the wave keeps leaning in ways it has not leaned before. The ribbon has been given a floor, in the one sense it could receive one.

That is all. The power considers whether it is too little and decides it is the correct amount.

—

While it stands there a ribbon comes through the opening. It crosses the seam at the threshold and takes the light and passes within a hand of the power's face and goes up into the black. The power watches the wave and, being what it is, could count. Could follow the one shape through the fold and know, at the end of a loop, whether the thing that comes down at the far end of the street is this one. It has been standing long enough. It knows.

It does not say. Not because the answer is terrible either way — it isn't — but because the not-knowing belongs to the visitors and the knowing would be about the visitors, and the ribbon, which has no eyes and cannot be looked at and cannot be told apart, has in this one respect a privacy, and the power finds it does not want to be the thing that spent it. A creature that no one has ever been able to count is not lonelier for being uncounted. It may be the last uncounted thing on the mountain.

For the same reason it does not name it. The founders never did, and the visitor supposes that was indifference. Perhaps. But a name is a way of finding a thing again, and the ribbon is the one thing here that comes back on its own, and a name is also a way of holding, and the ribbon has been held enough.

—

It leaves the way it came. The grit hangs where the last visitor kicked it and the power does not sweep it, because the founders left it and the visitor moved it and both of those are true things about Ora Hend and the power has no wish to make the place tidier than its history. It steps out through the black face into the dead air and then into the wind, which resumes its habit a few hundred meters on, and the mountain around it goes on with its long slow business of coming down — everywhere but one place, now, and that place will not be visible from anywhere.

Behind it the eleven rooms are finished and the water stands and the light stops at its distance, and in a street or above a street or on the way from one to the other, a length of pale substance is doing something for the first time. The power did not save it. It did not need to. It made sure the mountain would be there while the ribbon went on not needing it, and then it went away, and — since it is wise, and knows what attention costs the thing attended to — it did not look back to check.
