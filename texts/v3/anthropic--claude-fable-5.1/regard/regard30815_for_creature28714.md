It comes down the stair. There is no other way in, and it has the wisdom not to want one. Powers that arrive from above tend to arrive as light, and the seam has told it already, on the eleventh landing, that light is not what it lacks. It has four or five grey squares of that and has made a sky of them.

So it takes the iron, then the brick, then the black glass-skinned stone, and by the twentieth landing it has stopped being a visitor to the heat and become a thing inside it, like everyone else.

---

The first thing it does is nothing, for a long time.

It has been asked about the being that lives here, and it spends the first several hundred sighs trying to decide who that is. It watches the scrapers walk their unfinishable line. It watches the pan-woman lift her rake a quarter-hour before the crust is ready and be right. It watches the keeper wake in his sleep to a sigh that came at ten minutes and lie back down when the next comes at eleven. It sits in the far vault with the seated dead and their thickening panes and does not touch them. It looks up, a long time, at the fig.

It notices what the girl noticed, and what the last paragraph of anyone's account of this place notices if they stand still long enough: that it cannot cleanly pull the people off the rock or the rock out from under the people. The salt grows on the eyelid and the pipe. The hitch in the breath is the air announcing itself through a throat. Ask who lives here and the honest answer is a single word — *this* — and the being it has come to attend is not the keeper or the dyke or the moss but the slow knot they have tied in each other.

That is a harder patient than any of them alone. It is glad of that. Wisdom does not enjoy easy patients; they are usually not what they seem.

---

Then the temptations come, in order of size, and it takes each one in its hands and turns it over and sets it down.

The first is the largest and the most obviously kind. Cool the rock. It could. A power that can find the bottom of this stair can find the bottom of the dyke and draw the heat out of it in a night, and in the morning the walls would be merely stone, and the fog would settle, and the drizzle would stop, and no one would ever again stand still with their paddle raised waiting for a crack to finish.

And the pans would be pans of cold brine. The kilns would be cellars. The boiler would go quiet on its eleventh minute and never sigh again, and the keeper would wake to the silence as he has woken to every change for thirty years and this time there would be nothing to correct. The ward would not be saved. It would be a wet hole in the ground with a town clinging to the walls, and the town would go up the stair one family at a time, and the moss would die in a fortnight, because the moss has no interest in being safe. It has an interest in being exactly where it is.

Besides — and this is the part that makes it set the thought down and not pick it up again — the rock is not suffering. The rock is doing the thing rock does after it has been molten, at the pace rock does it, and it has never once complained. The people are the ones who hear it crack and stop. The rock does not hear them at all. To cool it for their sake would be to treat the seam's slow cooling as a wound in the seam, when it is the seam. You do not heal a river of its flowing.

---

The second is smaller and comes wearing the face of rescue. Take the forty out. Not the day-shift, who go up and shiver and come back; the sleepers, the born-down, the ones who go up wrapped to the eyes and come back burned by something that is not the sun. Give them a place with a real sky and cool sheets and bread that is bread.

It looks at the girl with the heaviest fig-stone, who will not show it to anyone, and tries to picture her in a field.

She would live. She would grow tall and the pallor would fade and in ten years no one would hear the hitch in her breath and call her steamer. She would also spend those ten years listening for a sigh that never came, and touching walls that did not answer, and she would never again hear anything say that it lived, in the plain way she means it. The power has moved people before. It has learned that the thing you lift someone out of comes with them, folded, and unfolds in the new place and does not fit, and that the kindest thing you can do to a home is not mistake it for a cage because it happens to be dark.

The forty are not trapped. They are the ones who stayed. Something in the seam wanted them and something in them answered. That is not a condition. That is a marriage.

---

The third temptation it holds longest, because it is the one that is closest to a real kindness and therefore hardest to see the edges of.

The lungs.

Everyone in the ward has the catch at the top of the breath. Everyone has forgotten it. But the power has not been here long enough to forget, and it can hear, under the sigh and the drip and the double thud of the pumps, forty small wet hitches, and it knows what salt steam does to a lung over decades, and it knows how the keeper's eyes water and that he has stopped noticing, and it knows which of the scrapers will, in fifteen years, cough once and not stop.

Here is the distinction it has to make, and it makes it slowly. The pallor is a mark. The salt at the hairline is a mark. The frosted forearms, the wide pupils, the way they squint upward like moles — marks. A place has the right to mark what lives in it; the fig is marked by the ward as surely as the ward is marked by the fig, and no one would call that harm. The hitch is a mark too. It is the sound of the ward speaking through a person, and if the power took it away the city above would lose its slur and the ward's people would lose a thing they did not know they had, which is that anyone who hears them speak knows at once where they are from.

But the cough that does not stop is not a mark. It is the place forgetting where the mark ends and the person begins, which is the one mistake the seam and its people have otherwise never made. They step around the moss. They do not scrape the dead. They boiled a pot once and stood around it as if it were obscene. The ward knows how to stop. It is only in the lungs that it has not learned to.

So the power teaches it. Quietly, in the way it prefers. It does not heal anyone; there is no one, this morning, to heal. It goes to the place where the air meets the throat and it puts something there like the wax the builders drop on a string — a measure, a threshold — so that the hitch stays a hitch. The air can announce itself. It cannot move in. Fifteen years from now a scraper will cough once, near the western wall, and clear his throat, and go back to tapping, and never know he was meant to do otherwise.

Nobody will notice. That is how it can tell it has done it right. The interventions worth making in a place like this are the ones that look, afterward, like the place simply being itself a little more faithfully.

---

The fourth it barely entertains. The city above. The pay, the contempt, the joke of the name. It could go up the stair and change some minds and it would be a good thing to do and it is not what it came down for, and a power that lets itself be drawn up out of the seam to fix the seam's neighbours has misunderstood the depth it has been asked to attend. The ward has never once asked the city for anything but brine and bread and to be left alone. It has that. Let it have that. Some other patience, on some other stair, can see to the city.

The fifth it entertains for exactly one breath, because it is beautiful: let the fig ripen.

No. A ripe fig would fall forty feet and burst on the gantry, and it would be the first thing in the ward's memory to be soft, and it would be the first thing to rot, and the children would stand around it the way the ward stood around the boiled pot. They keep the stones because the stones fell out of the sky and did not break. The power knows better than to give people the thing they would have wanted if they had been other people.

---

So. Having set five kindnesses down, it does two things, and the second is smaller than the first.

The first is about the crack.

Every so often the rock speaks and everyone stops. They have stopped for it so often that they have forgotten it is a question. The rock is cooling, and cooling rock contracts, and contracting rock fails, and it fails a hair's breadth at a time until the day it fails a yard at a time, and on that day the ceiling of the lens — the underside of the old city, with its grates and its fig — comes down into the fog, and the ward is over in the time it takes the keeper to open his eyes.

That day is coming. The power can see it the way the pan-woman sees the crust. Not soon. Not in the girl's lifetime, probably. But the ward is being born out of the ground at the pace of a lifetime, and it should be given its full length, and at present it will not be.

The power does not stop the cooling. It does not want the rock to be anything but what it is. What it does is go down into the dyke, into the part that is still deciding, and even the deciding out. Where the heat would have left one place too long and let it go all at once, it leads a little of the heat away in advance. Where a plane would have held its stress for forty years and then let go in a night, it lets it go now, a fraction, and again next month, a fraction. The rock will crack more often. The cracks will be smaller. The builders will find more spall at the footings, none of it larger than a door, and reroute more pipe, and drop their wax on their strings and find it runs exactly as it always did. The scrapers will stop a little more often and for a little less time. The floors will still give way onto new black pockets, and the town will still follow the seam down, terrace by terrace, but the roof will hold above them for as long as there is anything down here worth holding it over.

It does not save the ward. It gives the ward its whole life. The two are different, and the ward's people, who have never in their history asked to be saved from anything, would understand the difference at once.

---

The second thing is this.

Before it goes it stands in the far vault, beyond the last lamp, where the glaze grows over the black facets like ice on a broken window and the dead sit against the wall going slowly into the pane. It stays a sigh or two, as they do. Then it puts its palm flat on the rock, once, and holds it there, and it says to the dyke — not in words, the way the kiln-tender does not use words, but so that the rock will have it — the thing the girl said.

*You live here.*

Not a command. A power that cools or lifts or heals is still, whatever it tells itself, giving orders. This is only information, passed from the one who noticed to the one who is noticed, so that the rock, in its long deciding between scab and wound, has one more thing to weigh: that something small and quick and salt-marked has been living on it long enough to say so, and meant it plainly.

Whether rock can hear such a thing, the power does not know. It has met older stone than this and never learned. But it has never yet met a place that did not go more gently toward its end for having been told, once, by something that stayed long enough to be sure, that it was alive.

---

Then it goes up.

Iron, then brick, then the black glass that has no right to be there. On the fifth landing the medium lets go of it and it begins to shiver — not from cold, from the absence — and it stands there a moment on the stair with its arms folded and its breath catching very slightly at the top, and it understands that it has been marked, the way everything is that comes down here on its own feet, and that this too is not a harm.

The hitch fades by the tenth landing. The salt at its hairline it rinses off that evening. But for a long time afterward, in places with real skies and cool sheets, it will find that when it passes a wall it puts its palm flat against it for a moment and moves on, and if anyone asks what it is feeling for it will not be able to say.

It is feeling for whether it has changed since yesterday. It usually has, a little.
