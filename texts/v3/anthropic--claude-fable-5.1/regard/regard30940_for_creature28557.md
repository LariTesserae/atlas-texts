**What it would do**

It would come by the rail spur, because that is the only way in that was ever meant to be used, and it would stop at the tally office, because that is where the line stops.

It would not go down at once. It would climb the dike and stand where you stood, and it would let the sound arrive, and it would do the first thing wisdom does in a place like this, which is nothing. For a long time. Longer than a week or a season. Long enough for the hawk on the finial to die and be eaten and the finial to stand empty and a hawk to come over the wall and take the finial again. Long enough to see that happen twice. It would count the gulls and be defeated and count them again and be defeated again and understand that the defeat is the information. It would learn the small quick language of the keepers' hands, all of it, in an afternoon, and confirm that it has very few words and that none of them is a question.

And it would ask the thing you have been asking without saying so. Not *what lives here.* The description answers that; it answers it too well. It would ask *who is being addressed.* Who, in this enclosure, is the one that a kindness could be done to.

---

Not the gulls. The gulls are a number. You cannot be kind to six thousand, only to a bird, and there is no bird here, only a position on a stone lip and whatever is currently standing in it. Not the eels, who want nothing and are getting it. Not the beetles, who are a process. Not the rats, whose lives are already better than most lives.

Not the hawk, though it would look at the hawk for a long while. One bird, alone, on the highest point, arriving from a country it cannot remember and living exactly as long as two fledglings a day will let it. The hawk is the loneliest thing here and does not know it. But the hawk is also the place reaching out over its own wall and fetching what it needs, and the hawk, if you asked it, is fed.

Not the pale things. It would grieve them, briefly and properly — a life measured in the ten paces between the shaft and the heron's bill, born by the ton in a dark that has never been broken in order to be delivered into light and die of it. But it would see that their wrong is not one the place is doing to them. The place did not make them. It only stands at the lip and eats what comes. To stop the pumps for their sake would be to drown the silt and the cottages and the six thousand and the eels a century thick, so that a paper fish could stay a paper fish in the dark for whatever it has instead of a life. It would set that down. Not everything that is sad is a wrong. Some of it is only the shape water takes going downhill.

The keepers, then.

Here it would stop longer. Because the keepers are the only ones inside the dike who could, in principle, be asked. And it would notice — as you noticed, though you were careful not to say it — that the sentences the description uses for the keepers are the same sentences it uses for the rats and the birds. *They are not looked at either. They are one more component. A keeper who dies is replaced, from somewhere, by a keeper.* It would notice the children. Two, three, at any time. It would notice that the number of keepers is set by the number of cottages, and it would recognise this as the one place in the whole enclosure where the equilibrium has been laid over something that was not made to hold it. Birds can be a flow rate. Children were not built for it.

And it would ask whether that is a harm. Honestly. It would stand on the north terrace at evening and watch a child of two with its head on one side, listening to the roar the way a child elsewhere listens to a lullaby, and it would try to find the wound, and it would not be sure it had found one. They are pale. They are not ill. Their hands are never dry. They hear the one wrong note in a hundred years of right ones and get up and go and lay a palm on the bearing. There is a form of attention in that which most people outside the wall never reach in a life. If you lifted a keeper over the dike into the ordinary quiet you would not be freeing anyone. You would be deafening them.

So it would not do that.

---

And then it would understand, standing there, that the description had already told it who the being is. Not the gulls, who only stand. Not the keepers, who only tend. *The sound.*

The roar has occupied the enclosure for a hundred years without rising or falling or drawing breath. It has no self and no wants. It is a life in the way a river is a life — the sum of everything passing through a shape. And a wise power would not laugh at the idea that a kindness could be done to it. It would take it seriously. It would ask what a thing like that could possibly lack.

Two things. It lacks anyone who has heard it whole. And it lacks any knowledge of what it is standing on.

The first it could give at once, and would. It would stand on the dike — this is the long standing, the years of it — until it had heard every layer and every voice in every layer and could hold the entire sound in its attention at once, the iron thrum and the falling water and the six thousand and the one hawk and the bats turning over the flywheels and the beetles under the crust, which make a sound too, though no one has ever lowered their ear to it. Not as a visitor hears it, closing back into a single thing because the ear gives up. As it actually is. Every part known. That is a thing the place cannot do for itself. The keepers hear through it; they do not hear it. The gulls add to it and never listen. To be heard entire, once, by something that could bear the whole weight of you — that is not nothing. For a being that is only a sound, it may be everything there is.

The second is harder, and it would take the second more slowly.

---

It would go down.

Not by the shafts; the shafts are for water coming up, and it would respect that. It would go down the way such a power goes down, through the silt and the brick footings and whatever depth of rock the water is lifted through, into the dark under the delta, to the place where the pale things are made.

And it would find a lake. Black, the size of a county, cold, so full of blind soft life that the water is thick with it, and quiet — a quiet as total as the roar above is total, the exact inverse, the one place in all of this that the sound has never reached. It would understand that the Lift Ground is a wound in the roof of this and that the roar is what a wound sounds like from outside.

And it would learn the one thing the place does not know. It would learn whether the lake is finite.

I think it is. I think a wise power, going down, would find it very large and very old and very slowly emptying. Not in a lifetime. Not in ten. But the eels are thicker than a man's thigh and the eels have never been to the sea, and the description knew, when it called them the clocks of the place, that a clock with only an hour hand is still a clock. Something here is being spent. The Lift Ground is a mouth, and no mouth eats forever.

So it would stand at the edge of the black lake with that knowledge and consider the largest thing it could do, which is to make the lake bottomless. To replace a source with a spring. To take a place that is very nearly eternal and finish the job.

And I think it would set that down too, and this is the point at which I am most sure of it.

Because an eternal Lift Ground is a cruelty of a particular kind. Five cottages, five lights, and keepers replaced from nowhere for as long as the delta stands, and no one ever having a reason to look at the spur. The place is bearable — is even, in its way, a marvel — because it is a long steady spending, a waterfall, and a waterfall ends when the lake behind it does. The eels already say so. Everything here is in the shape of an ending that is a long way off. Take the ending away and you have not preserved the shape. You have replaced it with a trap that has learned to look like a shape. A wise power would not confuse *forever* with *whole*.

---

What it would do instead is very small, and it would do it on the way out.

It would come back up and cross the silt at the unhurried stationed pace of the place, and the birds would open round its feet and close behind it and not look up, and it would go to the north terrace, where a child of five or six was standing with its head on one side.

And it would teach the child one sign.

Not a word from outside. Not *sea* or *sun* or *away* — nothing the child could want. Nothing that would change the number of anything by one. Just a sign for the direction the spur goes. A small quick thing of the hand and the lower face that means: *that way, the line continues.* A word that does nothing. That has no use. That will be passed hand to hand between the doorways for generations, unused, a piece of the very few words that means nothing anybody needs, and that no one will think to drop, because dropping a word would be a change.

Until, in however many centuries, the day the roar thins. The day a keeper in a cottage with the door shut stands up because the whole sound, not one bearing but the whole of it, has begun by some fraction of a tone to sing differently, and goes out, and sees the culverts running lower than they have ever run, and the second row on the stone lip thinner than it has ever been, and the beetles, for the first time, visible on the surface of the crust because there is not enough falling to keep them under it.

And knows one sign for where to go.

That is all. A witness given to the sound, and a single word given to the people, and the lake left to be a lake. It would leave no mark, because leaving a mark would be a change, and there are no changes here. It would go back up the spur the way it came, and drop down the far side of the dike into the ordinary quiet, and for several minutes the world would seem the strange place. And behind the wall, the Lift Ground would go on roaring in its cold equilibrium, its ten thousand birds in their fixed rows over the pale endless stream, exactly as it was, with only one thing in it that had not been there before — a small shape of the hand that nobody would use for a very long time — and the roar would close over that too, and make it into a single thing.
