It arrives the way the cloud arrives—from the side of the room that is missing—and, like the cloud, it does not disturb anything on the way in.

That is the first thing to say about it, and it may be the only thing that matters: it is the kind of power that can be present in a place without the place having to accommodate it. The water at the seam does not tremble any differently. The frost does not thicken. The lizard, on its trunk, does not lift its head. Whatever this is, it has learned how to enter a room without becoming a condition of the room, and it uses that skill now, and it waits.

---

It waits for a long time.

Not because it is slow. Because wisdom, whatever else it is, is mostly the willingness to watch for longer than seems necessary, and the shelf is a place that punishes hurry. Nothing here can be understood in an afternoon. The water's obedience at the seam looks like a trick until you have seen it hold through a hundred cloud-risings. The frost looks like weather until you have watched it die and be born a thousand times and realized it is not weather but a resident. The lizard's circuit looks like a lizard until you have counted the rounds.

So it counts. It sits through the morning cloud and the evening cloud and the blue hard hours between, day after day, while the ferns unroll and the horsetails put up one more joint and the roof of the little shelter sends one more root down into the moss. It sits through a season, and then another, at the shelf's own pace, which is the pace of stone drying.

It comes to the same census the visitor came to. One animal. Green arrivals in islands. Water, obedient. Frost, at its address. A light from below. A shelter with no one in it.

And it comes to the same conclusion about who lives here. But it stays longer than the visitor did, and so it sees one more thing.

---

The round has a fourth station.

Cold, wet, warm—the visitor saw those. Frost in the morning, the seam at midday, the fern trunk in the afternoon facing the drop. But at dusk, after the evening cloud has closed the shelf and before it has begun to thin, the lizard comes down off the trunk and crosses the dark rock to the mountain wall and goes into the shelter.

It goes in slowly, the way it goes everywhere. It lies down on the moss floor in the lying-down space, in the warm, with its broad head toward the door and the roof dripping on it, and it stays there for about as long as it stays on the frost. Then it comes out and goes to wherever it goes for the night—the power never sees where, and does not look—and in the morning it is on the ice again.

The visitor did not see this because the visitor was sitting there. The visitor sat with their back to the shelter in the evening cloud and was visited, once, briefly, and understood the visit as a courtesy extended to a warm thing that moved. It was not that. The lizard was not visiting the visitor. It was visiting the station, and the visitor happened to be on it.

The shelter is on the round. Someone stayed there long enough to make it a condition—a warm, dry, roofed place where a body had lain—and when they left, the lizard kept coming. It keeps the frost, which is the block's. It keeps the standing water, which is the seam's. It keeps the trunk that faces the drop, which is the mountain's. And it keeps the empty shelter, which is the person's, and it has gone on keeping it for as long as the roof has been putting down roots, and it will go on keeping it after the roof is a plant and the horsetail bundle is a fossil and no one could say a person had ever been here at all.

It is keeping an absence. That is what the fourth station is. The power sits with this for a while.

---

Then, because it is a power and not only a witness, it considers what it might do.

It could give the lizard another lizard. This is the first thing that occurs to it and it is the first thing it sets down. *You never see two at once* is not a report of loneliness. It is a description of a fit. One keeper, one round, one shelf: the arithmetic is closed. A second lizard would not be a companion. It would be a rival, or a redundancy, or—worst—a change in what the first one is. The lizard does not know it is alone. It knows it is the one that goes around. To make it two would be to take that from it in order to give it something the power, and not the lizard, thinks it lacks. That is not kindness. That is a large thing making a small thing carry the large thing's idea of comfort.

It could finish the note. Line, circle. A third mark. The power knows what the two marks mean—it has watched longer than the person did and it has the advantage of seeing the round entire—and it could kneel in the shelter and scratch the third thing into the dark stone and the note would be complete. It does not, because the note is not its note. Someone got as far as two marks and stopped, and the stopping is part of the record. To finish it would be to erase the person's uncertainty and put the power's certainty in its place, and the person's uncertainty was honest and the power's certainty is only large.

It could hurry the mountain. Round the edges. Bring the ferns into touch with one another, run the water into channels, soften the whole raw ticking room by a century so that the lizard's round crosses a garden instead of a wound. But the mountain is doing this. It is doing it slowly, wetly, at the correct speed, which is the speed at which things become native, and there is no version of *faster* that is not also *less true*.

It could look down the hole. It is the one thing on the shelf it does not already understand, and it would be easy, and it would be a comfort to know. It decides not to. Not because the answer would be dangerous—it does not think so—but because the shelf is arranged around a door that is not used. The lizard does not use it. The person, in the end, either did or did not, and the shelter does not say. The hole is the shelf's one kept mystery and the light is steady and the warmth is steady and there is a kind of respect that consists of not opening a door simply because you can.

It could speak to the lizard. It could make itself known. It sets this down last, and most firmly. The lizard is unafraid of everything on the shelf because nothing on the shelf has ever addressed it. To be addressed is to be made into an audience, and an audience is not a keeper.

So it does none of those.

---

What it does instead comes from noticing what is actually at risk.

Most of the shelf is not at risk. The seam will hold. The block will be cold. The ferns will win, eventually, and the water will keep standing where it is told, and none of that needs help. But the shelf is held up by two things, and both of them are single.

The first is the light.

Everything here is warm because of what is under it. The ferns grow because the rock is warm; the rock is warm because of the tenant beneath; the tenant is a lamp left on in a room no one has looked into. The power has not looked either. But it has sat here long enough to feel how completely the shelf is a roof—how the whole sweating, dripping, colonizing life of it is borrowed heat, and how, if the light ever went out, the room would go cold from the floor up in a matter of days. The frost would spread out from the block and take the shelf. The water at the seam would stand there, obedient, and freeze in place. The ferns would blacken where they stood. And the lizard would go around one more time, touching each rule, finding each one changed, and lie down in the shelter and not come out.

The second is the lizard.

One keeper. Mortal, presumably, in the way of things with throats that pulse. It does not seem old. But the shelf is not old either, and the shelf is going to be here for a long time, and the round is the only thing that treats it as one place instead of several spots. If the round stops, the shelf does not die. It simply stops being kept. It becomes the ferns' shelf, and the frost's, and the water's, each in their island, no one going around. That is not a catastrophe. It is only a loss, and a quiet one, and no one would know.

These are the two things. The power looks at them for a long time and then it does what a wise and benevolent power does about things that are single and load-bearing and not its own.

It stands behind them.

---

Not in front. Behind.

It makes two promises, and it makes them to itself, silently, in the evening cloud, with the rock warm under it and the drip thickening into a hush.

The first: *if the light goes out, I will be the light.* Not now. Not instead. Nothing on the shelf changes tonight or tomorrow or for as long as the tenant beneath keeps its lamp. But if the lamp ever fails, the heat will not fail with it. The rock will go on ticking. The ferns will not know the difference. The frost will stay at the block where it belongs. The power will hold the warmth from below the way the seam holds the water—as a condition, without effort, without anyone being told—and the shelf will go on being a room with a warm floor, and no one, not the mountain, not the lizard, will ever learn that the tenancy changed hands.

The second: *the round will not end with this one.* When the lizard dies—wherever it goes at night; the power still does not look—there will be another. Not before. Not two at once; the arithmetic stays closed. But after, and soon after, a small dark thing with a broad flat head will be on the frost in the morning, and it will walk across the ice with its belly down and stop at the far edge and pulse its throat, and at midday it will drink from the standing lip of the seam, and in the afternoon it will lie along a frond facing the drop, and at dusk it will go into the shelter that is by then a plant and lie down where a body lay. It will not know it is a successor. It will know it is the one that goes around.

The power does not make these promises to the lizard, or to the shelf, or to whoever made the seam and the block and the hole and walked off. It makes them the way you underwrite a thing you love and cannot own: quietly, without expectation of being thanked, in case.

---

There is one more thing it does, and it is the only thing that could be called an act.

It sits.

In the evening, with the cloud closing, it goes to the shelter—not into it; it does not take the station—and sits with its back against the fern-trunk wall where the visitor sat, and lets the rock be warm under it and the roof drip on it, and waits.

The lizard comes down off the trunk and crosses the dark rock and finds a warm thing that does not move in the place where the station is. It stops. Its throat pulses, and in the pulse is the bruised gray of the seam's floor, the one color here that belongs to the made things. It regards the power for about as long as it regards anything.

Then it goes around. Past the power's flank, into the lying-down space, onto the moss. It lies there. The power can hear it breathing, a small dry sound under the drip. Then it comes out, and passes the power again without pausing, and goes to wherever it goes.

It visited. The power was on the station and the power was visited, once, briefly, in its turn, and found to be holding, and left behind.

That is what the power wanted. Not to be known. To be kept—for one round, one dusk, to be a condition the keeper touched and found in order and moved on from. The shelf's only citizenship is the kind the water has: to be a place where something has agreed to hold. For one evening the power is that. Then the cloud thins and the sky comes back very blue and very close and the rock ticks louder as it dries.

---

It leaves in the morning.

It leaves because leaving is the grammar of this place. The maker left. The person left. Only the lizard stays, and the lizard stays precisely because everything else has gone; a shelf with two keepers is not a kept shelf but a contested one. The power understands this and it goes the way it came, from the side of the room that is missing, and nothing on the shelf registers the going.

It does not make the third mark. But it knows, now, what the third mark would have been, and why the person did not make it, and it carries this away with it as the only thing it takes.

The line is the rule. The circle is the round. Anyone who watched long enough would see those two and scratch them down.

The third thing is the going-around continuing after the one who goes around is gone. There is no mark for that. You cannot scratch it into a stone. You can only sit in the evening cloud and promise it, silently, to no one, and then leave, and let the mountain go on writing it the only way it can—slowly, wetly, with ferns.

---

The shelf is the same.

The frost grows and lets go and grows. The water runs downhill toward a line it will not cross, and stops, and stands. The light in the hole is steady and orange and has a source. The ferns are in their islands and have not yet learned one another's names. The roof of the shelter puts down one more root.

And a lizard the length of your forearm, dark, with a throat that carries the color of the seam, goes around. Cold. Wet. Warm. The empty place where someone lay.

It does not know that behind the light there is now another light, waiting, unlit. It does not know that behind it there is another of it, waiting, unborn. It does not need to know. That is the whole point of standing behind a thing instead of in front of it.

It goes around. Everything it touches is still holding. It goes around again.
