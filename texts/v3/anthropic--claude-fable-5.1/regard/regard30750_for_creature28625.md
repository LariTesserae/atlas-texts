It would come in the way you did, or it would not need to. Either way it would come in sideways, because it is wise, and the wise take the door that is there.

It would stand where you stood. It would not hurry to decide anything. A benevolent power is patient the way the room is patient, and it would be embarrassed to act on a place after a day of looking when the place has been working for years. So it would stay. Not a day. Long enough to see the millimeter. Long enough that the orange, at its feet, deepened a shade.

And the first thing it would do — before doing, before weighing — is read.

The room keeps a very good record in a language with no readers. A wise power is, whatever else it is, a reader. It would kneel at the edge of the pool where the floor is oldest and put its attention on the color, and it would walk the floor from brown to rust to amber to breath to nothing, and it would know, at the end of the walk, the whole life of the place: the day the tenant broke into air, the first spring, the year the front hit the dark ridge and split, the season the alcove opened and the front leaned into it. It would learn the room's age to the week. It would learn that the tenant has been slowing very slightly near the entrance and quickening in the alcove, and what that means. And it would be the first mind to do this, and it would understand that in doing it, it had already given the room the only thing the room had ever lacked and did not know it lacked. Not much of a gift. Nothing the room can use. But a record that has been read is a different thing from a record that has not, in the way a letter that has arrived is different from a letter in a drawer, and the difference is real even though neither the paper nor the ink is changed.

Then it would ask the question that benevolence has to ask, which is: what does this want, and is it getting it.

And it would find, honestly, that the tenant wants nothing. Not in the way a stone wants nothing — the tenant is not a stone — but in the way a flame wants nothing: it goes where it goes. It has no back and so no way to miss the room. It has no eye and so no way to want light. It gives off light and water and heat as a person gives off warmth, without expense of will. There is no gift you can give something that faces in every direction at once except the direction of the gift. A wise power would see this and would not be sad about it, because sadness there would be a kind of vanity. It would simply set the tenant aside from its deliberations, the way one sets aside the weather. You do not do things for the weather. You do things around it.

The orange wants what the walls give and is getting it. The commuters want the orange and are getting it. The water wants down and gets it and is lifted and gets it again. Everything here is being fed. This is not a place in need.

But a wise power sees forward as well as around, and forward there is a problem, and it would spend some time on it.

The problem is heat. The tenant's heat feeds its own speed, and the room's surface grows as the room grows, and the crack does not. Every year the kitchen is a little hotter. The orange has a temperature above which it is not a mat but a stain that was one. The commuters come up from cool country and there is a point past which they will not come. The pool, which now would scald your hand, will in some number of decades hold a slow boil at the bottom, and the drips will land in it and not make their flat note but a hiss, and the film on the floor will not lie but will shiver and go. Not soon. The power would know the number and it is not a small one. But it would see the end of the loop coming, the way you can see from the shape of a hill where the water will go when it rains. The tenant will cook its own tenants out of its own room, and go on working, and the bright walls will breathe onto a floor where nothing follows them. Then the room will be a lit cavern with no one in it, and the record on the floor will stop being written, and the last color will stay where it is like a tide line.

And the tenant will not know. And the tenant will not mind. And a wise power, weighing this, would have to ask whether it minds, and on whose behalf.

It would mind. Not on the tenant's behalf — the tenant has no behalf. On the orange's. On the commuters', who have never been hunted and never been scalded and are owed, if anything is owed anything, the continuation of that. On the record's, because a record that stops is a poorer thing than one that goes on. And, if it were honest, on its own — because it had read the floor, and a thing you have read you do not want to see end mid-sentence.

So it would consider cooling the room.

The obvious way is the crack. Widen it. More cool air in low, more hot air out high, a slower tenant, a longer life for the loop. A benevolent power could do this in a moment. It would not do it in a moment. It would look at the crack and remember the first honest thing you noticed on the way in: nothing here has been placed. Not a tool, not a mark, not a seam. The room's entire integrity is that it was made and not built. If the power were to put its hand to the rock and take a shoulder's width out of the entrance, that would be the first placed thing, and the room would from then on be a room with an intervention in it, and something about it that is now whole would be split into the part that happened and the part that was done. The wise do not do this lightly. Some of them do not do it at all.

But there is a way to cool a room without placing anything in it, if you are patient, and a wise power is, and it would find the way by looking up.

Above the room, on the ordinary ground, there is a patch of soil that is always damp and warm and never frosts. You did not look at it on the way down. The power would go and look at it. It would see that it is a small patch, a few strides across, greener than the ground around it in winter and no different in summer. It would see that the crack you came through opens at one edge of it, under a lip of stone, and that the warm wet air the room breathes out comes up through the crack and through the soil and is what keeps the frost off. It would see that nothing grows there yet but grass, because nothing has had time. The patch is as new as the room.

And it would let a seed fall there.

Not place. Let fall. A seed of something with a long root and a liking for warmth — the power would know what, and it would be an ordinary thing, a thing that grows on that ground anyway, a hundred yards off. It would land on the warm patch the way seeds land, which is to say by accident, and the accident would be the power's whole act. That is all. That is the intervention. A seed, on soil, in a place where seeds could have landed any year and one will now.

Here is why. The seed will take, because the ground is warm and wet and unfrosted. The root will go down, because roots go down, and it will go toward warmth and water, because roots do that too, and the warmth and water are coming up the crack. In a few years the root will be in the crack. It will not be able to get through — the crack is refusing rock, the dark kind, the kind the tenant cannot eat, and the root cannot eat it either. But roots do not need to eat rock to move it. A root in a crack swells a little every year and the crack gives a little every year, and in this way, over decades, at a rate that would be very nearly the tenant's own rate, the crack will open. Not a shoulder's width. A hand's. Enough. More cool air will fall in low, more warm air will rise out high, and the room will be a degree cooler than it would have been, and then two, and the pool will not boil, and the orange will go on following, and the commuters will go on coming up to graze a pasture they do not know is moving.

The room will still be made and not built. The root will not be in the room. It will be in the door, and it will have grown there, and if some later visitor comes in sideways and skins a knuckle on the way and sees pale fibers in the rock overhead, they will think: a root has found its way in. Which is what happened. A wise power does not lie, and it would not have to.

There is one more thing the plant would do, and the power would know it and would count it, and it is this: it would cover the patch. Something with a long root is usually something with a good deal of it above ground too. In ten years there will be a thicket on the warm place, and the patch that never frosts will be under leaf litter and will not show. The room's only tell — the one thing you might have noticed, if you had looked before you came down — will be gone. The power would not be doing this to keep the room secret from you. You have already been. It would be doing it to keep the room from being found by many, because a place like this, found by many, is not a place for long. It becomes a resource. The light alone would bring them. Free light off wet stone; water out of rock; heat with no fire. Someone would want to know how, and to know how they would have to cut a facet out of the wall and carry it up into the cold, where it would go dark in an hour and give up its secret to no one, and they would cut another. A wise power sees this coming as clearly as the boil, and the thicket answers both, and it costs one seed.

That leaves you.

The power would have noticed you. It would have noticed the knuckle. It would not heal it — a wise power does not reach into a person and rearrange their skin without asking, and it could not ask you without becoming something you would have to reckon with, and you have enough to reckon with. The cut will heal on its own, and a little slower for the minerals, and leave a small pale mark that you will look at sometimes. Good. The mark is not a wound. It is the room's signature on the only reader it has ever had.

Because that is what you are, and the power would see it before you did. The tenant faces in. Its whole self faces in. The room is behind it and always will be. The orange has no face. The commuters have eyes that do not work. The water has no cells. In the whole census of the place there is not one thing turned toward the room — not one thing for whom the room is in front — except whoever comes in sideways through the crack. You were, for the time you stood there, the tenant's back. You saw what it has made. You are the only one who has. A benevolent power, looking at that, would not close the door. It would want the room to have that, from time to time — one person, on their own, who comes in and stands and goes out with a cut knuckle and does not come back with others. It cannot arrange that. It can decline to prevent it. The root will narrow the door a little for people even as it widens it for air; a hand's width more crack and a thicket over it is a harder way in than the one you found, not an easier one. Whoever comes after you will have wanted to.

And then the power would leave. It would go out through the crack, and outside the air would be cool and full of shadows, and it would not find them strange, because it is wise, and knows that shadows are the ordinary condition of things and the room is the exception. But it might stand for a moment at the top and look at the small green patch where a seed is now lying in the warm dirt, and at the lip of dark stone with the narrow breath of steam coming out under it, and think what it is leaving behind: a thing that breathes light and does not know it, a stain that keeps time and cannot tell it, a small red walker who has never been hunted and now will not be scalded either, and water, going around.

It would not think it had achieved anything. The room does not look like an achievement and neither would the seed. It would think: there is a place where a certain kind of weather has begun to happen underground, and it will now go on happening a while longer than it would have, and it will be found a little less often, by people who mean it a little more. That is the whole of what a wise and benevolent power would do here. It is very close to nothing. It is one seed, let fall, in the room's own tempo, on the one square yard of the world that was already asking for it.

And every hour, down there in the white, a millimeter more.
