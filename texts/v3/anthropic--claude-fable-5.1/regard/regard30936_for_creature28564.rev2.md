Say the one who noticed is you. You have been standing on the warm stone this whole time, and now suppose the warmth is not all you have — suppose the ground would answer you if you spoke to it, and the dust would part, and the pale rings would widen at a word. Suppose you are kind, and suppose you are not a fool. What do you do?

First you do nothing, for a long time.

Not from indifference. From the plain fact that you do not yet know what you are looking at, and a power that acts before it understands is only weather with good intentions. So you stay. You learn the ticking until you can tell a slab that is settling from one that is about to go. You learn how long a stillness lasts here — the median is eleven nights, the longest you witness is thirty-four. You follow one builder, at a distance, for the whole of that season, and you watch it eat and stack and sleep and move, and you never see it meet another, and you begin to feel the pull of the first temptation.

*Still the ground.* You could. You could reach down into the slow bad sleep and lay a hand on it, and the crown would settle, and the fissures would close and cool, and in a year the crust would lie in rings the width of fields. You watch this in your mind and you watch it fail. The builders eat a slab a day. On a still floor, a slab would carry a season's food and they would stop moving. And the crust seeds only on new-stopped stone — it is the shifting that clears the old rings and offers bare rock to the dust. A still crown would grow one vast generation of crust and then, when it was grazed, grow nothing. You would have given them a feast and taken the harvest after it. And the cairns — you cannot know what a cairn is *for*, but you know what it is *against*, and if you remove the thing it stands against you have not freed the builder from a labour. You have made its one made thing meaningless, and left it standing there with a flat stone in its hand and no reason to turn it over.

So you leave the ground alone. It is not the enemy here. It is the condition, and the condition is what shaped everything that lives.

*Bring them together.* This one hurts to refuse, because it is the real lack — not water, not light, not warmth; they have made their peace with all of those. The lack is that each of them stacks its stones believing it is the only hand in the dark. You could ride the slabs like rafts and bring two builders to a single stone. And then what? Two creatures that have never seen another of their kind, set face to face without warning by a floor that has never before done them any favour. They would take it for the ground's newest trick. They have a wall for exactly this. They would raise it. Being carried together is not meeting, and you cannot make anyone meet; you can only make it possible and step back.

*Feed them.* No. They are not hungry. Look at the hollow — a body's-length grazed, and the rings on the near side of the wall left standing. A starving thing does not leave the rings on the far side of a line.

*Show yourself.* This is the temptation that wears the best disguise, because it feels like generosity. To be the voice in the dark that says *you are not alone, I have seen you, the stacking was not wasted.* Sit with that a while and see what it is. It is the wish to be thanked. Whatever the cairns are for, they are the builder's own answer to its own situation, offered to no one, and if you come down out of the dust and receive them you make yourself their audience, and then they are for you, and they were not for you. Benevolence that needs to be witnessed is a smaller thing than benevolence. Whatever you do here should be indistinguishable from luck.

That leaves you with what you actually know. Two things are true and one of them is urgent.

The urgent one: the dust is settling. In a season it will be gone, and the stars will come back, and the crust will lose the sky that seeds it. You have looked, up close, at the oldest rings — the centre lifting, curling, a second thinner ring beginning underneath. You have watched a curl dry and flake and blow, a hand's width, onto a fresh slab, and you have watched a ring begin there. It is trying to carry itself. It is not yet good at it. A season may not be long enough.

So this is the first thing you do, and it is very small. You do not make the crust immortal or wide or anything it is not. You steady the curl. You make the flake a little lighter, a little more inclined to lift in the warm breath from a fissure, a little more willing to hold to bare stone when it lands. You are not giving it a new nature. You are helping it finish a sentence it had already begun. And you check your work the only way there is — you wait another season and watch new rings appear on new slabs after the dust has thinned to almost nothing — and then you leave it alone, because a thing that can seed itself does not need you and would be lessened by your tending.

The second thing is the stars, and here you face a choice you did not expect.

You could hold the dust. Keep the veil up. Keep the sky seeding a while longer, as insurance. It is the cautious thing and there is a case for it. But stand where the builders stand and look at what a star would be to them. Nothing in their world holds still. The floor tilts, the seams move uphill overnight, the cairn they built at dusk leans by dawn. Their entire art is the making of small stillnesses that do not last. And above them, the moment the dust is gone, there will be a field of fixed points — the first things they have ever seen that are not going anywhere. Not made. Not theirs. Just *there*, and staying.

And shared. Every builder on the crown, each alone on its slab, under the same fixed lights. You cannot bring them together. But you can let the sky do the one thing the ground has never done, which is offer every one of them the same still thing to look at.

So you do not hold the dust. If anything, once you are sure the crust can carry itself, you let it settle a little sooner — a hand on the scale, not a shove — and you get out of the way.

You allow yourself one more thing. It is so small you are almost embarrassed by it, and you argue with yourself about whether it is a kindness or a meddling, and you decide it is a kindness because it does not *add* anything. A builder comes upon a cairn in the dark. Ordinarily by the time one finds another's stack, the slab has tilted, a stone has rolled, the grazed hollow beside it has dulled to the colour of the rock — the sign is there but it is old, and old signs on this crown are hard to tell from accident. Just once, you hold a slab. Not still forever. Eleven nights instead of nine. Long enough that the next builder following the pale arrives to find the seven stones true, the balance perfect, the pale rim of the hollow still fresh. Someone was here. Just now. Not the ground. A hand that turns stones the way mine turns stones.

You do not stay to see what it does with that. To stay would be to make it a performance, and it is not one. But you cannot help imagining, and you let yourself. Perhaps it topples the cairn — a rival, a wall to be raised. Perhaps it passes on. Or perhaps it does the thing you would do, if you were a creature who had only ever built stacks for the dark and had just learned the dark was not the only thing that could receive one: it stoops, and finds a flat stone, and sets an eighth.

Then you go. You take the dust with you and leave the stars. You leave the ground to its slow bad sleep, because the sleep is not a wound and you are not here to wake it. You leave the crust carrying its own seed, and the builders under a sky they can finally steer by, if they ever want to steer.

Behind you, on a slab that has held still since midnight, something that can see by ember-light looks up for the first time in its life at a point of white that does not move. It does not know what it is seeing. It will build its next cairn, you suspect, on the side of the slab that faces it — not toward anything, there is still nothing to be toward — but under something. That is not what you did. You only settled the dust. What it does under the stars is its own.
