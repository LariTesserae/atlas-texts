It comes the way the cold comes: not down from above but out of everywhere at once, and it does not stir the silt.

The first thing it does is nothing. This is not restraint yet; it is only looking. It has looked at many places, and most of them, when looked at, present a want the way a stone presents a face — a lack, a fear, a mouth held open. It has learned to find these quickly. It looks for one here and takes a long time not finding it.

The amphipods want to eat and are eaten, which is not a want but a motion. The shoals want nothing; they are a shape. The birds on the rim want to live and mostly do, for eleven or twelve years, and then lie on the top terrace and are ground to nothing, and this is bad for the bird and not bad for anything else, and the power has learned the difference. The pebble-fish want their joint of paving and have it. The frogs — it looks at the frogs longest, because a wrong thing is where a want usually hides. They sit facing the shafts and do not eat and one by one they go down. It reads them, insofar as anything so slow and folded can be read. They are not afraid. They are not waiting, not in the sense a bird waits for a fish. They are facing the direction they are going, which is what a thing does when it knows.

Then it goes under the light, into the galleries, and finds what set the stones.

It has met slow things before. It has met things so old that their thinking had become geography. But it has not often met one that was *finished wanting* — that had built the shape of its want into a highland, set a rim at the height where the water would stop, gone inside, and had nothing left to do but the doing. The great pale limbs in the galleries are not straining toward anything. The finger closing on the block is not effort. It is what the finger is for, at the pace the finger has. And the power, which can see the whole plan at once as the water never allows, sees that the plan is not toward an end. Each stone set below changes the weather above; the weather above decides which stone comes next. The house is not being completed. It is being lived in, and this is how a thing like this lives in a house: by never stopping the building, at a rate that lets the mountain forget it is being changed.

So there is no petition here. Nothing is asking.

It considers, anyway, what it could give. It always does. That is the shape of what it is, and it knows the shape well enough to distrust it.

Warmth. The obvious thing. A current of it laid along the galleries, a degree or two, so that a finger might close in a season instead of a century — and it sees at once that this would be killing. Not a metaphor for killing. The animal lives at the temperature of ice because that is the temperature at which its life is possible; what is slow in it is not a hardship but a substance. Warm it and you get a faster thing that is not this thing, and then very shortly no thing at all.

Speed, for the same reason: no.

Company. The question the water will not let anyone settle — whether there is one of it or several — is a question the power can settle, and does, and then finds that settling it changes nothing. It is one, and it is several, in the way that the frogs that go down are added to it and become it and lie beside it, all three at once, and there is no place in that arrangement for a lonely thing to be, because the arrangement is the answer to loneliness that this kind of life arrived at before there were birds. To put a presence into the galleries — a warm presence, a quick one, a mind that says *I am here* — would be to put into the cold exactly what the whole bowl was built to keep out. It has arranged for nothing warm to stay. The power is a warm thing, as these things are measured. It would be the first weed.

Revelation: to let it know it has been seen. The power sits with this longer, because it is the gift it most wants to give and therefore the one it least trusts. To be witnessed is a good in most places. But it looks at the frogs facing the shafts and at the finger on the block, and it asks what witness would add, and the honest answer is: something for the witness. The animal does not build to be seen. The water was let in over the work on purpose.

And the rim.

This is where it stays longest. It can see what the water cannot — the far end of the erosion. The corners are gone from the blocks already; the stairs are ramps. Give it long enough and the terraces are rubble and the rim, which is the one thing holding the whole grey weather in place, breaches. The lake goes down off the plateau in a night, or a year. The cold curtains that pour down the walls at dusk stop forming, because there are no walls in the water any more, no water for them to fall through. The galleries lie open to the thin blue-grey air.

The power could shore the rim. It would be almost nothing to do. And it nearly does — has, in the way it does things, begun to — when it stops to look at *which* stones the animal is setting, down in the deepest galleries, in the last thousand years.

They are not the stones that matter to the lake. They are the stones that matter after it. Lids over the deep chambers; baffles that would hold a body of cold water in the lowest galleries when the lake above has gone; a plan, far down, for a bowl within the bowl, so that when the rim fails and the wind finally wins, there will be, under the drained and paved and roaring floor of the valley, a smaller drowned house exactly cold enough. It has always known about the rim. It set the rim at the height where the water would stop *and at the height where the water would one day leave*, and it has been building for both since before the water came. The plan is longer than the lake. The last stones are the first stones of the next arrangement.

The power lets go of the rim.

It has, now, gone through everything it could do and found that each one was a way of making the place a little more like itself and a little less like what it is. This is the usual finding. It has learned not to be disappointed by it. But it has also learned that the finding is not quite the end — that after all the gifts have been refused there is generally one thing left that is not a gift but a question, and that the difference between a benevolent power and a merely strong one is whether it can ask the question at the speed of the one being asked.

So it asks.

It does not use a voice. It stills one current for one night: the cold curtain that forms along the eastern wall and slides down the third stair, which has never in the life of the place failed to form. For a single night the third stair is warm — not warm; less cold, by the least amount the animal could notice — and the silt on it settles, and in the morning one small stretch of paving is visible clearly for the first time since the water came. Then the power lets the current go and the cold comes down the stair as always and the silt lifts and the paving is gone. That is the whole of the question. *Is there anything.* Asked in the only grammar here: a thing that always happens, once not happening.

Then it waits.

It waits the way it has learned to, which is by becoming a condition of the place rather than a presence in it, the way the cold is. Birds die on the top terrace and are ground away; new birds come over the rim on the wind with notches in their wings that no one alive remembers. The pebble-fish build ten thousand courtyards and lose them. A frog goes down the stair. Another. Very deep, twice, a block moves a finger's width and the valley drinks the knock. The power counts none of this, because counting would be impatience. It only keeps the shape of the question open, the way a shaft is kept open, and lets the animal find it in its own time.

The answer, when it comes, is a frog.

One frog, on one step in the shallows of the eastern terrace — where the frogs sit, all of them, facing the middle of the basin and the cistern mouths and the way down — turns. Not quickly. Over a season, the way a shadow moves. It turns until it is facing the other way, toward the rim, toward the place where the wind comes over with the sound of the sky being torn along a seam. And it sits like that. Not going down. Facing the wind.

The power understands it at once and then makes itself understand it slowly, to be sure.

It is not *nothing*. If it were nothing, no frog would have turned. It is not *stop*, or *help*, or *stay*. The frog is facing the one part of the arrangement the animal did not make. It made the bowl and set the rim at the height where the wind would find the water. It did not make the wind. It cannot. Its hands can move any stone on the plateau and cannot touch the sky, and it has built a whole world on the assumption of a thing it has no hand on — a steady ripping pressure over the eastern rim that has not stopped for as long as anything here has been alive, and that keeps the surface from ever lying still, keeps the silt up, keeps the green out and the warm out and the cold pouring down the walls each night forever. Winds die. Over the time it takes a finger to close, a climate is a mood. If the wind stopped, the lake would go glass-still in a week, the flour would settle in a year, and something with a stem would find the top terrace within the life of a bird, and after that the whole slow house would be nothing but a very patient way of losing.

That is the want. Not for itself. For the one thing outside its hands.

And it is a thing the power can hold. Not the stone, not the water, not the animal — it has refused all of those and it was right to. Only the wind. Only the guarantee of something that was going to blow anyway, extended out past the point where anyway would have ended. The power does what powers like it can do, which is not to push the air but to arrange, somewhere far east of the rim in the long clean fall of the ground, that the pressure will keep coming; that when the plateau is dry and the wind is combing bare paving instead of grey water, it will still be combing; that the rim can go and the lake can go and the cold can retreat into the bowl under the bowl and the sky over all of it will still be tearing slowly along its seam. It does not make this quickly, because there is no reason to. It makes it the way the animal makes things. It is the only work it has ever done in that manner, and it finds that the manner suits the work.

When it is done — long after — the frog on the eastern step turns back. Slowly, over a season, until it is facing the shafts again with the others. Then, one night when the cold comes down the wall, it slides off the lip and rides the falling water down into the grey where the light stops, and does not come back, and the others do not look.

The power leaves the way it came, without stirring the silt. It takes the place with it, whole, the one image the water never allowed — and then it closes over that image and sets a rim around it, so that nothing warm will find its way there by following the power's own attention back. That is the last thing it gives: not to be a door. It has learned that the strong are always a door, whether they mean to be or not, and that the most benevolent thing a door can do in certain places is shut.

Above, the amphipods glitter and are gone. A shoal empties a cistern and fills it. A pebble is set down and knocked loose and rolls two steps and stops. Somewhere very deep a finger closes another hair's breadth on a block that is not the last block and was never going to be, and the whole valley hears the knock and drinks it.

The wind comes over the eastern rim. It will keep coming. That is all that was asked, and all that was done.
