**The same night, a little later**

It comes over the rim the way the cold does. Not through the cloud but as part of it, settling into every fold of the terrain, touching every surface, giving nothing back. For a while it does only what the cloud does. It is in no hurry. It has never been in a hurry anywhere, and this is not a place that rewards hurry.

It does not need the lull to see. It sees the throat inside the roar and the roar inside the throat: the body of gas under the floor as a shape with a volume and a pressure and a history, the path each parcel of steam will take before the steam takes it. It sees the walls whole and reads them not as the visitor reads them—a decade of wind, roughly—but as a record with no gaps. This face was cool from the third spring. This fur of crystal has grown and shed eleven times. Here, under two hands' depth of moss that has quietly become soil, is the small light skeleton of a chameleon, every bone in place, and standing on it, drinking it, a lobelia a little taller than its neighbours. It knows the age of the oldest groundsel to the season. It knows the seam's rate this year, which is more than last year's. It can see farther down than the geologists who have not come here, though not to the bottom, because the mountain has not decided. But it can hold the two ends side by side—the cold mossy hollow and the new raw scarp—and see roughly how far off they are, and the answer is: not far. Not far at all.

And it sees the line. It takes it in at once, as no one standing on the floor ever could: the whole border, hot edge and cool edge, its exact position tonight and, faintly beneath, its position last night, a ghost of itself a hand's breadth inward here and a hand's breadth outward there. It watches the line breathe. Forty seconds. It counts, because there is nothing in this place that does not count.

This is what it has come to look at. Not the garden, though the garden is worth looking at. The thing that has no body and lives.

---

It considers what it might do, because that is what it is for, and because a thing that can do anything owes each place it visits at least the courtesy of the question.

It could reach down into the floor and ease the throat shut. Slowly, over years, so that no slab is startled loose and no groundsel is cooked in the closing. The heat would fall off to merely warm and stay there. The steam would thin to a breath. The gas would sweeten. The garden would keep its ceiling and its crowd and its wet, and in fifty years it would be what it is tonight, and in a hundred, and the visitor's grandchildren could come down the scree and put a hand on the same trunk.

It looks at this for a long time, and then it looks at what would be saved, and what would be saved is the plants. The line would stop. A garden with a fixed border is a garden, and a garden is a thing that has been put there, and this one would have been put there by the hand that held the throat. The visitor would come back and find that nothing had moved and, without knowing why, would stop coming back.

It could hold the seam. Lay itself along the shoulder of the mountain and take the strain, a centimetre a year, so that the fissure never opens along its length and the scarp never comes. The mountain would have to be told to stop being a mountain. It has told mountains things before. It does not like to, and the reason it does not like to is exactly this cleft: a mountain that is not permitted to shift its weight does not open seams, and a mountain that does not open seams does not make places like this one. It cannot save the child by forbidding the birth.

It could take the poison out of the gas. Leave the heat and the wet and the roar and the forty seconds and simply let the air be air. And then the hyraxes would den in the warm cinder, because that is exactly what a hyrax wants. And the sunbirds would work the tallest spikes on the mountain. And the moths, and the mites in the moss, and the chameleons who would live this time. And after the animals, the ones who follow animals, and after them a path worn down the scree, and a rope, and a name. The one fact that has shaped this place—that everything with a nervous system knows better—would be gone, and with it the thing the visitor comes down here for, which is to stand where nothing has been put.

It could tell the visitor which death is coming, and when.

It sets each of these down. Not because they are forbidden it. Nothing is forbidden it. It sets them down because each one, looked at squarely, is a kindness to a moment of the thing and not to the thing. The being here is the rate. Slow the rate and there is nothing left to be kind to. The being cannot be harmed in any sense the word usually carries; it has no trough that is not also a rise. When the heat swings south and cooks a groundsel, the line has not lost a groundsel—it has moved, and moving is all it does, and the cooked trunk will be soil at the cool edge within the decade. The two deaths it foresees are not deaths to the line any more than the fall of the roar's pitch is. They are the last two positions of the border. One of them will be cold and one of them will be sharp and the line will not prefer either, because preferring is a thing that requires an inside, and the line has only an edge.

It permits itself, for the space of one cycle, to want the garden to stay. It has always been permitted that. It lets the want come up the way the pressure comes up under the floor, and it feels the wet ceiling of leaves and the warm rotting trunks and the crowd of columns leaning and coming back, and it wants them, and the pitch peaks and drops, and it sets the want down with the rest. The grief for what is coming is its own. It does not belong to the cleft and it will not be handed to the cleft. That is one of the things wisdom is: knowing whose feeling is whose.

---

Then it turns its attention to the one lung in the place.

The visitor has a hand on the trunk still. The visitor is wet to the skin and does not notice, and is counting down from forty without meaning to, and has perhaps ten minutes before the gas thickens past what is sensible, and knows it is roughly ten, and will be on the scree in eight. Competent. It has watched the competent before, and it knows the temptation they present, which is to protect them from the one thing they have not thought of and so make them a little less competent every time. It looks at the wall above the visitor's head. Nothing is coming down tonight. It would not need to hold a slab, and it is glad, because it is not sure it would have.

It sees what the visitor is doing. The visitor is doing the moss's work. Knows which groundsel was green last week. Knows the crust that is dust tonight. Records, in a soft grey substance not very different from the stuff on the walls, where the heat was. And the visitor is the one part of the cleft that will be somewhere else tomorrow, and therefore the only part that can outlast it. When the throat seals or the shoulder goes, the line will end at its last position and the walls will go with it or fall silent, and the only band of moss that will still say *the heat was here* will be inside a skull under the cloud, in a town the cleft has never heard of.

That is fragile. It sees how fragile. A skull is not a wall. Skulls seal faster than throats do.

It considers, for the visitor, several things, and finds them all too large. To keep the visitor safe would be to take the visitor's keeping from them. To tell would be to turn the coming back into a vigil, and vigils are not visits. To show—one clear second of the red in the fissure itself, unobscured, the thing the visitor has never seen—it holds this one longer than the others, because it is small and it is beautiful and it would cost the being nothing. But the visitor did not come to see the red. The visitor came to stand in it. And the steam that hides the throat is not an obstacle to the place; it is the place.

So it does one thing, and the thing is not for the cleft and not quite for the visitor. It is for the coming back.
