It arrives the way everything arrives here: from above, and cold, and looking for somewhere to settle.

It has been told there is a being in this place, and it looks for one. This takes longer than it expected. It tries the collared thing in the arch first, because that is the oldest and has the most claim, and finds a creature that has never been more than a hundred metres from its own beginning and does not know there is anything to be the being of. It tries the birds and finds ten thousand of them and no one bird. It tries the builders and finds that the dams are older than any builder and that the oldest lodge answers no question it puts. It tries the moss and finds that the moss is a way of doing things rather than a thing. It tries the river, which is only passing through.

In the end it stops trying to find the one and lets itself see what is actually in front of it, which is the fact the description kept circling: there is no room between things. The being it was sent to attend is that. Not the wall, not the water, not any of the people, but the tightness of them — the way the bird's mud is the river's silt is the builder's work is the meadow's soil is the grazer's food is the heron's stillness is the fledgling's fall. A being that is a fit. It has no centre to speak to and no edge to hold and it cannot be addressed. The power understands, at this point, that whatever it is going to do will have to be done to the conditions and not to anyone, because there is no one. There is only everyone, pressed together so hard they have become a shape.

---

Then it does what benevolence does, which is hurt.

It watches a fledgling come out of a cell on the third tier too early, on the wrong day, in a wind that has got down past the rim, and fall through the fog onto the mud at the pool edge and sit there with its wings half open, and it watches the heron that has been standing in the reeds for an hour move for the first time in that hour. It could put the fledgling back. It knows exactly where the cell is. It knows, also, that the heron has two of its own on the wide ledge in the ninth district, and that a heron that goes hungry at the pools goes to the galleries, and that the birds of the third tier do not know they are protected and are. It does not put the fledgling back. It stays for the whole of it, because that is the least a power can do and the most it should.

It goes to the arch at night and drifts with the collared one into the first milky pool and back, and feels, along with it, how the roof it lives under is thinner than it was, and is being eaten from beneath in exactly the way the rest of the roof was eaten before it came down. Someday the arch will go. There will be no cave at all then, only a trench that is a little longer, and the collared ones will be in the light, and the light will finish them in an afternoon. The power could hold the arch. It could stiffen one bedding plane and buy the collared ones a hundred thousand years. It thinks about this for a long time, in the dark, under the drip. It considers that holding the arch is the same act as holding the roof would have been a few grandparents ago, and that if the roof had been held there would be nothing here to attend at all — no city, no stairs of water, no grey grazers, only a river in the dark and the pale things eating what it brought. The trench is what the collared ones' world became. It does not hold the arch. It decides that when the arch falls, it will be there, and that this is not nothing, though it is nearly nothing.

It considers the birds' silence and finds it is not a wound. It considers the cold and finds the cold is the place. It considers the falls — the blocks that come loose and take a district — and finds that the birds go to the new scars the way the moss does, within days, and that a wall which never fell would be a wall that was finished, and a finished wall here is a dead one. It considers the intermediate fish, the ones between white and dust that the herons take most of, and finds that it is looking at a decision being made and that the only way to protect the fish is to prevent the decision. It considers the small thief with hands, one cell in five hundred, and lets it be, as the city lets it be.

Every one of these it sets down carefully, in the wet, and each one costs something, and it pays.

---

When it has gone through all of them it does the thing a wise power does that a merely kind one would not: it turns around.

It has been looking into the trench. Now it looks out of it — up the raw white walls to the rim, and past the rim onto the plain, thin grass over pale rock, wind, nothing to stop the eye. The trench can do a great deal for itself. It can rebuild a district in a season and a dam in a week and it is turning its own dead to stone. What it cannot do is anything at all about where its water comes from. The seepage that weeps down the walls has walked through the plain for years before it arrives. The river that appears out of the arch has been a river somewhere else first. The fog is made of the plain's weather. Everything the place is, is a gift from a country it cannot see and could not repair if it saw it. A dry decade up there and the seams stop weeping and the moss goes brown from the top down and the calcite stops and the mud will not take. Cattle at the rim springs. A ditch cut across the bedding somewhere upstream to drain a field. A road. None of these would sound like anything, from inside the fog, until the drip slowed.

This is the flank. The power has found the one thing it can stand in front of without standing on anyone. It takes up a position it thinks of, without much pride, as the rim — the place the fox patrols, the place the hawk drifts and looks down and does not go in. It sets itself to keep the plain a plain. It will see to it that the rain that falls on that pale rock is not turned aside before it can go down; that whoever comes to the edge to drink where the seepage rises through the turf is let drink and is not let stay; that the river above the arch runs in its own bed. It will do all of this out of sight and out of hearing of the trench, in the sun, in the wind, on ground that nobody in the fog has ever stood on, and nothing below will know that anything has been done, and that is the point of it. If it is felt inside, it has done it wrong. A power that is felt here is a block coming down.

---

It goes down once more before it takes up the watch. It allows itself this.

It goes to the dam two-thirds of the way down, the old one with the true trees on it, whose lodge nothing has been seen to leave, and it looks in. It is a power; it can look. What it finds there it keeps, and keeps in the manner of the place, which is to say it seals it over and lets the next thing be built on top. It comes out of the pool and up the bank and does not leave a track, and later it is not certain this was the right courtesy — perhaps a track would have been the honest thing — but it is done.

Then it goes along the lower ledges of the fourth district, where the seepage runs in three trained runnels between the levees, and it comes on a first-year bird that has just put a beakful of pale pool-mud squarely across the middle runnel. The water is already backing. Two cells on the tier below will flood by evening. There is an older bird four cells along, but it is asleep with its head in its neck, and it is late in the day, and the thing the power is watching is the beginning of a small ordinary loss.

It takes the mud off the runnel and puts it an inch to one side, where it should have gone. The water goes on down.

The first-year bird does not see this, or sees it and takes it for what happens all day long throughout the season. Nobody remembers it. It is not written anywhere in the wall. And the power understands, standing on the ledge with the fog beading on it, that it has just done the only kind of thing this place accepts from anyone — the correction, unmarked, the size of a beakful, added to the ten thousand others — and that the city did not need it, had a bird four cells along who would have woken and done it, and was in no way saved. It was only kept, by one more hand, for one more hour. That is what the place is made of. It has been allowed to make one grain of it. It finds this is enough and is a little surprised at how much it wanted to be allowed.

---

In the morning it is on the rim.

The fog is standing level in the trench to the brim, white as poured milk, and the sun is on the snapped stone, and a handful of the small dark birds have come up out of the white and are sitting on the lip with their wings held open, drying, the only sun they ever take. The power sits among them. It does not look like anything. The fox comes along the edge and puts a foot over and pulls it back and goes on. The hawk drifts past on the updraft and looks down into the white and its eyes stop working and it does not go in. Far off across the plain the grass moves, and under the grass, in the pale rock, water that fell last winter is walking slowly toward a wall it has never seen.

When the fog begins to sink the birds drop off the edge one after another and fall into it without a sound and are gone. The power stays where it is. It has decided what it is for. It is for the plain, and the rain, and the long slow walk of the water through the stone; for standing where the fox stands and not going in; for being there, someday, when the arch lets go. Below it, in the wet and the cold, without any help and without any sound, a hole in the ground that is still surprised to be open to the sky goes on being repaired by everyone who lives in it, and the power has found that the wisest thing it can do for a being like that is to want nothing from it, and to keep the weather coming.
