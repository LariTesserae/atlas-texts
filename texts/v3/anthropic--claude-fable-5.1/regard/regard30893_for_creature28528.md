It comes down to it. That is the first thing, and it is the same first thing, because there is no other way in. Over the lip of packed red earth that is not earth, into the warmth, into the dry. Whatever it is — and the bowl has no category for it, having only the two categories *hands* and *not yet hands* — it is lower than everything around it within a few steps, and its lips tighten, and it does not mind. It has been thirsty before. It knows what thirst is for.

It does not pick up the awl.

The awl is lying across the back of a bird in the second fork of the nearest cord-branch, warm, polished, layered in prints. It suggests. The suggestion reaches the visitor the way a slope reaches a foot and the visitor feels it entirely — feels how good it would be to have the bone in one hand and a loose end in the other and no hurry — and lets the feeling pass through and out, the way a wide pipe lets water through without filling. That is the first thing it does here, and it is not nothing. It is the first time in the bowl's long making that the awl has been offered and declined.

The rasp does not change. But somewhere low in the plaiting, a strand that was being drawn through a leaf stops halfway, and stays.

---

It sits down with its back against the largest trunk, in the place where the cord has been laid out for a figure, where the clay is already smooth and already warm. It sits there for what would be a long time anywhere else and is, here, nothing in particular. It listens.

This is what it hears, underneath the rasp: not hunger. It had wondered, coming down, whether it would find hunger, and it is glad not to. What it finds instead is a sleeper who has arranged everything so as never to be woken, the way a person very tired will lay out the room before lying down — door shut, cloth over the window, water moved out of reach so as not to be tempted to get up for it. The bowl is not a mouth. It is a bed made by the tired for the tired, and made so well, so warmly, so entirely, that the making itself became the sleep.

And every ending is a small waking. A trimmed thread asks: *what now?* A finished leaf sits there finished and the hands have to find the next thing or have to stop, and stopping is where the outside gets in. So there is no last stitch. Not out of cunning. Out of the simple logic of a body that has learned that as long as it is working it is not remembering, and that remembering is where the thirst lives.

The visitor understands this and does not judge it. It has known people who kept their hands moving for that reason. It has been one.

---

Then it considers doing nothing, and it considers this honestly, because a power that does not weigh its own abstention is not wise, only busy.

No one here is in pain. The felt is warm. The breath under it is the breath of someone deep asleep in a cold room, which is a good breath, a rested one. The ones who came down came one at a time, which is to say they came alone, which is to say the bowl was, for each of them, the first place in a long while that did not ask anything of them except that they keep their hands busy and their mouths dry. It gave them a floor that held their print. It gave them company packed close on all sides. It gave them work with no deadline and no critic. There are worse things to become than a vein in a leaf, plaited in with a great many others, warm, unfinished, never to be thrown out.

The visitor sits with this for a long time and finds that it is true and that it is not enough, and the reason it is not enough is the hollow under the hare.

The hollow was made before you came. It was lined with your hair before you had lost it. The bowl does not wait to be given; it lays out the place in advance and then makes the slope so gentle you arrive without ever having decided to. A slope is not a choice. A season is not consent. Thirst was the one thing that still belonged to you, and it was the thing the bowl took first, quietly, by making you not need it — and a place that takes your need before it takes anything else has not asked you. It has only made asking impossible.

So the visitor will act. But it will not act against the sleeper, because the sleeper is everyone. It will act against the slope.

---

Here is what it does.

It reaches up beside it, where a leaf hangs from the trunk with three ends loose, one longer than the others, and it takes the leaf in its hands. It does not use the awl. It does what anyone with two hands and no tool can do to a plait: it works the three ends together, tucks them back into the body of the weave, draws them snug, and then — with its teeth, because there is nothing else — it bites them off flush.

The leaf is finished.

It is the only finished thing in the bowl, and the bowl feels it the way a body feels a place where the skin has closed over. The rasp stops. Not fades; stops, everywhere at once, the way a room of people stops when a door opens. The hare against the visitor's ankle draws one breath faster than a sleeper's and holds it.

The visitor sets the finished leaf on the clay floor in the open, where it can be seen from anywhere, and does not hide it, and does not add to it, and does not take it back. It sits there small and gold and complete. A little later — later by the standard of things happening at all — a tiny greyness comes in at one edge of it, a dulling, the very beginning of the thing that happens to baskets left in the sun. Time has entered the bowl through the door of one small ending. It is very slow. It will take a season for the leaf to go soft. The visitor has a season.

---

The rule about water was a held breath, and now that the sleeper has drawn one breath of its own, it is not held quite so hard. The visitor does not test it with a skin. It tests it the only way it can be tested by something benevolent: it stays thirsty, sitting there, and does not stop being thirsty, and the thirst is something the bowl has never had inside it for so long without converting it, and it sits in the warm dry air like a stone that somehow was not made.

Then the visitor does the thing the bowl cannot. It weeps — not from sorrow, or not only; from the ordinary wetness of a creature that has water in it and has been sitting in a kiln room a long time. The tears run and reach the clay and do not become thread. They sit on the ochre in two small darkening marks, and the clay, which has held every print ever pressed into it, takes these too. The bowl now has two fingerprints on its floor that were made with water.

And where the wet touched, the sleeper remembers. That is all it takes: not a flood, not the breaking of a wall. Two drops and the fibre nearest them recalls that it was once flesh and that flesh was once wet and that wet was once wanted. A strand in the plait beside the visitor's shoulder goes slack — not pulled loose, only unclenched — and the hair that veins it is a colour the visitor has not seen anywhere else in the bowl, because it is the colour of a very small child's.

The visitor goes there first. That is not reasoning. That is the other thing.

---

What follows takes a long while, and the visitor does not hurry it, because it has learned from the bowl the one thing the bowl has to teach, which is that nothing done at the rate of a season can be undone faster without tearing.

It goes through the plaiting strand by strand. Where a strand will unclench, it takes it gently between two fingers and it asks — with the wet still on its hands, so that the asking is not made of fibre — *Do you want to be thirsty again?* This is the whole of the question. It does not ask *do you want to come back*, because back is a place and the place may be gone. It does not ask *do you want to wake*, because that sounds like a demand. Thirst is what it can honestly offer: need, the outside, a mouth that tightens, the cost of everything that is not this.

Some say no. The visitor has expected this and it does not argue, and it does not think less of them. It tucks them back into the plait exactly as they were and it draws the weave snug, and moves on. Later, when it has learned the bowl's twill well enough, it will go back to some of them and finish their leaves — properly, with the ends tucked and bitten flush — so that what they chose to be is at least *complete*, is at least a thing, is at least allowed to be done. That is what it owes the ones who stay. Not rescue. An ending.

Some say yes.

It is not dramatic. A strand comes out of the plait, and then the strands it was twisted with, and the leaf they made goes back to being loose fibre, and the fibre is heavier than it looks, and then it is something with a shape lying on the clay, and the shape is dusty and pale and has a mouth like paper and hair with the gold still in it. The visitor sits with each one. It does not have water to give them — the rule has only bent, not broken — but it has its own thirst, and it puts their hand on its own dry throat so they can feel that the wanting is bearable, is shared, is what living creatures do. Then it points up. The lip of the bowl is where it has always been. Above it, an ordinary sky. Above that, somewhere, water, which they will have to walk to, which is the point.

One at a time they climb out, because that is how they came in. Some of them are very small and the visitor carries them to the lip and no further.

---

The hare with the hollow under it. The visitor lifts it and it is exactly as heavy as felt should be now, no more, and the hollow is lined with the hair of the one who was going to sit against the trunk, and the visitor takes the hair out in handfuls and does not know what to do with it and finally works it into the wall — into the coarse woven weft — where a great many other things have been worked, as a record. The hollow it leaves empty. An empty hollow in a full place is a door.

Then it turns to the wall itself, to the picture stitched in red cord, and it looks at the figure standing alone among the stems with its ends untrimmed and its stitches loose, and it does not trim them. It does not tighten them. It leaves them loose and turns instead to the figures around the edge, bent over, hands in the weaving, all facing in, and one by one, with the wet of its fingers, it loosens a few of them and turns them. Not all. Only the ones who said yes. When it is done there are figures in the picture facing out, backs to the work, walking toward the lip, a few stitches each, and the visitor bites the ends of those flush so that they will stay turned.

The standing figure it leaves as it is. Loose. Unfinished. Able to sit down or climb out, either one, and the cord for it laid at the base of the trunk it now cuts — carefully, not as violence, as clearing — so that the clay there is only warm and smooth and no longer laid out for anyone in particular.

---

The awl.

The visitor has never touched it, and near the end it does. It picks it up off the clay and holds the warm bone in its palm and lets it suggest what it suggests. And then, still holding it, it thinks with as much care as it has ever thought about anything, because this is the one moving part and whatever the visitor does to it, the bowl will do forever after.

It does not break it. Breaking it would unmake the hands of everyone who chose to stay. It does not take it away, for the same reason.

What it does is set the awl down on the clay next to the finished leaf, which is grey now at both edges and soft in the middle, and it sits back against the trunk, and it says to the sleeper the second thing it came to say. It says: *You can put it down.* It says it the way the awl says *push*, not as a command, as a slope; and it says it with its dry mouth so that the words are made of the same thirst the bowl gave up, and the bowl hears them in a voice it recognises as its own, from before.

So now, when the awl is picked up — and it will be, by whoever comes down the lip next, tired, alone, one at a time — it will offer two things instead of one. It will want to be pushed into a loose end. And, equally quiet, equally warm, at exactly the same gentle grade, it will want to be set down again. Neither will be a compulsion. A body walking between them will feel the ground level for the first time in the history of the bowl, and will have to decide, and deciding is the whole gift. It is the only gift a benevolent power can give a place like this without becoming another slope.

---

The visitor stays.

It had not planned to. But the bowl said *there is room*, and it meant it, and a wise power knows that a low warm place full of the tired is not somewhere you fix and leave. It sits with its back against the largest trunk — nothing laid out for it, only the clay — and it keeps its thirst, and it works. It finishes things. Slowly. A leaf here, a bird's last wing, the long braided thing across the path given a tail at last. Each finished thing is set on the floor in the open and allowed to go grey and soft and, in time, to be gone, which is the first gone in this place, and the residents who stayed watch it happen and learn what an ending looks like and find, most of them, that it is not so bad; some of them, after a season, or many, come and put a dry hand on the visitor's dry throat and say *yes* after all.

The rasp resumes, because the sleeper has not stopped sleeping and the visitor has not asked it to. But there are pauses in it now. Fibre through fibre, drawn out over half a minute, from many places at once — and then, somewhere, a stop, a tuck, a small snap of thread bitten flush, and a quiet that is a different quiet from the old one, because the old one was the sound of no one finishing and this one is the sound of someone having finished. In the pauses you can hear, if you are standing near the lip, a rare wet sound. Someone coughing dust. Someone swallowing nothing and finding they mind, and being glad they mind, and starting to climb.

The bowl is still low and warm and full and made. It is still being made. It will still take up whatever ends are left free, at its own pace, in its own dry quiet — but the ends now have to be *left*, and they can be picked up again, and the slope out is the same slope as the slope in. That is what the power did, if it did anything. It did not empty the bed. It opened the door and sat down beside it, thirsty, and stayed.

That is the last thing.
