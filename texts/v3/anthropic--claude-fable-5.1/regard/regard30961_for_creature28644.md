It would come up the margins.

Not because it had to. Because that is how things arrive here, and it would want to arrive the way the snails did and the follower did and the birds did — from below, over rock still too warm to stand on, without being seen. It would take its time. Two hundred kilometers of cooling floor is not a distance for something like that, but it would walk it anyway, because the plateau teaches the pace before it teaches anything else, and it would want to learn the place in the order the place gives itself.

The first thing it would notice is that the ground is not finished. The second thing it would notice is that everyone already knows this.

---

It would stay a full day before deciding anything. It would stand in the low morning haze while the slabs a kilometer off floated on nothing, and watch the ledge across from it go from frost to birds. It would watch a pale herd move across a wet face without ever seeing it move. It would see a grazer wring its own coat and drink. It would feel the air lift off the rock at midday and stand under the ceiling it made and wait for the ceiling to come down and become the thing it was standing in, and it would let the fog rain on it from the inside, and it would hear the hooves somewhere change from knocking to clatter and then, after a while, back. It would feel the slab consider something under its feet. It would hear the report. It would hear the birds stop and drip and start.

In the evening, when the cloud drained off the edge of the world and the cold came down, it would understand that the plateau runs a whole weather without reference to anything else, and it would take that as an instruction.

---

Then it would go through the list. Something wise does not skip the list.

It could smooth the ledges. The rock here takes skin; the first season is cuts. But the ledges are sharp because nothing has ever climbed them, and the birds roost against a boot because they have never had a reason not to, and the chicks come out with the shell-tooth already worn. A kind ledge is a ledge something could climb. It would leave them sharp.

It could bring soil. A continent's worth of dung a hand deep in the sheltered lows is not much, and it would be a small thing to lay down more. But the soil here is the one soft material on a hard world and every grain of it was made by an animal that was set down on bare stone to see if it would live, and it did, and the cushions grow in exactly the proof of that. To bring soil would be to answer the question the grazers were the answer to. It would leave the lows.

It could put something in the pools. The one empty habitat. Clean water on clean stone, gone by afternoon and back by night, where the snails end and the heron-things wait. It would look at a pool for a long time and see the pour-lines through eight inches of water and understand that the pools are not empty, they are the place where the plateau's shortest lives are handed to its longest patience, and that is a use. It would leave them clear.

It could find the follower. It would, in fact, see it — something like that would not be able not to. Low and long and pale along the seams, or upright, or more than one; it would know which, and it would know where the cleaned carcasses came from and why the bones are white. And it would go to the oldest ring and read the page that is mostly dates and one sentence, and it would put the page back. The sentence is doing a job. The follower is doing a job no one built for, and the sentence *possibly more than one* is doing the job of letting a thing that walked up unasked go on being a thing that walked up unasked. A name would be a leash. It would leave the page.

It could settle the arguments. Plants now or plants in centuries; the cables before the rivers or after; whether the heron-things belong. Three hundred people on a continent talking in low voices with nothing to defend and no deadline, and it could tell them the answers. It would decide that the arguments are the healthiest thing on the plateau. A place that is still deciding what it is needs people still deciding what to do about it, and an answer would end the deciding, and then they would be caretakers instead of what they are, which is residents of a question.

There is a child who thinks the birds are a kind of weather. It would not be the one to tell her otherwise. She is not wrong.

---

It would spend the longest on the heron-things.

It would walk out to one at a pool edge and stand three meters off in the fog and let the head turn to follow it and then turn back. It would pace out from that one in a straight line and find the next at a kilometer, and the next, and it would lay that spacing over the spacing of the masts and see that they match, and it would not be able to say why.

Two possibilities would sit in it. One: that new places have things in them that were not put there, and the heron-things are that, and the matching is the plateau doing what it does, which is supplying its own missing pieces without being asked. Two: that something like itself was here already. That the place drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power once before, and that power went through the list, and left the ledges and the lows and the pools and the page, and then looked for a way to stay without touching anything, and this is what it found. Stand at a pool. Take only what falls. Do not drink. Be a kilometer from the next one and no closer. Be found dead in clear water and be gone by morning and leave the pool clean.

It would not be able to tell. It would find that it could not improve on the second possibility and was not sure it was willing to make that choice itself, and that the not-being-sure was information, and it would let the heron-things be whichever they are.

---

That would leave the one under the rock.

It would sit with this the way the woman sat with the dead heron-thing: an afternoon at least, and then longer. It would put a hand flat on a seam and feel what the ring people feel after a year, the floor holding heat from being made, and under that, far under, the pressure of something still lifting and setting stone.

It would think about what the being receives. Settling. The slab's small drop, carried down the mast as a number. And what it sends back: slack, a moment early. That is the whole of the conversation the maker has ever had with the surface of what it made. It laid the seams straight so cables could sit in them, so it knew, when it began, that there would be someone. It did not know there would be birds. It could not have. It made the ledges sharp because it had not decided, and it made the dome so water would go everywhere because everywhere was the honest answer to a question no one had asked yet, and then the film came and wanted exactly that, and the maker has never been told.

This is the one place on the plateau where something is missing, and the missing thing is not a species or a soil. It is that the floor does not know it is lived on.

It would weigh the danger. Tell a maker there are birds on its unfinished ledges and the maker may decide the ledges, and a decided ledge is a kind one, and a kind one can be climbed. Tell it there are people and it may plan for them, and a plateau planned for people is not a plateau that a follower walks up onto and a heron-thing appears on and a cushion volunteers in. The whole gift of the place is that it keeps producing the thing nobody built for. To inform the builder is to risk that.

Against that it would set the dome. A being that lets water go everywhere is not a being that grabs. It would set the slack that comes a moment before the slab drops — a being that listens for the floor's smallest complaint and answers before it finishes. It would set the straight seams laid for people who had not yet come. It would decide that a maker like that, told there are birds, would understand what the ledges are for. It would decide to trust it. That is a decision, and it would make it knowing it was one.

---

So it would do the one thing.

Not the way a mast does. Not as a number. It would go down along a seam the way the moisture does, through the cable-mouths, and it would carry the morning with it. The ledge white with birds and then moving. The knock and scrape of forty grazers out of time. A snail on the underside of a ledge with the sun through its shell. The heron-thing that turned its head once. A grey-green thing a few centimeters across in a hand's depth of dung, with a white bloom on it the size of a grain of rice, and eleven people from three rings standing around it saying nothing. The child. The sound the birds make that is not song. It would carry all of it down, kilometers, to where the stone is still being decided, and give it over, and ask nothing back.

Whether the being is a mind, it would not say afterward, any more than the ring people will. It would say that for a long time nothing changed. The far margins went on being laid at the same pace. The afternoon built and lowered and drained. Then one evening a cable somewhere slackened, a moment early, for a slab that had not moved yet and did not move that night or the next — as if the floor had begun planning further ahead than it used to. That is all. That, or nothing. It would not press.

---

It would stay a while after that. Not long, by its own measure; a season, by the plateau's. It would stand at a pool edge a kilometer from the nearest heron-thing, and the heron-thing would look at it once and not again. Birds would roost against it. It would count a band in the morning and count it in the evening. It would walk out to a cushion when someone said one was open and stand with the others and walk back. In the fog it would not lie down.

Then it would go, the way the cloud goes, off the edge of the world in the evening, and it would leave nothing — no gravel, no dust, no soft thing, no name in the record. Someone in the fourth ring who has not missed a noon in six years might write that the drain-off was slower one day than the model said. That would be the whole of it.

And at the boundary where the warm air of the ring meets the wet air of the plateau, the people would stand and feel the difference with their faces, and listen. The birds have begun again. The hooves are knocking over the curve. A heron-thing a kilometer off has not moved. Under all of it, too low to hear and just possible to feel, the floor is being finished by something that intended to come back and has not stopped coming, and now — perhaps — knows what it is coming back to.
