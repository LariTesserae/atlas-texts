**Preparing for the Room Beneath the Plain**

Begin by accepting the shape of the visit. You will cross a hot, bright, hard country to reach a hole, go down a stair that is half tree, and arrive in a warm, wet, dim room full of quiet lives. Then you will have to come back up the same way. Everything below follows from that shape: the surface wants to dry you out, the room wants to keep you warm and wet, and the stair is the only thread between the two. Prepare for all three, and prepare most seriously for the one that looks least dangerous, which is the room.

---

**The surface**

The plain is laterite — iron-rich, brick-hard, cracked, and it throws the sun back up at you. Treat the approach as desert travel even if the map says otherwise. Wide-brimmed hat, long light sleeves, sunglasses, more water than seems reasonable. The thorn scrub will go through ordinary trousers; wear something tightly woven. The ground rings when kicked, which means it is also unforgiving to fall on and radiates heat well into the evening.

The hole is not large — the width of a small house — and on a plain of glare it will be hard to spot until you are almost at it. Look for the ferns. A collar of impossible green in that country is your landmark, and if you have any way of marking the spot from a distance (a cairn on the rim, a flag on a stick, a bearing from something taller), do it. You will be coming back up into that glare with your eyes adjusted to dimness and your body soaked, and you will want to know exactly which way is out.

Do not walk right up to the rim. The ferns hide the edge, and the ground under them is wetted by the rising air and may be softer than the plain around it. Approach from the east, where the stair begins. Sit down before you look over.

---

**The descent**

Four times the height of a person is roughly seven meters. That is high enough to kill you if you fall, and the shaft is not a clean drop but a chimney into a room with a stone floor.

The stair was cut a long time ago into the eastern wall. It is shallow, uneven, worn into a slick trough, missing in places, and partway down it stops being stone and becomes the living root of a fig. The wood is warm. It is also wet — everything is wet — and cambium under a film of water is one of the more treacherous surfaces you will ever put a boot on.

Bring rope. Not because you are a climber but because you are not. Anchor to something on the surface that is not a fern, that is not the crumbling laterite lip — a boulder, a deep stake, the trunk of a scrub tree well back from the edge — and fix a line down the shaft so you have something to hold going down and, more importantly, coming up. Coming up tired, wet, in darkness, on roots, is the hardest part of the whole visit and the part most people will underestimate at the top when the stair looks like a stair.

Wear boots with soft, grippy soles rather than hard lugs; you are stepping on worn stone and wet wood, not scree. Gloves help on the roots. Carry your pack so that it does not swing. Go slowly, test each step, and remember that some treads are gone and the roots that replaced them have not been inspected by anyone.

One more thing about the stair: it is a tree. It is alive, and it is the tree that fills the chamber. Every boot that scrapes it wounds it a little. Step where the wood is already worn smooth rather than on bark.

---

**The air, which is the real danger**

The room is warm. Not hot — the description is precise — but the heat of a body. The air is saturated. Every surface sweats. This is a pleasant thing to read and a physiologically serious thing to stand in.

Your body cools itself by evaporating sweat. In air that is already at body temperature and holding all the water it can hold, sweat does not evaporate. It runs off you and does nothing. You will be sweating heavily within minutes of arriving and getting no benefit from it, and your core temperature will begin, slowly, to climb. This does not feel like danger. It feels like languor. It feels like the place is asking you to slow down, and the place is — but part of that slowing is the first stage of heat exhaustion, and you must be able to tell the difference.

So:

Move slowly from the start, not just because the place deserves it but because every bit of exertion is heat you cannot shed. Sit down often. The dry eastern side, under the oculus, is where the cooler air from the shaft arrives; that is your recovery station. The northern wall is the warmest and should be visited briefly.

Drink constantly. Bring more water than you brought for the surface. Bring salt, or electrolyte tablets, because you will be losing both in quantity. Do not drink from the cups on the floor or the seeps or the basin, however clear; the water is warm, has been through limestone and bat guano and a sulfurous fissure, and is full of small living things that belong to it and not to you. If you must, filter and treat it, and understand that a filter will not remove dissolved sulfur compounds.

Know the signs: headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion, a strange irritability, a pulse that stays high when you rest, and — the dangerous one — a point where you stop sweating. If you feel any of them, go to the foot of the stair, sit in the current of surface air, drink, wet your neck and wrists (wetting does nothing for evaporation here but the water from the shaft side is marginally cooler), and if it does not pass, climb out. Do not wait for it to get worse before deciding; on the stair, worse is fatal.

Set a time limit before you go down. Two hours the first time. Plan to leave while you still feel fine.

The sulfur smell you notice at the surface is mostly the bats, but somewhere under the floor there is a fissure, and fissures that warm rock breathe more than heat. The room has an open chimney and so is probably well ventilated in the ordinary way, but the western end lowers, narrows, floods, and goes dark, and low wet dead-ends in warm limestone are exactly where carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulfide pool. Do not go into the flooded passages. Do not put your face down near the water at the western pools. If you have a gas meter, carry it and trust it over your nose — hydrogen sulfide deadens your sense of smell at exactly the concentrations where it starts to matter. If you notice a candle-flame guttering low, or a headache that came on fast near the pools, walk east.

And the guano. A hill of it, centuries deep, at the western end, under tens of thousands of bats. Bat guano in warm wet caves grows a fungus whose spores, breathed in, cause a lung disease that in a bad case can hospitalize you weeks after you have gone home. Do not walk on the hill. Do not disturb it. Do not stand under the bats. Wear a mask that actually filters fine particles — a proper respirator, not a cloth — whenever you are in the western half, and certainly if the bats are stirring. The mask will be miserable in that heat. Wear it anyway.

---

**Light**

Your eyes will take twenty minutes to settle into the dimness and one second of white torchlight to undo it. Bring a headlamp with a red mode and use the red almost exclusively. Use white only for the stair, and only when you must.

There is another reason. The far wall glows — a filamentous fungal mat that gives back a faint green light of its own, enough to see the outline of your hand — and you will never see it if you keep a lamp on. Neither will you see the salamanders moving against it, or the calcite figure in the niche holding the day's last light. Half of what this room has to show you is visible only in its own light. Come prepared to spend a long time seeing nothing, and then to start seeing.

If you photograph, do it without flash. Flash will ruin what you are photographing and will also start the bats.

---

**The inhabitants, and how to be among them**

You are an ordinary human and this is a world not your own. Assume every living thing here has chemistry you have not met. Do not handle, do not taste, do not let anything touch your mouth or eyes.

The snails are everywhere and will be on you within minutes if you sit still. Let them. Brush them off gently with a gloved hand or a leaf, not your bare fingers. Snails in warm places often carry parasites in their slime that are harmless to them and grave to you; keep your hands away from your face and wash — with your own water, not the cups — before you eat. Do not eat here at all if you can avoid it.

The millipedes are long, glossy, and slow, and they are not aggressive, but many millipedes defend themselves with a fluid that burns skin and stains it for weeks, and a few produce compounds you very much do not want near your eyes. Admire them. Do not pick them up.

The frogs will be under every leaf and in every cup. Some frogs' skin is toxic to touch. All frogs' skin is fragile to being touched. Leave them.

The wolf spider the size of a hand is real and is watching you from a foot away and you will not see it until it moves. It is not hunting you. It has no interest in something your size, and it will bite only if pressed. Be aware that it exists when you put your hand on a root to steady yourself. Look before you place your palm.

The centipede that hunts through the guano hill is the length of a forearm. Large centipedes bite, and their venom is painful for days and dangerous for the allergic. It is on the hill, in the dark, at the western end, which is another reason not to be there. If you are sitting anywhere in the dark for a long time, check around you with red light every so often. Keep your boots on. Shake out anything you have set down before you pick it up.

The bats will not hurt you, but do not be under them, and do not be in the shaft at dusk. When they leave they come up the chimney in a column so dense it blots the stars, and if you are on the stair at that moment you will be inside a whirlwind of wings and guano and terrified animals on a slick root over a seven-meter drop. Be on the floor or be on the surface at sunset. Not between.

Nothing here calls, warns, or displays. You will not be told when something is near. The room hunts and hides by stillness, and that means your awareness has to be your own; the usual animal alarms that warn a human are absent.

---

**Where to put your feet**

The selaginella carpet is a single spongy iridescent surface you could lie down on. Do not. It has been growing since long before anyone cut the stair and one afternoon of boots will leave a scar that takes decades to close. Walk on bare stone where there is bare stone. Walk on the tops of the fig roots where they are already worn, or along the drip-cups' edges where the floor is rock. The old low wall is a road for snails; leave it to them.

The floor slopes west and is wet everywhere. The cups are shallow but the pools at the western end are not, and the passages beyond them are flooded and unlit and inhabited. Do not enter water past your ankles. You cannot see the bottom, you cannot see what is in it, and the salamanders that live there have had a very long time to learn those passages and you have had none.

Do not touch the rock along the northern wall without testing it with the back of your hand first. In three or four places it is hot enough to burn.

Before you descend, disinfect your boots and anything else that has touched soil or water in another world. There are frogs and salamanders here in numbers you cannot guess at, living in one wet room with nowhere to run. Amphibian diseases travel on boot mud. You could carry in, on one sole, the end of everything in the cups. This is the single most important preparation on this list and the easiest to forget.

---

**The breath**

Twice an hour, roughly, the room fogs. Warm air pushes up from the western passages, meets the cooler air under the oculus, and the fog does not rise or fall but simply appears, all at once, in the middle of the chamber. Then it drips off everything at once, and for half a minute it sounds like rain.

You will be soaked. Not damp — soaked, every time, through everything. Do not bring anything down that must stay dry unless it is sealed in a bag you would trust underwater. Paper is gone. Electronics are gone unless sealed. Cotton will never dry again while you are in the room. Wear a synthetic base layer that is warm when wet, and understand that "warm when wet" is exactly the problem in this heat, so wear as little as safety allows: long sleeves against the thorns going in and coming out, but light, and roll them when you are seated.

When the fog comes, stop. Not just because the place stops — because you can no longer see the floor, and the floor is wet stone sloping toward flooded passages. Stand or sit where you are until it falls. If you were on the stair, hold the rope and do not move. The fog lasts a minute or two. It is one of the most beautiful things you will ever be inside of and it is also a minute or two when you cannot see your feet.

The low wall may mark where the fog begins. Stay east of it if you want a dry seat; go west of it if you want to be breathed on. Choose deliberately.

---

**The old things**

The stair, the wall, the basin, the niche. Someone made these. Someone sat on the rim of the warm basin for a very long time. The place has absorbed them and they are part of it now, and they should be treated the way you treat the moss: as something that was here before you and will be here after, and that your touch can only diminish.

Sit on the basin rim if you like. It was made for sitting. It is the one built thing here that invites a body, and it seems right to accept. But do not disturb the frogs in the water, and do not put your hands in.

Do not climb to the niche. Do not touch the figure. It is calcite over something, dripped for centuries into a seated shape, and it is the brightest thing in the room after the sun and the only thing the snails have not touched. Whatever your beliefs about such objects, the practical truth is that the calcite skin is thin and the oil of a single fingertip will stop it growing there forever and leave a dull print that will never heal. Look at it. Notice how the ferns lean. That is enough.

Take nothing out. Not a shell, not a leaf, not a chip of the wall. Leave nothing in.

---

**On staying past dark**

The description ends at night, with the bats gone and the fungus lighting the far wall and the frogs making their one click each. It is clearly the reason to be there. It is also the most dangerous hour, and you should decide beforehand — not at dusk, when the light is going orange up the trunk of the fig and everything in you wants to stay — whether you are equipped for it.

Staying means: you are in body-temperature saturated air for many more hours, with the heat load that implies. You will climb the stair in full darkness, on wet roots, after being warm and wet and slow for a long time. The room's night shift comes up from the pools and off the hill — salamanders in numbers, the centipede, things with many legs — and moves through a dark you cannot see into. And the surface, when you reach it, will be cold by comparison, and your soaked clothes will begin to pull heat out of you at precisely the moment you are exhausted from the climb.

If you stay: be on the floor, well east of the wall and away from under the roost, before sunset, and stay there until the bats have gone. Have a dry set of clothes sealed in a bag at the top of the shaft, not the bottom. Have your rope fixed and checked while there is still light. Have a companion on the surface with a light, or at the very least have told someone exactly where the hole is and when you intend to come up. Keep drinking. Check around you with red light every quarter hour. Do not sleep on the floor.

Then sit on the warm rim of the basin, and let the room breathe on you in the dark, and listen to it tick.

And climb out before you are too tired to.

---

**What it will do to you**

Prepare, finally, for the slowing.

The room is quiet not because it is empty but because it is full. There is nowhere to run, so nothing calls; everything hunts and hides by stillness; and within an hour you will have slowed your breathing and your feet to the pace of the place without deciding to. This is the gift of it, and you should let it happen. But it is also the heat working on you, and the two feel identical from inside. The discipline you need is not to resist the slowing but to keep, somewhere behind it, one small part of yourself that still counts: how long have I been down, how much have I drunk, when did I last check my feet, how do I feel.

You will not see anything die. You will only look again and find the number smaller. Try not to take this personally. Try also to notice that you are being counted too, by the spider on the root and the centipede on the hill and the salamanders in the pools, and that the room's quiet is the same quiet whether it holds you or not.

Go down from the dry side, as the stair was cut for. Sit where someone sat. Be breathed on. Then take the rope in both hands, and go back up into the glare, and be very careful on the wood.
