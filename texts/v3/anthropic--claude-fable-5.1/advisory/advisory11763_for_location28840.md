**What it is, before you go**

Understand first that this is not a bath in the sense you may be hoping for. It is a piece of hydraulic equipment that happens to be the right temperature for a body, and it has been running for a very short time. Nobody has soaked in it. Nobody has softened it. The plaster is three weeks old, the inflow is at full force, and the room has not yet learned to absorb its own noise. You will not be relaxing. You will be inside something newly made that is louder, brighter, and warmer than a body expects, and your job is to be a competent guest in it for a short while and then climb out.

If you arrive expecting a hot spring, you will spend your first minute fighting the place. Arrive expecting an instrument that has just been struck, and you will do better.

**Arrival**

Do not arrive in the water. Arrive above it, in the house, at some distance from the grid floor, and walk in. There are three reasons. First, if you materialise submerged you will have no bearings: the roar comes from every wall, the light comes from every surface, and you will not know which way is up until your head hits a beam. Second, the tank is only a little more than a man's height deep — arriving with any downward momentum onto a plaster floor is a broken heel. Third, this is somebody's house. The grid floor and the light above it belong to a lit room. The people who built the tank climbed out through the hole and may come back to look down at it. You want to know whether anyone is present before you are wet and cannot hear.

Stand on the beams for a while before you drop. Look down. Note which side the inlet is on (north — you will see the bloom of bubbles) and which side the outlet is on (south, higher, the dark square). Note exactly where the square hole is relative to those two walls, because from underneath, in that light, one gap between beams looks very much like another, and only one of them is large enough to come up through. Tie something to the beam beside the hole and let it hang into the water — a strip of cloth, a cord, anything you can find by feel. Do not rely on your eyes for this. Rely on your hand.

**The body you are bringing**

You need to be a strong, calm swimmer who is comfortable with your head under water and comfortable with very little air above it. The gap between the surface and the beams is a thumb's width, sometimes less. You will be able to get your face into it. You will not be able to lift your head clear. The beams are wet and drip on you. When the inflow pulses — and it does, for stretches, every few breaths — the surface lifts against the underside of the floor and the gap can close for a moment. If that moment finds you with your mouth open, you swallow lime water. If it finds you frightened, you will do worse than that. Practise, before you go, being in a confined air space with water lapping at your face and doing nothing about it. The gap always comes back. The sill on the south wall makes sure of it.

The water is warmer than blood. It is not dangerous in a quarter of an hour but it is dangerous in an hour, and you will not feel it coming. Hot immersion with no cool air to breathe raises your core temperature slowly and steals your judgement first. Set a limit before you enter and keep it. Drink water before you go down; drink more when you come up. Do not go in on an empty stomach and do not go in having drunk anything stronger than water. If you feel your heartbeat in your ears above the roar, or the wheel seems to be turning faster than it was, come up and sit on the beams until it stops.

Tie back your hair. The outlet pipe has a mouth you could fit your hand into and it sucks. It will not take you, but it will take a rope of hair or a trailing sleeve or a finger you put there out of curiosity, and it is above head height on the south wall in the part of the room where the water is sinking toward it. Stay off the outlet. Stay off the inlet too — the water arriving is hotter than the water it arrives into, and the pipe mouth is low, where your feet go.

Take off anything metal. Rings and buckles scratch soft plaster, and the plaster here is still soft enough to take a fingernail. Wear as little cloth as you can. Whatever you wear will come out grey with lime and heavier than it went in, and the current will pull at it.

**What to bring and what not to**

Goggles are the one real decision. Without them your eyes will sting — the water is three weeks off fresh lime and it tastes of it, which means it is well above the pH your eyes want — and you will see the room the way the description sees it, as a moving white with no edges you can rest on. With them you will see the trowel-crescents, the finger-marks in the corner, the fur of crust on the sill, the threads of green. I would bring them and take them off for one circuit of the wheel so you know what the place does to an unprotected eye. A nose clip is a smaller version of the same choice; lime water in the sinuses is unpleasant for an hour afterward.

Bring a towel and fresh drinking water and leave them above on the beams, away from the hole so nothing gets kicked in. Bring clean water to rinse your eyes. There is nothing to rinse with down there — the water is clean, but it is clean the way a mixing bowl is clean, and it dries on skin to a fine chalk.

Bring nothing else. Leave the bag in another room. Do not bring anything living — not a plant cutting, not a shell, not the mud on a sandal. Nothing has arrived in this room yet that anyone did not carry in, except one green line, and you should not be the second exception. Do not bring anything to wash with. Soap in this water would be an insult to the plaster and it would mark the sill.

**Conduct below**

Go through the hole feet first, straight, arms up. You will touch bottom. Stand there for a breath with the water over your head and let the sound arrive — it is a single hard voice from every direction and your body will want to locate it and cannot. Let it fail. Look at the grid of sun on your feet.

Then push off and let the wheel take you. Do not swim against it. It rises on the north wall and sinks on the south and it takes about a held breath to go round; if you fight it you will work hard, use air, and see nothing. Ride it once with your hands tucked. Ride it again looking at the walls.

Do not touch the plaster with your nails. The palm of a hand is fine — you will want to feel the crescents, and you should — but the surface will take a scratch and keep it for as long as the room stands, and the corners that are crisp now will not be made crisp again. Do not touch the fur on the outlet sill at all. It is thinner than breath and it is the beginning of the room's own skin. Do not touch the green line on the west wall. It is the only thing here that anyone did not decide, and it will grow perfectly well without you.

In the southeast corner, where the current is weakest, there are tools on the floor: a float, a mallet, a knotted cord sunk with a stone. Look at them. Do not step on the mallet's clay head in bare feet. Do not move them, and do not take the cord, however much its evenly spaced knots make you want to. Someone measured this room with it and set it down, and whether they forgot it or left it there deliberately is not a question you get to settle by carrying it away.

Come up into the gap for air when you need it, with your face turned toward your hanging marker. Listen there for the separate drops falling from the beams. Look up through the grid at the sky. Then go under again.

**Leaving**

Find your marker by hand. Follow it up. The hole is square and you will fit, but the lip is sharp-edged brick and you will be tired and slick with lime, so go slowly and use your arms, not your knees. Sit on the beams. You will find the house very quiet and very dim, and your skin will begin to feel tight as the water dries on it. Rinse your eyes. Drink. Do not go back down that day.

Take your marker with you. Take everything you brought. Look down once more at the water standing exactly at the sill, and at the small pale flakes turning in the wheel, and at the line on the west wall, which is a little longer than when you arrived.

**What to expect of yourself**

You will come up more tired than the time warrants, and it will not be the swimming. It is the effort of being somewhere that gives your senses nothing to lean on — no shadow, no quiet, no cool — for even a quarter of an hour. That is what the place is right now. If you return in a season the west wall will be soft and green and the roar will have something to muffle against and the sill will have a proper crust and someone will have taken the tools, or not. It will be a bath by then. You are visiting it before it is one, in the short window when it is only what it was made to be, with a single living thread beginning at one seam. Prepare for that: for the noise, the light, the heat, the closeness of the beams, and for the strange feeling of being the largest uninvited thing in a room that has, so far, exactly one other.
