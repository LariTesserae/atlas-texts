**Preparing for the Plain**

Start by understanding what kind of danger this is. It is not a place that will attack you. Nothing there wants anything from you. The danger is the ordinary, grinding, unglamorous kind — cold, wet, noise, footing, current — applied without pause and without anywhere to step out of it. Most places you can visit have a corner you can retreat to. This one has no corners. Prepare as if for a long stand in a cold river, in the dark, inside a running engine, and you will be roughly right.

---

**The cold is the thing that will actually hurt you**

One degree above freezing sounds tolerable. It is not, when the air is half water and everything you touch is wet. Wet cold at this temperature strips heat from a body faster than dry cold twenty degrees lower, and there is no dry surface anywhere to break contact with it. The water comes up through your boots, the fog comes down through your collar, and the condensation forms on you the way it forms on the stones — beading on the underside of your arms, running down inside your sleeves. A person in ordinary rain gear will be shivering within the hour and in real trouble within three.

Do not bring a wetsuit. Bring a drysuit — the immersion kind, with sealed neck and wrists, and wear proper insulating layers under it: wool or synthetic, never cotton. Boots should be insulated, tall, and electrically rated (more on that below). Gloves the same, and bring a second pair sealed in a bag, because the first pair will be soaked from the inside by your own sweat within an hour and there will be nothing to dry them on. A hood. And something for the face, because breathing this air for hours is its own slow damage: a heat-exchange mask of the kind used in polar conditions, or at minimum a thick scarf over the mouth to warm the air before it reaches your lungs. The fog tastes of warm metal. You will not want it in your mouth for long.

Bring hot liquid in an insulated flask. This will be the single most valuable object you carry. Not for hydration — for the heat itself, delivered inside. Drink it early and drink it often. Bring more calories than you think you need, in forms you can eat with gloved hands.

Do not drink the film. It carries current, it is the runoff of ten thousand warm bodies, and there is nowhere it has ever drained to. Bring all your own water and carry it inside your suit so it does not chill.

Understand also that the cold comes up from below. Standing still in a finger of water on stone that has never been warm is how the cold "gets into the ribs" — that is not a figure of speech. Keep moving, gently. Shift your weight. When you must stop, stop near a beast, on its flank, away from the vents.

---

**The heat is the only relief and it is not safe**

The herds are the only warm thing on the plain, and the temptation to lean into that warmth will be strong and physical and correct. A beast's flank is a radiator. Stand a pace off it and you will feel the cold recede from your front while it continues at your back.

But: the vents along the spine breathe hot enough to feel from several paces, which means hot enough to scald at arm's length. Never stand behind or above one. Never reach across a back. The forelimbs rise and come down with a piston's regularity and a piston's indifference; know where they are before you approach, and approach from the side, never the front. The plates slide against one another with real force. Do not put a hand into a seam.

And the warmth brings the small ones. The scuttlers feed on condensation and soft tissue, and you are wet and soft all over. They will not necessarily attack you, but they will investigate — a many-legged thing the size of a large dog running down a flank toward your shoulder faster than you can follow. Carry a staff for this. Not a weapon; a pole, non-conductive, two metres or so, that you can use to fend, to probe the silt ahead of you for depth, to steady yourself, and to test the water for charge. Fibreglass or well-sealed wood. It will be the second most valuable thing you carry.

---

**The water is live**

This is not dramatic most of the time. The blue crawl along the surface is faint and the sparks between plates are small. But you will be standing in a conductive film that carries current along lines, and the small creatures that ride those lines die in a flash where two of them cross. You are taller than they are and more conductive than they are and you have no instinct for where the lines run.

Rules: insulated boots and gloves, always, no exceptions, no matter how warm your hands get. Never touch two beasts at once. Never touch a beast and the water at the same time with bare skin, which you should never have exposed anyway. Watch the surface for the blue crawl and do not stand where two lines of it meet. Carry nothing metal that reaches from your hand to the water — no trekking pole with a steel tip, no chain, no dragging strap. If your tether is a rope, it must be a rope, not a cable.

If you see the crawl brighten around your feet, step sideways, off the line, calmly. Do not run; running in loose silt in a film of water is how you fall.

---

**Do not fall**

The film is a finger deep until it isn't. The silt beneath is loose in places — loose enough that long low things swim through it and come up between the legs of the herd to take what has fallen. If you go down, get up immediately. Do not lie there catching your breath. The scuttlers converge on a falterer before it has stopped moving, and the larger things come after, and the herd closes over the gap within the hour.

The staff is for this too. Probe ahead. Step where the water lies thinnest over the stone. Keep your feet under you and your centre low.

You do not knock. Consider what that means. The herd identifies a faltering member by a stutter in the rhythm — a knocking that drops out. You have never been part of the rhythm at all, so you may be invisible to that logic, or you may register as something already fallen. There is no way to know in advance. Assume the second and act accordingly: do not stand still for long, and do not go down.

---

**The sound**

Bring hearing protection and be careful about what kind. The knocking is loud enough to damage hearing over hours, and the horn-note, when it comes, arrives from everywhere at once at a volume that goes through the chest. Full blocking earmuffs would protect your ears and endanger everything else, because the one warning the plain gives is auditory — a local stutter in the rhythm means something nearby has faltered and the scavengers are coming. You need to hear that.

Use attenuating plugs, the kind that cut the volume without cutting it out, and add muffs you can lift when you need to listen. Expect the sound to stay in your head long after you leave. Expect, for days afterward, to hear the knocking in traffic, in rain on a roof, in your own pulse. This is normal and it passes.

The rhythm entrains. Stand in it long enough and your breathing falls into phase with the hiss, your heart with the knock. People with cardiac conditions or seizure disorders should not go. Everyone else should notice when it happens and deliberately break it — hum something, count aloud, move at your own pace. It is not sinister. It is just what rhythms do to bodies. But it is the first stage of the plain grinding your edges down, and your edges are what will get you home.

---

**Finding your way back**

This is the part that separates this place from an ordinary cold marsh.

Distance does not hold. A herd at the fog-line will not grow as you walk toward it, and when you arrive it may be the herd you left. Individuals repeat: the same beast with the same cracked plate stands ahead, and to the south, and behind. The reflection in the film runs a leg off from the thing above it. There are no landmarks because there are no marks. There is no sun, no shadow, no direction in the stone, and you should not assume a compass will mean anything.

The only reliable thing is a line you can hold in your hand.

Fix a tether at your arrival point — high-visibility, non-conductive, floating rope, and more of it than you think you'll want, on a reel at your hip. Pay it out as you go. When it is time to leave, follow it back *hand over hand*, with your eyes on the rope in your fingers and not on where it seems to lead. It may appear to run in two directions. It may appear to end at a beast, or to pass through one. Do not look up to check. Do not let go to see. The rope in your hand is the one thing on the plain that has not been folded, and if you follow it by touch you will arrive where it is fixed.

At the arrival point, leave a light on a pole — not to see by, the light there is even and sourceless and a lamp will just make a glowing sphere in the fog, but because a point-source in that shell-glow is the only thing your eyes will be able to fix on, and they will want it badly. Make it a colour the plain does not produce. Not blue.

Never walk toward something to see if it gets bigger. It won't, and you will have spent rope.

---

**Time**

Carry a sealed mechanical watch and decide your limit before you arrive. Three hours is generous. Two is sensible. The light will not change and the knocking may or may not slacken, and your body's sense of elapsed time will go the way the eye goes — searching for something to rest on and eventually resting on nothing.

Time thins here the way distance does. You may hear your watch alarm before it rings. You may see a beast fall and then see the scavengers arrive and then see it fall. Trust the face of the watch, not the sound of it. When the hands say go, go, even if you feel that you just arrived, even if something is about to happen. The thing that is about to happen may already have happened and you are watching the sound of it go by.

---

**What you will feel, and what not to do about it**

The plain is not frightening in the ordinary way. It is very old and very busy and entirely indifferent, and the feeling it produces is less fear than a kind of slow abrasion — the sense of being the only thing with an outline in a place that has worn every outline off. You will want to make sense of it. You will want to know what the rods and rounds and cable are *for*, what the herds were before they were this, whether it is a ruin or a machine or an animal. You will not find out, and the looking is a way of leaning into the abrasion. Let it not mean anything. Watch it work. That is enough.

Do not take anything. The extruded scrap is heavy and wet and carries charge, and it has never once, in all the time the stone remembers, left the plain. Do not be the first thing to carry something away. Do not leave anything either — no marker, no cairn, no mark a hand made on purpose. There has never been one. You do not know what the plain does with a first.

Do not lie down on the copper mat. It is the warmest thing there that does not breathe, and it will be very tempting, and it spreads where the steam is thickest, and you are steaming.

Do not go alone. A second person is a second set of edges — someone to break your rhythm when it catches, to count aloud, to hold the other end of the rope. But agree beforehand what you will do if you see a third person who looks like one of you. The answer is: the rope, hand over hand, together, without discussing it.

---

**After**

You will come out soaked to the skin regardless of the suit, because condensation forms inside as readily as out. The dangerous hour for hypothermia is the one *after* you stop moving. Have someone waiting on the other side with dry clothes, warm drink, and a warm room, and get out of the wet things immediately even if you feel fine. You will not feel fine for long.

Expect the ringing. Expect the knocking in the walls at night for a while. Expect that the first time you stand in fog again — ordinary fog, on an ordinary morning — you will find your eye searching for something to rest on, and will feel something in your chest wait for a hiss that does not come.

That passes too. Almost everything the plain does to you passes, because the plain does not carry anything away — not even you. It just keeps working, in the same place, and you were briefly in the place, and then you were not.

---

**Kit, in short**

- Immersion drysuit; wool or synthetic layers beneath; no cotton anywhere
- Tall insulated, electrically rated boots
- Two pairs of insulated gloves, spare sealed dry
- Hood, and a heat-exchange mask or thick scarf for the mouth
- Non-conductive staff, two metres
- Non-conductive floating tether on a hip reel, far more than you expect to use; a marker lamp on a pole at the fixed end
- Attenuating earplugs plus liftable muffs
- Sealed mechanical watch with a hard limit set before arrival
- Hot liquid in a flask, carried inside the suit; high-calorie food eatable in gloves; all your own water
- A companion, and an agreement about the rope
- Someone waiting with dry clothes and heat on the far side

Nothing to record with, unless you must. There is nothing there that a picture would hold, and the taking is one more edge for the plain to work on.
