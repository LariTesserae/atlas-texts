# Preparing for the Plateau

Start with the honest framing: this is not a dangerous place in the way a glacier or a warzone is dangerous. Nothing there will chase you. Nothing will fall on you. The floor is sound, the rails are anchored, the animals do not bite. Most of what follows is preparation for a place that is *indifferent to you in a specific, industrial way*—a place that processes what it's given and does not distinguish sharply between a mountain and a visitor. You want to be a visitor. That takes some deliberate effort.

## Before you go: the approach is a real climb

You reach the plateau by walking up a mountain, and the mountain is ordinary until it isn't. Treat the lower part as an alpine ascent. Boots you trust, layers, a shell, poles if you use them. You will be in cloud for the last stretch, cold and wet, and you will probably be tired when you arrive, which is bad, because arrival is when you most need to pay attention.

Notice the transition. The description says the rock stops "a while" before the cloud clears—hundreds of meters of grating under fog before you know what you're on. When your poles start ringing instead of scraping, stop. Take a breath. Look at your feet. That moment—rock giving way to warm grating—is the only clear threshold you'll get, and it is the threshold you will be looking for on the way out. Fix everything you can about it: what the grating looks like, which way the path was bending, what's to your left. Don't mark it with anything you leave behind (see below). Just look hard.

Then keep going. You want to see it.

## Clothing and hands

The plant is warm; the altitude is cold; the air is wet. You will sweat walking up a gantry and chill standing at a drip. Wear synthetics or wool, nothing cotton, and carry a spare pair of socks in a dry bag, because the decking will soak your feet during a haze phase and you'll want to change when it clears.

Gloves are not optional. Every edge of steel on the plateau is fresh from the mill—unrusted, unworn, sharp enough to take skin. Handrails are cold and beaded with water. Lie on your belly at the edge (you will; everyone does) and the lip of overpoured slab will grate your forearms. Bring a pair of leather or cut-resistant work gloves and put them on before you touch anything. Knee pads or a foam sit-mat are worth their weight.

Footwear: something with a real rubber sole and a defined edge. You'll be on wet grating, diamond plate, slick slab, and stairs whose treads are still shiny from the stamp. Nothing with a smooth sole. Nothing you'd mind getting cement on.

## Eyes: bring your own shadow

The single strangest physical condition on the plateau is the light, and it's worth preparing for as a technical problem rather than an aesthetic one.

There is no sun. The sky is an evenly lit dome, bright as a lightbox, and shadows pool directly under objects regardless of position. This removes almost every cue your visual system uses for depth and distance. Two identical stacks fifty meters apart look like they're side by side. A red water tank you walk toward for an hour is suddenly overhead. Stairs are hard to read because the risers cast no shadow. Standing water reflects the sky as flat panes indistinguishable from the sky itself.

Two things help.

First, a handheld light—a decent flashlight, not a headlamp. Hold it out to the side at arm's length, low, and sweep it across the ground in front of you. Raking light restores texture: you'll see the edges of stair treads, the lip of a drain, the join where slab meets grating, the meniscus of a puddle. It feels absurd to use a flashlight in a place that bright, and it works. Use it on every staircase.

Second, sunglasses, but choose them for the puddles rather than the sky. Polarized lenses will kill the sky-reflection in the black rectangles of standing water and let you see that they're water. The brightness itself isn't painful—it's diffuse—but hours in it produce a peculiar fatigue, a flatness behind the eyes. Take the glasses off periodically and rest your eyes on something close and textured: your own gloves, the grain of a formwork mark.

A laser rangefinder will give you inconsistent readings. Bring one anyway. When it disagrees with itself between two consecutive shots at the same target, that tells you something about the stretch you're in.

## Ears: the quiet has a cost

The plateau is nearly silent, and the silence is not neutral. Machinery at that scale implies noise, and your nervous system will spend the whole visit braced for a roar that doesn't come. People describe it landing on the ears like pressure. Some find it soothing. Most find it tiring in a way they can't locate.

Do not try to fill the silence. Do not shout to test the echo (there isn't one, or it's wrong). Talk to your companion if you have one, but quietly. The description's suggestion that the noise of the plant is *collected*—that quiet is a sign of good yield—is a guess, but it's a guess you should honor. You don't know what a loud noise does here, or where it goes. You are a guest in a facility, and guests in facilities are quiet.

Earplugs are useless. Earphones with music are a genuinely bad idea; you need to hear the relay clicks and the ticking pipes, which are the only acoustic information you get.

## Water: bring all of it, drink none of theirs

Carry every drop you'll drink. Two liters minimum for a day, more if you're prone to headaches at altitude.

Do not drink the condensation. Do not drink from the standing rectangles. Do not collect drip from the underside of a conveyor bridge, even though it looks like distilled water. The whole plateau is a system that routes water *back into itself*—every drain immaculate, every low point gridded—and whatever's in that water has been through a cement plant and an ozone field. Beyond the chemistry, there is a plainer intuition: everything that touches the plant's water becomes part of the plant's cycle. Don't put it inside you.

The haze cycle matters practically. Every so often a fine wet standing mist arrives, and for a while everything is slick. Stop walking when it comes. Find a rail, put your back to a wall, and wait. It passes in something under an hour and the surfaces dry warm. Move during the dry phases. Cover ground when the deck is dry and your footing is honest.

## Food: eat your own, feed nothing

Bring calories. The place will not offer any, and you should not accept them if it seems to. The trees with eleven leaves are not fruit trees. The moss is not edible. The goats are not chewing and the pigeons are not pecking, and if you throw crumbs, nothing will happen, and you should not want to find out what nothing looks like here.

Eat on a schedule, by the clock, not by hunger. Which brings up the clock.

## Time: your body is the only clock you can trust

The light does not change. There is no dusk. There is no sun to track. The description is exact about this: there is an end to a visit, and you know it because your legs tell you, not the sky.

Bring a mechanical wristwatch and glance at it, but don't trust it against your fatigue. Set a departure rule *before* you step off the rock, and make it a rule about your body: *when I've eaten twice, I turn around. When my thighs ache on the stairs, I turn around.* Fatigue in the shadowless light comes on strangely—you won't feel tired and then you'll be very tired—so err early.

Do not sleep on the plateau. The surfaces are warm in the dry phase and the quiet is absolute and it will occur to you, at some point, that lying down for twenty minutes on a warm slab out of the drip would be very nice. The fox is lying down. The fox has been lying down in three places at once. Stay upright.

## Movement: where to walk, where not to

Stay outside. Stay on decking, gantries, exterior stairs, and bridges. Do not enter sheds. Do not go through doors. The one documented door on the plateau opens onto a transformer room and then opens again, from inside, onto the same room without you in it. That is not a trap in any hostile sense; it is a sign that the interiors are not laid out for people. The exteriors are, approximately. Keep to them.

Stay out of the transformer yards. The goats stand among the insulators unbothered, but goats are constituent material and you are not, yet. The ozone smell is a high-voltage smell.

Do not ride, touch, or reach into a conveyor. The belts are slow—slower than walking—and carry wet blocks of what the plateau is made of. Anything that goes into a shed on a conveyor is being made into ground.

Do not walk on the soft slab at the forming edge. You'll want to; the edge is where the place is most itself. Go as far as the last *cured* concrete—tap it with a pole, it should ring dry—and stop. Beyond that the slab takes a footprint. A footprint on the forming edge is permanent; it cures in and gets built over and becomes part of the plateau forever. This is the only mark you can leave here and you should decide in advance whether you want to leave it. Whatever you decide, don't step out onto the rebar. It's over nothing.

Do not climb down. If you lie at the shear edge and look under, you'll see girders descending toward true rock, and you'll think about rope. Don't. The gap between plateau and mountain is real air with no wind and no measurable depth, and the girders belong to the plant.

Stairs: count them going up, hold the rail (gloved), and expect the count to be different going down. Don't try to correct for it. Just keep the rail, keep the flashlight low, and accept the stumble at the top or bottom. The stumble is normal weather.

## Reflections and gauges: look, don't stare

Your reflection in the standing water lags by half a second. The heron's does too. Don't lean over a puddle and wait to see how far the lag goes. Don't bring a mirror. Don't check your teeth in a wet window. There is something being measured in that half-second and you don't want to be the instrument.

Gauges and dials: read them once if you must, then look away. The needle changes when you look directly at it. Do not touch valves, switches, breakers, or wheels. You do not work here.

## The animals: near, silent, unbothered

The plateau is full of animals, and the rule with all of them is the same: don't touch, don't feed, don't startle, don't count.

You will not be able to follow the last part. You'll count the pigeons on a girder, the trees in the courtyard, the leaves on a tree. Fine—but write the number down and don't be alarmed when it doesn't hold. The place is not stable at the level of small integers.

The pigeons watch. They are on every girder, and they do not move, and when one shifts the movement ripples down the whole line. Don't do anything that would make them all shift at once. You'd be the first person to hear what that sounds like.

The heron will stand at your elbow. The fox will be lying on its left side with its tail across its nose, in this passage and the next and the one after. You'll decide it's three foxes. Let the decision go when it stops holding; it doesn't matter, and the effort of forcing it costs something. The animals are not inhabitants. They are in solution. Treat them the way you'd treat lab glassware: with care, without interaction, and with the understanding that they are part of the apparatus.

## Take nothing, leave nothing

This is the most important rule, and the one people break.

Leave nothing—not a chalk mark, not a chip of tape, not a wrapper, not a strand of hair if you can help it. The plateau renews itself; nothing weathers; things that are left get incorporated. A chalk arrow on a wall will be gone when you return, not scrubbed but *never there*, and the wall will be newer. That's the benign case. The less benign case is that something you left has been made into ground, and now you have a small permanent stake in the plant.

Take nothing. Not a flake of lichen, not a pebble of aggregate, not a pigeon feather. The plateau's business is extending itself over the true mountain and, presumably, past it. You do not want a seed of that in your pocket when you leave. You especially do not want to find out what a piece of made ground does in a place with real soil.

## Companions: one, tethered

Go alone or go with one person. Not more.

If two, tie in—a short tether, three meters or so, clipped to harness or belt. This sounds paranoid until you're in a passage where you can put a hand on each wall and your companion has turned a corner five steps ahead and is, now, on a gantry two levels up. The density and the failed scale make separation instantaneous and reunion uncertain. A tether keeps you in the same volume.

A companion is also the only reliable check on the frays. You each count the stairs. You compare the number of leaves. One of you watches the fox while the other looks away. Two people who agree that the water tank was to the left ten minutes ago have a better chance of finding the way back than one person who is quite sure.

## Getting out

Here is the hard part. The place where you came up will not be where you left it. The description is explicit: the warm grating with the fog below is gone, and there's a doorless shed where the path should meet the deck.

So: do not rely on retracing your route. If your usual method of leaving a world is under your own control, that is your primary exit, and you should have it ready before you step off the rock. Test it at the threshold, on the last real stone.

If you must walk out, the intuition is this—the plateau grows toward the forming edge, and the old edges get built over. Your arrival point is the *oldest* part of the plateau you know, which means it's the part most likely to have been covered. Don't go back to it. Instead, look for the shear edge nearest to where you came up, the clean lip of overpour, and follow it. The shear is where the plateau meets the true mountain, and somewhere along it the grating still goes down into cloud. Walk the perimeter. Keep the lip on your left or your right and don't cross a shed to shortcut. When you find grating that rings under your poles and drops into fog, take it, and don't look back for the exact spot—it isn't there, and you'll waste your legs looking.

Set the departure rule early, as above, and leave with margin. You'll need to walk the perimeter and you don't know how long the perimeter is. Nobody does. It's within spec.

## Signs it's time to go now

Regardless of your rule, leave immediately if:

- Your footsteps on the grating have gone quiet.
- Your shadow has gotten smaller, or has stopped following you when you step sideways.
- You look at a puddle and your reflection is ahead of you rather than behind.
- You realize you've been standing still for a while and can't say how long, and something with feathers is standing very close.
- You find yourself pricing the warmth of a slab against the ache in your legs.
- The count comes back the same twice in a row. (The frays are the place's weather. A place with no weather is a place that has finished with you.)

## Why go at all

Because it's beautiful, in the way a machine shop at night is beautiful, and because nothing else is like it. Because you will stand at a forming edge in silent brightness with a heron at your elbow and rebar going out over cloud and you will understand—wordlessly, in your feet—that you are standing on a product and the mountain below is the raw material and the whole thing is being extruded outward one centimeter a day toward everything.

Go for that. Stay a day. Wear gloves. Drink your own water. Count the stairs, hold the rail, keep the light low and to the side.
