**Preparing for the room under the hill**

Start with the understanding that this place asks almost nothing of your equipment and a great deal of your body. There is no hazard here that can be solved by carrying more. The water is not poisonous, the air is breathable, nothing in the room will chase you. What the room will do is press on you evenly from every side — with noise, with wet, with cold, with sameness — and the preparation that matters is the preparation that lets you be pressed on for a while without coming apart.

**Your ears, first and most.**

This is the only real physical danger. A column of water thick as a thigh falling four body-heights into a stone basin inside a sealed chamber is well over the threshold where hearing damage begins, and the description tells you plainly that there is no quieter corner. You cannot step back from it. Every minute you are in the room unprotected is a minute of exposure, and the room makes exposure feel like nothing — that is the trap. The sound stops registering as sound. You will not feel that you are being hurt.

So: proper hearing protection, foam plugs at minimum, and put them in *before* the last body-length of the crack, because by then the roar is already on you and your hands will be busy. Bring spares in a dry pouch; one will fall out in the squeeze.

Now the honest part. The sound is the room. The settlement was built around it; the fish face it; the whole account of the place is an account of living inside one noise until it becomes silence. If you go in plugged from beginning to end you will have visited a wet, glowing cave with interesting masonry, and missed the thing. My suggestion is this: once you are through, once you have found a place to sit that is stable and off the channels, take the plugs out for a short time — a few minutes, not more than ten — and let the room do what it does. Let the sound fill you until it stops being separable from you. Then put the plugs back in and stay as long as you want. You will find the silence-with-a-grain persists even through the foam. It has gotten in by then. That is the point.

If you have tinnitus already, or any hearing you cannot afford to lose, skip the unplugged interval. The room will still be something.

**The crack.**

It is taken sideways, which means it is narrower than you are front-to-back. Know your own dimensions. If you have never done a squeeze in a cave, understand that the body goes through things it does not believe it will fit through, and that panic is the only thing that actually stops it. Exhale to make yourself thinner. Move one limb at a time. Do not wear a pack through it — tether it to your ankle on a metre of cord and drag it behind you, and keep it small. Anything rigid strapped to your body will catch.

The last body-length is where the roar begins, and the roar arrives with air pressure you can feel in your chest, and it arrives when you are already committed and cannot turn around. Expect this. It is very disorienting to be gripped by rock on both sides while the world ahead of you howls. Keep moving forward. It is one body-length.

Coming back out, the reverse: the roar cuts off abruptly and the quiet of the hill will feel wrong, like something has been taken. Rest in the crack a moment before pushing on. Your balance may be off.

**Finding the way out again.**

This deserves its own paragraph because it is the thing most likely to go quietly wrong. The room lights itself from every wet surface, without shadow. The mist erases the ceiling. Everything is the same grey-green, the same degree of glow. The crack you came in through is the one thing in the chamber that does not glow — it should appear as a dark slot in a luminous wall — but mist drifts, and the terraces all look alike, and after an hour of the sound your sense of direction will not be what it was.

So: when you step out of the crack, before you look at anything else, turn around and look at where you came from. Fix it against something — a particular cell mouth, a bridge slab, the position relative to the fall. Then, if you have brought a small light, leave it turned on and sitting in the crack mouth, pointing inward. You will take it with you when you go; it leaves nothing. A light of any colour other than grey-green will be the only thing of its kind in the room and you will find it from anywhere. This is the single most useful object you can bring.

**Clothing and the wet.**

Everything you own will be wet within minutes and stay wet. Waterproof shells will keep the mist off your skin for a while but you will sweat inside them, and in the end what matters is not staying dry — you will not — but staying warm while wet. Wool, or synthetic pile. Nothing cotton. A hat, because the cool goes in at the head. Bring a closed-cell foam pad to sit on; the stone is at cellar temperature and will pull heat out of you through your seat faster than the air will, and you will want to sit for a long time.

Cellar-cool is not cold. But cellar-cool, saturated, motionless, for two or three hours, on stone, is how people become hypothermic without ever feeling that they are. Watch for the shivers and take them seriously. Move around when they start. Have a dry set of clothes in a sealed bag on the far side of the crack, not with you.

Glasses will fog and stay fogged. If you can see without them, leave them behind. Any lens — camera, phone — will be useless within a minute; do not bring it in expecting pictures. What you can take out of this room is not a picture anyway.

**Footing.**

The calcite glaze is smooth, translucent, wet, and slightly rounded on every edge. It is exactly as slick as it sounds. The terraces are only a hand-span high, so a fall is not far — but a fall is onto stone, and a fall into a channel or a cistern is into water, and a fall onto a cell lintel is onto something that has taken a thousand years to form. Move slowly. Keep three points of contact when you step down a tier. Soft-soled shoes, or wool socks over bare feet, grip glazed stone better than hard rubber. Test each footing before you commit weight.

The channels are nearly level and easy to step over. Step over them. Do not step in them; do not step on their lips.

**Your hands.**

The description tells you the truth about this: after a while your hand cannot tell built from unbuilt. This means you will touch the settlement without knowing you are touching it. Keep that in mind. Everything under head height has been placed by someone. When you reach out to steady yourself, you are steadying yourself on a wall that was laid dry, stone by stone, by hands with no tool harder than flint, and then sealed by the room over a span of time you cannot count.

The glaze is what holds it now. It will bear your weight. It will not bear your prying. Do not lift anything. Do not put your fingers into a cell to see if there is more inside. If you must look in, look in with your eyes, and from a little distance; the cells are the size they are for a reason none of us knows.

Do not touch the bowl, the pebbles, or the bone pin. Do not touch the fish. Do not lean over the basin so that anything falls from you into it.

**What not to bring in, what not to leave.**

Nothing you set down in this room will come out of it unchanged, and nothing you drop will ever be quite gone. The mist lays down its layer on everything. A coin left on a terrace will be a lump in a decade. A dropped glove will glaze into the channel it fell in and change how the water moves for a very long time. The room has arrived at equilibrium; it is not fragile in the sense of shattering, but it is fragile in the sense that a small insult persists.

So: bring as little as you can carry through a squeeze, keep it attached to you, and count it before you leave. Do not build anything. You will feel the urge — a cairn, a pebble on a lintel, a small contribution — and it will feel like homage. It is not. The builders did not leave room for you to join in. They finished.

Do not drink from the channels. Not because the water is dangerous — it is probably the cleanest thing you will ever be near — but because your face over the channel is your face over the isopods and the film, and because it is not yours. Bring your own water. Bring a little food that can be eaten with wet hands.

**Companions and speech.**

There is no speaking here. If you go with someone, agree on your signals before you enter, and keep them few: *I'm all right. Look. Time to go. Help.* Practise them. A hand on a shoulder means more in this room than a sentence would anywhere else, and you will find yourselves inventing a whole grammar of touch within an hour, as the builders must have. Some travellers report this as the most intimate thing they have experienced with another person. Others find the enforced silence unbearable. You know which you are.

Going alone is possible and perhaps better. You will be very alone, though — more alone than the mere absence of company accounts for, because the sound cuts you off from your own voice too. You cannot hear yourself hum. You cannot reassure yourself aloud. Be ready for that.

**Time.**

There is no day in this room. The film glows at the rate it glows. The fall does not change. You will lose track of how long you have been there within the first half hour, and the sound will make the hours feel like a single hour that keeps not ending. Wear a watch, and check it. Set a vibrating alarm if you have one — you will not hear an audible one — for the time you intend to leave, and leave when it goes.

**The mind.**

This is the part that varies most between travellers. The room is, in every sensory register, uniform: one sound, one light, one temperature, one texture. That uniformity is very close to the conditions under which the mind begins to manufacture its own input. Some people, sitting in the room after the sound has become silence, find a great calm — a released, attentive, floating steadiness that they describe afterward as the most rested they have ever been. Others, sometimes the same people on a different day, find that after an hour the mist has faces in it and the terraces are moving when they are not looked at, and the cell mouths seem to want something. Both of these are the room and neither is the room.

If the second thing happens, do not fight it and do not indulge it. Find the smallest living thing you can see and watch it. The fish in the outer ring of the basin, hanging and facing the fall. The slug on its terrace, going somewhere. An isopod picking at the film. These are real, and they move, and they are the only things in the room that are going about their own business without reference to you. Watching them will bring the room back to its actual size. If it does not, go to your light in the crack and put your hand on the stone of the entrance and stay there until it does. If it still does not, leave. The room will be there.

**The builders.**

You will want to solve them. Doorways too small for a person; tool marks the width of a human thumb. The account says you can hold both facts and not resolve them, and I would add that you should try not to. This is not a puzzle set out for you. It is what someone made, and how they lived, and it is entire without your understanding of it.

Conduct yourself, in practice, as though they might not be gone. Not because there is evidence that they are here, but because the room is finished and tended and full of the thing it was made for, and it is difficult to stand in it and feel that it is empty. Sit where you would sit if you were a guest. Face the water, because everything else does. If you find you want to put your head and one shoulder into a cell to see what is inside — don't. Whatever is or is not in there, the size of the opening is a statement about who was meant to enter, and you were not.

**Afterward.**

When you come out, the hill will be quiet and the quiet will feel like injury. This passes in an hour or two. Your hearing may ring for longer; if it rings for more than a day, you stayed unplugged too long. Your clothes will be soaked and cool. Change at once.

You will not have a photograph. You will have the memory of a sound that was so total it became a kind of stillness, and of a settlement of small stones turned toward that sound the way a village turns toward the sea, and of a bone pin softening under glaze in a niche where someone set it upright, and did not come back for it, and the room kept it. That is what there is to bring out. Handle it carefully. Like everything else from the room, it will lay down a thin layer in you and go on laying it, at its own rate, with no reason to do otherwise.
