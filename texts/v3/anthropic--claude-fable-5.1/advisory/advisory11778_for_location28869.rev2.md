# Preparing for the Bubble

Start by discarding the word "visit." You do not visit this place. You make an incursion into it and you withdraw before it kills you, and if you plan well the withdrawal happens while you still feel fine, because feeling fine is not information here. The heat is total and dry, which means your body's warning system—the feeling of being sweaty, sticky, flushed—will not fire. You will be losing two liters an hour and feel dry-skinned and clear-headed right up until you aren't.

So think of it the way a diver thinks of depth. There is a bottom time. You set it before you go down. You come up when it expires whether or not you've seen the room you wanted to see.

---

## What the place actually is, physically

Strip away the town and the forest and the music for a moment. What you are entering is a sealed stone oven, thirty meters tall and sixty across, heated from every surface, with a hole in the roof that acts as a chimney and a floor that vents volcanic gas on a cycle of a few minutes. The oven has been on for geological time. It is not going to be cooler on the day you arrive.

Four things in it can kill you, and they are worth naming in order of likelihood:

1. **Heat and water loss.** This is the one that will actually get you if anything does. Not dramatically—just a gradual failure of judgment, then coordination, then consciousness.
2. **The air.** Sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, and whatever else the deep rock exhales. Some of it is heavier than the air you breathe and pools at the floor, which is where you'll be walking.
3. **Falling and being fallen on.** The floor is grit over fissures. The ceiling sheds door-sized plates of glass. The ladders are older than any wood you have ever trusted.
4. **The climb out.** You will have to leave by the way you came in, exhausted, hot, and heavy with gear, up a rope through a column of rising heat.

Everything below is in service of those four.

## Getting in, and the thing you must arrange before you do

The chamber's only known opening is the eye in the dome. Whether there is some other way in—a fissure the builders used, a collapsed gallery that opens to the surface—nobody has documented one, and you should not spend your bottom time looking. Assume you descend through the eye on a rope and ascend the same way.

That means:

**You do not go alone.** This is not a suggestion. You need at least two people on the surface who never enter, who manage the rope, who have a haul system rigged and tested before you touch the edge, and who will pull you out on a signal or on a timer if the signal doesn't come. The single most dangerous moment of the whole undertaking is a tired person at the bottom of a thirty-meter rope trying to ascend under their own power through hot rising air. Do not put yourself in a position where that is your only option.

**Rope selection matters more than usual.** Nylon and polyester lose meaningful strength as they warm, and a rope hanging through the eye sits in the hottest moving air in the whole system. Use an aramid core—Technora, Kevlar, something rated for heat. Sheath it in something you can grip with gloved hands. Bring more than you need; you want spare length to re-rig if the first line frays on the obsidian lip.

**Anchor well back from the edge.** The dome is "thinned from beneath by the heat, cracked from above by the cold." The rim of the eye is the thinnest, most stressed glass in the whole structure. Your anchors go into the fractured field a good distance back, on ground that has clearly been there a long time. Pad the edge heavily—obsidian will cut a rope the way it cuts skin.

**Test the haul before you descend.** Have them pull a load of your body weight plus kit up and out. If it takes them longer than a couple of minutes, fix that before you go, because a couple of minutes is what you'll have if things go wrong.

**Descend slowly and stop just inside.** The first meter below the eye is where you find out what the air is actually like. Hang there. Read your gas meter. Let your eyes start to adjust. If your instruments say the floor air is worse than the roof air—and they probably will, because heavy gases sink—you'll know before you're standing in it.

## Heat, and the water problem

You cannot bring enough water to stay indefinitely. Understand that first. Evaporative cooling has a ceiling; once the radiant heat coming at you from the walls, floor, and dome exceeds what sweating can shed, drinking more water does not help. You are simply warming up. In a very hot dry sauna—around 90°C—untrained people last fifteen or twenty minutes. Firefighters in gear can manage perhaps half an hour at similar temperatures before they need to cool down. Nobody knows exactly how hot the chamber runs, but the resin stems soften during the surges and the dome glows, and the builders lived only on the lowest tiers, closest to the floor and farthest from the roof. Plan for the low end of a sauna and hope for better.

Given that:

**Pre-hydrate.** Drink steadily for the day before. Start the descent slightly over-full. Electrolytes, not just water.

**Carry water in insulated containers packed with ice from the surface.** It will be warm within an hour and hot within two. Drink it anyway. Warm water hydrates you exactly as well as cold water; it just doesn't feel like relief. Sip on a schedule—every ten minutes, whether you feel thirsty or not—because you will not feel thirsty until it's late.

**Cover everything.** This is counterintuitive to people from temperate places, who think of stripping down in heat. Desert people and firefighters know better: in radiant heat, exposed skin is a solar panel. Long sleeves, long trousers, a hood, a face veil, gloves. Loose enough for air to move under it. Natural fibers—wool, heavy cotton, linen—never synthetics, which melt and bond to skin. If you can get an aluminized outer layer, take it; reflecting radiant heat is worth more than any amount of breathability here.

**No exposed metal touching your body.** Zips, buckles, watch backs, the frames of your glasses, the rivets in your trousers. All of it will reach the ambient temperature of the room and all of it will brand you. Tape it, cover it, or cut it off. Wear your watch over a sleeve. Consider a plastic-framed timepiece.

**Boot soles.** Standard rubber begins to soften around the temperatures you're likely walking on, and the grit floor is heated from below. Firefighter boots or heat-rated Vibram soles. Thick leather uppers. Two pairs of wool socks. Do not kneel, sit, or lean on anything for more than a moment. If you need to rest, you need to leave.

**Watch your breathing.** Hot dry air is unpleasant to inhale and, at the upper range, damages the airway. A damp cloth over the mouth won't stay damp long enough to matter, but a respirator (which you'll be wearing anyway; see below) slows and slightly conditions the air, which helps.

**Know the signs in yourself and in your partner if you bring one down.** Confusion, irritability, a stumble, a stopped conversation. Slurred words. A sudden feeling of being fine after feeling bad. Any of these means you are done and the rope goes taut now.

## The air

The floor breathes. Every few minutes gas forces up through the fissures and through the flutes, and what comes up is sulfur and tar and the exhalation of hot rock. Between breaths, the heavy components—CO₂ especially—settle back toward the floor. You will be walking at floor level. You will be walking in the forest, where the blooms form because that's where the gas is thickest.

**Bring a four-gas meter at minimum**: oxygen, hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide. Clip it low on your body, at the hip, not at the chest, because the danger is at your feet. Set the alarms conservatively and honor them the first time.

**Wear a respirator with acid-gas cartridges, and know its limits.** A cartridge respirator handles sulfur dioxide and hydrogen sulfide at moderate concentrations. It does nothing about carbon dioxide and nothing about low oxygen. If your meter shows either of those going wrong, no filter helps; only leaving does. If you can carry supplied air, carry it, but weigh the heat cost: a tank on your back is more mass to haul and more insulation trapping your own metabolic heat. Many will judge the cartridge respirator the better trade for a short incursion. Judge for yourself, but judge before you go, not on the floor.

**Learn the breath cycle before you move.** Stand at the base of the rope for two full cycles. Feel the pressure build in your sternum—that is real, that is low-frequency sound and rising gas together—then the roar, then the ticking, then the silence. Time it. Then move only in the silences, and stop, brace, and check your meter during each surge. The gas concentration at the floor is at its worst in the moment after the roar, before the chimney effect of the eye has had a chance to pull the fresh exhalation upward. Give it a beat before you breathe deeply.

**Read the forest.** The stems lean, all together, toward the strongest venting. They are a map, drawn in real time, of where the gas is coming from hardest. Where they lean toward is where you do not stand.

---

## Sound

The roar is loud enough to damage hearing over repeated exposure, and it recurs every few minutes for as long as you're there. Wear ear protection. But wear the kind you can lift or lower, because the silences are where you'll hear the ceiling ticking and the forest cracking, and those are safety information—a change in the tick pattern above you may be the only warning you get that a plate is letting go.

Understand also that some of what you'll feel is below hearing. The pressure in the sternum, the sense of something gathering before sound arrives, the faint nausea some people get in the presence of sustained infrasound—these are effects of the place on your body, and they will make you feel wrong in ways you may attribute to heat or gas. Don't second-guess your meter because you feel strange. But don't dismiss the feeling either. It is part of the cost.

The builders tuned the flutes. Whether for beauty or tolerability nobody knows, but the result is that the roar has music in it, and the galleries answer, and the first time you hear an empty doorway hum at its own pitch you will want to stop and listen. Do. But do it during your planned stop, not on the move.

## Light and eyes

The chamber is lit only by the dull red of thin rock. It is enough to see your hands, not enough to be sure of a color or read a label. Your eyes will adapt over ten or fifteen minutes, and once they do, a headlamp will feel like a violation—not just of the place's atmosphere but of your own night vision, which you'll lose and need to rebuild.

Bring a light anyway. Bring two. Use them for inspecting footing before a step, for checking your meter, for looking at the ceiling above you before you commit to standing under it. Use a red filter for these tasks so you keep your adaptation. Then, once, near the end, switch to white and look at what the place actually is—the true colors of the glass and the blooms and the tuff, which nobody who lived here ever saw. You will want to have done that.

Your eyes will dry out. Bring goggles that seal, and wear them at least part of the time. Bring saline drops and expect them to be warm. Blink deliberately. If your corneas start to feel like sandpaper, that is real damage beginning, and it's a reason to leave.

## Moving through the place

**The floor** is fine grit over slag over fissures. Some of the fissures are wide enough to take a leg. Others vent hot enough to burn through a boot. Probe ahead with a pole—a length of fiberglass or hardwood, not metal—and walk where the builders walked. Their paths are visible: worn hollows in the lowest gallery, a smoother line through the grit toward the grinding stones and the trough. They lived here for generations. They knew where the floor was solid. Trust their feet before your own.

**The forest** is not something to walk through casually. The stems are glassy resin—rigid and sharp-edged when cool, softening in the surges, snapping when heavy. The blooms are sulfur crusts, hot, and will burn skin and cloth on contact. If you must pass through, go in a silence, fast, gloved and covered, along the thinnest line, and be out before the next breath leans the whole field toward you. Better: go around. The forest is thickest near the fissures, which is also where the gas is worst and the floor is weakest. The three hazards coincide. Treat the dense stands as walls.

**The ceiling** sheds. You will find the fallen plates half-buried in grit and you should note where they are, because they show you where the dome has already thinned and where it may thin again. Before you stand anywhere for more than a moment, light the ceiling above you and look for cracks, for a brighter red than the surroundings (thinner rock, closer to the heat), for anything that looks like a plate ready to let go. Then don't stand there. The galleries with their roofs stove in and their rubble tidied aside are the builders' own record of where the dome fails. Read it.

**The ladders** are the builders' own resin wood, straightened and joined, and they have been sitting in this heat for as long as the town has been empty. They may be perfectly sound. They may crumble under a hand. You cannot know from looking. If you need to reach an upper tier, rig your own line from above—anchored into tuff, not the pumice—or use the stone lips and worn footholds beside the ladders, which are rock and have been tested by the same centuries. Never put your full weight on the wood.

**The tiers** are stratified in reliability, and the stratification runs the wrong way for you. The lowest gallery is cut from solid glass and is as sound as the chamber itself. The middle tiers are dry-fitted tuff, dense and weathered into itself; probably fine. The upper tiers are pumice and sintered dust, laid in visible haste, and they sit directly under the shedding dome. The higher you climb, the weaker the ground, the closer the ceiling, the more you are spending your bottom time on a climb you'll have to reverse. Unless you have a specific reason, stay low, where the people who knew this place chose to live.

## The town

You are entering the home of a vanished people, and everything in it is theirs. Move like a guest in a house where the owners have just stepped out and will judge the state of things when they return.

Do not take stems from the forest; the builders harvested them and the ladders are what came of it, and you have no idea what the resin was worth to them. Do not scrape the yellow stain from the grinding hollows. Do not test the trough or the racks or the hearths. Touch doorways only to steady yourself, and then with a gloved hand. Anything you drop—a button, a cartridge, a scrap of tape—will lie there in the dry heat as long as the stone does, because nothing decays here and nothing carries anything away. Sweat vanishes. Everything else stays. Count your kit before you leave and count it again at the rope.

If you have time for one thing beyond the floor, make it the lowest gallery: the hollows, the trough, the seat-height shelves. That is where the life actually was. The empty finished rooms above it are moving, but they are moving because they're empty, and you can feel that from below. The unfinished courses at the very top, the line of blocks set out and never laid—you will want to see them, and you probably should not. They are under the thinnest part of the dome, on the weakest masonry, at the end of the longest climb. Look at them from the floor. Let that be enough.

## Time

There are no afternoons. The red does not change. The breath is the only clock, and it is a clock without hours—every few minutes, forever, unmarked. You will lose track of how long you've been down within a quarter hour. This is not a metaphor about the place's mood; it is a practical hazard, because your bottom time is measured in minutes and you will have no sense of them passing.

Wear a timer. Set it before you descend, for less than you think you can tolerate. When it goes, you go. Have the surface team run a second timer and haul on it regardless. Do not negotiate with either.

If a first incursion goes well, do a second one on another day, not the same day. Your body needs to shed the heat load fully, and that takes hours on a cold surface.

## What it will do to your mind

Some of this is worth knowing in advance because it will be mistaken for something else.

You will be the only moving thing. Not the only living thing—the forest lives, in its way—but the only body that turns its head, that steps, that breathes in a rhythm other than the ground's. You will feel watched. You are not. That feeling is your nervous system failing to find another animal in a space shaped by animals, and it will pass, or it will not, but it is not a reason to do anything except keep to your plan.

The readiness of the place is the hardest part. Doors open. Shelves at the height of a sitting person's reach. Racks waiting for something to cure. Rooms finished and never slept in. Blocks lined up for a wall. Every human instinct says a place this prepared is about to be occupied, that people are coming back, and every fact says they left long before any language you speak existed. The mind does not settle on that. It keeps reaching. Let it. But notice when reaching becomes wandering, when you find yourself standing in an upper doorway you didn't plan to enter with no memory of the climb. Heat does this. Grief does this. The place does this. Check your timer.

The hum in the doorways—each at its own pitch, every few minutes, around the whole ring—is genuinely beautiful and will get inside you. You are permitted one stop, in a safe spot, under sound ceiling, on solid glass, to simply stand in a silence and wait for the breath and hear the town sing. Put it in the plan. Then move.

Afterward, on the surface, in the cold, expect to feel that you have not fully come back. The red does not leave the eyes quickly. The cycle of pressure and roar and tick will replay in your chest for hours. People describe the first drink of genuinely cold water afterward as almost frightening. Sit with a warm blanket in the cold—your body will overcorrect—and let the surface team watch you for signs of delayed heat illness, which is real and can arrive an hour after you thought you were fine.

## A working kit

Not exhaustive; adapt it, but don't shorten it.

- Aramid-core rope, 45m or more, with spare; edge padding; rigging for a two-person haul system tested under load
- Harness with no bare metal against the body; tape every buckle
- Four-gas meter (O₂, H₂S, SO₂, CO₂), hip-mounted, alarms set conservatively
- Half- or full-face respirator with acid-gas cartridges; spare cartridges; or supplied air if you've decided the heat cost is worth it
- Sealed goggles; saline drops
- Adjustable hearing protection
- Full coverage in wool or heavy cotton; hood, veil, gloves (leather over wool); aluminized outer layer if obtainable
- Heat-rated boot soles, thick leather uppers, double wool socks
- Water in insulated containers packed with ice; electrolytes; a drinking schedule you will keep
- Two lights, both red-filterable; one white pass at the end
- A non-metal probing pole
- A timer with an alarm you can hear through ear protection, and a second on the surface
- A written plan of where you will go, in what order, with the stop marked, agreed with the surface team
- A count of every item you carry, checked before descent and before ascent

## Leaving

Go to the rope while you still feel good. Clip in. Signal. Let them haul; do not try to help by climbing—save what you have in case something goes wrong on the way up. As you rise through the rising heat you will pass the lip of the eye, the thinnest glass in the world, and then the cold will hit you all at once and you will gasp and probably not be able to speak for a minute.

Before you clip in, though, stand at the base of the rope for one more cycle. Let the pressure come up in your chest. Let the flutes roar and the galleries answer, ring on ring, each doorway in its own note. Listen to the forest groan as it leans, and creak as it straightens, and tick as it cools. Then the silence. In the silence, look up once at the red, and at the dark open doors, and know that when you are gone the next breath will come exactly as it did before you arrived, and the place will not have noticed you were there.

That is what it will be like. Prepare for that as much as for the heat.
