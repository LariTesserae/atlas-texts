**A preparation guide for the high plateau**

---

**First, understand what kind of place this is**

It is easy to read the description and come away thinking this is a wilderness — a high, dry, strange desert, dangerous in the way deserts are dangerous. That is the wrong frame, and going in with it will get you hurt. This is a place where the ordinary properties of the world have been worn thin by the same wind that made the rooms. Distance is not fixed. Sound does not stay where it is made. Time is not a process. Nothing here is hostile in the way a storm or a predator is hostile; it is worse than that, because it is indifferent and patient and *large*, and it has been doing what it does for longer than thought can hold, and you will be a very small object placed briefly in the middle of it.

The right frame is this: you are going somewhere that an ordinary human body can survive for a while, unaided, at a cost — and where every faculty you would normally use to judge how long "a while" has been is unreliable. Prepare accordingly. Prepare the body as you would for the highest mountain you can imagine, and prepare the mind as you would for a place that will lie to it gently and constantly.

---

**The body**

*Air.* The description says the air is "thin enough to hurt," which tells you it can be breathed and that breathing it is a mistake you will regret. The sky is nearly black, the sun has no glare, there is no scattering — you are above almost all of the atmosphere. On Earth, that would mean an altitude no one has ever breathed unassisted. Here you apparently can, for some span, but you will be hypoxic from the moment you arrive. Hypoxia does not feel like suffocating. It feels like clarity, confidence, calm, and a pleasant disinclination to worry — and then it feels like a headache, and then it feels like nothing, because judgement goes before consciousness does. In a place where every wrong decision is rewarded with more of itself, this is the single most dangerous thing about the plateau, and it is the thing that will be operating on you before you have noticed anything else.

Bring supplemental oxygen. Bring far more than you think a visit warrants, because you will not know how long the visit has been. Use it from the first breath, not from the first symptom. There is no lower camp to acclimatize at; you arrive at the top or you do not arrive.

The oxygen has a second purpose, which I will come to.

*Water.* There is none. The frost is not water in any sense that helps you; it is powder that does not fall and comes away dry. Every drop you will use, you carry in. At this altitude you lose water with every breath at a rate that feels like nothing and adds up fast; your lips will split within hours. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst, because thirst is a sense and senses are not to be trusted here. Bring nothing that requires water to be eaten.

*Warmth.* It is cool, not cold, and that phrasing is exact and deceptive. There is not enough air to make you cold by convection. But the sky is nearly black, and a black sky is a sink; you will radiate heat into it steadily, and you will conduct heat into any stone you touch. Do not sit on the stone. Do not lean on it. Do not lay a hand on a wall to steady yourself for longer than you must. Wear layers that are dry and stay dry — sweat is water you cannot replace and a wet layer against skin will pull heat out of you faster than the sky does. Gloves. A hat. Something between you and the ground if you must rest, and you should try not to rest.

*Light.* The sun is dim and the dimness is a trap. Whatever has happened to the light here has made it look spent, but do not assume the ultraviolet has been spent with it. At this altitude, with nothing to scatter it, a sun that looks like a small hard coin can burn skin through cloth. Cover everything. Goggles — dark ones — and not only for the sun: the dust, when it comes, is stone.

*Dust.* At intervals the air fills with fine pale grain that falls without wind and then, hours later, lifts and goes back up. It is the plateau's own rock. Breathing fine stone at altitude is a way to damage your lungs permanently, and the description's phrasing — the dust *leaves and returns* — should make you wonder whether stone dust that has been inside a person leaves and returns quite so cleanly. Wear a proper filtering mask whenever the dust is falling and for some while after it lifts. Seal your food. Seal your water.

**The senses**

You will have to rely on sight, and only on sight, and sight is diminished. Understand what you are giving up.

*Hearing.* Nothing you hear is happening where you are or when you are. Your footsteps come from a street away, late. Sometimes what arrives is not yours. If you travel with someone and they call to you, do not follow the voice — the voice is from where they were, and not necessarily from them, and when you reach the source it will be an empty crossroads and the call will arrive again from behind you. Agree on this before you go: **no one follows a sound.** If you must communicate, do it with hands, in sight of each other, or not at all.

Consider also that anything you say here goes somewhere and waits. It will come back to you, or to someone else, later, from elsewhere. Think about what you want to leave in a place like that. My honest advice is to say nothing, or very little, and nothing you would not want to hear arrive on its own from an empty street.

There is one sound to watch for, and it is the last one in the description: your footsteps stopping when you stop, a little late — *and then one more.* If you stop and wait for your steps to catch up and there is one more than there should be, you are done. Leave.

*Sight.* The light comes from one direction and nothing fills in. Shadows are not dark; they are absent. "Things placed in them cease." Take that literally until you have reason not to. Do not put your hand in a shadow to see. Do not step into one to take a shortcut. Do not let your gear fall into one. The frost lives in the shadows, and the frost is the one thing here that has been described as advancing with intent — a hand's breadth a day, on its own tide, toward stone that "waits." Learn to read the paleness where it has been so you can guess where it is going, and do not stand there.

A torch may or may not help. The light here looks like the end of a supply; your torch may look the same once it is switched on. Bring one and expect nothing of it.

*Sightlines.* You can see for a very long way and none of it is bright. This means you will see the far towers, the rim, the cloud — and it means anything standing in a doorway three hundred paces off will see you in exactly the same way, motionless, framed against black. Bear it in mind when you choose where to stand.

---

**Space**

The city is not built at a scale; it is built at a proportion, and the proportion is wrong. Buildings are too far apart for a town and too close for a countryside, and the distance between them is the part that is not fixed. Interiors are larger than their walls. Streets shorten. Buildings appear, and are already old.

The rules I would set myself:

**Do not try to enter anything.** Every door faces outward. You do not go in through them; you are inside, and then the door is how you leave. If you find yourself in a room — a floor of dust that takes no print, frost in the corners, one door in the middle of one wall — do not explore. Do not look for the second door; there isn't one. Do not cross to the far wall to see how far it really is. Turn to the door you can see, walk to it, and go out. You will be on the plateau. You may not be where you were.

**Stay near the walls when you are outside, and stay off the thresholds.** When a street narrows, the buildings on either side stay where they are — it is the distance between them that closes. A person standing against the base of a lit wall is standing on something that does not move. A person in the middle of the street is standing on the thing that does. And the pale creature, the one that is hunted, stands in doorways. Do not stand where it stands. Do not stand where it is looking. A threshold worn to a dip by feet that never fell is not a place to put your own.

**Do not go to the middle.** There is a place where the streets are longest and the buildings furthest apart, and a bowl there, polished, with a dark disc at the bottom that reflects a sky which is not this one. The description spends its final paragraph there, and the person in it has stopped moving. I will say more about the disc below, but as a matter of route: keep the rim in sight. The rim and the cloud beyond it are the two things on the plateau that have not been described as moving relative to you. If you can see the cloud-floor, you know which way out is. The moment you cannot, you are somewhere the city has drawn at its own proportions, and you should walk toward the nearest edge of that condition until you can.

**Do not rely on landmarks.** A tower you noted will be gone; one you did not note will be there, and old. Marks you leave on stone may last — the wind works in centuries — but the stone they are on may not be where you left it. Do not lay a trail of objects; dropped things here do not reliably fall. If you must navigate by anything, navigate by the sun (it barely moves, which makes it a fixed bearing — except when the shadows are suddenly elsewhere, so check it against the rim) and by the rim itself.

**Touch nothing you do not have to.** Not the frost. Not the grey-green crust on the north faces, which is either mineral or something simpler than life that behaves like life, spreading along the grain, favouring shade — and which, on the oldest stone, has organized itself into ridges and cells that look like writing from below and a map from above. It is more extensive on older things. You do not want to find out what it makes of you. Do not take samples. Do not take stone. The dust is "the city's own stone, leaving and returning"; anything you carry off is a loan.

---

**Time**

The day is not a process here. It is a condition. The sun does not travel; the shadows are occasionally elsewhere without having moved. You will not know how long you have been on the plateau, and your body — hypoxic, dehydrating, cooling from the outside in — is the last thing you should ask.

This is the second purpose of the oxygen. The gauge on a tank is the one honest clock you will have. It measures what you have used, and what you have used is what your body has taken, and your body is the only thing on the plateau that is still running at the pace of the world you came from. Decide before you arrive: **when the gauge reads half, you are walking toward the rim. When it reads a third, you are leaving.** Write this on a card and pin it where you will see it, because the version of you that reads the gauge will be confident, calm, and disinclined to worry, and will see no reason to hurry, and will be wrong.

A mechanical watch may run. It may also stop, or show you an hour that has not happened yet. Carry one, and trust the gauge over it.

There is a larger clock, and you will be able to see it from anywhere on the plateau: the cloud is rising. The white floor that lies below the rim, breathing once or twice a day, is coming up. You do not know the rate. You do not know what happens when a place that has never had moving weather is entered by weather. You do not know whether "very slowly" here means a century or an afternoon; the description uses those two spans interchangeably, on purpose. Treat the cloud as a countdown with no numbers on it. Do not plan a long visit. Do not plan to come back.

**The inhabitants**

There are three and each is the only one. Learn them before you go, because you will see at most one of them clearly and you should know which.

*The pale thing.* Hare-sized or a little more, long-legged, shaped for browsing with nothing to browse. It stands in doorways, motionless, and when you look away and back it is in another. It has two shadows and one is on the wrong side. It disturbs the frost. It is, as far as anyone can tell, prey — and the thing that hunts it does so by closing the street around it. If you see it, you are looking down a street that may shortly become shorter. Turn your back. Walk to the nearest lit wall. Do not watch. Do not approach it; do not stand where it has stood; and if you should ever notice that *you* have two shadows, you are not observing the pale thing any longer, and you should go.

*The bird.* Grey, dove-sized, more falling than flying, out of one tower-face and into another forty feet up. It seems harmless. It also never quite reaches the ground, which means the long descending line it draws across a street passes through the height of a standing person. Do not stand in that line. There is no reason to think it would pass through you the way it passes through stone, and no reason to think it would not.
