**Before anything else: understand what kind of visit this is**

This is not a place you survive so much as a place you *endure gracefully*. Nothing here wants to kill you. The water is not deep in any dramatic sense, the people are not hostile, the machine will not hunt you. But the place works on you steadily, the way it works on iron — a little every hour, from every side, without pause — and if you go in unprepared you will come out with your boots rotted, your notebook a pulp, your hearing dulled, and a rash you didn't notice starting. The dangers are cumulative and quiet. Prepare for attrition, not for incident.

Also understand that you are going somewhere with exactly one idea in it. The cane, the works, the resin, the barge. If you need variety to stay sane, budget your stay short. If you are the kind of person who can sit inside a single sound for days and find it deepening rather than narrowing, you may find it hard to leave.

---

**Getting in**

You arrive on foot or by cart along the causeway, and you should assume the approach is longer than it looks on whatever map you have, because the causeway curves and the cane closes over it and there is no landmark to measure progress against. The stack is your only beacon and you will lose sight of it under the leaf-crown for long stretches. Trust the causeway; do not trust its edges. The half-metre drop is real but the edge is soft and cut by cartwheels, and if you step off you will not fall into water so much as into root-mat with water under it, and how far under is a question nobody has answered for you. Carry a stick. Walk down the middle. If a cart comes, stand aside on the crown, not the shoulder.

Go in daylight. Not because the dark is dangerous but because there is no light on the causeway at all, and the only cue you'd have for staying on it is the sound of your own footfall changing from crunch to squelch.

Start hydrated. Start rested. The heat begins before the town does.

---

**What to wear, and what not to bring**

Think of every object you carry as something you are about to leave in a warm bath for the duration of your visit, and pack accordingly.

*Iron and steel.* Anything unpainted and ferrous will begin to fern within days. Buckles, knife blades, zips, the springs in a clasp, the screws in a pair of glasses. Bring brass, bronze, stainless if you have it, plastic, horn, wood. Oil what you can't replace. If you carry a blade, carry one you're willing to lose or one that isn't steel.

*Leather.* It will mould, soften, and stink. Boot leather in particular. If you must wear leather boots, wax them heavily beforehand and expect them to be finished by the time you leave. Better: rubber, or a synthetic that drains. Whatever you wear on your feet must grip wet plank — the gangways are slick with a green film that behaves like soap — and must not hold water against your skin all day.

*Cotton.* Cotton holds wet and never gives it back here. Wool is better, oddly, even in the heat, because it holds warmth when wet and dries from the inside. Best of all is anything that wicks and shakes dry. Bring more socks than you think is reasonable. Bring them in a sealed bag. Change them at midday and at night, and hang the wet pair in the drying wing if the workers will let you — that is the one dry room in the town, and the only reason the cat is fat and happy.

*Paper.* It will swell, soften, and grow spots. Ink will run. If you must take notes, use pencil on waxed or oiled paper, or on a slate, or don't. If you carry a book, carry one you don't love. Wrap anything precious in oilcloth inside a second oilcloth and accept that this only slows the damage.

*Electronics and optics.* Condensation is constant. A lens will fog the moment you uncap it and stay fogged. Anything with a battery compartment or a seam is a place for water to sit. Silica gel helps for a day. If you need to record, record with your eyes and ears and write it down later, somewhere dry.

*A hat with a brim.* The leaf-crown drips continuously onto the causeway and the gangways. The drip is warm and slightly sweet-smelling and you will stop noticing it, but it will run down your collar all day.

*Ear protection.* Bring good plugs and something over the ears as well. The knock — four times a minute, forever — is the sound that gets under people. You will need to sleep near it. More on this below.

---

**Your body in this place**

*Heat.* The air is warm from below as well as above and it barely moves. You will not feel yourself sweating because the sweat does not evaporate; it just joins the general wet. This means your usual cues for heat exhaustion are muted. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst. Sit down in the shade of the loading shed — it's open on the canal side and gets whatever wind the place has — every hour or two even if you feel fine.

*Water.* Do not drink the marsh water, the tank water, or anything that has run off a roof through a gutter with cane growing in it. Boil what you drink, or drink what the town drinks — ask where their water comes from. The stack breathes out distilled water all day; there may be a condensate line somewhere in the works, and if there is, that is the cleanest water for miles. The absence of frogs in the retting tanks is not a curiosity. It is information. Treat the pale scum as something you don't touch with bare skin.

*Skin.* Everything you have that stays damp will grow something: between the toes, under a belt, in the crease of the elbow, at the collar. Bring a drying powder and use it morning and night. Bring an antifungal. Wash with clean water and let your skin be *air-exposed* for as long as you can each evening even though the air is not dry, because still-damp is better than cloth-damp. Small cuts will not close well here; clean them, cover them, check them.

*Hearing.* The town has stopped hearing the works. You have not. After a day the grind will become a floor under everything, the hiss a kind of weather, but the knock stays — *chock* — and your body will learn to anticipate it, which is worse than hearing it. Wear your plugs in the works. Wear them on the gangways. Consider wearing them to sleep. When the knock stops for the shift change, take them out and stand still, because that ten minutes of hearing the cane is worth the trip.

*Eyes.* No hard shadows anywhere means your depth perception goes a little soft. Plank edges, the gap between gangway and pile, the lip of a trough — all of it reads flatter than it is. Go slowly for the first day until your eyes recalibrate to a world lit from underneath.

---

**Moving through the town**

Stay on the gangways. Under the gangways is the callus — the cut-and-regrown root-mat — and while it holds the houses, it has been thickened by a century of cutting precisely *where the town is*, and you don't know where that thickness ends. Where a plank has rotted and cane has come up through the gap, the gap is real. Step over it, not on the cane.

You will not be able to see the sky. Some people find this restful and some people find it unbearable within a few hours, and you may not know which you are until you're in it. If it's the second, the places to go are the loading dock — which faces the canal and the one open horizon the town has — and the top of the causeway where it first enters the pan of made ground. Go there when you need to breathe.

Do not pull the cane. Not from a gutter, not from a gangway gap, not from beside your host's door. It is not weeds. Every culm growing into someone's space is a bundle that person will cut and carry to the works in due course, and it's theirs. You may be handed a knife and shown how to cut a bundle, and that is a courtesy worth accepting, but you don't take the initiative.

The houses have oil light only. Bring your own lamp or accept theirs. There is nothing to do in a house after dark except talk, and the talk will be about the works, the strips, the barge, the rain, and the dead people the strips are named for.

---

**In the works**

The works will be shown to you because it is the thing the town has to show. Go in with plugs in and hands in your pockets.

The rollers are set so close a hand would not pass between them, and a hand that tried would not come back. Stay behind whatever rail there is. Stay well clear of the feed and the ram — the ram is on a cam and it does not wait, and the beam it strikes is cupped from a century of being hit by something that does not check first. Do not touch the engine, do not touch the brass, do not lean on the flywheel rail. The floor is wet everywhere and is wetter near the troughs where the juice sheets off, and that juice is slick in a way water isn't.

The drying wing is warm and smells of hay and turpentine and is the only place your clothes will dry. You will be tempted to spend all your time there. Do not sleep on the press-cake; it is fuel, and it's the cat's.

The resin blocks in the loading shed smell wonderful. You may be given a small piece. Keep it wrapped; it will make everything in your bag smell of it for a very long time, which you may or may not want. Don't handle it for hours at a stretch with bare hands — you don't know what it does to skin over time, and the town won't have thought about it.

---

**With the people**

They are not unfriendly. They are *occupied*, in the way people are occupied who have inherited a machine and a rhythm and a job that is simply what is done here. You will be a novelty for about a day and a fixture after that, and the second state is the more comfortable one for everyone.

Do not arrive with questions shaped like accusations. "Where are the frogs?" asked in a certain tone is an accusation, and it will be met with a blank look, not because they're hiding something but because you have asked about the absence of a thing the way one would ask why a room has no ceiling. The cane is what grows here. If you want to understand the absence, watch instead of asking: watch the egret not eat, watch the scum on the tanks, watch what happens to a leaf that falls in the water. Draw your own conclusion and keep it.

Do not offer to fix anything. Do not suggest the engine could be replaced, the causeway raised faster, the thatch removed now that there's sheet over it. Everything here that still works is kept, and the suggestion that it should be otherwise is a kind of rudeness they won't name but will feel.

Ask about the strips. Ask whose names they carry. This is the town's history and its map and its calendar all at once, and someone will be glad to tell you, and you will learn more about the place from which strip is being cut this year than from any other question.

Bring things that are dry and stay dry and can't be made here: good salt, tea, tobacco if they use it, glass, matches sealed in wax, brass fittings, a whetstone, needles that won't rust. Bring things that are *small*. The town has one kind of wealth and it goes down the canal once a month; it will not have much use for currency and you shouldn't assume there is any.

The children know the egret and don't chase it. Follow their lead in this, and in most things about how to move here.

---

**About the absence**

You will notice it after an hour. No birdsong under the machine noise, no frogs, the insects like a rumour. The egret that comes, stands, and leaves. A traveler's instinct is to find this ominous, and it is worth holding that instinct at arm's length while you're there and examining it properly afterward.

It may be the noise. It may be the monoculture — one plant, one root-mat, nothing for anything else to eat. It may be the resin: a liquor that boils down to something smelling of turpentine is a liquor with something in it, and the works has been sheeting juice into the water for a century. Most likely it is all three, braided, and the honest position is that you don't know and won't find out in a short stay.

What you should take from it, practically: don't eat anything from the water because there isn't anything, and if you find something, that's stranger than finding nothing. Don't swim. Don't sit with your bare feet in it at the end of the day the way you might in a marsh anywhere else. Treat the water as a weak solution of an unknown preservative and act accordingly. It may be that this is why the piles last and the plank rots so slowly — the town may be pickled — but that is a comfort for timber and not necessarily for you.

---

**Timing, and leaving**

Plan to stay through at least one shift change so you can hear the cane. Plan to stay through one night so you understand what the knock does to sleep. Plan, if you can, to be there when the barge comes, because the barge is the town's other horizon and the only time the place looks outward, and because it is your easiest way out — down the canal, dry-footed, with the works dwindling behind you.

If you leave by the causeway instead, leave in the morning, with full water, and with your boots already given up for lost. The walk out feels longer than the walk in because you now know exactly how far it is.

Before you go: shake everything out. Check the seams of your bag for growth. Wipe down anything metal and oil it. Change your socks one last time in the drying wing and say goodbye to the cat. Your clothes will smell of hay and turpentine and warm wet leaf for weeks, and for a while after that you will hear — in a quiet room, in the moment before sleep — a wooden knock, four times a minute, that isn't there.

That is normal. It passes. Slower than you'd like.
