**Preparing for the Header Garden**

Start by understanding the shape of the visit, because the shape is the difficulty. It is three things in sequence: a long cold climb, a warm immersion, and a long cold descent made wet. Each part is survivable on its own. The trouble is in the joints between them, and almost everything you carry or decide should be aimed at those joints.

---

**The climb**

The ladder is bolted to a stack of pressure vessels standing on concrete somewhere below the haze. You are climbing to an altitude where your fingers ache and there is rime in the shadow of every flange. Treat that as a mountaineering problem, not a maintenance problem, even though the ladder is called a service ladder.

Get to the base early in the day and rested. Do not begin the climb tired. If there is any way to spend a night at the base before going up, do it; the height is real and you will be exerting yourself in thin air for a long time. Wear gloves that grip cold steel and that you can climb in for hours — thin leather or a grippy synthetic, not bulky insulation. Your hands will hurt. Accept that. Carry a second pair of gloves sealed in a dry bag, because the pair you climb up in will be wet when you come down and wet gloves freeze to riming rungs.

Bring a simple harness and two short lanyards with hooks. Clip to the rungs as you go. Nobody is watching you and nobody will catch you. Rest often, clipped in, and eat and drink while you rest, because the rest of the day will not offer easy chances to do either.

Watch the light. Every hour you spend on the climb is an hour you will not have for the descent, and the descent must not happen in the dark.

---

**The threshold**

The hatch opens straight onto the surface of the water, and there is nowhere to stand. You will be in to your neck before you've decided. Know that going in, and make the decision on the ladder, not in the water.

The problem is your clothes. You climbed in cold-weather layers; you cannot swim in them; and you cannot easily strip on a ladder in freezing thin air. So plan the sequence: pause at the hatch rim, shed your outer shell and pack, and lower yourself in wearing a thin base layer or nothing — the tank is body temperature, and the dome is within arm's reach of the surface, ribbed with I-beams. You can hang your dry bag from one of those beams in the warm humid airspace where you can reach it again. Put in it: your dry clothes, dry gloves, a towel, your water, food, your watch if it isn't waterproof.

On the way out, reverse this inside the warmth before you open the hatch to the cold. Dry off as well as a towel in that humidity lets you. Dress fully. Then open the hatch and go.

The temperature drop at the hatch is not a discomfort; it is the most dangerous moment of the whole visit. You will have been in 37-degree water for hours. Your blood vessels are open, your core is warm, and you are about to hit thin freezing air, wet, and then haul yourself onto a vertical ladder. People faint under those conditions. Sit at the hatch rim, clipped in, for a good while before you commit to the rungs. Let your heart rate settle. Eat something. Only then descend.

---

**The water**

It is the temperature of your own body. This is more strange than it sounds. You cannot cool down in it — no evaporation, no conduction — so any exertion builds heat inside you with nowhere for it to go. Swim slowly. If you feel flushed or your head throbs, stop and float. Your skin will soften and prune after an hour and will be tender after several; there is no fixing this, only limiting it.

You will need to urinate. Warm immersion does this to everyone within an hour or two. Understand what you are floating in: the tank is the city's filter, and what leaves it at the bottom is what the city gets back clean. You would be adding yourself to that. Empty yourself before the climb, drink modestly, and keep the immersion short enough that this does not become a question you have to answer.

Do not drink the water. The upper reaches near the leaves have passed through roots, but the water entering through the penstocks is what a city puts into water, and you have no way of knowing where the gradient sits at any given moment. Bring your own and drink it at the hatch.

---

**Breathing**

The airspace between the surface and the dome is thin — close enough to touch — and it is warm, saturated with humidity, and at the same altitude you climbed to. Every breath is as thin as it was on the ladder, but now it is also wet and hot and you are working. Expect to feel short of breath doing very little. Do not confuse this with panic. It is just the height.

The leaves lie against the underside of the surface, crowded under every glass block. Where you come up for air, you come up among them. They are flat and yield, and you can lift a face through them, but they are dense, and after any time below you will be surfacing through a canopy that has closed over where you went down.

Go under, if you go under, near where you know the surface is open — near the hatch, near an I-beam you can grip. Bring a mask; the stencilled numbers on the turbine casings are legible from the surface and the vascular channels in the stems read like wiring against the light, and you will regret not seeing them clearly. Do not bring fins. They will tangle and bruise the stems and give you speed you should not have here.

---

**Depth**

The tank is the size of a small church and continues a long way down. There are catwalks at three levels. There are turbines on a common shaft at the bottom, turning one revolution every several minutes.

My honest advice is not to descend to them. A blade moving once in five minutes is not a fast blade, but it is an enormous one, and the gap between blade and casing does not care how slowly it closes. The stems lean aside for it. You would not. Below the turbines are the outflow and the resistance coils; the outflow is drawing water down out of the tank, and the coils are electrical elements holding a church-sized volume of water at body temperature on a cable as thick as your arm. You feel their hum in your teeth through the shaft housing. Do not go and find out what you feel through the coils themselves.

If you must go down: not deep, not alone in spirit — meaning, with the way back planned before you leave the surface — and never through the shaft's line. Part the stems with your hands. They are supple and slightly cool and they close behind you without sound, which is beautiful and also means your route is gone the moment you take it. A short thin cord from your wrist to the hatch rim would help at the surface. Below the first catwalk, a cord becomes a thing that can wrap a shaft, and you should not have one.

Freediving at altitude is harder than at sea level. Your breath-hold will be shorter than you expect. Surface early.

---

**Sound, and the one loud thing**

The tank is very quiet, and the quiet is total enough that you will hear your own blood. At some interval you cannot predict, a check valve seats itself with a knock that arrives through your chest rather than your ears. It is startling underwater. It is not danger. Know it is coming so that when it comes you do not inhale.

Everything else you hear, you will be making. The stems release bubbles when you push through them, and the bubbles climb and tick against the glass. That ticking is the loudest thing that happens here, and it happens because of you. Move slowly and it stops.

---

**What you are, to the place**

The tank is sealed against animal life. Nothing has been let in — not a snail, not a larva, not a film on the walls. This is not incidental; it is the design. The garden works because nothing eats and nothing fouls.

You are an animal. You are the first one. You bring skin, hair, sweat, the microbial load of a body, whatever your clothes have carried from wherever you were last. There is nothing in the tank to eat what you shed, so what you shed will drift and catch on stems and hang there, and stay. You cannot make this zero. You can make it small: wash thoroughly before the climb, wear clothes that are clean and simple, take nothing in that can flake or unravel, leave nothing behind, and keep the visit short.

Do not take anything out. Not a leaf, not a cutting, not a root. The plant walks itself from stem to stem along whatever it can hold, and it does this in warm clear water with nothing to stop it. Put it in any other world's water and you have made a decision on behalf of that world. Its whole nature is to fill the space it is given.

And be ready for how it feels to be the mote in a place built to have no motes. There is no other gaze in the tank. Nothing sees you. The quiet you will feel is partly that — the absence of any creature reacting to your presence. Some people find that peaceful. Some find it very lonely. You may find you are both, and that it changes hour to hour.

---

**Time**

The place moves slowly, and it will slow you down with it. The turbines seem stopped. A root you watch for an hour has not moved. The water is warm and holds you, and the light comes down white and cut into squares, and you will float with your face in it among the leaves, and you may fall asleep. This is the gentlest way the garden could kill you, and it is a real way.

Wear a watch you can read wet. Set a hard time to be out of the water and dressed, and set it against the light, not against how you feel — the garden is lit only by daylight through the glass blocks, and when the day goes the tank goes dark and the ladder below the hatch goes dark with it. A wet person descending a frozen ladder at altitude in the dark does not arrive. Two hours in the water is enough to know the place. Three is more than enough. Leave with the sun high.

---

**What to bring**

Little. In a dry bag hung inside from the dome: dry clothes, dry gloves, towel, water, dense food. On your body for the climb: harness, two lanyards, climbing gloves. Into the water: a mask, a watch, and, if it comforts you, a short cord for the surface. Nothing that sheds. Nothing to do. There is nothing to do here, and the visit is better if you do not pretend otherwise.

---

**Disposition**

Go to be still. The garden is a factory that has become a forest with a filter in it, and the plants seem to know this in their patience, and the only way to see what they have done is to match their speed for a while — to float and watch the shaft's paint stripe crawl, and the yellowed stems lean into their neighbours, and the light lose its edges in the water. You will not see a root move. You will believe that it moves, and that belief is what the place offers.

Then get out while it is bright, dress in the warmth, sit at the hatch until your body is your own again, and go down slowly with dry hands. The garden does not need you and will not notice you leave. Take that as the kindness it is.
