Most of what you need to know about preparing for this place follows from one fact: nothing that happens there is ever undone. That single principle governs the gear, the conduct, and the state of mind. Everything else is detail.

**Getting there, and getting back out**

Before you reach the room you have to cross the plateau above it, and the plateau is the more dangerous half of the trip in any conventional sense. Dry rock, sun that works like a kiln, wind that finishes the job. Approach in the early morning or the last hours before dark. Wear a brimmed hat, cover your arms, and carry at least twice the water you think you'll want, because you will drink half of it before you go underground and you will need the rest in a way I'll explain.

The entrance is a slot in a rockfall, and the passage beyond it turns twice. Rockfall passages are not corridors. Expect to stoop, then to crawl, then to stoop again, over and around blocks that came down in one piece and have not been tidied. Basalt that cooled fast and then sheared under pressure leaves conchoidal edges — the same kind of edge that made flint useful to whoever carried that stone in — and some of them will be at the height of your hands and knees. Wear a helmet. Wear gloves with a real palm. Wear trousers you don't mind sacrificing at the knee. Use a light in the passage; this is not the part where you practise seeing in the dark, this is the part where you don't open your scalp.

Memorise the turns going in. Two turns is not many, but in absolute darkness two turns is enough to get you facing the wrong wall in a dead end, and the chamber's only exit is a slot you may not be able to find by feel if you've wandered from it. Note which way you turned and how far apart the turns were. On the way out you will be going from a room where you have deliberately let your eyes forget what they were for into a passage where you need them again, so carry the light on a lanyard, not in a pocket you'll have to hunt through.

Someone should be on the surface who knows when you went in and when you expect to come out. This is ordinary caving practice and it matters here more than usual, for reasons that have nothing to do with rockfall and everything to do with what the room does to your sense of time.

**Air, dust, and what you'll be breathing**

The chamber is an old gas pocket with a single narrow entrance around two bends. It is not sealed, but it is very close to still, and the description tells you exactly how still: your own footstep raises dust that is still hanging an hour later. That is an atmosphere with essentially no exchange. Two consequences.

First, the dust. It is the fine product of rock ground against rock and it has been lying undisturbed for a very long time, which means it is the finest fraction — the particles that never settled out. You will raise it with every step and every step's worth will stay in the air at head height for the remainder of your visit, and you will breathe it. Bring a fitted particulate respirator — an N95 at minimum, a P100 if you have one — and put it on before you enter the room, not after. You will be tempted to take it off because it makes noise and because the place seems to want your face bare. Leave it on.

Second, the gas. Basalt is not a strong radon source and the pocket's original volcanic gas is long gone, but the near-total stillness means your own exhaled carbon dioxide has nowhere to go either. Over a few hours alone this is not dangerous. Over a long watch, with two people, it can start to become the kind of thing that gives you a headache you attribute to the silence. If you own a small CO₂ meter, bring it. If you don't, know the signs — headache, a sense of breathlessness that doesn't match your exertion, a feeling that the air has thickened — and treat them as your cue to leave, not as an interesting phenomenon.

**Heat and water**

The room is at something close to the temperature of a held hand, and it never varies. This sounds comfortable and it is, which is the problem. When ambient temperature approaches skin temperature, your body loses the ability to shed heat by simple conduction and relies almost entirely on evaporation. The air is dry enough to crack lips, so evaporation works beautifully — you will not feel hot, you will not feel damp, and you will be losing water steadily the entire time you are there. You will not feel thirsty either, because thirst lags, and because the place has a way of making the body's small signals seem like commotion best ignored.

So: drink on a schedule, not on demand. Every half hour by the watch. Bring lip balm and use it before you go in. Bring more water than the length of your planned stay justifies. If you leave with water still in the bottle, good.

Drink through the respirator's edge or lift it briefly and replace it. Do not eat inside. A crumb dropped here is a permanent addition to the room, and there is nothing to carry it away.

**Light, and the discipline of not using it**

The whole character of the chamber is built on darkness so complete that light arrives only as a memory, perceptible at the edge of vision after your eyes have adapted. Full dark adaptation takes thirty to forty-five minutes and is destroyed in a second by a phone screen. If you want to see the place as the description sees it — the waxy sheen on the fracture faces, the pale bloom giving back the not-light — you have to commit to the time.

Bring a headlamp with a red mode and a very low white mode, and treat white light as an emergency tool. Cover every other screen with tape. Turn off anything that has an indicator LED. Switch your watch to a face that doesn't glow, or wear an analogue one with a hinged cover.

Then stand near the entrance, kill the light, and wait. The first twenty minutes will be nothing. This is normal and it is the first lesson the place teaches. Do not fill the nothing by moving around.

**Sound**

You will make more noise than you have ever made in your life. A zip, a swallowed sip, the crinkle of a jacket, the respirator's exhale valve — each of these will sound like something dropped in a church. There is no way to prevent this entirely, but you can reduce it: soft-shell clothing rather than nylon, no velcro, no metal fittings that touch other metal fittings, a watch that doesn't tick, everything electronic silenced, not on vibrate. Move your arms slowly enough that fabric doesn't hiss.

You should also know what you will hear when you are finally still. Your own pulse, first in your ears and then, as it seems, in the room. Your breathing. Possibly a high tone that has always been in your ears and that you have never had a quiet enough moment to notice — most people have a little tinnitus and most never find out. None of this is the place. All of it is you. Once or twice you may hear a genuine external sound, the tick of a grain of bloom letting go of the wall, and it will be shockingly loud. Don't startle. Startling raises dust.

**What not to touch, and why**

Everything, really, but there are four things in particular.

The white mineral bloom on the walls is where the water is. It is also chalk-and-metal to the taste, which tells you it is some mix of carbonates and evaporite salts, possibly with things in it you don't want on your skin and certainly don't want in your mouth. Don't touch it. Don't lick anything. If you must lay a palm on the north wall to feel the exchange the description talks about, find a patch of bare basalt, and know that your handprint will be there — a faint smear of skin oil on a surface that has not been touched since the roof came down — for as long as the room exists.

The roots hanging from the ceiling seam are dry and papery and anchored in whatever moisture the deep rock hoards. They are the plateau's shrubs reaching down at a rate that took decades. Brushing one will snap the lateral threads it has spread across the bloom. Give them a wide berth; the room is twenty paces across and you don't need to be under them.

The seedling below them, colourless and thin as a wick, is the single most fragile thing in the chamber. It has spent everything it had and is holding. It may have been holding for twenty years. It is on the floor, in the dust, in the dark, roughly under the roots, and if you are walking without a light you will not see it. Do not walk in that part of the room at all.

The lichen on the far wall at knee height is the only clear evidence of the room's antiquity, because it grows by a hair's width in a lifetime and has grown to the size of a hand. If you touch it you will remove, in one second, several centuries of what it did. Keep your hands off the walls below waist height everywhere.

And the flint. It is half sunk in dust at the eastern end, near the entrance — which means near where you will be standing. It has three flakes off one edge and it is the only thing in the room that is not exactly where the world put it. Someone knelt there. Do not pick it up, do not turn it to look at the worked edge, do not brush the dust from it to see it better. Look at the spine that's showing, and let that be enough. If you are carrying anything you might set down — a bottle, a light — set it on the rubble of the entrance and not on the floor.

**Where to put your feet**

You will leave footprints and they will never fill in. Accept this and then minimise it. Wear soft, clean soles. Enter and leave by the same line of steps. Do not cross the room to look at the lichen close up — the lichen is grey, brittle, no bigger than a spread hand, and you know what it looks like already. Go in as far as the entrance rubble and perhaps a few paces beyond, stop, and stay stopped. A person who stands in one place raises dust once. A person who wanders raises it continuously and leaves a scrawl across a sheet that has no ripple in it.

Don't sit in the dust; a sit-print is worse than a footprint. If you want to sit, sit on a block of the fallen roof near the slot. Don't bring a mat, which leaves a rectangle and sheds fibres. Don't wear fleece, which sheds fibres. Everything that comes off you — hair, skin, lint — is a deposit. You cannot stop it. You can wear a hat and long sleeves and keep it small.

**The clock**

Set an alarm before you go in. A silent one, a vibration against the wrist, for a duration you decide on the surface in daylight when your judgement is your own. Two hours is generous. Three is a long watch. When it goes off, you leave, and you do not negotiate with yourself about it.

The reason is in the last paragraph of the description and it is not a metaphor. The room is at body temperature, it is silent, it is dark, it has no day or night, and every signal your body uses to keep time — light changes, temperature drift, ambient sound, the sensation of the air moving — is absent. You are the only clock in there and you are a bad one. Sensory deprivation of this kind reliably produces time distortion within an hour and can produce mild hallucination — flashes at the periphery, a sense of the walls moving, a conviction that someone else is present — within two or three. These are not dangerous in themselves. What is dangerous is that they arrive alongside a steady, pleasant loss of urgency. The place's whole gravity is toward stillness. Your breathing slows toward the rock's, your eyes stop looking, and the part of you that would normally say *it's time* has gone quiet along with everything else. You will not decide to stay too long. You will simply not decide to leave. The alarm decides for you.

Have your surface partner treat a missed rendezvous as a real problem, not a lapse. Dehydration in warm dry air plus mild CO₂ plus the psychology of the room is a combination that could plausibly leave someone sitting on a block of basalt in the dark, comfortable, unworried, and in genuine trouble.

**Coming out**

The passage will feel loud and bright before you have even reached the first turn, and the grey at the end of it — the memory of light — will look like full day. Take the two turns slowly. Stop at the slot for a minute before you go through. Have sunglasses in a pocket you can reach with your eyes shut, and put them on before you emerge onto the plateau, because a plateau under a kiln sun after three hours of true dark is not an experience you want to have with unprotected eyes.

Then drink. Then sit in whatever shade there is and let your body remember that it has a temperature of its own and a pulse it doesn't need to listen to. The transition back is abrupt in a way the transition in was not. Give it half an hour.

**A last word on what you're doing**

You are going into a room that has finished happening, and you are an event. There is no way to visit without changing it — your footprints, your dust, your breath's moisture on the walls, your skin oil, your carbon dioxide slowly diffusing out through two turns of passage over the following weeks. The best you can do is be a small event, a single line of steps in and the same line out, a period of standing still, and then absence. Whoever left the flint did roughly that, and it took them one knee-print and one object to become the only history the room has.

Go in wanting nothing from it. Stand still for a long time before there is anything to see. When the watch says leave, leave.
