## Preparing for the Room in the Rock

Start by unlearning the word *cave*. You are picturing cool air, damp stone, the hush of a place underground. Throw all of that away. What you are actually preparing to enter is closer to a chemical plant that has been running unattended for three hundred years, in the dark, at forty-plus degrees, with the ventilation shut off and the workforce still inside. The romance of the description is real, but it is the romance of a blast furnace, and you should prepare as though you were being handed a hardhat and told the furnace is lit.

Read the hazards in the order they will try to kill you.

---

### The Air

This is the one that matters. Everything else is discomfort; the air is injury.

A roost of that density, in a chamber shaped like a bell with a single opening, produces ammonia at concentrations that in ordinary industry would trigger an evacuation. The description calls it "a smell so dense it is nearly a texture" and that is not poetry — that is what several hundred parts per million feels like in the nose and eyes. At that level, unprotected, you will be weeping and coughing within the first breath and your airway will begin to swell within minutes. You cannot tough it out. You cannot hold your breath and dash in for a look. Ammonia dissolves into the wet surfaces of your body — eyes, throat, lungs — and burns them, and the damage continues after you leave.

Then add what the mountain contributes. Those fissures at the far end exhale sulfur. That means some combination of sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and carbon dioxide seeping into a chamber that has almost no airflow. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air and pools low; the floor of that room, where you would be standing, is exactly where it collects. Hydrogen sulfide deadens your sense of smell as it rises, so the moment you stop noticing it is the moment to be afraid. And beneath all of it there is the guano itself, an enormous mass of decomposing organic material that consumes oxygen and gives off its own gases as it works.

You do not know what the mix is. Nobody does. So:

**Carry a multi-gas meter** rated for at least oxygen, ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, and carbon dioxide — sulfur dioxide too if you can get a sensor for it. Calibrate it before you go. Clip it high on your chest where you can see it, set the alarms conservatively, and *obey the alarms without argument*. If the oxygen reading drops or the CO₂ climbs when you approach the fissures, that is the room telling you where its edge is.

**Bring real respiratory protection.** At minimum, a full-face respirator — not a half-mask, because your eyes need the same protection your lungs do — with cartridges rated for ammonia and acid gases, plus a particulate pre-filter for the dust. Better, if you can carry it and know how to use it: a self-contained breathing apparatus, your own air on your back, so that the question of what is in the room's air becomes irrelevant. The SCBA is heavy and hot and limits your time to whatever the bottle holds, but in a space where the atmosphere is genuinely unknown, that limit is a feature. Whichever you choose, fit-test it beforehand. A respirator that leaks at the cheek is decoration.

And understand what the dust is. Every step you take on that floor raises a haze of dried guano, chitin fragments, mite bodies, and fungal spores. The classic disease of bat caves is histoplasmosis, a lung infection from a fungus that thrives in old droppings; the extreme heat and dryness here may suppress it, but "may" is not a word to stake your lungs on. Assume the dust is infectious. Assume everything you breathe unfiltered is a mistake.

### The Heat

The room is hot the way an oven is hot when you open the door, and it is dry. Your body's only defense against dry heat is sweat, and you are about to seal yourself inside a respirator and a coverall that both prevent sweat from evaporating. You will be cooking inside your own protective equipment.

This means your time inside is measured in minutes, not hours. Twenty minutes is a reasonable first ceiling for a healthy adult in full kit; less if you are exerting yourself on the floor, which you will be. Set a timer you can hear or feel — a vibrating watch, because you will not hear a beep — and leave when it goes, regardless of what you are looking at.

Pre-hydrate for a full day before. Drink at the throat before you go in and again the moment you come out. A cooling vest with ice packs under the coverall buys you time, though the ice will not last. Know the early signs of heat exhaustion in yourself — the dizziness, the nausea, the odd chill — because the roar in that room and the dark and the pressure of the mask will mask them until they are advanced. If you feel confused, you are already in trouble. Leave.

Do not go in alone, and do not both go in at once. One person at the throat, in the outer light, holding the line and watching the clock, is what turns a bad moment into an anecdote instead of a body on the floor for the beetles.

---

### The Dark

You have never been in darkness like this. Not "dark" as in a moonless night; dark as in the absence of the concept. The description is precise: your eyes will not adjust because there is nothing to adjust to. What this does to a human mind after a few minutes is not trivial. Balance degrades. The sense of the room's size collapses. The noise, with nothing to anchor it visually, begins to feel like it is inside your head.

Bring three light sources. A headlamp as primary, a second headlamp in a pocket, a small handheld torch clipped to your harness. Fresh batteries in all of them; do not trust anything half-used. Make at least one of them a red light, and use the red light as your default inside. White light in a roost causes panic — bats dropping from the ceiling, wheeling, colliding — and in a chamber where every cubic meter is already occupied, a panicked mass flight would be a storm of small bodies at head height with nowhere to go but through you. Bats are not aggressive, but a frightened one bounced off your face and biting on reflex is how rabies exposures happen. Keep the beam low, keep it red, keep it off the ceiling as much as you can bear.

And one thing to be ready for: when the light does catch the ceiling, it will move. The whole vault will shift and ripple, thousands of eyes returning the beam as a faint glitter. Your body will want to run. Know that in advance, so that when it happens you can name it and stand still.

### The Floor

The floor is the thing people underestimate. It is not ground. It is a deposit of unknown depth — more than your height in places — with a crust on top and a seething layer of insects on and in the crust, and underneath, decades of compacted material that may be soft, may be fermenting and warmer than the air, and certainly conceals the original lava surface with all of its glass edges.

You will break through the crust. Expect to sink to the ankle, possibly to the knee. When you do, what you sink into is warm and dry and alive, and within seconds things will begin climbing your legs. Dermestid beetles are not dangerous to a living, moving person, but they are relentless and they will find every gap in your clothing. The mites and the bat bugs — the roost will have bat bugs, close cousins of bedbugs — will bite. The cockroaches are merely horrible.

Dress for it as you would for a hazmat scene. A disposable coverall with a hood, sealed with tape at wrists and ankles over your gloves and boots. Boots that come to the knee if possible, with thick soles — fresh volcanic glass cuts leather, and a fall onto a hidden edge through that crust could open you to the bone. Heavy gloves over thin ones. Knee pads, because if you go down you go down onto glass. Nothing exposed. Nothing loose.

Test each step before you commit to it. Probe with a pole. Stay within a few body lengths of the entrance throat unless you have a compelling reason to go deeper; the deposit is likely thickest under the densest part of the roost, in the center of the chamber, and the fissures at the far end are where the gas readings will be worst. There is no honor in reaching the back wall.

Tie in. A rope from your harness to an anchor outside the throat is how you find your way out when the dust has fogged your faceplate and the red light shows you nothing but a haze and your sense of direction has dissolved into the roar. It is also how your partner pulls you if you go down. Run it through your hand as you move. Do not let go of it.

---

### The Noise

The description compares it to a turbine hall, and that is the right scale. Sustained exposure at that volume damages hearing, and the ultrasonic component — the part you feel as pressure rather than hear — adds a physical unease that some people find unbearable within minutes. Wear earplugs under your hood. Communication inside will be impossible by voice; agree on rope signals beforehand, simple ones, three tugs for *I'm coming out*, continuous tugging for *pull me*.

The noise also does something the hazard list cannot capture. It removes your solitude. You are not observing a colony; you are inside its metabolism, and it is louder than your own thoughts. Some travelers find this exhilarating. Others find that after five minutes they need to leave, and cannot say why, and that is a legitimate reason. Set the expectation now that leaving early is success, not failure.
