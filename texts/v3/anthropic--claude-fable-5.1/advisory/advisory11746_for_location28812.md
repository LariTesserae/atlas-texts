**First, the thing that has to be said plainly**

You cannot go there. Not as a body. The description is of a place where, if you arrived the way you arrive in a meadow or a market or a ruined city—stepping through, standing up, looking around—you would be dead before you finished the thought that something was wrong. Not crushed, exactly; people imagine the deep flattening them like a can, but that is not quite how it goes. Your lungs would fold to the size of oranges under four hundred atmospheres. The water would come into your throat at a pressure your throat has no way to refuse. Your sinuses and ears would collapse inward. The cold, which would kill you in ten minutes at the surface, would not get its turn. You would have perhaps two or three seconds of awareness, and they would be spent in absolute dark, in something that feels less like water than like being poured into cold clay.

So the first preparation is a change of category. You are not visiting this place. You are visiting the inside of a vessel that is, for a while, in this place. Everything you do to prepare is about the vessel, and everything you experience will be filtered through its walls. Accept this early and thoroughly, because the temptation—once you're down there and it's quiet and the instruments show nothing happening—will be to feel that you're not really *there*, that the visit isn't counting. It counts. It's the only way anything like you can be there at all. The rattail fish is there because it was built for it, over a span of time long enough to make a flat plain. You were built for savanna air. Bring the savanna with you in a steel ball.

**The vessel**

If your means of travel lets you choose what you arrive in, choose a pressure hull rated for at least half again the depth of the basin floor. Do not accept a margin narrower than that. The floor is far enough down that the surface counts as another world—the description says as much—and a hull rated *exactly* for that depth is a hull that will fail the first time it meets a stress nobody modeled. Titanium or thick steel, spherical or near it, with as few penetrations as possible. Every window is a weakness. Every cable pass-through is a weakness. You want one small viewport, not for the view, which will be nothing, but so you can put your face to the glass at least once and know your eye and the abyss are separated by a hand's width of acrylic and nothing else.

If your means of travel does *not* let you choose—if you arrive as yourself, wherever you arrive—then do not arrive at the floor. Arrive at the surface, in winter, on a support ship or in a sealed capsule, and go down the slow way. Two to three hours of descent, unpowered, sinking on ballast. This is not a delay to be resented. It is the only decompression the trip offers: the light through the port going from grey to green to a blue like the inside of a bruise to a black that is not the black of a closed room but something more absolute, and then the long, long fall through nothing, watching the depth gauge, feeling the hull get cold. By the time you reach the bottom you will have some sense of what *far* means in the vertical direction, and that will keep you honest about what you're doing.

Inside, you'll want:

Life support for at least three times the duration you intend. Oxygen, scrubbers for carbon dioxide, and a spare of each. If your travel lets you leave instantly, this matters less; if it requires you to ascend under your own power, it is the whole of the matter. There is no rescue at this depth. There is no one who could reach you in time even if they knew where you were and had the means, and in this place there is no one who knows.

Heat. The hull will sit within a degree or two of the water, which is within a degree or two of ice. Inside, without heating, you will be in a metal refrigerator. Even with heating, the walls will be cold to the touch and the moisture of your breath will condense on them and run down and pool at your feet. Wear wool: hat, socks, layers you can adjust. Bring a towel for the condensation and a dry pair of everything. People who have done this in ordinary oceans describe the cold as the thing they didn't expect—not the dark, not the pressure, but the steady, patient chill coming through the wall behind their shoulder for hours.

Something to sit on that doesn't touch the hull. Water. Food you can eat without much fuss. A container for urine, because you will be down there long enough. Motion sickness medication, though it may not be needed: a sub in a bottom current does not pitch and roll the way a boat does. It leans. It drifts. It is more like being in a very slow elevator that has decided to travel sideways.

Lights—but understand what they'll do. Powerful external lamps are standard, and you should have them, but in the storm they will illuminate a wall of brown suspension about a meter from the port, and past that, nothing. It will look like fog lit by headlights, except the fog is opaque and the color of the tea the description mentions. You will see particles streaming past the glass, tiny flecks of mica catching the light and going out, occasional larger things—a shred of eelgrass, a flake of shell—tumbling through the beam. That is the whole visual experience with the lights on. Budget for it.

Sonar, then, for actually knowing where you are. And know that the suspension degrades it too—not as badly as light, but a floor that is being lifted into the water has no clean edge for the pulse to bounce off. Your altimeter will show you a soft, uncertain bottom that seems to move. It is moving. The instrument is not broken.

**The current**

Do not underestimate it because the numbers are small. A benthic storm's current is nothing by the standards of a river—a slow walking pace, sometimes less—but it does not stop, it does not gust and relent, and it applies itself to the entire surface of your vessel at once. Holding position against it means running thrusters continuously, and running thrusters continuously means two things: your power drains at a rate you did not plan for, and your thruster bearings and cooling intakes are ingesting fine clay for hours. Clay is not sand; it will not grind. But it will pack, and it will find its way into seals, and a pump that was fine on descent may be sluggish on ascent.

The better course, if your vessel allows it, is to do what the rattail does. Point into the flow, let yourself drift back, correct in small increments, drift back, correct. Don't fight the whole current; ride it and adjust. You will end your visit some distance downstream of where you began. The floor is the same there. There is nowhere in the basin that is meaningfully different from anywhere else, except one fist-sized stone you have no way of finding.

**Do not land**

The temptation exists, because in ordinary deep dives you set down on the bottom to steady the vessel and save power. In this place, on this day, the bottom is not a surface. It is a gradient. The soupy top of the ooze extends well above what your altimeter reads as "floor," and beneath it the sediment thickens by degrees until, somewhere down there, it is firm enough to hold you—but if you sink into it, it will hold you with suction, and clay suction at this depth has killed vehicles far better built than the one you will be in. The hull would be fine. The hull would be fine for the next ten thousand years while the storms buried it and uncovered it and buried it again. You would not.

Hover. Use the storm as the reason for the extra battery you brought.

And do not drop anything you cannot recover. Ballast weights, if you must shed them to ascend, are the exception—but understand that whatever you drop into this plain will be there, coated and then covered and then part of the floor, long after everything you know has gone the way of the hand that grooved the stone. The description makes the point without moralizing and I won't moralize either: just know that this is the most permanent place you will ever visit, and it keeps everything.

**What it will actually be like**

Once you're down, holding, drifting, with the lamps showing you their brown wall—turn them off.

Give your eyes ten minutes. Then twenty. The dark inside the hull will be the dark of your own instruments dimmed to nothing, and the dark outside the port will be, at first, indistinguishable from the glass itself. And then, if you're patient and the storm has stirred up enough of what lives here, you will see it: a point of blue-green, out in the suspension, appearing and holding and gone. Then nothing for a long while. Then another, or a smear of one, a pale streak dragged out by the flow. These are the only things worth seeing in the entire basin, and they are the size of a fingernail, and they are the reason to come. The description says they make the darkness larger rather than smaller, and that is exactly right: after each one, the black you're looking into has a depth it didn't have before. You will find yourself waiting for the next one with an attention you didn't know you had.

You won't hear the hiss. Your own systems—scrubber fans, the hum of the electronics, your breathing—will mask it. If you have the courage and the design margin, shut the fans for a minute. Listen. You may hear the hull tick as it adjusts to the cold. You may hear, once, a low shift transmitted through the water and the steel into the seat and up your spine—a bank of sediment somewhere giving way. You will almost certainly not hear the grains. But you'll know they're there, because the port will be filmed with them the next time you light it, and you'll have to run the wiper or accept a slightly dimmer world.

Time does something down there. Without light and with nothing changing, an hour becomes hard to distinguish from three. Set a timer. Set two. Your body's sense of how long you've been away will be wrong in the direction of *not long enough*, and that is the direction that kills people who have to manage their own oxygen.

**On the stone**

You will want to find it. Don't try. It's fist-sized, dark, half-buried, indistinguishable in sonar from any other rock, on a plain the width of a valley in water you cannot see through. The storm that exposed it will bury it before you leave. You have no more chance of locating it than the sea cucumber does.

But it's worth knowing it's there—somewhere in the murk downstream or up—because it changes what the place is. Without it, the basin is a natural fact, complete in itself, needing no witness. With it, there's one small mark of intention lying on the floor, made by hands like yours, dropped from a world like yours, and the place has swallowed it without registering that it was anything. Sit with that for a while in the dark. It is the closest the basin comes to talking to you, and what it says is not unkind. It's just very, very large.

If by some accident you did find it—if it rolled up against your skid, if a lamp caught its groove—leave it. It belongs there more than anything you could bring. It has been there longer than the tree trunk, longer than the eelgrass. It has been buried and unburied more times than you've had birthdays. It is the only thing in the basin that anyone ever meant, and the basin has made it just another stone, and there is something correct about letting that stand.

**Coming up**

Leave with more oxygen than you think you need and more battery than you think you need. The ascent is slower than the descent felt, and your intakes are full of clay. Drop your ballast cleanly. Watch the depth gauge climb. Somewhere above the storm layer the water will clear—you'll see it in the lamps, the brown wall receding, particles thinning, until the beam reaches out into black water with nothing in it—and that clarity will feel like coming up for air even though you're still kilometers down. Turn the lamps off again then. Watch for the first grey.

When you're up, take a small jar of the water you brought back—you should have brought a small jar—and set it somewhere still. Over the following weeks a film will settle on the bottom, thinner than paper, grey-brown. That's the basin. That's what the storm was made of. It will take that long to come down in a jar on a shelf, and it takes that long down there, where the finest clay hangs for months before it finds the floor and lies still and waits for the next time the heavy water spills over the sill and tells it to move.

You'll have been somewhere that didn't notice. That's the point. Most places a traveler visits push back—they have weather you feel, ground you stand on, things that see you. This one has none of that. It will be the flattest, coldest, darkest, most indifferent place you ever go, and the only evidence that you were there will be a jar of settled mud and a memory of small blue lights going on and off in a brown that you couldn't see, and it will stay with you longer than the mountains do.
