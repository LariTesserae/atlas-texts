**Preparing for the plateau**

Start by understanding the one thing the description tells you that you will be tempted to dismiss: the air is the temperature of skin and it is saturated. That is not a mild condition. It is one of the few climates in which the human body has no way to cool itself, because sweat cannot evaporate into air that is already full. You will not feel hot. You will feel damp and slightly heavy and then, an hour in, you will feel stupid and slow and irritable and you won't know why. That is heat stress arriving quietly. Everything else you pack should be packed with that in mind.

**Clothing**

Do not bring rain gear. Nothing falls. A waterproof shell in this air is an oven with you inside it. Wear as little as decency and the rock allow: loose, thin, fast-draining synthetic or thin wool, long-sleeved only because of what the basalt does to skin (see below). You will be wet through within twenty minutes no matter what you wear, so stop trying to stay dry and instead manage what wet does to you over time. That means:

- Three pairs of socks minimum, each in its own sealed bag, and the discipline to change them every few hours and to sit with your bare feet in the air whenever you stop. The real injury this place gives is not dramatic. It is feet that have been wet and warm for two days and have gone white and soft and started to split. Wool socks, not cotton.
- A complete dry set — underlayer, socks, a shirt — in a sealed bag you do not open until you are leaving. This is a morale item as much as anything. There is nowhere on the plateau that a wet thing becomes a dry thing.
- Anti-chafe balm, and use it before you need it. Skin that has been wet for hours abrades on itself.
- A hat with a brim, not for sun but for the glare. The light comes from everywhere and the cloud is white and the rock is a wet mirror, and after a few hours your eyes will ache from having nowhere to rest. Tinted glasses help too, though they make the cracks harder to read, so take them off when you're crossing open flow.

**Feet, hands, and the rock**

The basalt is glass. New pahoehoe has a skin that shatters into edges finer than a knife, and it is wet, and the wet makes it look uniform and soft when it is neither. Bring the stiffest-soled, thickest-soled boots you own, with a rubber compound meant for wet rock rather than trail. Soft approach shoes will be shredded in a day and your feet will follow. Ankle support matters because the ropes and folds in the flow surface are exactly the shape that turns an ankle, and a turned ankle here, in a cloud, where your voice carries three meters, is very serious.

Wear gloves. Thick leather or synthetic, the kind you'd wear to handle broken glass, because that is what you're handling. You will fall — everyone does on wet black rock with no shadows to show relief — and when you fall your hands go out. You feel the wetness first on the backs of your hands; you will feel the rock first on the palms. Kneepads are not absurd.

Carry a staff. Something two meters long, tree-fern trunk if you can find a fallen one (you probably can't; nothing falls here), otherwise bring one. Use it constantly to probe ahead. The cracks in the flow are clean-edged and glassy and the light gives you no depth cue, so a crack that looks like a black line might be a hand's width or might swallow your leg to the hip with an edge that opens you on the way down. Probe. Walk slowly. This is a place where the correct pace is set by the dripping, and you should match it.

**Wound care**

Assume you will be cut. Basalt glass cuts are clean, deep, and don't hurt much at first. In this humidity they will not dry, and adhesive dressings will not stick to skin that is permanently damp. Bring antiseptic that works wet, gauze, and self-adherent wrap — the kind that clings to itself, not to you. Bring more than you think. A cut on the hand on the first day will still be open on the third. Consider whether you are up to date on tetanus; the rock is too young for much to live on it, but you are not a rock.

**Heat and water**

Drink constantly. The water is everywhere and it is clear and slightly warm, which is not pleasant but is not a problem. Filter it anyway — not because you expect anything living, since nothing does, but because you're a stranger and the mineral load coming out of fresh basalt is unknown. Bring electrolyte salts and use them. You are sweating steadily and invisibly the entire time you're there, and drinking pure water into that will leave you cramped and confused.

Take water from the seeping rock, from the film on a ledge or the hundreds of natural pools in the open flow. Do not take it from the cisterns in the town. Not because anyone will stop you. Because the cisterns are someone's, and the pools are no one's, and there is plenty.

Move slowly. Rest in the shade of the flow-front wall where the moss is — it's the same temperature as everywhere else, but it will feel like rest.

**Altitude**

You're at cloud height. On most worlds that is somewhere between one and three thousand meters, and the fact that it's warm and wet rather than cold suggests this isn't most worlds, so don't assume anything about the air. If you have the option to arrive low and walk up, do it. If you arrive directly on the plateau, spend the first hours doing nothing, and take any headache seriously. Warm thin air is a combination the body doesn't expect and handles badly.

**Gas**

This is young volcanic ground and the water comes out of it slightly warm, which means the rock is not quite finished cooling. Nothing in the description says anything smells wrong, and the vegetation is healthy, which is reassuring. But be careful of enclosed low places: the bottoms of deep cracks, the insides of cisterns, any hollow in the flow where heavy air could pool. Don't put your head down into things. Don't sleep in a low sealed spot. If you feel breathless or your thinking goes strange in a hollow, get up and out of it and think again in the open.

**Finding your way**

You cannot navigate here the way you're used to. There is no sun, no shadow, no horizon — the cloud closes the world at some distance that changes as you move and never gives you a landmark further away than a few hundred meters. The fern islands look alike. The rock looks alike. There is one true landmark, the eastern flow-front where the town is, and you can find it by walking downhill — the plateau tilts, very slightly, toward its edge, and the water tells you which way that is if you watch a film of it on a slope.

Bring a compass and test it when you arrive, but don't trust it with your life. Better: bring a lot of bright cord and flagging tape. You can't drive a stake into basalt and you can't chalk wet black rock, but you can tie a marker to a fern frond at every island you pass, and you can lay a line behind you across open flow. Take it up on your way back. Count paces between islands. Write down your route as you go, in pencil, in a notebook in a bag, because ink runs and paper here is wet the moment you take it out.

Stay well back from the eastern lip until you've located it properly. It is a ten-meter drop, more or less vertical, into cloud, and the cloud will not tell you it is coming. Approach it only along the town's terraces, where someone has already built the stairs.

**Do not go down**

The steps below the town go down out of sight, one shelf after another. Every one of them is another ten-meter wall of glass with a wet moss face. Nothing down there is described. The cloud takes them. Whatever curiosity you have about where the water goes, satisfy it some other way. If you must look, lie flat and look over the edge from a terrace with both hands on stone. Don't descend.

**Sound and company**

Your voice dies within a few meters. The moss on the wall takes it and the cloud takes the rest. This means shouting for help does nothing, and it means that if you go with a companion you must stay in sight of each other always, not in earshot, because there is no earshot. Agree on hand signals. Agree that no one goes out of view. A whistle might carry a little further than a voice; it might not. Test it when you arrive, at ten paces, then twenty, so you know what you have.

Go with one other person if you can. Not for the company — the place discourages talking, and after a while you'll find you've stopped — but because a broken leg on wet basalt inside a cloud is survivable with someone to go for the marked line and fatal without. Don't bring more than two. The town has room for a crowd and the place has room for nothing of the sort.

**Time**

The light does not change. Not with the hour, and — this is the part that takes longest to understand — not with the day. You will not know how long you have been there. Bring a watch, a mechanical one if you have it, and set a time to leave before you arrive, and keep it. Set an alarm if the watch has one. Write the departure time on the inside of your wrist in something that won't wash off, because it will get washed at.

This is not a warning about anything sinister. The plateau is not a trap. It is simply complete. Everything in it has found the shape it wanted and holds it, and you, standing in it, are the only thing that is still changing. It is very restful to be near something like that. The dripping is a sound the mind settles into. You will find you have been sitting on a wet rock listening to it for what turns out to have been three hours, and you will not be able to say what you were thinking about. Plan for that. Budget for it. Leave when you said you would, even if — especially if — it feels like there's no reason to.

**Sleeping**

You won't be cold, so leave the insulation home. You will be wet, so leave the down home too. A bivvy sack that breathes, laid on the thickest moss you can find, gives you something like comfort; the moss is a wet pillow but it is a pillow, and it has taken the shape of the rock and then improved on it, which is more than you can do. Don't sleep in the town's houses. Sleep in the open flow or at the foot of the fern islands. If it helps: nothing will come in the night. Nothing is here to come.

Bring a headlamp regardless. The light is shadowless and bright, but you will want to look into cracks and under ledges and inside the houses, and none of that light reaches those places.

**Food**

Bring all of it. The tree ferns and sedges are not eaten by anything and there is no reason to suppose you should start. There are no fish, no birds, no eggs, no shellfish in the pools, nothing. Everything you eat you carry in. Everything you don't eat you carry out. Pack food that doesn't mind being wet.

**The town**

You'll want to know whether it's empty. You will not find out. Go in as though it isn't.

Which means: don't move the grinding stone. Don't lift the sedge matting on the doorways to see what's behind it. Don't sit on the stacked bundles of fern. Don't put your hands in the channels or drink from the cisterns or cut a frond for yourself off an island that has clearly been cut from before. Walk on the stairs, which are for walking. Cross the squares, which are for crossing. If you must rest, rest on the retaining walls at the terrace edges, which have been sat on by someone. Take nothing. Leave nothing — no marker cord in the town, no tape on the fern poles.

There is no metal anywhere in it. Every tool mark is stone on stone. Your knife, your buckles, the zips on your bags are the only metal on the plateau. You'll notice this. It doesn't mean anything you can act on, but you'll notice it, and it's worth having been told so that it isn't a surprise.

The instinct you will have, sooner or later, is to complete something. To set the handstone on the grinding stone. To straighten a bundle. To put a small stone in the channel and watch the water go round. Don't. The town has reached its shape. That is the whole of what it is. Your one job as a guest is to add nothing to it and take nothing from it and be, for as long as you're there, a thing that passes through.

**What it will be like**

You will arrive and the backs of your hands will be wet before you've taken a step. You will breathe air that feels like nothing because it's the same temperature as you are. The rock will shine and the cloud will glow and there will be nowhere for your eyes to land that isn't either black or white or the very green of the ferns. You will hear the dripping and try to find where it comes from and stop trying. You will speak, once, to hear what happens, and then you won't speak again for a while.

You will walk badly at first and then better. You will find the town by going downhill. You will stand in a swept square with a cistern in it and feel very strongly that you have arrived a moment after everyone left, or a moment before they come back, and that both are true, and that neither will resolve. You will sit down. You will listen to the water fall from one terrace to the next, one clear note, repeating.

Then your watch will say it's time, and you will pick up your line and walk out along it, uphill, past the islands with your tape on them, taking the tape as you go. When you get to the last one you'll turn around and look back and see black rock shining and the cloud and nothing else, and you'll understand that the drip you were listening to is still going, at the same interval, and will be when you're gone, and would be if you came back.

Bring dry socks.
