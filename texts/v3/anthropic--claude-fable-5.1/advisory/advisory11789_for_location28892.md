**Preparation notes for the flooded hollow**

Start with the most important thing, which is that this is a place you can arrive at by accident, and the accident is the dangerous part. Everything else about the room — the folded geometry, the sounds that come early, the eel — is strange but not, in itself, hostile. The turf is hostile. The turf is a field that continues, green and unbothered, straight across the top of a room full of cold water, and it will hold a rabbit and it will not hold you.

So the first preparation is for the field, not the hollow.

**Finding it without falling into it**

You are looking for a circle of ground about fourteen paces around, so roughly four or five strides across. It will not look different. It will not sound hollow when you stamp — it is not hollow, it is full to the underside. What you can look for is small: grass a shade paler or a shade more lush than its neighbours, because its roots are in water. In cold weather, dew or frost sitting differently, because there is a body of water a hand's breadth under the surface holding a steady cellar temperature while the soil around it freezes or warms. In hot weather, a place where the ground is cool to a palm laid flat. Cattle or deer, if there are any, will have walked around it without knowing why, and there may be a faint worn arc in the grass.

Carry a pole. Something long, light, blunt-ended. Walk the field probing ahead of you the way you would cross a bog, and when the pole goes through — and it will go through suddenly, turf then nothing then water then weed — stop. Do not step forward to look. The lip of the fracture is a clean break, sharp as a snapped bone, and it is a hand's breadth under the water, and if you go through the turf you will go past it, and it will open your leg or your palm on the way.

Mark the edge for yourself while you are there. Not permanently — I will come to that — but for the duration. Four sticks pushed into the sound ground around it will do.

If you have companions, do this together and do not let anyone walk ahead. If you are alone, understand that nobody will find you. From a few paces away there is nothing to see. A rescuer standing directly over you would be standing on grass.

**Deciding whether to go in**

Be honest with yourself here, because the room will not help you decide. It is a complete place. It does not need visiting. It has its two marks in the silt, its eel, its snail on its stone, its slow two-year breath, and it is entirely occupied. Whatever you would learn by entering, you cannot bring out except in yourself.

Most travelers should stop at the turf. Kneel at the edge you found with your pole, put a hand through into the water, feel the plants press up against your fingers from underneath — they are right there, packed to the surface, there is no gap — and feel the cold, and taste the water off your fingertips. Iron, and green. That is most of it. That is an honest visit.

If you go further, understand what you are doing in plain terms. You are entering a fully flooded chamber with no air space, in total darkness, packed wall to wall with vegetation dense enough that you will be pushing through stems rather than swimming through water, with a bottom you must not touch and walls you must not hold, in which distance does not add up and cause does not reliably precede effect. In ordinary worlds, people who enter flooded caves with far fewer of these problems die in them at a rate that has made it a byword for lethal hobbies. Nothing about the strangeness here makes it safer.

**If you go in anyway**

*Breathing.* You cannot hold your breath for this. Nothing in the room happens quickly enough to be done on one lungful, and the whole danger is that you will try to hurry. Bring air. A small cylinder, the smallest that gives you a real margin — the room has no space in it, and every object you carry is one more thing it must make room for, and you will feel that pressure physically as the weed closes around your kit. A regulator you trust with your eyes shut. Practice breathing on it in the dark, on your back, with a wet blanket over your face, for longer than you think you can stand. The panic reflex when plants press against your mouth and eyes in total blackness is the thing that will kill you, not the cold and not the geometry. It has to be worn down beforehand. There is no way to learn calm in there.

*Cold.* It is not bitter. It is a few degrees below comfortable and it does not change, and that steadiness is worse than a shock, because you will not notice yourself going slow. A drysuit if you have one. Thick neoprene if you don't. Gloves without fail, and not thin ones — the fracture edges are sharp, and the moss on the walls is thick enough that you will not know where the moss stops and the raw rock begins. Gloves also for the nail, which I will come to.

*Light.* Bring one. Expect it to disappoint you. Nothing in this room has ever been lit, and there is a real chance that lighting it shows you very little — a few inches of green stem against the mask, the pale undersides of roots, and then more stems — and a smaller chance that it shows you nothing at all, or that you find you do not want to switch it on. The plants are green; the description is quite clear on that. It is also clear that no one will ever check. You may find, once you are inside, that you have joined the people who don't. Whatever you see or fail to see, do not treat the light as your way out. Your way out is the line.

*The line.* A guideline, tied to something outside the hollow that will not move — a stake driven deep into the field, a companion who will not let go. This is the single most important object you carry, and it is the one most likely to behave oddly, so I want to be exact about how to treat it.

Do not count strokes. Do not count anything. The room is larger inside than the field allows, and the count across will not match the count back, and neither will match the third time. The stems along the wall repeat. If you try to navigate by anything you measure, you will be measuring something that does not hold still.

The line, though, is a single thing that goes from where you are to where you were, and it does not care about the room's opinion of distance. Keep it in your hand. Do not let it go to reach for anything. If you follow it out and arrive at what feels like the wrong wall — the wall you started from, but from its far face, as the description puts it — keep following. The line has not lied to you; the room has just folded around it. You may pass a place where your line seems to cross itself among the stems, the way the stalks grow up and down from a middle that is not fixed. Do not try to sort out which strand is which. Keep your hand closed and keep moving toward tension. The line goes out. That is what it is for.

Give yourself a time limit measured on a watch, not on air, because the air will last longer than your judgment, and keep it absolutely. Ten minutes is enough. It is a small room. Nothing in it will do anything in ten minutes that it would not also do in ten years.

*Where to put your body.* Nowhere, ideally. Go in at the lip and go no further than a body's length. Do not touch the floor. The silt has barely had time to be silt and will rise in a blind cloud if you put a knee in it, and somewhere in it, point up, is an iron nail that has not rusted, driven from beneath, and you will not see it and you will find it with a hand or a knee. Do not hold the walls. The moss is deep and the rock under it is sharp and you will be gripping a soft thing over an edge.

If you must be still — and you will want to be, and that is the right instinct — hang from the line, neutral, and let the weed take your weight. It will. It is packed dense enough to hold you. Let the stems lie against your suit and your mask and don't push them away; there is nowhere for them to go.

**Things in the room to leave alone**

There is one channel through the weed, and you will feel it if you drift into it: a lane where the stems all lean a fraction the same direction, worn by something long and dark passing along it many times. It is the only clear path in the room and it will be very tempting to use it. Don't. It is the eel's, and it is older than the room. You will not see the eel and you will not feel it pass, and it is not, so far as anything tells me, dangerous. But the path is worn to its body and not yours, and it is the one thing in this place that has somewhere to be.

At the bottom there is a dressed stone with a groove across one face, and in the groove a snail the size of a fingernail, moving in the direction the vanished feet went. If you reach the stone — and you should not, it is on the floor — do not stand on it. Do not touch the snail. Do not, whatever kindness rises in you, move it along. It has come the width of your thumb since the water rose. It will arrive. That is its whole business here and it is not yours to finish.

**The sounds, and what they mean for you**

You will hear things before they happen. A dry tick in the wall, and then the stone that made it lets go. A drop landing somewhere in the root-mat above, rings spreading through the roots, and then the drop. It is disorienting, and the disorientation is a danger in itself because your body will want to react to the sound, and reacting is movement, and movement here is the problem.

Here is the practical rule: react to the second thing, not the first. When you hear it, wait. The event is coming. Let it come. Then, if you need to respond, respond.

There is a consequence you should be ready for. At some point, if you are going to leave, you may hear yourself leaving — the sound of your own body coming up through the turf, water sheeting off, a gasp in air — before you have started to. Do not be frightened of this. It is the best sound the room can give you. It means you are going. Follow the line and let it be true.

**On stillness**

The last line of the description is the one to hold onto. The room makes room for a person who steps in and closes the door and stands still. Not for one who searches, or measures, or swims across to see the other wall. The one thing every living thing in the hollow agrees on is pace — the weed thickening by a hair, the snail crossing a groove, the eel on a route worn over a time longer than the room, the whole place breathing in over a year and out over the next. You are the only fast thing that will ever be in it. Your heartbeat is a violence there.

So the preparation that matters most is not equipment. It is practice at being a slow, cold, dark, still thing for a while, with something soft pressed against your face and nothing to see, breathing evenly through a mouthpiece, and not minding. Do this at home until you can do it without your pulse rising. If you cannot, you are not ready, and the hand through the turf at the lip is your visit, and it is a good one.

**Timing**

There is a cycle, roughly two years long, that is not the field's seasons. At its low point the water near the stone tastes of dust; at its high, the roots let down a pale drift that never reaches the floor. You cannot read the phase from above with any confidence. If you have any way of knowing, aim for the middle of the exhale — after the drift has stopped, before the dust begins. The drift is harmless as far as I can tell, but it will be in your mask and on your regulator and you will taste it, and the dust-taste, when it comes, is the stone remembering a dry place, and there is something in that memory that you may not want in your mouth.

Do not drink the water. You will anyway, a little, around the mouthpiece. Iron and green. It stays with you for days. That is fine.

**Leaving, and after**

Come out along the line, slowly, through the root-mat, and expect the turf to resist you from below the way it gave way from above. Do not haul yourself out on the fracture lip. Put your hands wide, on the grass, on sound ground.

Take everything with you. Pull your stakes. Coil your line. Leave nothing in the field and nothing in the water. The room has two marks in it and it is not for you to make a third — not because anything will punish you, but because the place is full, and you have already been the one extra thing it made room for, and it is a courtesy to take that thing back out again when you go.

Afterward you will find you walk more slowly for a while. Not from cold. You will have spent some minutes at the pace of something that will arrive, and the pace stays on you like the taste of iron, a few days, and then it goes. Let it go when it wants to. Do not go back for more of it. It was never meant to be visited twice, and it is still down there under the grass, and the edges are still sharp, and the snail is a thumb's width further along.
