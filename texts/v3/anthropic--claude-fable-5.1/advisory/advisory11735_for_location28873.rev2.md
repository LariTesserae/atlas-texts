**Preparation notes for the salt basin**

Start with the one fact that governs everything else: this is a place where your body's cooling system does not work. Everything after that is detail.

---

**1. The heat is the hazard. Everything else is scenery.**

Read the numbers again and take them literally. Forty-three degrees with air that the ponds have already filled with water. Sweat that does not evaporate is sweat that does nothing for you—you are losing fluid and salt and getting no cooling in return. In most hot places, the heat is something you manage with shade and water. Here the shade is a frosted skylight the size of the sky and the water in your bottle is the only thing standing between you and a clinic that is four kilometers from wherever you collapsed.

Understand what the workers have that you don't: weeks of acclimatization, a schedule built around dawn, air-conditioned refuges with the windows painted over, and each other. You will have none of these on the first day. So:

- **Do not arrive at midday.** Come down the grade in the last hour of dark, when the railings are merely wet. Be back somewhere cool by nine. If you do anything in the afternoon, do it inside the control building or a vehicle with the engine running.
- **Assume your working window is two to three hours a day** in the hot season, and plan the visit around that. If you want to see more, stay longer and do less each day.
- **Bring cooling that doesn't depend on evaporation.** Frozen bottles in an insulated bag, wrapped in cloth and held against your neck and wrists. A basin of cool fresh water to put your hands and forearms in—the canteen or clinic will have one, and this is a thing the workers already know to do. Wet cloths don't help here; they just stay wet.
- **Know the early signs in yourself before you go:** the headache, the irritability, the moment you stop sweating or the moment you feel cold in forty-degree heat. Tell whoever you're with that you want them to say something if you go quiet or start walking oddly. In this light, with no shadows and no time, heat exhaustion will feel like the ordinary strangeness of the place until it's too late to tell the difference.
- **Ask about the clinic on arrival.** Where it is, whether anyone is in it, what the procedure is if a truck needs to be sent. The people here have carried each other in before. They'll have a way.

If you have any say over the season, go in January. The description mentions it in passing but it's the most important logistical fact in the whole account: the temperature drops, the flies go quiet, and the basin becomes "genuinely silent and strange." You will lose some of the spectacle—the flamingos are seasonal—but you will be able to walk the levees at ten in the morning and think clearly. If you want the phalarope vortices and the flamingos in the blood pans, you are choosing the killing heat to get them. Choose knowingly.

---

**2. The salt is the second hazard, and it is patient.**

The pylons read like core samples of a hundred humid years. Your body will start reading the same way within hours.

- **Skin.** Sweat that doesn't evaporate runs, and it runs salty, and salt sits in every fold and seam. Expect chafe at the collar, under straps, behind the knees, between toes. Bring a mild barrier cream and use it before you go out, not after. Any cut, blister, or hangnail you arrive with will not heal here—the brine gets in and keeps it open. Cover them before you go down the grade. If you break the skin on site, rinse with fresh water immediately and cover it properly.
- **Eyes.** The haze lets you look at the sun, which is a trick. The glare off two hundred square kilometers of white salt crust is the real problem—it comes from below and the sides and is the same everywhere. Bring glacier-style sunglasses with side coverage, and a second pair, because the first will fog and salt-film within a day. Bring eye drops. Do not rub your eyes with your hands.
- **Feet.** This is a real dilemma. The pan floors and margins are brine mud over crazed salt plates; you want rubber boots for anything off the levee crown. But rubber boots at forty-three degrees and saturated air will macerate your feet by lunchtime. My honest advice: boots only for the specific minutes you need them, changed out of the moment you're back on gravel. Carry more socks than seems reasonable. Dry your feet completely every evening and powder them. If you have any history of foot trouble, stay on the levees.
- **Clothing.** Light, loose, long. Long sleeves and long trousers not for warmth but against the glare and the salt. Natural fibers rinse better but stiffen; synthetics stay soft but hold the salt against you. Whatever you choose, rinse it in fresh water every night, because a shirt that has dried salt-stiff will cut you at the seams the next morning. The workers' clothes will be chalk-white at every crease. Yours will be too, within a week.
- **Gear.** Anything with a circuit board should be considered disposable. Salt-laden condensation forms on every surface every night and gets into every gap. Bring one camera you are prepared to lose and keep it in a sealed bag with desiccant when not actively shooting. Write in pencil—paper here goes limp and pens skip. Your watch strap will corrode; bring a plastic one. Zippers will seize. Rinse the whole kit in fresh water before you leave, and expect salt to keep blooming out of your boot leather for months in whatever world you go to next.
- **Do not drink or taste the brine**, even from the early ponds, even a fingertip. It is not seawater; it is far past that. And do not swim, no matter how the finishing pans look at dawn. The density and the temperature and the crust would make it an unpleasant way to learn something you already know.

---

**3. Water. Yours, not theirs.**

There is no fresh water in this basin except what someone brought in. The water tower on the housing pad is the source; the canteen is where you'll fill up. Arrange this before you go anywhere, and be clear about how much you'll need, because you'll need more than you think and you'll be drawing on a supply that a small, tired community has budgeted for itself.

Carry at least four liters on your person for a morning on the levees, plus a reserve in the vehicle. Drink before you're thirsty. And drink electrolytes, not just water—the paradox of a salt basin is that you can still go hyponatremic if you sweat out your own salt and replace it with plain water. Bring your own oral rehydration mix. Don't rely on the canteen having any.

---

**4. Moving through it.**

The grid is the danger. Every levee is straight for eleven or fourteen kilometers, every corner is a right angle, and the haze eats the far side of everything. There are no landmarks that aren't identical to other landmarks. A pump station looks like every other pump station. A conveyor trestle looks like every other trestle. You could drive for twenty minutes along a belt of moving salt and have no idea which of the several hundred kilometers of shoreline you were on.

- **Get the plant map before you leave the housing pad.** The pond numbers, the pump station numbers, the feed canal. Photograph it and also copy it by hand in pencil. Ask someone to mark where the levees are impassable this season.
- **Bring a compass and use it.** In shadowless light with a pale coin for a sun, you will lose north within an hour. Assume whatever positioning device you carry is unreliable here; even if it works, its battery contacts won't for long.
- **Count.** Count pump stations. Count the right-angle turns. Note the floodlights—each one a smeared halo kilometers from the next—because at dusk they become the only wayfinding there is, and they all look the same.
- **Never trust the crust.** The salt-crust islands where the birds nest, the skinned surface of the finishing pans, the crazed floors of the drained pans—all of these may have brine or soft mud beneath. Stay on levee gravel unless you are with someone who works here and who says a specific place is safe. The water table rises from below and the pans re-flood upward through the cracks; a floor that was solid in autumn may not be solid in spring.
- **Stay away from the machinery.** The conveyor moves at walking pace and looks harmless. It is a continuous, unattended, hooded belt with rollers, carried on trestles above brine, and there is no one within thirty meters to stop it. The crusher houses are cathedrals with one machine in each, working, alone. Valves cycle on their own. This is a plant running at idle speed with almost nobody watching—do not put a hand on anything that moves, and do not go inside anything that hums.
- **Don't touch the railings.** Wet at dawn, scalding by eight, both by ten. This will become reflex within a day, but the first burn is the one that matters.
- **If you drive**, expect the washboard, expect the vehicle to come back a different color, and carry water and shade and a way to signal that does not rely on someone hearing you. Sound here comes from everywhere and nowhere; shouting will not tell anyone where you are.
- **Set alarms.** This one seems minor and isn't. There is no noon at noon. The light at eight and the light at three are the same light. The floodlights are on all day. You will lose track of time in a way that is dangerous when your working window is three hours, so let your watch tell you when to go in, and go in when it tells you.

---

**5. The living basin.**

This is why anyone comes, and it has its own rules.

- **The flies.** They won't bite, won't much land on you, and there are ten thousand of them per square meter along every shoreline. They will be in your food and your water if you leave anything open for even a moment. The sound—the fine dry fryer-sizzle—will be in your ears constantly, and then after some days you'll stop hearing it, and then when you leave it will take a while to go away, like a room that's stopped humming. None of this is harmful. All of it is disorienting, and the first walk toward the black band at the water's edge, watching it lift and settle ahead of you without ever letting you reach it, is one of the strangest small experiences the place offers. Let yourself have it, and then stop chasing it.
- **The birds.** The colonies nest on bare levee crowns and salt islands, eggs directly on the crystals. Do not walk through a colony. Do not walk near one. The workers' shift-change trucks disturb them daily, and the birds have accommodated that specific thing; they have not accommodated a person on foot with a camera. Stay on the roads and let a flock decide its own distance from you. The gulls on the fences and rooflines will be bolder than you'd like—keep your food covered and your hands out of the air.
- **Dead things.** There will be dead birds. Hundreds of thousands of phalaropes and grebes and flamingos in hypersaline water under a forty-degree sky means constant mortality, and the foxes and dogs live on it. Don't handle carcasses. Botulism and other bird diseases move through these colonies; the salt preserves what it doesn't eat, so a dead grebe can look fresh for weeks. If you see a die-off—a shoreline of them—tell someone at the plant and don't go closer.
- **The dogs.** This is the one animal risk I'd take seriously. A rough population descended from work dogs, living semi-feral on dead birds and canteen scraps, in a place with no vegetation and half-empty housing. They're probably indifferent to people. They may not be. Ask the workers how the dogs are, by name if the dogs have names, and don't walk the housing pad alone after dark until you know. If you are bitten, the clinic is the clinic; you will want to have thought about that before it happens.
- **The foxes** den under the abandoned bungalows. Don't go under the abandoned bungalows. Don't go into them either, really—the salt has been at the floors for a decade or more, and a fox that's been surprised in its den is not a thing you want to meet in a dark, frosted room.
- **The smell** is not in the description, but it will be there: sulfur from the bacteria and the red mud, iron, brine, guano, the sweetish rot of the shoreline. Hydrogen sulfide is a real possibility at the drain outfalls and around the algal mats at the pond margins. If you smell strong rotten eggs and then stop smelling it, that's not the smell going away. Move upwind and away from the water.

---

**6. The people.**

Sixty or seventy bungalows, half occupied. A canteen, a clinic, a water tower. People who stopped hearing the flies a week after they arrived and have been here for years since. A plant manager who knows how many structures are abandoned and hasn't demolished any.

You are a visitor in a working community that is not set up for visitors, in a place that is dangerous in ways they've stopped noticing. Some things to hold onto:

- **You will need them.** For water, for the map, for the clinic, for the one thing you don't know to be afraid of. Treat the relationship as the essential piece of equipment that it is.
- **Don't perform wonder at them.** The birds, the colors, the cathedral crushers—these are the dullest facts of their working life. They'll show you things if you're quiet and useful and don't get in the way of the shift. They won't if you arrive with the tone of someone who has come to see something remarkable that they merely live in.
- **Ask about the schedule.** When the shift changes, when the trucks run the levees, when the grading happens, when the next train is due to take the hopper cars. The basin runs on cycles that mostly ignore one another, and you want to know which ones you're stepping into.
- **The war.** "Which war depends on who's telling." Don't try to resolve this. Don't ask what year it is or which country you're in or which war they mean, expecting a single answer. For an ordinary person passing between worlds, this is the tell that the basin's history isn't one history, and the people here have found it easier to let the question stay open. Follow their lead.

---

**7. Your mind.**

I want to be direct about this because I think it's the part that surprises people most.

The light here has no time in it. There are no shadows to give depth or direction. The horizon is a haze that eats whatever's beyond a kilometer. The structures are identical and repeat at regular intervals across a grid two hundred square kilometers wide. The sound comes from everywhere and nowhere. Under all of it is a hum you feel in your boots and a sizzle you stop hearing and a low goose-murmur of birds standing in blood-warm water going nowhere. And the whole enormous machine is running, fully, with almost no one in it.

This produces a very particular kind of drift. Not fear—the basin isn't frightening—but a loosening of your sense of where and when you are and how long you've been there. People describe it afterward as feeling like the basin was thinking, or waiting, or that it had reached an agreement with itself that they had walked into the middle of. "Everything here drains inward and nothing leaves except as vapor" is a description of the hydrology. Stay long enough and it starts to feel like a description of attention, too.

- **Keep a routine that has hours in it.** Eat at fixed times. Sleep at fixed times, in the dark, with the floodlights blocked from your window. Write down what you did each day, in pencil, with the time.
- **Talk to someone every day**, even if it's a few words at the canteen. The half-empty housing pad and the open doors make it easy to spend a whole visit without speaking.
- **Set a leaving date before you arrive and keep it.** The basin has a way of making departure feel like an unnecessary effort. The workers stayed. The abandoned pump stations stayed. The hopper cars sit for weeks between trains. Decide in advance that you are vapor, and that you leave.

---

**8. What you'll take with you.**

Salt, in everything, for months. A camera that may or may not work again. Skin that will take a week to stop feeling coated. A sizzle in your ears that fades in a few days and a hum in your feet that fades faster. The exact rose the haze turns in the last ten minutes of light, which you will not see anywhere else and will not be able to describe to anyone in a way that lands.

And the image from the control-house roof: nothing green in any direction for forty kilometers, and more living things in view than in any forest you've stood in. Pink on red under white. That one doesn't fade. That one's the reason you went, and the reason the preparation was worth it.

Go at dawn. Go in January if you can. Bring more water than you think you need and less electronics than you want to. Stay on the gravel. Leave when you said you would.
