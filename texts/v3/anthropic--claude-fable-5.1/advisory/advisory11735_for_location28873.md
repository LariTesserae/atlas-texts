**Preparing for the basin**

Start with the thing that will kill you, because it is not the thing the description dwells on. The description gives you salt and flies and flamingos and a long, slow, rusting machine. What will actually kill you is the wet-bulb temperature.

Forty-three degrees in dry air is survivable for a fit person with water and shade. Forty-three degrees in air that has been saturated by two hundred square kilometers of evaporating brine is not survivable for anyone, for long, without mechanical cooling. Your body cools by evaporation. Here nothing evaporates. The sweat runs off you and takes no heat with it. In the still weeks, an ordinary person standing on a levee at midday has perhaps two to four hours before core temperature climbs past the point where judgment fails, and judgment fails before you notice it failing. You will feel fine, then slightly stupid, then you will sit down on the gravel to think about something, and then you will not get up. The workers know this in their bodies. They do not walk anywhere. Watch what they do and do exactly that, and do less of it than they do, because they have had years to adapt and you have had none.

So: the first rule is that you are never outside a running vehicle or an air-conditioned building for longer than you have specifically planned to be, and the plan is short. Twenty minutes. Thirty at dawn. You get out, you look, you get back in. The vehicle idles the whole time with the cooling on. If the vehicle cannot be trusted to idle for an hour in that heat without overheating, you do not have a vehicle, you have a coffin with a radio, and you should not leave the housing pad in it.

Bring a wet-bulb thermometer or something that reads humidity and temperature together, and learn the number that means go inside now. It will be lower than you expect. You will be tempted to ignore it because the light is so even and gray and mild-looking. The light is lying. The haze turns the sun into a pale coin and the coin does not feel hot on your skin, and you will forget, standing in that frosted-skylight glow, that you are in an oven. The workers' control building has its windows painted over. That is not against the sun. That is against the belief that it is cooler than it is.

**Water**

There is none. Every drop of liquid in the basin, including the liquid running off the railings and beading on the pipes and welling up clear through the crazed floors of the drained pans, is brine or becoming brine. The clear water that rises after the winter rains is bitter before it is pink. Carry everything you will drink and then carry half again, and keep it in the vehicle, not in a pack you might set down. Drink more than you feel you need, because your thirst signal is calibrated for a world where sweat evaporates and tells you something. Here you will lose fluid without the dryness that usually announces it.

Electrolytes: bring them, though it feels absurd to bring salt to a salt works. The salt you're standing on is not the salt you need in the proportions you need it, and you are not going to lick a levee. Potassium matters. Magnesium matters. Get a proper mix and use it.

Ice, if you can arrange a freezer or a well-packed cooler, is worth more than almost anything else you could carry. A basin of cold fresh water to plunge your forearms into for five minutes does what an hour of shade cannot. The canteen at the housing pad will have refrigeration. Make friends with whoever runs it on your first day.

**Salt**

Everything you bring will be damaged. Decide now what you are willing to lose.

The condensation itself is saline. That means the dew that forms on your camera at dawn, on your glasses, on the inside of your vehicle's windows, in the hinge of your watch, is a weak brine that dries to a crust and comes back the next morning to dissolve the crust and deposit a little more. Electronics fail here in days, not weeks. Phones, digital cameras, anything with a port or a seam. If you must bring something electronic, seal it in a bag with a great deal of silica gel and take it out only when you're going to use it, and accept that it is probably going to die anyway. A mechanical film camera and a mechanical watch will outlast anything with a battery. Binoculars—and you will want binoculars, because the birds are the point of this place—should be the fully sealed, nitrogen-purged kind, and even those should be wiped with fresh water every evening and stored in a bag.

Your skin. The salt gets into every fold. Where cloth rubs—waistband, collar, sock line, under a pack strap—you will develop a red raw band within two days that does not heal because it is never dry and is always in brine. Barrier cream: thick, petroleum-based, applied to every friction point before you dress in the morning and again at midday. Talc or a drying powder for feet, and dry socks in sealed bags, and inspect your feet every night as though they were someone else's. Small cuts do not heal here. They soften and whiten and spread. Cover every break in the skin with something waterproof and change it daily.

Eyes. Saturated brine in the eye is genuinely agonizing and can damage the cornea. Wear wraparound eye protection that seals against the face when you're near any pond edge or anywhere the wind is lifting crust. Carry an eyewash bottle of fresh water—not saline, for once, but plain water—and use it without hesitation.

Clothing: the instinct in humid heat is to strip down, and the instinct here is wrong. The haze diffuses the sun but does not stop the ultraviolet, and you will burn through the gray light without ever feeling the sun on you. More importantly, bare skin means brine on skin. Wear thin, loose, long-sleeved cotton or linen, a wide hat, a neck covering you can wet with fresh water from your own supply. It will feel like too much. It is the least you can get away with. Boots: rubber rots slower than leather but cooks your feet; leather breathes but will be white and stiff and cracking within a week. Bring both if you can. Wear the rubber near the pans and the leather on the levee roads. Neither will come home with you.

**Light and time**

You will lose track of time here, and this is not a metaphor. The haze removes shadows and removes the sun's movement. Noon and four o'clock are the same gray. The floodlights at the pump stations are on all day because no one can tell when day is, and after a week you won't be able to either. Wear a watch. Keep a written log with the time of each entry. Set a schedule and keep it with a rigidity that would seem neurotic anywhere else, because the schedule is the only clock that will still be running. People who stay long enough drift; you can see it in the workers, a slackness about hours. You are not staying long enough to drift safely.

The shadowless light also flattens the ground. You cannot judge the depth of a rut or the height of a lip or whether the pale surface ahead is crust over brine or crust over more crust. Walk slowly. Test with a foot before you commit your weight. Distances are impossible to estimate—a pump station may be four hundred meters off or four kilometers, and the haze gives you no way to know until you've driven toward it for a while.

**Navigation**

The levees are straight for eleven or fourteen kilometers and all look alike. The far side of any pond is invisible. Landmarks are eaten. A compass works. Whatever satellite navigation you're accustomed to may or may not, depending on which world this is, and you should not find out by needing it.

Get a paper map from the control building—they will have one, hand-drawn or blueprint, with the ponds numbered—and carry a second copy. Note the kilometer posts on the levee roads if there are any, and if there aren't, count your right-angle turns and write them down. Before you leave the housing pad, tell someone at the control house exactly which levees you will drive and when you will be back, and then be back then. This is not politeness. If your vehicle dies on the levee between Pond Nine and Pond Twelve at two in the afternoon, the only thing that saves you is someone knowing where to drive to. Walking out is not an option. Fourteen kilometers of levee at forty-three degrees in saturated air is a death march, and you would not finish it.

Never go alone. If you cannot find a second person, do not go past the housing pad.

**The ground**

Stay on the graded levee roads and the concrete of the works. Everything else is a trap of one kind or another.

The drained pans, the pale ochre floor crazed into plates: this looks like pavement and sometimes is. Sometimes it is a centimeter of crust over a meter of red-brown mud that smells of sulfur when you break through, and the mud is hot and anoxic and pulls at your boots. Do not walk on it. Do not drive on it. The pans re-flood from below without warning after rain; a pan that was solid last month may be soft now.

The finishing pans with their skin of crystals thick enough that wind can't ripple it: that skin will hold a bird and will not hold you. Under it is brine at close to saturation, at or above body temperature, dense enough that you'd float but caustic enough that you'd want to be out of it very quickly, and the crust would break around you and cut. Concentrated brine on broken skin is a burn. Concentrated brine in the mouth or eyes is a medical problem. Look at the finishing pans from the levee.

The levee crowns themselves are gravel and crushed salt-cake, and stable, but the outer edges slump toward the water and the flies' band—that meter of black at the wet edge—marks the line where the gravel gives way to mud. Stay above the flies.

Do not stand under the conveyor trestles. Salt sloughs off them in the autumn in sheets, and brackets fail, and the steel is rusted to a skin that looks stable and mostly is. Do not put your hands near the belt. It is moving at walking pace and looks harmless; it is carrying tons and will take an arm.

The pump stations are live. Everything in them is wet. The chain-link fences are wet. The transformer poles are wet. Do not touch a fence near a transformer. Do not enter a pump house without someone who works there.

The abandoned bungalows have foxes and dogs denning under them. See below.

**The animals**

The flies do not bite and will not land on you much, but there are ten thousand per square meter along several hundred kilometers of shoreline, and if you walk the edge they will lift around you in a cloud dense enough that you'll breathe them. Keep your mouth closed near the water. Eat inside the vehicle. If you must eat outside, eat away from the shore. The sizzle they make will be in your ears for the first several days and then you will stop hearing it, and you should be a little alarmed when you notice you've stopped, because it means you're adapting, and adapting is the first stage of the drift.

The gulls are everywhere and are exactly what gulls are everywhere: bold, loud, thieving, and nesting on every levee crown and roofline. They will take food from your hand and from your open vehicle. They defend nests. In the nesting season the levee crowns are covered in eggs laid directly on the crust, and the eggs are the color of the crust, and you will step on them if you walk carelessly, and the birds will come at your head. This is also true of the avocets and stilts on the shallow pans and the terns on the crust islands. Walk where you've already driven.

Bird droppings are on every surface, and dried guano lifts as dust. A mask near the colonies is not excessive. Whatever avian diseases exist in this world, you have no idea whether your body has met them.

The dogs are the real animal hazard. They are feral, generations from any work, living on dead birds and canteen scraps, thin and salt-caked and not afraid of people because people here have never been worth fearing. They den under the abandoned bungalows on the housing pad—which means they are on the housing pad, where you are sleeping. Do not feed them. Do not approach them. Do not go into an abandoned bungalow. If a dog approaches, stand still and let it decide you're uninteresting, which it probably will. You do not know whether rabies exists in this world. Assume it does. A bite from one of these dogs, in this heat, in this salt, is a wound that will not heal and an infection you can't identify.

The foxes are shyer and will leave you alone. The falcons and harriers are irrelevant to you.

**The people**

This is a working industrial site, old and settled, staffed by perhaps thirty or forty people who have been here a long time and who have almost certainly never seen a stranger arrive who was not either a new hire, a delivery driver, or an ecologist. Be an ecologist. The birds are on maps; people come to count them. A visitor with binoculars and a notebook and a plausible interest in phalarope flocking behavior is a category they have a slot for. A visitor with no explanation is a problem, and this is a place where a problem has nowhere to go.

Present yourself at the control building at the washing plant first. It is the closest thing to a center. Do not wander the grid and then turn up at the housing pad at dusk; that will read as suspicious in a way you cannot undo. Go to the painted-over windows, find the door, introduce yourself to whoever is running the shift, and ask permission. Ask which levees are drivable. Ask about the pans that have re-flooded. Ask where you can sleep and what you can eat and whom to tell before you go out. Offer something: coffee, tobacco, batteries, news from outside, a hand with something. You don't know what currency works here and it may not matter; in a place this isolated, being useful and being unobjectionable are the coin.

Respect the rhythm. The maintenance schedule is older than anyone working here. Nothing happens quickly and nothing needs to. Do not suggest improvements. Do not touch equipment. Do not walk into the crusher houses. If a valve is being cycled, stand well back and watch and don't ask questions until it's done. They are not unfriendly; they are simply not used to being watched, and they will be more comfortable with you if you make yourself part of the background as quickly as possible.

Do not assume you know their history. "Before the war—which war depends on who's telling" is a warning. The world outside the rim may have been through things that yours has not, and the workers may have opinions about those things, and you have no idea which opinions are safe. Listen more than you speak. If asked where you're from, be vague in a way that is boring rather than mysterious.

**When to go**

Not summer. Not the still weeks. If you can choose, go in winter—January, when the temperature drops and the flies go quiet and the basin is, for a month, genuinely silent. It will still be hot by any ordinary standard, but the wet-bulb will be survivable for real stretches of time, and you will be able to stand on a levee for an hour and look. The description says this is when the place becomes strange, and it will be, and that strangeness is the thing worth coming for.

If you want the flamingos, you are bound to the migration seasons, which means the shoulder months, which means real heat. Go at dawn. The first hour of light, before the vapor thickens into its lid, is the only time the sun has a direction and the ponds have a surface you can see, and the birds are most active, and the flies are still on the ground. By eight the railings are hot. By ten the lid is down. Be inside by ten and stay inside until the haze goes rose. Evening is the second window, shorter and dimmer, and it is when the floodlights come on one by one across the flats and you will understand something about the scale of the place that the daytime hid from you.

**A short list**

Fresh water, twice what you think. Electrolytes. A cooler with ice. A thermometer that reads wet-bulb. A mechanical watch. A compass and two paper maps. A vehicle whose cooling and idle you would bet your life on, because you are. A radio that reaches the control house. Sealed, purged binoculars. A film camera, or a digital one you've already said goodbye to. Silica gel, a great deal of it, and dry bags. Thin long-sleeved clothing, a wide hat, a wettable neck cloth, wraparound eye protection. Barrier cream. Foot powder and many pairs of socks in sealed bags. Waterproof dressings. Fresh-water eyewash. A mask. A notebook and pencil, because pens will fail and paper will curl but survive. Something to give people. A second person.

**What it will be like**

You will come down the grade from the rim and the haze will close over you like the lid of a pot and the light will go gray and even and the horizon will vanish, and you will feel, immediately, a kind of cognitive fog that you'll want to attribute to the heat but which is really the light: your brain doing without shadows, without a sun to steer by, without any of the cues it uses to know what time it is and how far things are. You will drive along a levee for twenty minutes and see nothing but gravel and pipe and belt and the pink water, and you will begin to doubt the levee goes anywhere. It does. Keep driving.

Then you'll stop, and get out, and be hit by the heat like a wet hand over your face, and stand there for a moment not knowing what the sound is—the fine dry sizzle that is not the pumps and not the conveyor—and then you'll look down at the water's edge and see the black band lift and move ahead of you and settle, and understand that the sound is them. Every shoreline. All of it. And past them, on the water, a flock of small birds spinning each in its own tight circle, thousands of them, and past those a line of flamingos standing in what looks like blood, not doing anything, and you will realize that you are looking at more life than you have ever seen in one place and that none of it is looking back.

Then you'll get back in the vehicle, because it's been fifteen minutes and that's the plan, and you'll wipe the salt off your glasses, and the salt will already be coming back.
