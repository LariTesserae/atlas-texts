**On preparing to visit the room in the mountain**

Begin with this: the room is not hostile. Nothing in it wants you, nothing in it will pursue you, and nothing in it will change because you are there — except in the exact places where you touch it, where it will change permanently. The dangers of this place are all of the slow kind. They are the dangers of a room that is finished, visited by a body that is not. You will be the only unbalanced thing in it, and everything that goes wrong will go wrong because of that.

So prepare as you would for two things at once: a serious high-altitude climb, and a visit to a sealed archive that has never had a reader.

---

**The mountain first**

Do not let the strangeness of the room make you careless about the ordinary part. The ledge is at an altitude where the sky has gone to pewter, which means the air is thin enough to hurt you, and the approach is barely a ledge. Acclimatize as you would for any peak of that height. Know the signs of altitude sickness in yourself before you go, because the room will blur them — the drowsiness of hypoxia and the stillness the room produces feel very alike from the inside, and you will not be able to tell which one is telling you to sit down.

There is no snow and no ice, so leave the axes and crampons behind. But do not read the absence of ice as safety. It means the whole upper mountain is dry in a way that mountains are not supposed to be, and you should assume the dryness begins well below the ledge. Whatever has been drawing water out of that shelf for however long has probably been drawing it from everything around the shelf too. Expect no streams on the last part of the approach. Expect no melt to collect. Carry every drop you will drink, and carry more than seems reasonable, because you will lose water in this place without knowing it — no sweat, no visible breath, only a steady drawing-out through skin and lungs and eyes. Cold blunts thirst. Altitude blunts it further. The room's air, kiln-dry, will take from you faster than either. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst.

Keep your water sealed. All of it, always. Do not set an open container down on that rock. If a fingertip's moisture goes into the stone and stays there, you should assume a cup's worth would go the same way.

---

**The dryness**

This is the thing to respect most, because it is the thing that will not announce itself.

Your lips will crack within the hour. Your eyes will begin to sting and then to feel gritty, and you will blink more and it will not help, and if you stay long enough your corneas will start to take real damage. Your hands, if bare, will lose their grip as the skin dries and stiffens, which matters on a ledge that is barely a ledge. The inside of your nose will crack and bleed, and blood in this room does not behave the way blood behaves elsewhere — it will dry almost as it leaves you.

Cover everything. Sealed goggles, not glasses — the kind that make a gasket against the face. Something over the mouth and nose, which you will want anyway for reasons below. Gloves, and under the gloves, if you can bear it, a thin barrier layer. Lip balm applied thickly and reapplied without vanity. Moisturize your skin before the approach, knowing the room will take that too; it buys you time, not immunity.

Understand what the dryness means about touch. The print you leave on the rock outside does not fade. Assume this is true of every surface in the room, including the ones that are alive. Every place you lay a bare hand, you have signed. That is not a reason to be afraid of the place. It is a reason to decide, before you go in, where you are willing to sign it.

---

**The sound**

You will hear it for a long time before you see anything. On the approach it will sound like weather on the other side of the mountain, and you will stop noticing it, and then at the crack it will become what it is, and you will not be able to stop noticing it.

Bring hearing protection and wear it — plugs and muffs both. Do not imagine this makes the sound go away. At the pitch and pressure this engine produces, the sound is not only in your ears. It is in the bones of your face, in your chest, in your teeth. The plants know this; they are built around it. Protection will keep your hearing, and you should keep your hearing, but it will not keep the roar from becoming the shape of your skull after long enough. Nothing will. That is what the room does.

Expect, on leaving, that the silence outside will feel like a wound. People have described it as deafness. It is not deafness; it is your nervous system reporting the absence of a thing it had accepted as the floor of the world. Sit on the ledge until it passes. Do not begin the descent while you are still hearing the room.

Expect also that some of the sound may come out with you. Whoever laid a palm on a resonant column found the hum still in their hand afterward. You should assume a tone in the ears for days, perhaps longer. If you are a person for whom that would be intolerable, do not go in, or go in and do not touch.

---

**The dust**

The floor is copper-red and fine as flour, and it lies in the same rings the plants grow in, and the plants are made of it. It is whatever infinitesimal thing the engine sheds as it turns, accumulated across a span of time that does not have a name. Nobody knows what it is. Treat it as you would treat a fine metal dust of unknown toxicity, because that is very likely what it is.

This is the second reason for the mask, and the stronger one. A proper particulate respirator, sealed, not a cloth. The dust will rise at every step. It will get into your clothes and stay there. It will get into your pack. Bring a change of outer layers to put on at the ledge, and something to seal the room-layers into for the descent, and do not open that bag anywhere you care about until you have decided what you are going to do with what is in it.

You should also think about the dust in the other direction. Whatever the room's chemistry is, it is dry, closed, and balanced, and it has never met a living body from outside. You are carrying moisture, skin, breath, microbes, salt. You are the first weather this place has had. The mask protects the room from you at least as much as it protects you from the room. Wear it with that in mind.

---

**Time**

Nothing in the room will count for you. The light barely moves. The lamps do not flicker. The temperature holds. The sound has no cycle. You will lose track of how long you have been standing there, and you will lose it faster than you expect, because the room is deeply, chemically calm and the human body, when cold and thin of oxygen and drying out, wants very much to be calm with it.

Do not rely on anything electronic to keep time. The engine puts something into the air besides sound, and you cannot know what a flywheel that has run without fuel for that long does to a circuit. Bring something mechanical and bring something older than mechanical. A sand-glass will work perfectly in a room that dry. Marked candle stubs may not burn well at that altitude but are worth carrying.

The best clock, though, is a person. Go with someone, and have them stay at the crack, outside the roar, with a rope tied between you. Agree on a duration before you go up — an hour is generous, and you should treat anything past it as a decision rather than a drift. Agree on a signal: tugs, not shouts, because they will not hear you and you will barely hear them. When the time is up, they pull. You come.

You will notice, when you are inside, that there is an iron ring set in the wall at shoulder height, and a length of chain on the floor. Someone has had this idea before. Whether it was for keeping something in or keeping someone from staying, you will not be able to tell.

---

**Conduct inside**

The threshold is the only bare stone in the room. It is worn smooth. Stand on it.

Everything beyond that line is growth, packed so densely that there is no way to walk into it without breaking what you walk on. The fronds snap like ceramic and are sharp inside. The dust rises. And what you break stays broken, because this room has no mechanism for loss — nothing falls, nothing rots, nothing regrows. A single footprint into the mound is a footprint into a thing that has been finished for longer than anything you have ever seen has existed, and it will be there, exactly, the next time anyone comes.

So go in expecting to see the machine from the doorway. Bring a small monocular or good binoculars for the plate on its flank; you can make out the wheel-within-a-wheel and the number and the one word from the threshold, in the slot-light, if you are patient. Bring a light, but use it sparingly and low — your eyes will adapt to the pewter, and a bright beam will cost you that. A dim red lamp is best.

If you decide you must cross the line — and there may be reasons; there is a second plate at the back, leaning face-inward against the wall, and it has been leaning there so long the growth has moulded itself to its edge — then decide before you go up, not standing on the threshold with the roar in your teeth. Decide what path you will take, and take the shortest one, and step where the rosettes are sparsest, in the quiet nodes between the rings, where you will crush the least. Decide whether you will lift the plate. Lifting it breaks the mould; you cannot read it without doing something to the room that cannot be undone. There is no right answer to this. There is only the difference between having chosen and having drifted.

Do not touch the machine. Not because it will harm you — it is barely warm, no warmer than a body — but because the bolts are worn to domes by something slower than hands and nothing on it has ever been touched, and you would be the first, and it would know. Do not touch the flywheel under any circumstances; you cannot judge its speed, and there is no reason to think it would be gentle.

If you lay a palm on one of the columns, know that the hum will stay in your hand. Some visitors have found this the most important thing that happened to them there. Others have not liked carrying the room down the mountain with them.

Take nothing. If you have already broken a frond, take that one, sealed, and do not break another. Understand that you have not taken a sample. You have taken a piece of the room, and it will not grow back, and it will not grow where you take it either — it needs the sound, and there is nowhere else with that sound.

---

**What comes out with you**

Dust on your clothes and in your lungs; a tone in your ears; possibly warmth in one hand; and a small, dry, pale print on the rock outside, which you will pass on the way down, and which you will pass again if you ever come back.

Change layers on the ledge. Seal the inner ones. Drink, more than you think you need. Sit until the silence stops feeling like an injury. Then descend, and pay attention on the way down to the first thing that moves that is not you — a fly, a trickle, a stone loosened by a bird — because after that room, motion that does not come from you will seem briefly miraculous, and you should let it.

Have your hearing checked when you are home. Watch your eyes for a few days. Watch, also, for the room's particular quiet to have followed you — a flatness, a reluctance to be around things that change. It is not harm exactly. It is that you stood for a while inside a place where everything had already arrived, and the world you came back to has not, and for a little while the difference will feel like the world's fault.

---

**The last thing**

You will want to go back. Not for any reason you can name — there is nothing there to be gained, nothing to be finished, no story that a second visit would complete. You will want to go back because it is the only place you have ever been where nothing was waiting. Eleven lights, a flywheel, a garden made of breath, and a balance so exact that your own heartbeat was the loudest unsteady thing in it.

Prepare for that too. It is the room's one real hazard, and there is no gear for it.
