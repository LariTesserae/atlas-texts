# Preparing for the Shelf

Start from the honest position, which is that most of what you will bring is wrong for this place and you will find out which parts in the dark. The shelf defeats the usual expedition logic because the usual logic assumes at least one of three things: that you can see, that you can get dry, or that you are the only thing there. None of these hold. Prepare instead for the intersection of three environments that rarely coincide — a wet cave, a polar night, and a bat roost the size of a province — and accept that the compromises will be ugly.

---

## The problem you should think about first: nothing dries

Everything else follows from this. The air on the shelf is at saturation and below freezing. Your body is at thirty-seven degrees and giving off water. Every fibre you wear will take on the fog from the outside and your sweat from the inside and there is no gradient anywhere on the shelf along which that moisture could leave. Waterproof-breathable fabrics rely on the air outside being drier than the air against your skin. Here it isn't. They will behave like plastic bags, which is fine, as long as you know that is what you have bought.

So: plan to be wet for the entire visit, and choose everything on the basis of *how it behaves wet*.

- **Against the skin**, wool or synthetic only. Nothing absorbent. Cotton on the shelf is a slow way of dying.
- **Over that**, an insulation layer that keeps its loft when soaked — closed-cell foam, synthetic fill, pile. Down is worthless within an hour.
- **Outside**, a proper waterproof shell, not to keep the fog out but to keep your body heat in with the moisture. Some travellers go further and wear a vapour-barrier layer directly over the base layer, sealing the sweat in against the skin so it never reaches the insulation. It is unpleasant. It works.
- **Feet**: sealed rubber, not leather. Neoprene socks or vapour-barrier liners. Bring more pairs of socks than seems reasonable and rotate them not to dry them — they won't — but so that the pair against your skin is the one that has been warmed against your torso for the last six hours. Check your feet by touch every time you stop for long. The combination of constant wet and warmth macerates skin quickly, and you will not be able to see a problem developing.
- **Hands**: this is where the compromise bites hardest. You will navigate by touch, so you need dexterity and sensation. You also need to be able to put your hand flat on a wall covered in armoured, clicking, many-legged things and not flinch. Thin neoprene gloves are the usual answer. Bring several pairs; they tear on the fracture edges.

There is one mercy. The construction is warmer than the air, and a body pressed against it loses heat much more slowly. You will learn to sleep sitting against walls. You will learn which walls — the older, thicker ones near the centre carry more heat, and the water running down them is only just above freezing but the mineral underneath is warmer than that.

## Getting there, and altitude

The climb is a thousand metres through cloud that has become a substance. Below the shelf's thermal influence, that cloud freezes onto everything it touches. Expect rime on every hold, verglas on every ledge, and a wind you cannot see coming. This is ordinary mountaineering in appalling conditions and should be treated as such: you rope up, you carry crampons for the approach even though you will take them off on the shelf, you go slowly. Do it in whatever passes for daylight below the cloud if any exists, because above the cloud there will be none.

The shelf's altitude is not stated, and you should not guess low. "The height where the air gives up its water" places it in the middle to upper troposphere; call it three to five thousand metres and acclimatise below for as long as your schedule allows. Altitude sickness in the dark, in the noise, among the animals, would be very hard to distinguish from the ordinary disorientation of the place, and by the time you were sure, descending would be a serious undertaking.

The basalt on the approach and on the shelf itself is fresh. Fresh basalt is sharp. Fresh volcanic glass is sharper than any blade you own. Everything you carry that is soft — rope, pack fabric, the skin of your hands — will be cut on it eventually. Reinforce or double what you can. Carry a needle and heavy thread and expect to use them by feel.

## The light

The account says it plainly: a light is the loudest thing on the shelf. For a kilometre around, everything stops. The seal-things fall silent. The climbers freeze. The roosts hold their breath. You will feel it as a pressure, the sudden absence of a sound you had stopped hearing, and it is one of the worst feelings the place can give you — the sense of several hundred million things noticing you at once.

Practically, this means your headtorch is an emergency instrument and nothing more. If you must use it, use the dimmest red setting it has, point it at the ground, and use it for seconds. Some travellers have found that the animals tolerate a very faint red glow better than white. None have found that they tolerate it well.

This has consequences for everything else. You cannot read a map, a watch face, a compass, a gauge, or a label. Every instrument you bring must be readable by touch or by sound. Tactile watches exist and you should bring one — the shelf has no day, and the water's pitch is a clock only after you have learned it, which takes weeks. Bring a compass with a clicking bezel. Bring a thermometer you can read by feel, or memorise the feel of the air at the temperatures that matter. Pack your bag so that every item has a fixed place and a distinct shape.

Above all, **train before you go**. Spend nights blindfolded in a familiar place and then in unfamiliar ones. Learn to walk with your hands leading. Learn to trust your feet to tell you slope and wetness and texture. Learn how long it takes your inner ear to give up on the idea of a horizon. The account describes eyes beginning to invent after an hour; this is real and it does not stop. You will see things on the shelf. Movement at the edge of vision, faces in the fog, light where there is none. Knowing in advance that these are your own visual cortex filling a vacuum does not make them go away, but it lets you file them correctly.

---

## The sound

You will want earplugs on the first night and you must not use them. The roar is your only map. Once it separates into layers — the hiss of water under everything, the groan of the pools, the click of the walls, the breathing of the towers — you will find that you can locate yourself by it. The groan means the seal galleries, the old centre. The click thins toward the edges where the walls are still ankle-high. A roost startling and settling tells you where a tower stands and roughly how tall it is. A pool that has gone quiet tells you something is in it that the seal-things do not like.

Sleep is going to be bad. Sleep is going to be bad anyway — there is no day, your circadian rhythm will come apart within a week, and you will be cold and wet and sitting up against a wall. The noise is one more thing. Plan for it: bring a sleep schedule and keep it by the watch, rigidly, whatever your body says. Set a rhythm of activity and rest that is yours, because the shelf will not give you one.

---

## The air in the rooms

The account mentions rooms with no door and rooms with seven, galleries that fit no gait, cells hollowed where the water ran inside the towers. It also mentions that the towers are full of bats.

Do not go inside.

Any enclosed space on the shelf is a hazard for at least three reasons. First, groundwater rising through cooling volcanic rock frequently carries carbon dioxide, and sometimes hydrogen sulphide, and both are heavier than air and pool in low, doorless rooms where they cannot be seen or, at low concentration, smelled. Second, a roost of ten thousand bats produces guano in quantity, and guano in a warm wet enclosed space produces ammonia and grows things that should not be breathed. Third, you cannot see, and a room with no door is a room you may not find your way out of.

Bring a gas meter that alarms audibly or by vibration. Bring a respirator — not for constant wear, the fog would clog it in minutes, but for the moments when you have to move through a gallery where the roosts are dense. The film that grows on the mineral surfaces is bacterial or simpler and lives on the water's chemistry; assume it is sulphur-based, assume the water carries what it eats, and assume that the air over the warm pools has more in it than water vapour.

## Water and food

There is water everywhere and none of it is safe. It is coming out of the rock carrying enough dissolved mineral to build a wall, and whatever else was "written into it" is in solution too. A filter will not remove dissolved solids. Boiling will not remove them either, and boiling means flame, and flame means light. Reverse osmosis will work if you can pump it, slowly, by hand, in the dark. The alternative is to carry your water, which for a stay of more than a few days is not possible at altitude in wet cold when you are already carrying everything else.

Most travellers do a middle thing: they carry water for the first days, they collect fog-condensate on sheets where they can (it is nearly pure, but the yield is small), and they treat the spring water with a hand RO pump and drink it sparingly, watching for the signs of heavy-metal exposure by the only means available — how they feel. Take mineral-binding supplements. Do not drink from the pools the seal-things lie in.

Food: there are no plants and there is nothing here you can eat with confidence. The climbers are armoured and grazing on a film of unknown chemistry; the seal-things are large, protected, and it would be an act of violence against the place to take one; the bats are bats. Bring everything. Wet cold at altitude will demand five thousand calories a day or more, and food that can be eaten by feel, without cooking, without unwrapping anything that rustles. Fat and sugar. Weigh it out before you go and know exactly how many days you have, because you will not be able to count it on the shelf.

## The animals

The dominant fact of the shelf is not the city or the dark or the cold but the biomass. Every surface you touch has something on it. The something is usually alive and usually many.

**The climbers** are on everything and you will put your hands on them constantly. Whether they bite, sting, or are merely disgusting is not recorded. Assume they can break skin. Wash your hands in fog-condensate before you eat. Keep your hands away from your face. Multiverse travel means nothing on the shelf has met your immune system and nothing in your immune system has met the shelf; a bite that would be nothing at home may be very much something here, and there is no doctor for a thousand kilometres.

**The seal-things** are the size and weight of seals and they move through the lower galleries in numbers. They are not aggressive in the account but they are large, packed close, and half-blind, and a body their size shifting in a crowded pool will crush you without meaning to. Do not enter the pools. Do not lie down in the galleries where they haul. Do not stand between a pup and the water. Step where they have stepped — the account says this, and it is the single best piece of advice for the place, because their paths are the only surfaces on the shelf where nothing is growing and no flow is building. They have already found the safe ground. Use it.

**The bats** will fly into you. Not deliberately; you are an obstacle at head height that was not there yesterday. Wear a hood. Keep your mouth closed when a roost startles overhead. Their echolocation is at the edge of human hearing and after some days you will begin to hear the shape of the space around you in it, which is a gift the place gives freely.

**The predators** are the reason to keep moving in a pattern. There are slow pale things the size of dogs that take climbers off the walls, something in the pools that takes pups, and something in the fog above the bats that is known only by a whistle. You are novel. You are also large, upright, warm, and slow, and nothing here has evolved to eat you, which is protective only up to the point of hunger. Sleep with your back to a wall and your legs drawn in. Carry a stick — not as a weapon, as a probe, so that what your hand finds first is the end of the stick and not the end of your arm. When the pool near you goes quiet, move.

The whistle in the fog: nobody has seen what makes it. If you hear it directly overhead, get low against the mineral. That is all anyone can tell you.

## The city

You will be walking through a settlement being built, at the rate of a fingernail, by water that knows where the walls go. Every time you interrupt a flow you stop a wall, and the stopped wall stays stopped, and the fault is permanent. The account describes a scar that will still be there when the towers are thirty metres high. Leave as few as you can.

This means:

- Do not touch running water if you can avoid it. Do not put your hand in a trickle to feel where it goes. Do not step in a sheet flow. The wet places that are building are the places you must not stand.
- Do not camp on anything that is wet and level and clean, because that is a floor under construction. Camp on the worn paths, on finished mineral, on bare basalt at the edges.
- Do not mark your route on the construction. The mineral will heal a scratch but not a diverted flow, and you cannot tell in the dark which is which. Mark the approach below the shelf with cairns of loose basalt. On the shelf itself, navigate by the sound-map and by counting.
- Do not take anything. The mineral is pale and porcelain-fine and the temptation to break off a piece of a stair that fits no gait is real. Every piece is a stopped flow.

Then there is the other thing, which the account approaches carefully and you should too. The water runs differently near you. It hesitates. When you return to a place, the construction has moved on slightly not as it was moving before. Whether the city is aware of you is unprovable. Behave as though it is. Be the same every day — same routes, same camp, same hours. Do not test it. Do not stand in a flow to see what it does. You are a guest of something that has not decided about you, and the correct posture for that is stillness, consistency, and a very small footprint. There is no reason to think it is hostile. There is also no reason to think that its response, if it has one, would be at a speed you could react to.

---

## How long to stay

Shorter than you want. The account describes the traveller's heartbeat slowing to meet the place, and this is not a figure of speech. Prolonged darkness, constant noise, wet cold, sleep collapse, and the weight of several hundred million living things paying you no attention produce a state that some travellers describe as peace and others as dissolution, and from inside it is hard to tell which. Set a departure date before you arrive. Write it down somewhere you can read by touch. Keep it.

Go with someone if you can. Not for safety, though that too — for the ordinary human function of a voice that says your name. The shelf does not know your name. Nothing here does. After enough days that begins to feel like a fact about you rather than a fact about the shelf.

---

## The descent

The only way off is the way you came, or the fracture cliff, and the fracture cliff is not a way. Retrace the approach. Your cairns will be rimed over; find them by hand. The cold below the shelf's warmth is a different and cleaner cold, and you will be surprised how much you have come to rely on the mineral against your back. Move fast through the cloud. When you come out beneath it into the ordinary dark — lichen on the rocks, ice where ice should be — the silence will be the strangest thing you have felt in weeks, and the first thing you will do is stand still and listen for the groan.

---

## What it is like

You should know this before you go, because the equipment lists do not say it.

It is like standing inside a lung. The whole shelf breathes — the water in, the steam out, the roosts rising and settling, the pools groaning on a tide that has nothing to do with any moon. You are wet and cold and cannot see, and every surface you touch is warm and alive and did not expect you, and none of it is for you. The stairs are the wrong height. The rooms have the wrong number of doors. Something is building this in the dark for a purpose you will not learn, and everything that could crawl or fly or haul itself here has done so and is living in the walls of it like barnacles on a hull, and the building goes on anyway, at the speed of a breath, the same in every direction at once.

After the first terrible night, something in you gives up trying to be the centre of it. That is the thing to prepare for most, and the thing you cannot prepare for. You will be one warm body among hundreds of millions, and you will be almost entirely unnoticed, and the shelf will hum around you exactly as it hummed before you climbed up through the cloud and exactly as it will hum after you go down. Some people find this unbearable. Some find it the only rest they have ever had.

Bring dry socks. Bring more than you think. They will not stay dry, and you will want them anyway.
