**Preparing for the fig**

Start by accepting that you are going to be wet for the entire duration of your visit, and that this is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be lived inside. Most of what follows is downstream of that. The people who live in the lattice have organized their whole material culture around the pursuit of *less wet*, and they have had generations to get good at it, and they are still damp. You will be wetter than they are. Plan accordingly, and then let go of the plan a little.

---

**How to think about the place before you go**

This is a montane cloud forest settlement built into and onto a single colossal strangler fig, at an altitude where cloud passes through the crown several times a day. It is cool — not cold, but cool in the specific way of wet high places, where you are comfortable while moving and chilled within minutes of stopping. The tree is the ground, the walls, the roof, the rope, and, twice a year, the economy. Nothing in the settlement is more important than the tree, and you should assume that everything you touch is either the tree or was made from it.

If you arrive during fruiting, understand that you are arriving in the middle of the busiest weeks of the year, when the settlement is at once harvesting and under a kind of friendly siege by every fruit-eater on the mountain. Nobody will have time to explain things to you. The place is loud past the point where explanation is practical anyway. You will learn by watching, by copying, and by being shouted at, and the shouting will not be unkind — shouting is simply how people talk to each other across the hollow when a hundred barbets are hammering without pause.

If you arrive outside fruiting, the place will be quieter, damper in feel, more inward. People will have time for you. You will see how things are made and mended. You will not see the spectacle. Decide which you want. If you can, arrive a few days before the fruit begins to ripen so that you can learn the walkways while they're still empty and the noise hasn't yet risen to weather.

---

**Your feet**

Everything else in this section is secondary to your feet.

The surfaces you will walk on are grey bark with a film of green on it, split cane raised on pegs, lashed platforms, ladders whose rungs have been grooved by hands, and stone steps with heel-shaped hollows worn into them. All of it is slick. All of it is somewhere above a long drop — not always a fatal one, because the lattice has many levels and things to catch on, but a fall through this structure would be a fall through a cage of hard fused stems, and the people here treat falling with the seriousness that people elsewhere reserve for fire.

Hard-soled boots are wrong. They will not grip the green film, they will not feel the groove in a rung, and once wet they will never dry — you will be wearing two small swamps by the second morning and growing things between your toes by the fourth. The locals are almost certainly barefoot or nearly so, and the grooves in the ladders are the shape of bare feet and hands. If your soles are soft, you cannot simply go barefoot — you will be slow, careful, and in pain within a day, and you will bleed on their platforms, which they will politely not mention. The compromise that works is a thin, soft, rubber-soled shoe with a flexible sole that lets you feel the surface, or a closed-toe sandal you can strap tightly. Bring two pairs and rotate them; hang the wet pair under the overhang where the air moves. They will still be wet. They will be *less wet*.

Walk with your weight centered and low, and keep one hand for the tree at all times. Watch how the children move along the walkways — fast, loose, never reaching, always with the next handhold already known — and then do not try to move like that. Move like an old person. The old people here move very well.

Before you put a hand into a root crotch, look into it. There is a slow snake in the description of this place wrapped in exactly such a crotch, waiting for a frog. It is not waiting for you, but your hand looks enough like a mistake.

---

**Your skin**

Constant wet, moderate cool, and organic surfaces are ideal conditions for skin trouble. Expect maceration in the creases — between toes, behind knees, groin, under the arms, anywhere a strap rests. Bring a small amount of something to dry and protect skin, a talc or a zinc paste, and use it every night whether you think you need it or not. Bring a little antifungal. You will use it.

Fig latex deserves special mention. Every broken stem, every harvested cluster, every snapped aerial root weeps a white milky sap, and the sap of figs is irritating to skin and photosensitizing — it makes the skin it touches burn under light. The harvesters have hands like leather and have long since stopped noticing. You have not. If you help with the harvest, and you should offer to, keep the sap off your face and wash your hands and forearms in the drip-water before you go anywhere the light comes through the lattice. You may notice the women at the mashing trough working with their hands wrapped in leaf. Notice why.

The green film on the bark is algae and moss and it is harmless, but it stains, and the fruit pulp stains purple, and by the end of your stay your hands and everything you own will be the same color as everyone else's. This is fine. It is a kind of introduction.

---

**Your lungs and your stomach**

Two things here fall from above in quantity: fruit and droppings. The birds during fruiting produce a continuous purple rain of the second, and at dusk the bats arrive in such numbers that the people close their leaf-shutters against what the bats let fall. Accumulated bat and bird droppings, in a warm damp environment, grow fungi whose spores you do not want in your chest. The interior of the lattice, where dead wood has become soil in the dim, is exactly the place where such things concentrate. Do not go poking around in the deep interior stirring up dust — there is very little dust here, thankfully, because there is very little dry, but the principle holds. Do not sweep old droppings. If you develop a persistent cough with fever a week or two after your visit, tell whoever treats you where you were.

The saying — *don't stand under a fruiting limb with your mouth open* — is, in its most literal reading, about this. Keep your mouth closed when you look up. Wear something on your head. A close-fitting hat with a brim, one you don't mind ruining. The people will wear leaf or nothing, having learned where not to stand; you have not learned that yet, so wear the hat.

The fruit is safe and good — small, purple-red, fig-sweet, slightly grainy with seeds. It is pollinated by tiny wasps that live and die inside it, and you will eat some of them, and this has been true of every fig ever eaten by anyone. The dropped fruit ferments quickly; you will smell it everywhere during fruiting, a sweet vinegar rot underneath the green cloud smell, and some of the animals get visibly drunk on it. Eat from the baskets, not from the ground. The fig paste keeps and travels and is probably what you will be offered as a meal. There will be bird, likely, and things gathered from lower on the mountain; ask before eating anything that looks like fungus.

Water is the interesting question. The place is soaked but the water is on everything and gathered nowhere clean by default. The bromeliad cups are full of frog eggs, larvae, snails, and algae — do not drink from them, and do not disturb them; each cup is a frog's whole territory. The people almost certainly collect drip water from leaf-runnels and roof edges into cane or gourd, and this is your best source too, but it has run over bark covered in bird droppings before it reached the container. Bring a small filter or purification tablets and use them without making a display of it. If you're offered water, take it graciously and treat it later if you can. If you can't, you'll probably be fine, and you'll probably also have a few uncomfortable days. Bring something for that too.

---

**Your ears and your nerves**

You cannot prepare for the noise except by knowing it's coming. During fruiting the sound of this place is continuous — barbets without gaps, pigeons in a mass, monkeys shrieking across the hollow at each other from dawn, frogs ticking from every fork, the rushing-air arrivals of hornbills that you feel in your sternum, people shouting coordination, fruit pattering down, and under all of it the drip. After a few hours you will stop hearing it as sound. It will become a pressure, like altitude. You'll find yourself exhausted by mid-afternoon and not know why. That's why.

Earplugs are a temptation. Consider not using them, at least in the day, because you need to hear the shout that means *not there, the limb is about to drop* and you need to hear a hornbill arriving behind you. If you must, use them at night, but know that you'll miss the four-second hole in the noise when the cloud comes through, which is one of the strangest and best things about the place — everything goes quiet at once, and in the quiet you hear a cluster let go somewhere high and come down through the leaves like the start of rain, and then the birds begin again.

Learn to read faces and hands. The people communicate a great deal without words because words are expensive here. A pointed chin, a flat hand, a jerk of the head toward a limb — these carry the harvest. You'll pick them up faster than you expect.

---

**What to bring, and what not to**

Bring: soft wet-capable shoes, two pairs. Wool or synthetic layers — never cotton, which will be cold and wet and heavy and will not dry until you leave the mountain. A thin wool hat for nights. A brimmed hat for days. Skin protectants. A small first-aid kit with attention to blisters, cuts, and fungal issues. Water treatment. A waxed or synthetic notebook and a pencil, if you record things; ink runs and paper rots. Anything electronic will fog, sweat, and fail; either seal it in a dry bag you open only under an overhang, or don't bring it. A light poncho for when the cloud comes through — not a jacket, which will trap your own heat and make you wetter from the inside. A small bag of coarse salt.

Do not bring metal tools with any intention of trading them. This is a place where a stone blade is a unit of exchange and the whole built world is held together with cordage twisted from the tree's own roots. A steel knife here is not a gift; it is a disruption to a very old balance, and whoever ends up with it will suddenly matter more than they did, and the settlement has stayed stable precisely because nothing in it is trying to win. If you carry a knife for your own use, keep it out of sight and use it privately, and do not cut the tree with it.

Salt is a better thing to offer, if you want to offer something — valuable at altitude, consumed rather than kept, distorting nothing. Better still is labor. During fruiting there is more work than hands. Learn to hold a basket steady under a harvester, or to sort fruit by ripeness on the mats, or to carry full baskets to the platform. You will be slow and clumsy and in the way, and then after a day you will be slightly less so, and someone will hand you a cane hook, and that will be your welcome.

---

**Getting up and getting around**

You approach from below, through the buttress roots, on the only stone in the place — steps carried up generations ago and worn to heel-hollows. The stone is the last thing you'll touch that isn't tree. Above it, everything is root and lattice.

The lattice is a hundred feet across and hollow, with daylight through it, and houses inside it at several levels under the overhang of limbs. There is an inside and an outside to the trunk, and the harvest happens mostly outside and up, on the limbs, while sleeping and sorting and living happen inside and under. Spend your first day learning the interior — where the ladders are, which platforms connect, where the market platform is, where people relieve themselves (ask; there will be a designated edge, and the ants below will be dealing with it). Do not go out onto the limbs until someone takes you.

When you do go up, understand that the ripening moves around the crown following the light, and everything follows it — birds, monkeys, harvesters. In the morning the east face is where everything is; by late afternoon it's the west. The side that has been worked over is empty, dripping, littered with skins and ants. Do not wander onto the abandoned side alone thinking it's safe because it's quiet. It's where the snakes are hunting and where nobody will hear you if you fall.

---

**The animals**

The monkeys hold a quadrant of the crown and defend it against the other troop with shrieking that has been going since dawn. They will defend it against you too. They are not dangerous unless cornered, but they are strong and bold and they will take things from your hands and they bite. Stay out of their quadrant. The harvesters do — *leave that, the monkeys have it* is a real instruction, and it's about efficiency, not fear; the monkeys waste more than they eat and there's plenty elsewhere.

The hornbills are the size of a dog and they land on the edge of the sorting platform and look at the fruit with intent, and nobody stops them because it is a hornbill and you don't. Do not be the visitor who tries to shoo a hornbill. They have a bill that can crack things and a temper, and more to the point, the people would find it obscene. The birds were here before the people. The people know that.

The pigeons in a mass will shift and spill when the children's slings move them, and a sheet of pigeons coming off a limb toward you is startling but harmless; keep your footing and your mouth closed. The ants run the roots in two-way columns and will bite if you stand on their road. Look before you set a foot down for long. The flies rise from dropped fruit in clouds and are merely unpleasant. There may be leeches lower on the mountain; check your ankles at night regardless.

The frogs are in the bromeliads, one to a cup, and they are the soundtrack of the place and its thermometer — they never stop. Leave the cups alone. Everything in them is somebody's whole life.

Watch for the hornbill's wing-rush, for the sudden collective dip of a branch, for the four-second silence. These are the tree's own weather warnings.

---

**Dusk and night**

At dusk the bats come. In such numbers that the light changes. The people close the leaf-shutters, and you should be inside before they do — not because the bats are dangerous, but because what falls from them is, over time, and because it's simply filthy to be out in. If you're caught out, get under an overhang and keep your face down.

Nights are cool and loud in a different register — frogs, drip, bats, the occasional monkey argument, the sound of a house drinking. Cane floors on pegs are hard and let air through from below, which is the point; you will want insulation under you, not just over. A thin closed-cell pad is the single most useful bulk item you could bring. Sleep in your wool. You'll wake damp. Everyone does.

Dawn is the monkeys. You will not need to be woken.

---

**Conduct**

This is a settled place, and you can tell by what's worn smooth. The lashings on the old platforms have been renewed so many times that the wood beneath has a groove the exact shape of the cord. That groove is the ethic of the place: repair, not replacement; taking one fork of a root and leaving the other; taking some fruit and leaving most. Be the kind of guest who fits the groove.

Do not cut anything living. The aerial roots are their cordage crop and are managed over years — a tip cut now yields a harvestable fork a decade later, for someone else's children. Do not pick fruit from a limb someone else is working. Do not take from the sorting mats. Do not enter a house without being drawn in. Do not stand on the market platform gawking while people are moving baskets around you.

Which brings us back to the saying. *Don't stand under a fruiting limb with your mouth open.* It means: watch where you are. It means: don't wait for things to fall into your mouth — work for them. It means: don't talk when you should be paying attention. It means: don't gape at what's above you and miss what's happening at your feet. It means: the tree is generous, but its generosity is indiscriminate, and it drops fruit and pulp and bird-mess and the occasional whole cluster on the wise and the foolish alike, and only the wise have moved. You will be told this saying on your first day. You will understand a new part of it on each day after.

---

**Time, and leaving**

The tree does not have a schedule; it has a tendency. The fruiting will last some weeks and move around the crown and taper. If you can stay through the tapering you'll see the settlement exhale — the birds thin, the monkeys drift down the mountain, the noise lowers by degrees until one morning you hear individual barbets again, and the frogs, who have been going the whole time, become audible as frogs. The people will be tired and purple-handed and rich in paste and full cylinders, and they will finally have time to show you how a house is grown.

When you leave, go down the stone steps in the morning before the cloud comes through, when the green film is at its least treacherous. Everything you're carrying will be wet, stained, faintly fermented, and colonized by something small and green. Let it dry slowly wherever you land next. Some of it won't recover. Your feet will take a week. Your ears will ring in the quiet, and you'll find yourself, for a while, uncomfortable in places where nothing is dripping.

You won't have changed the place. That's the goal. The tree has been fruiting for longer than there have been people to harvest it, and the settlement has learned to live inside its tendency the way the houses live inside the lattice — taking the shape they're given, sweating, holding on. For the length of your visit, you do the same. Keep your mouth closed. Keep one hand for the tree.
