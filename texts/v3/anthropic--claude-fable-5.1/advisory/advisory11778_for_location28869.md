# Preparing for the Bubble

Start by understanding what kind of place this is, because the description will mislead you if you let it. It reads like a ruin. It is not a ruin in the way you are used to—a cool, quiet thing you can wander through at your own pace. It is a working industrial process with a town glued to the inside of it. The process is still running. The town is incidental. You are going to visit a kiln that happens to have architecture in it, and every decision you make should proceed from that.

Three things in this place can kill you, roughly in this order: the air, the heat, and the way out. Everything else—the glass, the falling ceiling, the ladders, the forest—can hurt you badly but will probably let you leave. The air will not ask.

---

## First: decide whether you should go at all

Be honest about the body you are bringing. This is not a place for anyone with a heart condition, asthma, any respiratory sensitivity, poor heat tolerance, or a recent illness. It is not a place for anyone who has never worked in serious heat. If you have never spent a full shift in a foundry, a boiler room, a desert in August, a firefighting drill, you do not yet know what your body does at the edge, and this is a bad place to find out.

If you can, spend ten to fourteen days before the trip acclimatizing: daily sessions of an hour or two of exertion in heat, working up. It sounds excessive. It is the single cheapest thing you can do to raise your odds. An acclimatized body sweats sooner, sweats more, loses less salt, and keeps its heart rate lower under the same load. You will need every bit of that margin.

Do not go alone. This one is not negotiable. You need at minimum one person on the surface at the rim who does not descend, who keeps time, who can haul, and who will not be argued out of pulling you up when the clock runs out.

---

## The air

The place breathes. Every few minutes gas forces up through the fissures and the flutes. The description says sulfur, tars, "whatever the deep rock exhales." Take that phrase seriously, because "whatever the deep rock exhales" in a volcanic setting means, plausibly: sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, and possibly hydrogen fluoride or hydrogen chloride in traces. Any one of these is a problem. Together they are a problem you cannot smell your way out of—hydrogen sulfide deadens your sense of smell within minutes at moderate concentrations, so the moment you stop noticing the rotten-egg smell is not the moment it went away. Carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide have no smell at all. Carbon dioxide is heavier than air and pools in low places, and the floor of this chamber is the lowest place in a basin that "has nothing left to fall."

The forest confirms all of this. Those yellow blooms are sulfur deposits, forming where the growths meet the gas. The stems are built from tars and resins. You are looking at a field of things that eat exactly what will poison you, and they are thriving.

So:

**Bring a multi-gas detector.** Four-gas minimum—O₂, H₂S, CO, and a combustible/LEL sensor—and if you can get a unit with an SO₂ cell, get it. Calibrate it before you go. Set the alarms conservatively and do not silence them. Clip it low on your body, at the hip or thigh, not on your chest, because the heavy gases will be at your knees before they are at your face. Check the oxygen reading constantly. If it drops below 19.5%, you are leaving. If any single toxic reading spikes into the alarm range during a breath cycle and does not fall back to baseline in the silence after, you are leaving.

**Bring real respiratory protection.** A dust mask is worthless. A half-face respirator with acid-gas cartridges is the absolute minimum and I would not personally go down with it. A full-face respirator with combination acid gas/organic vapor cartridges protects your eyes as well as your lungs and is a reasonable floor. But understand what cartridges do not do: they do not supply oxygen, and they do not filter carbon monoxide or carbon dioxide. If the chamber's baseline atmosphere is low in oxygen or high in CO₂—which, given a sealed pocket with gas venting into it and only one hole out, is entirely plausible—then no filter will save you.

The gold standard here is supplied air: a self-contained breathing apparatus, the kind firefighters wear. It is heavy, it is hot to wear, it limits you to thirty or forty-five minutes on a cylinder, and all of those are actually features here, because they force the visit to be short. If you can arrange it, do. If you cannot, then the compromise is: full-face respirator with the right cartridges, gas detector at your hip, and a hard rule that you never go more than two minutes' walk from your rope.

**Time the breath before you descend.** Lie at the rim and watch and listen. Count the cycle: how long from roar to roar, how long the roar lasts, how long the silence. Do this for at least half an hour. You are learning the rhythm you will move to. Down there, you move in the silences and you stop and brace in the roars. During a roar the gas concentration at floor level is at its peak and the flutes are jetting hot gas from their mouths. Do not be near a flute mouth when it fires. Do not be crouching or kneeling when it fires—get your face as high as you can.

**Stay off the plume at the surface too.** The eye is the exhaust. Everything the chamber breathes out rises through that hole and drifts. Your surface partner should be positioned where the heat shimmer does not lean toward them, and should have their own detector.

## The heat

Rock that glows a dim, steady red is somewhere around 500 to 600 degrees Celsius. That is the ceiling where it is thinnest. The walls and floor are cooler than that—people lived here, resin growths stand in it—but "cooler than glowing" still covers a range from unpleasant to lethal. You will not know the actual air temperature until you measure it, so **bring a thermometer** with a probe and a range well above anything you expect, and lower it on a line before you lower yourself. Lower it to the floor and hold it there through at least two breath cycles.

If the floor-level air is above about 60°C, an ordinary human should not descend for more than a few minutes, and only in full protective gear. Above 70°C, do not descend. Even at 50°C, sustained exposure is dangerous work.

The heat here has a particular character you should prepare for mentally: **it comes from every direction and there is no shade.** In ordinary heat you find a wall, a shadow, a breeze, and you recover. Here the walls are the radiators. The floor radiates up. The ceiling radiates down. Your body cannot shed heat by radiation to a cooler surface because there is no cooler surface. It can only shed heat by sweating, and the air is so dry that your sweat will vanish the instant it forms. You will not feel wet. You will not see yourself sweating. You will be losing well over a liter an hour and your only evidence will be a thirst that arrives late, a headache, a heart that will not slow down.

This means:

**Drink on a schedule, not on thirst.** A quarter liter every fifteen minutes, minimum, with electrolytes. Carry it in an insulated container or it will be hot enough to be useless within minutes. Pre-hydrate for a full day before. Weigh yourself before and after if you can; every kilogram lost is a liter you failed to replace.

**Wear a radiant barrier.** An aluminized heat-reflective outer layer—the kind foundry workers and wildland fire crews use—reflects a substantial portion of radiant heat before it reaches you. Under it, loose natural fiber. No synthetics touching your skin; they melt and stick. Cover everything. Exposed skin here is skin being cooked slowly from all sides.

**Consider active cooling.** Phase-change cooling vests work for perhaps an hour and then become insulation. Ice vests are worse—the ice will be water in ten minutes and hot water in twenty. If you use one, understand its lifespan and treat its expiry as a departure signal.

**Protect your eyes and mouth from the dryness itself.** Sealed goggles or the full-face respirator. Saline drops. No contact lenses—they will dry to your cornea. Lip balm before, during, and after. Breathe through the nose if the mask allows; the mucous membranes in your nose and throat will crack in this air and you will taste blood.

**Watch for the signs in yourself and your partner:** confusion, irritability, stumbling, stopping sweating, headache, nausea, a pulse over 150 at rest. Any of these means the visit is over. Heat stroke does not announce itself with a warning you will recognize from inside; it announces itself by making you stupid, and then by making you unconscious. Set a hard time limit before you go down and give the power to enforce it to the person at the rim.

**Your gear will suffer too.** Lithium batteries fail, swell, or vent above about 60°C. Cameras, phones, headlamps, the gas detector itself—all of them have thermal limits. Keep electronics inside the reflective layer against your body where they can steal a little of your cooling, check them often, and assume they will die before you want them to. Bring a mechanical watch. Metal parts—carabiners, buckles, the rope hardware—will get hot enough to burn skin through a thin glove. Wear leather gloves and keep them on.

## The way out

Read the description again. The chamber is entered through the eye in the ceiling. The dome is forty high. Everything at the bottom is forty meters below the only exit.

This is the piece that turns a difficult visit into a potentially fatal one. Going down is easy. Coming up is a forty-meter climb, in heat, dehydrated, in a respirator, on a rope that has been hanging in a hot gas plume the whole time you were below.

**Rope.** Ordinary nylon climbing rope loses strength dramatically above about 150°C and begins to melt around 250. The air in the eye—the throat of the exhaust, where all the heat and gas rises—may well be hotter than the chamber floor. Use an aramid rope: Kevlar, Technora, or a Technora-sheathed line. They tolerate heat to around 400–500°C before degrading. They are expensive and stiff and worth it. Bring two, and rig both, so there is always a redundant line.

**Anchors.** The surface around the eye is "a field of fractured black glass." Obsidian is brittle, sharp, and full of hidden fractures. It will cut rope on any edge and it will not hold a cam or a bolt reliably. Do not anchor at the rim. Anchor well back, on solid ground if you can find it, or on a deadman buried in the grit, or on a vehicle if you brought one, and run the rope over padded edge protection—leather, or a sacrificial sleeve of heavy canvas—at every point it touches glass. Check the edge protection every time someone comes up.

**Haul system.** Do not plan to climb out on your own strength. Plan to be hauled. A 3:1 or 5:1 mechanical advantage rig on the surface, with your partner operating it, means that when you reach the rope exhausted and half-baked you clip in and start walking your hands up the wall while they pull. Rehearse this on the surface before anyone goes down. The moment you most need to leave is the moment you will be least able to.

**The descent itself.** You are dropping down a chimney into the exhaust. Go through the eye quickly and get out from under it once you are on the floor—the plume above you is the hottest, most gas-laden air in the place. Use a descender you can operate with gloved hands. Do not touch the rope with bare skin. Have your gas detector on and reading as you descend; if it alarms in the throat, you climb back up before you ever reach the bottom.

**Keep the rope in sight.** Always. The chamber is eighty paces across and lit by dim red, and the galleries and doorways are visually repetitive in a way that will disorient you. Mark the landing zone with a light or a chemical glowstick—anything you can see from anywhere on the floor. Do not go up the ladders. Do not go into a doorway you cannot see the rope from. If you lose the rope in this place you will be walking a ring of identical dark openings in a heat that is killing you while the floor roars.

## Everything else that will hurt you

**The glass.** Obsidian fractures to an edge finer than a surgical scalpel. The surface field is made of it. The floor grit is partly made of it. The fallen ceiling scales are door-sized sheets of it. Wear heavy leather boots with high-temperature soles—not rubber, not foam, which will soften and melt on hot grit; something like a Vibram fire-resistant compound or a proper foundry boot with a felt-lined leather sole. Gaiters over the top. Leather gloves, and never put a hand down on a surface you have not looked at. Assume every edge is sharp. Assume every hot edge is sharper.

**The floor.** It is "grit and slag over a lattice of fissures" and "not solid." Probe ahead of you with a pole—a length of steel or a metal-tipped staff, since anything wooden or fiberglass will not survive. Step where the builders stepped: the grinding hollows and the worn footholds tell you where the ground held for centuries. Do not walk on any surface that reads as fresh, dark, or unmarked.

**The ceiling.** Scales fall. Door-sized plates of glass let go from forty meters up. A helmet is mandatory and will do exactly nothing against a direct hit; wear it for the small fragments. Your real protection is attention. Look up when you first arrive and note where the ceiling is brightest—that is where it is thinnest and closest to falling. Do not stand beneath those spots. Listen to the ticking during the silences; a change in the ticking, a sharper crack, a sound you have not heard yet, means move. The builders tidied the rubble from the roof-falls and rebuilt. They knew where it fell. The galleries with the most repair are the ones under the worst ceiling. Stay away from them.

**The ladders.** They are made of resin, and they are old, and they soften when the breath comes up. Do not climb them. Not one rung. If you must reach a higher tier, and you should not, rig your own line from above. A ladder that has held for centuries in this heat has been slowly cooking the whole time and you have no way to know whether it has one more climb in it or none.

**The forest.** The stems are hard, glassy resin—sharp when they break, and they break easily. They are hot. When a breath comes up they go soft and lean toward the venting, all at once, which means that if you are standing in the field during a roar, a thousand stems will shift against you. The yellow blooms are sulfur; break one and you release a fine dust that burns the eyes and airways. Do not walk through the forest. Walk the cleared paths the builders left—the floor of the lowest gallery, the approaches to the flutes. If you must pass close to the growths, do it in a silence, do it slowly, and do not brush them.

**The flutes.** These are the organ pipes: standing tubes of glass through which gas jets during every breath. The mouths are the hottest and most toxic points in the chamber. Some are natural, some tuned by the builders. They are the most striking thing here and you will want to stand next to one when it fires, to feel it. Do not. Keep ten paces back. Feel it from there. You will.

**Noise.** The roar is loud and has infrasound in it—you will feel the pressure in your chest before your ears register anything. Sustained exposure will damage hearing. But full earplugs cut you off from the ticking and the forest cracks, which are your early warning for ceiling falls and for changes in the cycle. The compromise is filtered earplugs—musician's plugs, or shooting plugs—that knock the peaks down while leaving the quiet sounds audible. Or wear nothing and accept that you are limiting your stay to well under an hour anyway.

**Light.** The chamber is lit by the dim red of the ceiling, enough to see your hands and shapes. You will want a headlamp for the hazards—for reading the floor, for seeing edges—and you should carry one. But be aware that white light will destroy your adaptation to the red and make everything outside the beam go black. A red-filtered lamp preserves your night vision and matches the place. Use the white light when you need to see a specific thing clearly; use the red for moving. Carry a spare, because the heat will kill the first one.

## What it will be like

Now the part that is not about equipment.

You will come through the eye into a heat that does not feel like weather. It feels like being inside a body. The air pushes back. The dryness will hit your eyes and throat before the temperature does, and you will feel your lips tighten and your skin go papery within the first minute. Then the temperature will arrive properly and it will feel wrong in a specific way: there is no side of you that is cooler. Your back is as hot as your front. Your feet are as hot as your head. Your body's instinct to turn away from heat has nowhere to point.

Then the ground will breathe under you and you will feel the pressure in your sternum and then the roar will come, and the flutes will sound, and the whole ring of doorways will hum around you in the red, and every hair you have will stand up. It is genuinely one of the most extraordinary things a person can stand inside. It is also the moment your gas detector is most likely to alarm. You will want to stay in that moment. Do not let yourself. Move in the silences, brace in the roars, watch the detector.

The town will do something to you. That is worth preparing for. It is not the emptiness of a ruin, where things have fallen down and softened and become landscape. It is the emptiness of a place that is still arranged—the hearths, the shelves at seated reach, the racks, the half-laid course of blocks at the top waiting for hands that did not come. Everything is ready. Everything is hollow. And it hums. Each doorway carries a note, and in the roar the whole ring sings the same slow chord it has sung for centuries to no one. Some visitors will find this beautiful. Some will find it unbearable. Most will find it both, in alternation, several times a minute, in time with the breath.
