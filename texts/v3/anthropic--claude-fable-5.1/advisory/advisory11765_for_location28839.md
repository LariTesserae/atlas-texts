**Preparing for the plateau**

Start from the simplest fact and let everything follow from it: you will be wet, cold, half-deaf, and unsure what time it is, for the entire visit, and there is nothing on the plateau that will help you with any of that except the mounds, and you must not let the mounds help you.

That is the whole shape of the problem. The only warmth is in the one thing you should keep your distance from. The only landmarks are the one thing that repeats. The only clock is the one that disagrees with itself. Everything you bring is a substitute for something the plateau offers and you are refusing.

---

**Body**

Treat it as a high, wet, windless moor with no sun, and pack for that first. The altitude is real—you are above the cloud deck—so expect thin air, a headache the first hours, and reduced judgement, which matters more here than on an ordinary mountain because the place is already working on your judgement from the outside. Acclimatise somewhere else if you can. Arrive rested.

The cold is the insidious kind: a few degrees above freezing, saturated air, no wind to feel like an enemy, and so you underdress and are hypothermic by the time you notice. Wool base layers. A shell that is actually waterproof, not water-resistant, because the fog condenses on everything and your breath alone will soak your collar. No cotton anywhere on you. Two pairs of gloves, one for handling wet things and one that stays dry inside your jacket.

Feet are the thing that will end the trip early. The whole surface is under an inch of water and the stone beneath is worn to something like soapstone—not slippery exactly, but it gives no purchase and it never dries. Knee-high waterproof boots, or good boots with sealed gaiters. More socks than seems reasonable, in a dry bag, and a strict rule that you change them whenever you stop for more than half an hour. Look at your feet every time. Trench foot starts in about twelve hours under these conditions and you will not feel it happening because your feet will already be numb.

Ear protection. Not because the thud is loud enough to damage you—it isn't—but because it is constant and arrives from everywhere and sometimes before its own cause, and after a few hours the ear stops trying to locate it and something in you goes slack. Foam plugs let you keep your own footsteps and your own voice while taking the plateau down to a murmur. Take them out periodically to check the pattern; a change in the rhythm is information.

A headlamp, with the understanding that it will be strange. The light is a fixed hour before dusk, dim but not dark, and there are no shadows because there is nothing to cast them and no direction for the light to come from. Your lamp will cast the first hard-edged shadow that ground has seen in a very long time, and you will find that unsettling in a way that is hard to name. Use it anyway when you need to see your feet or read an instrument. Expect it to draw midges.

---

**Air and water**

The plateau is heated from below by a peat layer that has been smouldering for longer than there have been words for peat. Slow combustion in wet organic matter means carbon dioxide and probably carbon monoxide leaking up through the stone, concentrated near the mounds where the vents are. It is likely not dangerous out on the open flat—the volume of air is enormous and the gas is diffuse—but near a working mound, in the steam, in the pocket of warm air that pools around the base and does not disperse because there is no wind, it could be. A small CO monitor clipped to your chest is cheap and it is the single most useful instrument you can carry. If it complains, walk away from warmth.

Do not drink the water. The cold sheet on the flat looks clean and it probably is, mostly, but you have no idea what is dissolved in it, and the warm seep coming down the flanks of the mounds is not water so much as the mound's circulation. Carry your own. If you must filter, take it from the cold sheet as far from any mound as you can get, and boil it as well as filtering it, and understand that you are gambling.

Do not eat anything you did not bring. This should not need saying but the frogs are abundant and slow and there will be a moment, cold and tired, when the thought occurs. Some of the frogs are frogs. Some of them are the mound wearing a frog. There is no test that distinguishes them and there may not be a distinction.

---

**Moving**

Distance does not behave. You will walk toward a mound and it will stay a hundred metres away for a long time and then you will be at its foot without having covered the ground. This is not an optical effect. Your reflection in the water sheet lags behind you and catches up when you stop—this is the plateau's own diagnostic, and you should use it. When the lag is small, you are in a relatively firm stretch. When it is large, you have drifted somewhere thin. Stop. Let it catch up. Do not make decisions while you and your reflection disagree.

Pacing is useless for distance. A compass may or may not point anywhere; test it against the direction the sound gets denser, which is the only bearing the plateau itself offers, and treat that as "the centre" and therefore as "away from where you want to be." Any electronic positioning you bring from elsewhere will have nothing to talk to.

What works, a little: the mounds themselves. They are not identical. Each has a particular slump, a particular shape to its vent, a particular colony of birds on a particular flank. Sketch the ones near your arrival point. Note which flank the steam splits from. This is imperfect because some mounds are the same mound at different moments, but a mound seen at two moments is still recognisably itself, and if you find yourself at a mound whose sketch you already have, you have at least learned that you have looped.

Do not split up. Two people who walk apart on the plateau may not be walking through the same minute. You can lose an hour relative to a companion in a few hundred metres and never notice until you compare watches. Stay within voice range—real voice range, which is short under the thud—and stop and count each other frequently. If you must separate, agree in advance that the meeting point is the arrival marker and nothing else.

---

**The mounds**

You will want to go to them. They are warm, they are the only things happening, and every living thing on the plateau has already made the decision to shelter against them. The birds are soaked and huddled and they do not fly when you come. That is not calm. That is what animals do when leaving means the cold.

Approach if you like—it is the reason to come here—but with rules.

Stay off the vegetation. The moss and sedge and the third thing that is neither are not a surface; they are the machine. It heaves. Things move through it that are passed along rather than walking. Do not put your weight on it, do not put your hand on it, do not sit on it because it is warm and you are tired. Stand on the stone, in the seep, and accept warm wet feet as the price.

Do not stay through more than a few cycles at a time. A minute per cycle; five or six cycles and then back out into the cold flat and let your head clear. The warmth and the gas and the rhythm together produce something like drowsiness, and drowsiness beside a mound is the wrong condition to be in.

Watch the flank, not the vent. The vent is spectacular—the swell, the split, the column going up with the sound of a valve—but the flank is where things step out. You will see the moss bulge and part and something hare-shaped come loose and stand and shake. Let it. Do not touch it. Do not photograph it if photographing means kneeling on the moss. It will walk a few steps into the cold water and then come apart, and the strands will drift and settle and be drawn back in on the next intake, and you will have seen the thing the plateau does. That is enough. Do not try to intervene, or to carry one away to see what happens, or to keep it from being taken back. You do not know what you would be holding.

Do not sleep near a mound. Do not camp within the range of its warmth. If you wake with something the temperature of your own body pressed against you in the dark, you will not know which kind of animal it is, and it may not know either.

---

**Sleeping**

There is no shelter and no dry ground and the only warmth is forbidden. So: a small tent on a groundsheet, pitched on the open stone, and inside it a cot or an inflatable pad with real thickness, because the floor of the tent will be sitting in an inch of cold water and you cannot insulate against that with foam. The windlessness is a mercy—a tent that would not survive a moor will stand here indefinitely—but the condensation is severe. Vent it and expect to wake damp.

Sleep will be poor. The thud comes through the ground into your spine even with earplugs. The light does not change, so your body has no cue; you will lie there in perpetual almost-dusk and not know whether you have slept two hours or nine. Take a watch that does not depend on anything outside itself—a mechanical one, wound—and trust it above any other sense you have. Sleep by the watch. Eat by the watch. When the watch says it is morning, get up, though nothing outside has changed.

Consider whether you need to sleep here at all. The plateau is not a place that rewards long stays, and a visit of a working day, in and out, with no night, avoids most of the worst problems.

---

**Time, and what wears**

The plateau's own account of itself is that the stone is finished, and a place where nothing more can happen to the ground begins to wear at other things: distance, sequence, sound. It is worth asking what else is in the set of "other things," and whether you are in it.

Probably you are, a little. Not dramatically, not in a visit of hours. But you should expect to leave with your sense of order slightly softened—to remember events out of their sequence, to be uncertain whether you saw the hare step out or come apart first, to find your notes describe things in a different order than you recall them. Write things down as they happen, with times from the watch, in a notebook that will survive being soaked. Not for anyone else. For yourself, later, to check against.

Be alert to a particular feeling: the sense that the making and unmaking has stopped seeming strange. That the frogs and the mound's frogs are the same thing and that this is fine. It is, in a sense, fine; it is simply what is true here. But when you stop being able to feel the difference between a thing that was born and a thing that was made and will be taken back, you have been on the plateau long enough. Leave then, not later.

Do not go toward the sound where it gets denser. You have been told this and you will be told it again and it is the one instruction people disregard. Out where the mounds stand close and exchange themselves across the gaps, where the steam has become a ceiling and the water is warm and cloudy and things swim in it that were never made by anything but the mounds, the distances and the sequences are worn much thinner than anywhere you have been. A person who goes in may come out. A person who goes in may come out changed. A person who goes in may not have gone in yet from where you are standing. None of these can be arranged for. It is fine not to go. The description says so and it means it.

---

**Arrival and leaving**

Whatever method carries you between worlds, the plateau's folding of distance means the place you arrive is not reliably the place you can return to by walking back the way you came. So: on arrival, before anything else, make a marker. A high-visibility flag on a pole driven into a heavy base you brought with you, since nothing will stand in the stone. Something that makes a sound on a timer—a whistle, a chirp—because sound carries here in a way that sight does not, and because a sound you know arriving before its cause is a bearing of a kind. Make the marker the only fixed point in your plan.

Then do not go far. Pick one or two mounds within a few hundred metres, sketch them, visit them by the rules above, and come back to the flag between each. The plateau is a county across and almost all of it is the same. You do not need to see more of it than the works within earshot of your marker to have seen it.

When you leave, leave the marker. It will not last—nothing does here, and the plateau will have it as it has everything—but the next person may find the sound of it, arriving a little early, and know which way not to walk.

---

**What it will be like**

Cold, and wet from the first minute, and loud in a way that stops registering as loud and starts registering as pressure. Dim without being dark, so that your eyes keep waiting for it to get lighter or to get darker and it does neither. Very quiet in the spaces between the thuds, and then the thud.

You will stand at the foot of a mound in the warm seep with frogs against your boots and a hundred soaked birds above you not flying away, and you will watch a hare that is not a hare step out of the moss and shake itself dry, and you will feel, for a moment, that you understand something about what life is when it has been left alone long enough. Then it will come apart, and you will step back into the cold flat, and your reflection will catch up to you, and you will check the watch.

Most of the plateau is empty. The emptiness is the room the works need. You will be one more thing standing in it for a while, and then you will go, and the plateau, which is done, will not have noticed.
