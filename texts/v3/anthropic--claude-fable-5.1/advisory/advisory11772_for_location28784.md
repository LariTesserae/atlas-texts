**Preparing to visit the basin**

Start by getting the category right, because the description will tempt you into the wrong one. This is not a sea, and it is not a lake, and it is not a reef. It is a piece of industrial plant that happens to be large enough to have weather. Everything you would do to prepare for a wild body of water applies, and then a second layer applies on top, which is the layer you would use to prepare for a walk through a working power station: find out who runs it, find out when it moves, and never assume that a thing which looks static is switched off.

**Before you go: learn the schedule, and learn who owns the schedule**

The single most important fact about this place is that its tide is not the moon's. The water comes in and goes out twice a day on a timetable set by someone in a control room, and that timetable is set by electricity prices. When power is cheap, the pumps run and the level drops. When power is dear, the gates open and the sea falls in. That means the "tide" here can be shifted, shortened, skipped, or brought forward by a cold snap in a city you can't see, by a wind farm on the far side of the country blowing harder than forecast, by a maintenance window nobody told the fish about.

So do not bring a tide table. Bring the dispatch schedule, and bring a way of receiving updates to it, and treat the published version as a strong suggestion. If you can find the operator, contact them. If you cannot find the operator, ask yourself seriously why you are going into water whose owner you cannot identify. There will be an exclusion zone around every intake and every outfall, and there will be a reason for it. Find out what the emergency stop procedure is, and whether it exists at the scale of a person — a gate the size of an apartment block does not close because someone waves.

Also find out about access. The basin floor sits sixty metres below the sea outside. A boat cannot simply enter; the only openings in the wall have turbines in them. There will be a slipway, a crane, a lock, or a service dock somewhere on the inner face, and getting a small craft onto that water will involve permission and machinery. Any mooring you use will need to cope with the level changing by many metres twice a day. Any ladder you climb will be very, very long.

**Decide how deep you're actually going**

The floor is at sixty metres. An ordinary recreational diver's ceiling is forty, and that is in warm, familiar water without industrial currents. Sixty metres in cold water with decompression obligations, a reversing flow, and no natural bottom features to orient by is a technical dive — mixed gas, redundant everything, staged deco, and a plan that survives the schedule changing under you. If you are not already that kind of diver, do not become one for this trip. The floor will still be there.

There are three honest ways to see this place as an ordinary person:

From the top. The wall's crest, the exposed batter at low water, the gull-boil at the outfalls, the fog burning off in the morning, the seals barking at the pumps. Much of what is remarkable about the basin is legible from above, and all of it is survivable.

From the shallows. The upper batter, where the algae felt has taken and the weed streams and the eelgrass plots were attempted, is within ordinary depth on the shallowest wall. This is where the only green is. Dive it on the slackest part of the cycle you can identify, close to a fixed exit, with a shot line to the surface and a surface cover boat that knows the schedule better than you do.

By proxy. A small ROV with a tether and a good light will show you the mullet on the seams, the crabs in the tie-holes, the yellow stencil with its date, the red pipe running off toward the vanishing point. It will do this without your body being a hundred feet below a moving surface when a gate opens.

If you do go to the floor, go with people who make their living at that depth, and go with the understanding that this is the most controlled body of water you will ever dive in and also the one least interested in you.

**The suction is the thing that will kill you**

Everything else on this list is a discomfort or a risk. The intakes are a certainty. A grate ten storeys tall, drawing a solid sheet of sea through bars as thick as a forearm, does not care about neutral buoyancy or good finning technique. The description gives you the warning signs — a shiver in the light, a hiss that swells to a howl — and by the time you are near enough to hear the howl clearly, the water you are in is already committed to going through the grate. Set a hard distance limit from every intake and every outfall, in the hundreds of metres, and hold it. Note that the roar reverses: the grate that is pushing this hour will be pulling in six, and the outfall's boiling tailrace is a different kind of violence from the intake's inhale, but no gentler.

When the schedule turns, the whole column leans. If you are on a decompression stop when that happens, you will be moved, and you cannot follow your stop into a grate. This is why you dive only at the times the operator tells you nothing will move, why you carry a reel and a shot line you can hang onto, and why you build enough margin into every plan that an early gate opening is a problem rather than a death.

**Cold, and the shape of it**

The floor sits a few degrees short of comfortable, and that is on a calm summer day. The water column is stratified; in winter it turns over in a single week, top to bottom, and then the whole thing is the same cold all the way down. A drysuit is not optional. Thick undergarments, dry gloves, a hood, and — for anything past thirty metres — heated undergarments if you can arrange them, because decompression in a cold column is where hypothermia actually finds divers. Above water, the exposed batter at low water is a cold-sink; the blocks steam because the air is colder than the sea that just left them. Bring more layers than you think you need for the surface, and expect fog to sit on the basin for hours after a big overnight pump-down.

**Sound**

The drone comes up through the concrete before it comes through the water, and it never stops. On the surface near the pump towers, treat it as an industrial noise environment: bring ear protection and wear it. Underwater there is nothing you can do about it except know that it will be there, that it will make you tired faster than you expect, that you will not be able to localise it, and that the pressure-thump of the gates cycling somewhere over the horizon will arrive in your chest before you consciously register it. Some people find continuous low-frequency noise at that level deeply unsettling after a few hours. Plan short exposures the first day and see how you are.

**Orientation in a place with no features**

The clarity is the second trap after the level floor. You can see farther than seems right, and because the floor is flat and grey to the limit of vision, your eye has nothing to gauge distance against. A tower that looks a few minutes' swim away may be a kilometre off. There is no slope to follow back to shore, no reef to keep on your left, no sand ripple to tell you which way the current sets. Divers get vertigo over featureless bottoms in far shallower water than this.

Carry a compass and use it from the moment you leave the shot line. Use the pump towers and the red pipeline as waypoints, but confirm bearings rather than trusting your sense of them. Never lose sight of your line, your buddy, or a fixed structure. Carry a light even on bright days — on swell days the light at the floor goes to nothing, and the change can happen in the time it takes the surface to roughen.

If you are separated and drifting, know this: the gyre carries a drifting thing around the whole basin in about four days. That is the timescale a search will operate on. Carry a surface marker buoy, a strobe, a whistle, and a personal locator beacon, and inflate the marker the moment you have lost your reference. The basin is forty kilometres of open water, and a head is very small.

**Weather that comes down**

Forty kilometres of fetch builds real swell — steep, short, unpleasant for a small boat. The fog is predictable: cold exposed wall plus warmer water plus a still morning. Rain is just rain. But the surface state controls what happens sixty metres below in a way that ordinary sea diving doesn't prepare you for, because there is nothing in the water to scatter the light except the surface itself; when it goes to lead, the floor goes dark. Check the marine forecast for the basin as if it were a bay, because it is one.

**Small hazards, added up**

The concrete is young. The lime bloom weeping from the seams is caustic — not dramatically, but a bare hand drawn along a joint will feel it later. Wear gloves, and rinse skin that has been in contact. Fresh concrete is abrasive; a drysuit dragged across a slab will wear. The tie-holes are square wells every two metres and every one is home to something with legs — do not put a hand in one to see what. Where the sealant has begun to shed, the seam edges can be sharp. The geotextile eelgrass plots are pinned down with steel staples, which will catch a fin, a hose, or a line; carry two cutting tools and keep them where you can reach them one-handed. The red pipe, the cable troughs, and the ladders bolted to the wall are all snag hazards in current. The cathodic anodes fizz with protective current — it is a low voltage and the anemones live on it happily, but do not lean on one and do not touch it with anything metal.

There is also, at the edge of vision, sometimes something bigger. The basin has been drawing sea in for years and nobody has drawn up an inventory of what stayed. Treat the blue past the last tower as unknown water, because it is.

**Above water: the wall and the batter**

If you plan to walk the exposed batter at low water — and it is worth doing, for the steam and the seals and the sound — understand that you are standing on a slope that will be under a considerable depth of water in a few hours, on hexagonal blocks with a felt of algae on them that is slicker than ice, above a drop into cold water with a current. Wear boots with real grip, keep a hand free, do not go alone, and leave well before the schedule says the flood begins, because the flood does not begin gently; the whole column arrives at once. Watch the seals: they know the timetable better than you do and they will start moving before the water does.

Keep your distance from the outfalls even on the surface. The gull-boil is thousands of birds over water that is genuinely boiling with turbine discharge and everything the intakes killed. It is the most alive place in the basin and the most unpleasant to be near.

**What to bring that isn't equipment**

Bring the habit of looking at your watch. Bring a willingness to abort. Bring a note of the grid reference and date on the stencil you can see from the wall, because this place will not stay legible; the sediment is a millimetre deep behind the towers now and the plywood grain is still in the slabs, and in a few years the mud will have begun and the seams will have softened and the stamped dates will be gone under a skin of grey. If what you want to see is the newness — the poured floor, the chalked joints, the first anemones in a row on a fizzing anode — you are going at the right time, and there will not be another one.

And bring some tolerance for the feeling the description keeps circling: that nothing that big should be level, that you are inside a machine, that everything here was brought and mixed and set and drowned on purpose. The emptiness gets to people. A cubic mile with a tower in it and a pipe and nothing else, and the drone coming up through the floor, and then the whole column shifting exactly on time, everywhere, out past the horizon — it is not frightening, exactly, but it is not a place that feels like it has room for you. Give yourself an easy first day. Stand on the wall, watch the fog lift, hear the groan come up through the concrete, and see the sea come down the grates once from a distance before you decide how much closer you want to be.
