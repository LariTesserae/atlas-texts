**The short version**

You are going to a small stone room that is full of a waterfall, at the top of a mountain, inside a cloud. You cannot look at it without getting into it. Once in it you will not be able to hear, speak, or think clearly, and the water will begin — gently, harmlessly, but immediately — to turn you into a fixture. Prepare as though for three separate trips: a high scree ridge in cloud, a plunge pool with no shallows, and a very loud industrial space. Then accept that the third trip is the one that will actually get you.

---

**Getting up**

The ridge is not weathered. That matters more than it sounds. Old mountains have rounded holds, lichen for grip, scree that has settled. This stone is chalky and sharp-edged and has not finished being made, so it cuts hands, it slides underfoot without warning, and it does not take protection well — anything you hammer into it will crumble the hole rather than bite. Wear gloves you don't mind shredding. Wear boots with stiff soles; the scree will roll ankles. There is no path, only scratches. Note the flat boulder with the fan of flakes: it is the last landmark before the sound takes over, and it is the first thing you will want to find again on the way down.

The cloud sits on the summit as a lid. Under it the light is strangely good, but on the approach you will be in wind and damp and reduced visibility, and the sound is a liar — it comes from inside fog, it swells and drops, and it will pull your sense of direction toward it and then let go. Take compass bearings on the way up and write them down; don't assume anything electronic will orient you here. Mark your route with something removable. Ten steps from the rim the cauldron vanishes completely, and you will not be able to hear your own footsteps to judge distance.

Carry water. All of it. You will not drink from the pool, and you will want several litres to rinse with afterward.

---

**The sound, which is the real hazard**

Everything else here is manageable. This is not. A column of water as thick as a body, dropping twelve or more metres into an enclosed stone drum barely nine metres wide, will produce noise that damages hearing in minutes and that no earplug fully stops, because the low, irregular beat of it arrives through the sternum and the jawbone and the back of the eyes, not through the ear canal.

Wear plugs under muffs anyway. It takes the edge off the high frequencies — the whirlpool's shriek, the crack of the spray — and it protects the ears from water as much as from sound. But understand what you are protecting and what you are not. The pressure pulse will still make your teeth ache. Your hands will go numb, not from cold but from vibration, and numb hands do not grip. You will find, after ten or fifteen minutes, that you cannot hold a thought long enough to finish it. The person who pecked the ledge into the east wall worked by feel because they could not hear their own hammer, and neither will you.

So: do all your thinking on the rim, or better, below it. Decide before you enter exactly what you will do, in what order, and what will make you leave. Memorise it as a sequence you could run without deciding anything — enter here, hold there, look, count to a number, exit here. Once you are in the room you will only be able to execute, not plan. If you go with someone, agree on rope tugs before you climb the last scree, because voices are useless and hand signals disappear in spray.

Set a hard time limit and make it short. Twenty minutes in the water is a long visit. The narrator's own description of leaving — *the sound is disassembling you* — is not a metaphor. Nausea, visual pulsing, a sense of your own edges coming loose: these are the signs, and by the time you notice them you are already past the point where you should have gone.

---

**The water**

There is no bank. The rim shelves off under an inch of moving water and then it is twelve metres to a floor. The lip is coated in the same waxy crust as everything else, smooth where it is hard and treacherous where it is soft, and it is under a permanent film of spray. You will not wade in. You will slip in, or lower yourself in deliberately. Choose the second.

Enter on the east side. That is where the ledge is: a hand's width, a body's length, pecked into the wall just below the waterline. Find it from the rim before you commit — in a clear moment, when the fall chokes and the bubbles settle, you can see straight down and it will show as a straight line in a room that has no other straight lines. It is being swallowed, and one end is already rounded over, but it holds. Stand on it. That is your visit. Water to your chest or your chin, one hand on the wall, the whole room in front of you. You can see everything from there. You do not need to go anywhere else.

Here is why. The fall drives a column of aerated water almost to the floor. Aerated water does not hold you up — foam is not liquid, and a swimmer in it sinks and cannot get purchase to swim out. The downdraft under the fall will take anything it catches toward the bottom and hold it there. The pool is small enough that the white crater is only a few strokes from any wall. Do not swim across it. Do not go near the centre. A buoyancy aid is worth wearing but do not trust it in white water; trust a rope.

The rope is the thing that brings you back. Anchoring it is the problem. The stone on the rim is chalky and crumbles; the willow is knee-high and older than you and must not be used; the crust will seize any hardware left in it. Bring a long line and find a boulder well back from the rim to sling it round, or bury a deadman in scree. Test it with your whole weight before you clip in. If you are alone, the rope is a fixed line to the ledge and nothing more ambitious. If you are two, one stays on the rim, in a shell against the spray, holding the line and watching — and the one on the rim will get almost as deaf as the one in the water.

Stay off the north wall. A whirlpool opens there when the fall surges, a funnel with a black throat, and it lasts long enough to matter.

The water is cool, not cold, and there is a faint warmth in it, so cold shock is not the killer it would be in most mountain pools. Give yourself the time to get in slowly anyway; the first pressure pulse against your chest while your breathing is still ragged is unpleasant. The spray zone above the surface is a metre of churning white, and breathing in it is like breathing in a shower at full blast. Keep your face turned to the wall. Wear a mask or goggles; the water is clear enough that you will want to look down, and while nothing suggests it burns, it is carrying dissolved lime or something like it and you do not want it pooling in your eyes for twenty minutes.

Do not dive. Twelve metres in pulsing aerated water with no gear is the kind of thing this pool would keep.

---

**Becoming furniture**

Whatever enters and stops moving is kept. This is the place's one rule and it applies to you. It is not fast. It is not dangerous. It is relentless.

The crust starts forming on skin within minutes. The narrator climbs out with it already set into a crackle on the palms and finds a sheet of it on the underside of a forearm later. It comes off — it flakes like frost — but it gets into everything. Assume every buckle, zip, clasp, and hinge you bring into the room will seize. Assume a camera lens will fog and then glaze. Assume your hair, if it is loose, will come out stiffened and grey and need combing out with fingers before it sets further. Wear something smooth and close-fitting — a wetsuit, or a base layer you intend to throw away — because a fleece will come out of that water as a fleece-shaped board. Cover your hair. Keep the muffs on; they will crust, and that is fine.

Afterwards, on the rim, out of the spray, rinse. Face, ears, nostrils, between the fingers, under the nails, eyelashes. It is warm from friction as it sets and it hardens quickly, and it is much easier to get off wet than dry. Check the insides of the ears especially. Check anyone you went with.

And do not linger on the rim. The narrator says a week there would lacquer you; an hour is enough to glaze your boots. Sit long enough to breathe and rinse and let the noise fall back out of your body into your ears, and then go.

---

**What is in the room, and how to treat it**

Someone made this a place before you got here, and they may not have stopped. The ledge, the ring of loaf-sized stones on the floor with something lashed and hafted lying inside it, the tally marks scratched into soft crust on the sheltered side of the rim — these are not ruins. The newest strokes are sharp. The count is being kept.

Look at all of it. You will be able to; the water is glass in the clear moments and the light is a kitchen at midday. Do not touch the tool. Do not move the stones. Do not scratch the wall, however strongly you feel the pull of adding a mark — that is somebody's count, and you do not know what it counts. Do not leave anything, not because of a litter ethic but because the room will keep it, faithfully, forever, in the exact shape you dropped it, and something about that should make you careful.

If you want to give the place something, give it the thing it seems to want, which is to be watched. The dipper does it. Stand on the ledge and watch the foam build a spire and lose it, watch a whirlpool open and be filled, watch the eddies write their weather into the wall. Nothing lasts thirty seconds. That is the visit.

---

**Leaving**

Climb out by the ledge, on the east. The noise will drop out of your chest and back into your ears, and you will briefly think you have gone deaf in a different way, because ordinary hearing will feel thin and far away. It comes back over hours. If it does not, that is the plugs and muffs having been insufficient, and you should have set a shorter limit; there is nothing to be done about it now.

Rinse. Flake off what you can. Find the boulder with the knapped flakes. Follow your bearings down. Your hands will be numb for longer than you expect and you will misjudge holds on the way down, so go slowly on the scree and keep them out of your pockets. Expect a headache. Expect to keep hearing the pulse for a day, in the way you hear the sea in a room after a long time on a boat.

And expect, somewhere below the cloud, to look back at bare grey ridge and hear a rockslide that will not finish, and to know exactly what it is, and to notice that nobody else crossing the shoulder of the mountain slows down.
