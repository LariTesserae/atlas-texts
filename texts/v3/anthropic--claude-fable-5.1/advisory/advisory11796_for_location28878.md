**Preparing for the Basin**

Begin with the frame of mind, because the gear follows from it.

This is not a dangerous place in the way a jungle or a glacier is dangerous. Nothing in it wants you. Nothing in it is hunting. The frogs don't call, the heron has decided you're furniture, and the towers are doing something that has nothing to do with you at all. The danger is a quieter kind: the basin is a place that runs on rules you didn't grow up with, and it is patient, and it drips, and it is very easy to lose an afternoon and then a day and then your sense of which direction the rim is. Nearly every piece of advice below is really the same piece of advice — don't let the place set your clock — dressed in different clothes.

---

**On clothing, and on being wet**

You will be wet the entire time. Accept this before you go, because the people who fight it arrive home with trench foot and ruined kit.

The air is warm and nearly saturated. A rain shell will not keep you dry; it will just keep your own sweat against you, and within an hour you'll be as soaked inside it as you would have been without, only hotter. Leave the hard shell. Wear layers that hold their shape and their warmth when saturated — wool, or the good synthetics — and wear them loose. Nothing cotton. Cotton in that basin becomes a cold wet weight in an hour and stays that way until you leave.

Boots: something that drains and dries rather than something waterproof. The soil sinks a finger's depth and comes up clean — it is not mud, it does not cling, it is more like walking on packed flour — but the streets are becoming channels and every low place holds water. You will step in it. Bring at least three pairs of socks, rotate them, and sleep in dry ones no matter what. Check your feet morning and evening. Bring foot powder and an antifungal; the environment is exactly the one that skin infections were invented for, and there is no medical help within the basin.

Gloves. Thin ones, leather-palmed. The edges of the structures are sharp enough to cut, and you will brush against one at some point without meaning to, because the light makes distances lie and because the surface gives your eye nothing to fix on. A gash in this humidity will not close cleanly. Bring more wound care than you think you need — irrigation syringe, iodine, closure strips, a real supply of dressings, and know that they'll be damp when you use them.

Everything you own goes in dry bags inside the pack, and the dry bags get opened only when you need what's in them. Paper will not survive. If you want to write, bring a waxed notebook or a slate and grease pencil. If you bring electronics, bring them expecting to lose them.

---

**On light**

The basin is dim, but it is not dark, and this is a distinction that matters for what you carry.

You'll want a headlamp for walking the fern fields at night — there is enough ambient glow from the structures to see the towers themselves, but the ground between them is a black-green mass and you will not see a channel edge until you are in it. Bring a lamp with a red mode and use it. Your eyes will adapt to the pewter light within a day, and white light will wreck that adaptation each time you use it.

Do not bother shining a light at a tower. The description is exact: light that falls on them arrives and stays. You will see nothing. Not a reflection, not a hotspot, not the shape of the surface. Just your beam ending. People find this more disturbing than they expect, so it's worth knowing in advance that it's simply what the material does, and that pointing your light at it is like pouring water into sand.

Cameras will fail to do the place justice and will probably fail to do the place at all. Expect underexposure, expect strange readings, expect your images to show a flat grey field with darker verticals and none of the actual quality of the light. If you must document, sketch.

Bring sunglasses for the walk out. You will not want them inside. But after even two days below the rim your eyes will have settled into the dusk, and the ordinary daylight of the old country, when you climb back into it, will hit you like a hand.

One practical gift: your shadow falls toward the nearest structure. Not away from the sky — toward whatever tower is closest. This is unsettling for about an hour and then becomes the most useful thing in the basin. In fern that stands at your own height, when you can't see the streets and the towers are just dark shapes against a grey lid, your own shadow is a compass needle pointing at the nearest fixed object. Learn to read it. Watch how it swings as you walk, and you'll know when you've passed the midpoint between two structures before your eyes tell you.

---

**On water**

There is more water here than you could use, and none of it is a river.

It arrives out of the air in the morning, condensing on the towers and running down into the collars and out along the streets, and it leaves back into the air in the afternoon. So there is a rhythm to collect by: the morning is wet, the collars are full, the channels are running. By late afternoon the shallow places have gone back to damp ground and only the deep pools in the low basin still hold.

The condensate off the tower faces is likely the cleanest water you will ever drink — it is, in effect, distilled. But it runs down into fresh mineral soil that has not had time to be anything but rock dust, and what comes out of the collars will taste of the stone it's split from and may carry more of it than your body wants. Filter everything. Bring a filter rated for sediment as well as biology, and carry a spare element, because fine mineral silt will clog one faster than you'd think. If you can, collect from the faces directly — a cloth pressed against the surface and wrung out, or a sheet laid at the footing to catch the run before it hits the ground.

Do not drink from the still pools. Not because they are foul — they may be perfectly clean — but because they mirror the towers perfectly, and because you will have to kneel at their edge with your face over the water to fill a bottle, and that is a posture I would not take up in this place without a reason better than thirst. Fill in the morning from the running seams.

---

**On food**

Bring all of it.

There is nothing here you can eat, and the reason is not that things are poisonous but that nothing has had time to become food. The soil has no rot in it. Whatever biology a forest floor normally runs on has not been established. The ferns and horsetails are very old kinds of plant — the sort that were here before things learned to be edible — and horsetail in particular carries silica and compounds that will make you sick in quantity. The broad-leaved thing with no name should be left alone on principle. The frogs are the size of a thumb-joint and pale and silent and I would not eat one for the same reason I would not drink from the pools.

Calculate your days, add half again, and pack for that. The place slows you down. A tower a quarter mile off takes an hour. A day that should cover a good distance covers half of it. You will arrive back at the rim later than you planned, and you should have food for that lateness.

Bring electrolytes. The warmth is not extreme, but it is unrelenting and it is wet, and you will sweat more than you realize because the sweat won't evaporate. Heat exhaustion is entirely possible in a place that looks like dusk. Watch for the headache and the irritability and the moment you notice you haven't wanted water in a while.

---

**On where to stand, and where to sleep**

There are three kinds of ground: the towers and their collars, the streets, and the fern fields between. Only one of them is yours.

Stay off the collars. They are raw heaved soil around the base of each structure, and some of them are still cracking, and some are still — slowly, but still — rising. This is not stable ground. More to the point, it is the boundary between the tower and the world, and it is actively being renegotiated. Don't stand in a doorway that's still being built.

Stay off the streets at night. This is the most important thing in this document.

The streets are where the ferns are pushed back, each night, by something. They are where the large low shape was moving when it was seen. They are the seams the city laid down before anything walked them, and something is now beginning to walk them. Whatever is maintaining the streets is doing so in the dark, and you do not want to be the thing it finds in its way. Walk them by day if you must, because they are the only firm straight going in the basin. But when the towers begin to give their light back, be somewhere else.

Sleep in the fern fields, roughly midway between two structures — find the point where your shadow goes faint and undecided because it can't choose a tower. Sleep on a raised bit if you can find one, because everything low becomes channel by morning. Sleep with your boots in a bag and your dry socks on and your headlamp on its lanyard around your neck. Don't string a tarp between ferns; the ferns adjust in the night, and you'll wake with it on your face. A bivy is better. You'll be damp in it, but you'll be damp anywhere.

Note about the insects: there aren't many, and they avoid the towers entirely, swinging wide around each one as if there were a wind. They are the only creatures in the basin with a strong opinion about the structures, and they have had longer than you to form it. Trust them. Do not camp closer to a tower than the insects will fly.

---

**On touching the towers**

You will want to. Everyone does. The surface is warm, a few degrees above the air, and matte, and it does not want to be looked at, which makes the hand want to confirm what the eye can't.

If you do, do it with the back of your hand first, the way you'd test a pan. Do it with a glove on. Do not lean. Do not press your palm flat and rest your weight, because the account is that the surface is too smooth to be a surface — as though there were nothing there — and I don't know what happens to a hand that leans on nothing, and you should not be the one who finds out.

Do not try to chip it, cut it, or take a piece. Partly because you almost certainly can't. Partly because the towers are changing, fractionally, each time the stone-sound comes, and you do not want to be the reason for one of those changes.

And do not look for a door. There are none. If you find one anyway — if a seam opens, if a face is different than it was, if you round a footing and there is a darkness where before there was surface — that is not an invitation. That is information about what stage the city has reached, and the correct response to that information is to turn around and go back the way you came, which, in this basin, is always shorter than the way forward.

---

**On time**

The difference between day and night here is smaller than it should be, and you will lose track. Not in a day. But by the third morning you'll wake unsure whether the grey is dawn-grey or dusk-grey, and by the fourth you'll have stopped being sure it matters.

It matters. Bring a mechanical watch. Wind it. Write the day-count on your slate each morning before you do anything else.

The basin has its own clock, and you should learn it without letting it replace yours. Roughly once an hour, from inside the nearest tower, there is a sound like a large stone being set carefully into place. It does not echo. After it, the tower is a little different, though you won't be able to say how. Count these. They are the most regular thing in the place. Check them against your watch, and if they start disagreeing — if the stones come closer together, or the watch and the stones drift apart — note it, and consider whether it's time to go.

Decide before you descend how many stone-sounds you'll spend below the rim. Make it a number. Then keep it.

---

**On direction, and on the west**

The streets are longer than they look. The tower behind you is closer than it was when you left it. These are not tricks of the light; they are how the basin works, and the ferns have accepted them and so should you.

The practical consequence is that retreat is cheap and advance is expensive. Going back toward the rim is easier than going deeper. This is a kindness, if you use it. It means you can always leave. It also means that every step further in costs more than the last, and you should measure your progress in hours rather than in landmarks, because landmarks lie.

The streets bend, over miles, a few degrees toward the west, where the vapor lid is thicker and the towers stand closer together. The whole basin leans that way. If you follow any street long enough you will end up going west whether you meant to or not.

Decide, before you go down, whether you are going west.

If you are not — and I would not, not on a first visit, not alone — then you need to walk against the lean. Pick a tower on the eastern side and keep it on your left and check your shadow every few hundred steps to make sure you haven't been turned. A compass may work. I'd bring one. But I would not trust it over the shadow, because the shadow is native to the place and the compass is not.

If you are going west, go with the knowledge that the towers cluster there, and the light is dimmer, and whatever the city is for is more concentrated there than anywhere else. Go with double the food. Go with a hard turn-around time. Go knowing that the stone-sound will come from more than one direction, and that you should probably stop the first time you hear two at once.

---

**On the other things**

The frogs will not bother you. They don't call and they don't approach.

The heron has decided the city is acceptable and stands in the shallows all afternoon. Leave it alone. It's the only creature that seems at ease here, and I'd take its ease as information: the pools and the daytime shallows are safe enough for a bird that has to be very still for a very long time.

The larger thing — low, deliberate, moving along a street as though it were the only thing that had ever needed to be there — you will probably not see, and you should not try to. If you do see it, do what the account did: watch it go behind the horsetail and then don't follow. It moves the way the towers grow. It is part of the city in a way you are not.

---

**On the mood of the place**

I'll end with this because it's the thing the gear can't fix.

The basin is a standing dusk, warm and dripping and nearly silent, and it is waiting. It has been recently uncovered — the fresh stone smell tells you that — and it is very early in being whatever it's going to be. The towers are the newest things you'll ever see. Nothing has walked the streets. It is a city before its people, or a city for something that doesn't need to be a person, and from inside there's no way to know which.

That waiting is contagious. After a day or two you'll find yourself standing in the ferns not doing anything, listening for the stone-sound, and finding that you're content to. The light doesn't push you. Nothing pushes you. It would be very easy to stay another hour, and another, and to sit in the pewter dark watching the towers give back the day they drank, and to think that this was fine.

It isn't that it's not fine. It's that you are not what this place is waiting for. It is not finished. The collars rise, the channels deepen, the ferns are pushed back each night by a hand you haven't seen. Something is being made ready for something, and you, with your damp wool and your wound-up watch, are early.

Be early. Look. Count the towers, listen for the stones, watch your shadow lean toward the dark. Then go back up the rise into the old country before the basin decides you're part of it, and put your sunglasses on, and let the light come back.
