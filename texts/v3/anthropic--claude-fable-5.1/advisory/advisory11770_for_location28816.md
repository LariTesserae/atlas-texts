**Preparing for the pocket in the pale mass**

Start by understanding what kind of place this is, because it will shape every choice you make. It is not dangerous in the way a cliff or a predator is dangerous. Nothing in it wants anything from you. It is dangerous in the way a very long, very still afternoon is dangerous: it will not hurt you, but it will not stop you from hurting yourself, and it will give you no signal when the time has come to leave. You are the only thing in that room that can run down, dry out, go deaf, or lose track of the hours. Prepare as though you are the hazard, because you are.

---

**Getting in, and getting out again**

The entrance is a gap the width of a shoulder between two leaning ribs, and you pass it sideways with your arms slack. Rehearse this. Not the squeeze itself—you will manage that—but the discipline of going in carrying nothing on your back. A pack will wedge. A pack will also press against the ribs, and the ribs take impressions and hold them; you will scar the doorway on your way through, and possibly narrow it. So: everything you bring goes through the gap ahead of you, by hand, or comes in on a line you pull after yourself once you are inside. A slim chest rig or a belt is fine. Anything that stands off the body more than the depth of your hand is not.

The ribs "close behind you." Take this as a description of how it looks—the packing is so dense that once you are inside, the gap is just one more seam among hundreds—rather than as a warning that you will be sealed in. But treat it seriously anyway. The single most important thing you do in this place is mark the way out, and you must do it *before* you take a step away from the ribs. Nothing in the room will help you find it again. Every surface is the same white, lit from inside, with no shadow to give a seam depth. Your own palm-print, pressed into the rib as a marker, will vanish into the thousands already there. Chalk on chalk is invisible. Bring something that cannot be mistaken for anything in the room: a length of dark cord tied loosely around the rib (loosely—it will bite into the paper if you cinch it), a strip of colored tape, a small dark object wedged in the gap itself. Choose a color that will be the only one of its kind in the room. Red, blue, black. Then stand in the center, turn a full circle, and confirm you can pick it out from every direction.

Do not go through any other gap. The description tells you plainly that this pocket is one of "uncountable others." The gaps you move through inside the room are not all dead ends; some will let a body through into the next cell, and the next, and they are all lit the same, all ringing the same note, all identical to the one you left. There is no sound gradient to follow home, because the sound comes from everywhere. There is no light gradient, for the same reason. A person who wanders two pockets deep into that mass without a line is a person who may never find the leaning ribs again. If you cannot resist exploring, bring a spool of dark line, tie it off at the entrance, and never let go of it. Better still, resist.

---

**The sound**

Read the description once more: loud enough to feel in the roof of your mouth, without lull, without variation, and you stop noticing it after a while. That last part is the danger. A sound you have stopped noticing is still doing whatever it does to your hearing. If it is loud enough to feel in bone, it is loud enough that unprotected exposure over hours will cost you something permanent.

Wear hearing protection from the moment you are through the gap. Foam plugs under over-ear muffs, both. Understand that this will not remove the sound—if it is felt in the palate, it is arriving through your skull, and no earplug stops that—but it will bring the airborne part down to something your ears can tolerate for a while. Set a hard limit on your stay based on that, not on how you feel. How you feel will lie to you; you will adapt to the note within the first half hour and it will seem quiet. It is not quiet.

If you go with a companion, agree on hand signs beforehand. You will not hear each other. You may not even hear yourself. Some people find this profoundly disorienting; the sound of your own voice is a thread back to yourself, and here it is cut. Speak anyway, if it helps, but do not expect it to work as communication.

Expect the note to follow you out. The description says so, and it is right: your ears will manufacture it for hours, possibly days. This is ordinary tinnitus and it will likely fade, but arrange for a quiet, dim place to return to. Do not go from the pocket into a city.

---

**The light**

Even, directionless, shadowless. This sounds gentle and is not. Human eyes judge distance and shape by shadow; take shadow away and every rib, shelf and cell-mouth flattens into the wall behind it. You will misjudge how far away things are. You will reach for a shelf and find it a hand-span nearer or farther than you thought. You will walk into struts. Move slowly, and move with a hand out in front of you, the way you would in the dark—because in terms of what your eyes can tell you about depth, this is a kind of dark that happens to be white.

Bring tinted eye protection. Not for glare, exactly—the light is described as faint, glowing, not blazing—but for relief. A uniform bright field with nothing to fix on wears the eye out and, over time, starts to make it invent things. People who sit in featureless white fields for long enough see movement that is not there, colors that are not there, sometimes figures. This room is not quite featureless—it is packed—but it is *monotone*, and the effect is similar. Carry one small dark object—a black stone, a dark cloth—and look at it regularly. Close your eyes for a full minute every so often. Give your vision an edge to rest against.

And there is no day here. It does not get darker. Your body will have no idea how long you have been inside. Bring a timepiece with an alarm you can feel—a vibration—since you may not hear a chime under the note. Set it before you go in. When it goes off, leave. Do not negotiate with yourself about it; the room will not help you decide, and you will find that you have no independent sense of whether you have been there twenty minutes or four hours.

---

**The dryness and the cold**

The cold is manageable: the chill of a cellar, steady, no wind. Dress as you would for a long time standing still in a stone room—layers, a hat, gloves. Standing still is the key phrase. You will not be exerting yourself, so you will not generate much heat, and the cold will seep in slowly. Better to be slightly overdressed.

The dryness is the more serious thing. "Your breath does not show because there is no moisture in you that the air wants" is a strange sentence and a warning. The air is so dry that even the water leaving your lungs is drunk up before it can fog. That water is coming from you, continuously, and there is nothing in the room to replace it. Every drop you will drink, you carry in. Bring more than you think you need—twice your normal ration for the planned duration—and drink on a schedule, not on thirst, because in cold dry air thirst arrives late.

Bring lip balm and use it before you feel the need. Bring eye drops; your eyes will dry, and with the constant light you will be blinking less than you think. Bring saline for your nose, or expect a nosebleed by the second hour. Cover as much skin as is comfortable. Wear gloves for the same reason, and for another: the mineral paper takes impressions and holds them, and every bare-handed touch is a mark that will outlast you. Touch it once, bare, because you should know what it feels like—chalk-soft, cardboard-grained, cool—and then put the glove back on.

Expect static. Very dry air, a body moving in layered clothing, fine dust everywhere: you will crackle. It is harmless but startling, and it will make the dust cling to you. Which brings up the dust.

---

**The dust, and what you leave**

The floor holds a fine dust lying exactly as it fell, with no tracks in it. The moment you step off the entrance, that stops being true. There is no wind here to erase what you do. Your footprints will be the only footprints, and they will stay.

Sit with that before you go, because it changes how you move. You cannot leave no trace here; you can only choose what trace to leave. Some travelers decide to stay at the ribs and never step onto the dish of the floor at all, and see the room from its edge. Some walk a single deliberate line to the center and stand there and walk back on their own prints. Whatever you decide, decide it outside, with a clear head, rather than inside under the note.

Do not disturb the dust more than you must, and for your own sake wear a mask. It is mineral, chalk-like, fine; you do not want it in your lungs, and in that dryness it will hang in the air for a long time once lifted.

Do not touch the plants. There are six or so, and each holds, folded in its leaves, the only water in the room. Pressing a leaf may cost the plant that water. They have been growing for longer than can be measured at a rate that cannot be observed, and they have never been touched by anything with hands. Look at them. Notice that they lean toward the brightest wall, and that none touches another. Leave them.

Do not take samples. The material softens rather than cracks; breaking off a frill will not give you a clean fragment, it will crumble the edge of something that has held its shape for an age. And you do not know what the substance does outside the conditions that made it. It may be nothing. It may dissolve in the first humid air it meets. It may keep ringing. Whatever the answer, the room has no way to grow it back.

Do not lean on anything with your full weight, and do not climb. The shelves and struts hold up "nothing in particular." They have never been asked to hold a person.

---

**The air itself**

The room is described in terms that assume you can breathe it, so assume you can. But notice: no wind, no draft, a pocket sealed on all sides by dense material. You will be exhaling into stagnant air. The room is not tiny—it goes up tier on tier into brightness—and the paper is thin enough for light to pass, so some exchange likely happens through the walls. For a stay of a few hours this is almost certainly nothing. For anything longer, it is one more reason not to stay longer. If you begin to feel a headache, a heaviness, a shortness of breath that the cold does not explain, that is the signal. Go out.

---

**The part no gear addresses**

Everything above is about keeping your body intact. The other preparation is harder to specify.

This is a place where nothing is happening and nothing has happened for a very long time. It is complete. It does not need you and will not respond to you, and there is no mystery in it that will resolve if you wait. The walls are covered in palm-marks made by hands you will never meet, and you will add your own, and the next visitor will not meet you either. Some people find this unbearable within minutes—the sense of being the only event in a room built to have no events. Others find it the most restful place they have ever stood, and that is its own problem, because the rest is a kind of undertow. "You could stand in the center with your arms out for as long as you liked, and the only thing that would change would be you." That sentence is the whole risk. The room will let you stay until you are deaf and desiccated and lost in time, and it will never once suggest you go.

So decide before you enter what you are going in *for*. To see it. To stand in it. To touch one wall once. To listen until the note becomes the shape of the room. Whatever it is, know it, and know that when it is done, you leave—not because the room tells you, but because you told yourself.

Then turn sideways, let your arms go slack, and go in.

---

**Afterward**

Come out into somewhere dim and quiet and let your eyes adjust to shadow again; it will feel strange to see one. Drink. Expect the note in your ears for a while and do not be alarmed by it. If it has not faded in a few days, have your hearing checked. Wash the dust off before it gets into anything else.

And expect to think about the room at odd moments for a long time. Not with fear. Just the way you might think of a very old step, dished by feet you never saw, still there, still holding its shape, still ringing, in a light that never goes down.
