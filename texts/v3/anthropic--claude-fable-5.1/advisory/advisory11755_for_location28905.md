**Preparing for the Unfinished Range**

*Notes for an ordinary traveler*

---

**First, understand what you are visiting**

You are not going to see a wonder. You are going to a workplace — a very large, very slow, very old workplace — where the product happens to be geology and the workers happen to be patient. If you arrive expecting spectacle, the plateau will disappoint you within an hour: it is dim, it is damp, it is mostly empty ground, and nothing about it will finish while you watch. The people who live there know this and have built their lives around it. The best thing you can do before crossing the seam is to arrive already knowing it too.

The second thing to understand is that this place is, in a quiet and non-hostile way, *indecisive*, and that its indecision leaks into whatever stands on it. You are a traveler between worlds. You are, by definition, someone not entirely committed to any one ground. Take that seriously. Most of the advice below comes down to a single principle the stone-setters already live by: stay on finished rock as much as you can, and when you can't, keep one hand on something that has already decided what it is.

---

**Your body**

The plateau sits at something like six thousand metres. The strangeness of the place tends to make people forget this, and it is the thing most likely to actually hurt you.

Do not go up in one push. Whatever world you are coming from, spend days — a week is better — at intermediate altitudes before you begin the final climb. The approach from below is described as "ordinary," and it is, but ordinary at that height means headaches, nausea, sleeplessness, and the very real possibility of fluid in your lungs or brain if you push too fast. Learn the signs of altitude sickness before you go. Learn that the only treatment is descent, and that on the plateau descent means a long walk back to the seam and then a full day down. There is no clinic up there that can help you with this. There is coal smoke, thin air, and people who have adapted over years.

Hydrate more than feels reasonable. The damp air will trick you into thinking you don't need to; the altitude means you do. Water on the plateau arrives as condensation — it drips from the foundry gutters, beads on the iron, collects in the low places as fog — and it tastes of metal. Bring a filter and something to boil in. Warmth is not in short supply near the kilns; clean water is. Bring electrolyte salts. Bring more than you think.

The rock is warm. The air is not. This is the trap of the first hour past the seam: you put your hand on the ground and feel bread-warmth and start shedding layers, and the air at six thousand metres, under a small white sun that gives off less light than it should, takes the heat out of you anyway. Dress for a cold place with a warm floor. You will be sitting on that floor a great deal — it is what everyone does in the evenings — and the layer between you and it should be something that won't hold the damp.

Speaking of damp: it is never dry here and never quite wet, and that is worse for clothing than either extreme. Cotton is useless. Wool is better and will smell. Synthetics dry fastest and are probably your best bet for anything against the skin. Bring twice the socks you think you need and accept that none of them will ever be fully dry again. Bring something waxed or oiled for your outer layer, because rain gear designed for rain does nothing against a fog that simply settles into you.

Your ears will suffer. The kilns produce a roar low enough to feel in the sternum, and the steam hammers in the finishing sheds carry for miles in the thin air. Bring earplugs — the kind you can wear for weeks — but learn, in your first days, which sounds you must not block out. The rock cracks as it cools, a sound like green wood splitting that goes on for a long time, and you want to hear that, because it tells you where the ground is changing. At night the whole plateau ticks like a hull. Most visitors find this unbearable for three nights and then cannot sleep without it.

---

**Your mind**

The light will get to you before anything else does. It is dim at noon, dimmer than cloud alone explains, and the sky is the colour of a bruise. The electric light in the dormitories is yellow and weak and flickers when the kilns draw. You will spend your whole visit slightly unable to see well, and this produces, in most people, a low steady melancholy that has nothing to do with anything wrong. Know that going in. Bring a good lamp with spare fuel or cells — and know that the condensation will be trying to kill it from the moment you unpack it.

Bring things to read. People read up there anyway, in light too dim to read by, and it is the most reliable form of company. Large print if you can get it. Paper that tolerates damp. Bring more than you need and leave them behind when you go; they will be valued.

Prepare to be *slow*. Nothing here can be hurried and nobody will try. A kiln fires for six weeks and sets a ring of stone a hand's-width deeper. If you arrive with an itinerary, the plateau will not accommodate it — the rail lines are literally longer some mornings than others, and the drivers simply take longer. Your visit will be organised around firings whether you like it or not. When a kiln is lit, the cluster around it goes quiet for days and the only sound is the kiln. When it is banked, the hammers start. You will either be in the silence or in the hammering, and if you want to experience both, you are committing to a stay of weeks.

Decide in advance how long you are staying and tell people. This matters more here than in most places, for reasons that will become clear.

---

**What to carry**

Everything you bring should be chosen with two questions in mind: *will the damp destroy it?* and *does it need to know what it is?*

Electronics are a poor bet. The people who live here have full-time jobs keeping condensation out of their wiring and they are losing. If you carry anything electronic, carry it in sealed bags with desiccant, expect it to fail, and do not depend on it. This includes whatever you use to find your way home between worlds; if that device is electronic, protect it above everything else and test it daily on set rock.

Mechanical things fare better. A wound watch. A compass, though see below about what a compass is worth. A hand-cranked lamp. Pencils rather than pens — ink bleeds in this air and a pencil will write on damp paper. A notebook of good heavy stock. You will want to write things down, partly because memory does odd things in a place where light arrives late.

Bring a good broom. This sounds absurd. It is not. The moths cluster on warm rock at night in patches like fur, in numbers that make the stone-setters sweep the working faces every morning, and if you sleep anywhere near warm ground you will wake furred. A hand brush is the minimum. A proper broom will make you friends.

Bring dry goods to share — tea, tobacco if you use it, anything that keeps. Everything up here comes up the incline railway from the valleys alongside the coal, and gifts from below are received the way gifts from anywhere far away are received. Bring a small quantity of something that is unambiguously *from* your own world; you will be asked where you are from, and it is easier to show than to explain.

Do not bring anything you would be devastated to have change. This is not a joke. Out past the last kilns, surveyors' handwriting alters mid-page. Nothing violent, nothing hostile — the ground hasn't decided, and things on it are asked to share its indecision. A precious object carried onto raw ground may come back subtly other. Leave your irreplaceable things at the seam, or in a dormitory on set rock, or better, at home.

---

**The climb and the seam**

The approach is ordinary mountaineering: switchbacks, scree, krummholz, then lichen and cold. Treat it as such. Good boots, poles, layers, enough water, an early start. It takes most of a day.

You will know the seam when you reach it. It is not a fault. It is more like the join between two poured slabs — a line in the ground where the rock on one side is grey and cold and the rock on the other is grey-brown, fine-grained, and warm. Stop there. Sit on the cold side for a few minutes and let the fact of it settle. This is the edge of the works and the last unambiguous ground you will stand on for a while.

When you cross, cross deliberately. Put a hand on the warm rock before you put a foot on it. Feel the give — faint, like a loaf an hour from the oven, as though it were still deciding how hard to be. It will hold you. But it will also, in the softer places, *remember* you a little. Pressure-marks in the working faces are part of how this stone looks. Don't stamp. Don't kick at it. Don't press your thumbnail into the quartz veins to watch the dent close over, though everyone does exactly that once, and the setters will only sigh.

From the seam, follow the infrastructure. This is the single most important navigational rule on the plateau and it holds everywhere: the rails, the cable-ways, the knee-high iron pipe with its lagging coming off — these all lead to people. The buildings are scattered a kilometre or two apart across a space you could walk for days, and in the afternoon fog you will not see one from the next. But the pipe will still be there under your hand. Walk beside it.

---

**Living on the plateau**

Sleep in the dormitories. They are built on raw ground, yes, with deliberate slack in the joints and foundations rechecked monthly, and the marmots have colonised every one of them, and the light is bad. Sleep in them anyway. They are built by people who understand the ground, they are dry by local standards, and they are indoors, which keeps the moths off. If you must sleep outside, sleep on a finished ring — the glossy, almost glazed stone around the old kiln sites — and sleep with your head toward the kiln. The setters do this. The rings are warm, and they are the most decided ground on the plateau.

Stay away from the firing chambers. The kilns are cast-iron, the size of churches, and set into the ground with their fires below the surface, and the zone immediately around a firing kiln is not for visitors. You will not be told this in so many words because it will not occur to anyone that it needs saying.

Learn the rhythm of the firings within your first day and live inside it. When the cluster you are in goes quiet, be quiet. This is not superstition; it is that people are listening to the kiln, and to the rock around it, and a stranger's chatter is genuinely in the way. When the hammers start, the ordinary business of the town resumes — coal hauling, roof patching, the eternal war on condensation in the electrics — and that is when you can be useful, and being useful is the whole of how a visitor is judged here.

Offer to help with something that never ends. Do not offer to help with the stone; you do not know what you are doing and you could set a face wrong for a decade. But roofs need patching, wiring needs drying, lagging needs replacing, moths need sweeping. Ask. Take the worst job. Do it for a week without complaining and you will be welcome for the rest of your stay.

**Water and food.** Drink from the gutter-drip after boiling and filtering. The metallic taste does not wash out. Eat what you are offered and share what you brought. There is no market. There is a company store somewhere near the incline railhead and it stocks what came up on the last coal train.

**Light.** Trust your hands over your eyes. On the plateau proper the lag is small — a hammer falls and its flash arrives a half-second late, a lantern's shadow trails a little behind it — but it is enough to spoil your depth judgement, and the fog finishes the job. Keep a hand on the pipe. Step where the setters step. And when in doubt, remember the rule: *do not trust any shadow you can't put your hand into.* If a shadow falls across your path and you cannot reach down and touch the thing casting it, wait for the world to catch up before you proceed.

**Time.** The plateau's days are, mostly, honest — the kilns fire, the sun rises small and sets somewhere in the murk, the cloud comes up in the afternoon. Keep a wound watch anyway and check it against the firings. Nobody on the plateau relies on clocks much; they rely on *this kiln is in its fourth week*. Adopt the same habit.

**Money.** Ask what is used. Bring something universally valuable and small — it will not be needed much. This is a company town and most exchange is not monetary.

---

**The animals**

Feed the marmots. It is bad practice. Everyone does it. Refusing to do it marks you as someone who has not understood the place.

Beyond that: the ibex-things will not trouble you if you do not trouble them. Their hooves splay flat on the soft stone and they stand in herds of forty and fifty on the open ground, cropping at what appears to be nothing, and they are used to people. The choughs and the great door-winged scavengers ride the kiln thermals in gyres you can see from anywhere; they nest in cold chimneys, so if you see a chimney with no smoke, do not put your hand inside. The voles live in the pipe lagging; if you sit on the pipe, expect company. The moths have been covered.

Toward the raw ground, the fauna becomes less committed, and here the advice is simple: **do not count.** There are herd animals out there with too many legs until you look at them directly, at which point they have the right number and always did. Looking directly does no harm. Counting, and then recounting, and then going closer to be sure — this is how people wander onto ground they meant to stay off. The bird that comes over at dusk, grey and long-winged, is a raven over set rock and something else over raw, and nobody has seen it twice in the same form. Let it be what it is when it passes over you. You are not going to identify it.

The oldest kiln-master keeps a notebook of such sightings and believes the ground is not changing the animals so much as *offering* them — that undecided rock has not decided what lives on it, and so generously provides everything. Whether she is right or not, the practical upshot is the same: the strange animals belong to the strange ground. Do not follow them onto it. Do not feed them.

---

**The raw ground**

Past the last kilns, where nobody has begun to fire the rock at all, the plateau stops being a workplace and becomes something else. You will want to go there. Everyone does. Here is how to think about it.

The rock out there has not yet decided how many surfaces it has. You can walk into a hollow and, without turning, walk out of a different one half a mile away. A ridge that looks like a day's walk is a day's walk, but the day is longer than a plateau day, and you come back to find the kilns banked for a full night you did not live through. Surveyors' handwriting changes on the page. Nothing out there wants to hurt you. It simply has not made up its mind, and while you are on it, neither entirely have you.

For a traveler who is already, by nature, a person between worlds, I would treat this as a genuine risk in a way it is not for the stone-setters. They are anchored. They have thirty years of one ground under them. You have a habit of crossing. I do not know what raw ground does to someone with that habit, and I do not think anyone does, and I would not want you to find out alone.

So: **if you go, go with a surveyor.** They go out regularly and they know the habits of the ground as well as anyone can. Go in daylight, such as it is. Go on foot, roped if they rope. Tell people at the cluster when you expect to return, and understand that *their* clock is the one that counts — if you come back after a night you did not experience, you were gone a night, and you should behave accordingly.

Carry nothing you love. Carry a notebook you are willing to have altered. Write your name on the first page before you cross the last finished ring, and look at it when you come back. This is not superstition; it is a check.

Do not try to *see* out there. The light lags badly on raw ground. You can watch evening come down over a distant ridge an hour before it reaches you, or watch the sun set on a peak that is by every measure further west than you are. Your eyes will tell you things that are not so. Your hands will tell you what is under them. Trust the hands.

And do not stay. The raw ground is not a place to camp, not a place to wait, not a place to sit and think. Walk out, see it, walk back. The setters go out for a day and return, and the oldest of them do not go out at all any more. Ask yourself why.

---

**The people**

A few thousand of them, patient, damp, practical, organised around the firings. They are a company town and they will treat you as company towns treat strangers: politely, with a little suspicion, and with growing warmth in proportion to your usefulness.
