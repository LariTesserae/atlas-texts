**Preparing for the Tableland**

The first thing to understand is that this place will not try to kill you. That is the danger. Almost everywhere a traveler goes, the environment announces its hostility — cold bites, sun burns, rock cuts, predators watch — and the announcing keeps you careful. Here, nothing announces. The ground is soft and warm. The air is mild. The animals ignore you. You could lie down anywhere and be comfortable, and you will want to, and the place will let you. Everything that goes wrong on the tableland goes wrong slowly, cumulatively, in the manner of the place itself, and by the time you notice you will have been in trouble for a while. Prepare accordingly. Prepare for a place that erodes rather than strikes.

---

**The air**

You are very high. The warmth will lie to you about this, because every instinct you have connects altitude to cold, and when the air is damp and sweet and your hands don't stiffen your body will assume it is somewhere reasonable. It is not. There is less oxygen here than you are built for, and your body will pay for that whether or not you feel cold.

Expect the usual: headache, poor sleep, a hunger that comes and goes strangely, a shortness of breath that never quite resolves, a tendency to make small stupid errors after the first few days that you will not recognize as errors until later. Because there is nowhere to descend to — the whole tableland is high, and there is no summit and no valley, no down worth the name — you cannot manage altitude the way you would on a mountain. You will not be able to climb high and sleep low. You will simply be high, for as long as you are here, and either your body will adjust or it will not.

If you have any option to acclimatize before arrival — time spent at moderate altitude in your own world, or a staged approach if the crossing allows it — take it. If you have access to medication for altitude sickness, bring it and know how to use it. Bring a way to measure your own oxygen if you can; you will not be able to trust your judgment about how you are doing, and a number is harder to argue with. Learn the signs of the serious conditions — the fluid in the lungs, the swelling in the brain — and understand that here, the only treatment for them is leaving. Know how you will leave. Know it before you go.

Be aware also that the air near the ground may not be the same as the air at your face. The mat is digesting itself; something warm and slow and very large is respiring beneath you, and the gases of that respiration have to go somewhere. In the channels, in the low places between domes, in any hollow you dig, the air may be heavier, sourer, lower in the oxygen you are already short of. Do not sleep in channels. Do not put your face down into a hole you have dug. If you feel suddenly worse in a low place, get up onto a dome.

Everything you do here will take more effort than it should, and the ground will add to that — more on the ground shortly. Move slowly. The animals are slow for a reason. Match them.

---

**The wet**

This is the hazard you will actually contend with every hour of every day, and it is the one most likely to end your journey early.

You will never be dry. Not for a moment. The air is saturated; there is a skin of water on every surface; a fine warm rain comes and goes without warning and without ever quite stopping. Your clothes will be wet when you put them on and wet when you take them off and wet in the bag you stored them in. Anything that can absorb water will absorb it and hold it. Anything that can rot will begin to.

The body does not like this. Skin that stays wet softens, whitens, wrinkles, then cracks. Between the toes, in the folds of the groin, under the arms, anywhere skin touches skin — these places will break down first. Fungal infection is not a possibility here; it is a schedule. Small cuts will not close. Blisters will not dry. A callus you brought with you will peel away in a week and leave something tender underneath.

Your feet are the whole problem in miniature. Everything you know about footwear is wrong for this place. Boots will fill with water and never empty, and the leather or fabric will start to come apart, and your feet inside them will be in the worst possible environment: warm, wet, dark, sealed. The traveler who arrives in good mountain boots will be in agony within days.

Think instead about what the animals do, which is nothing. Consider going barefoot or nearly so — open sandals, or a thin sock of something that dries fast and drains, or the kind of soft water-shoe worn for wading. The mat is soft. There is nothing to cut you. What you need from footwear here is not protection but drainage, and the freedom to take it off constantly. Bring several pairs of whatever you settle on. Expect to lose them.

Then: skin care as a discipline, not an afterthought. Bring more antifungal powder and cream than seems sane. Bring a mild antiseptic. Bring a soft cloth and use it, several times a day, to dry the folds of your body as well as you can, knowing they will be wet again in minutes. Change socks — if you wear them — at every rest. Sleep with your feet out of anything. Air is the only thing here that helps, and it barely helps.

Clothing: fast-drying synthetics that hold no water, minimal, layered only for the dimmer hours when it is slightly cooler. Nothing cotton. Nothing wool — it will hold water and rot. Bring a good waterproof shell not for the rain, which is warm and harmless, but so you have something to put on when you need to feel dry against your skin for an hour and psychologically reset. Keep it in a sealed bag. Take it out only for that.

Everything you need to stay dry — spare clothes, food, paper, electronics, medicine — goes in genuinely waterproof containers, and those go in a second layer of waterproofing, and you assume both will eventually fail. Silica or another desiccant in every sealed bag. Check your gear every evening. Discard anything that has started to grow.

And your gear will start to grow. This place is a single organism that colonizes everything; it has no interest in you but no reason to spare your possessions. Straps, seams, the inside of your pack — check for the pale fuzz. Wipe it off. It will come back.

**The ground**

The ground gives with every step, a hand's depth or more, and closes slowly behind you. This is not like walking on sand, which also gives but is at least dead. It is like walking on the back of something asleep.

The exertion is real. Every step costs more than a step should; you are lifting your foot out of a soft grip, and you are doing it in thin air. Distances that would be a morning's walk elsewhere are a day here. Plan for a third of the distance you would normally cover, and be ready to halve that again on bad ground.

The ground is not uniform. It goes from felt to rope to domes, and each is its own walking. The felt is the easiest and the most deceptive — it looks like a lawn and swallows the foot silently. The ropy braid catches and trips; you will roll an ankle on it if you do not watch your feet, and you will not be able to watch your feet in the dim. The domes are the hardest to cross and the best to rest on, high and slightly firmer, and between them are the channels.

The channels are the thing to fear. The water in them is so still it has no surface. You will not see it. You will step from a dome onto what looks like a low stretch of mat and go in to the knee, or the thigh, warm and green and full of a fine suspension that will stain your skin, and if you are carrying weight and go in awkwardly you can hurt yourself, and if you go in face-first you can drown in something you did not know was there. Probe ahead with a pole. Carry a pole. A long, light staff is perhaps the single most useful thing you can bring — for probing, for balance, for pulling yourself out of a channel, for testing the depth of the mat, for hanging wet clothes on, for propping up a shelter where there is nothing else to prop it on.

Be careful how you sit and where you dig. The upper layers of the mat are full of burrowers, soft and slow and blind, and they will not hurt you, but sinking your arm into something and finding it moving is a shock that makes people jerk and fall. If you dig for any reason — and try not to, the wound stays open a season — dig slowly.

The grazers are the size of an ox and do not appear to see. They move at the pace of a shadow, so you will not be run over, but a herd at rest looks exactly like a cluster of domes, and if you climb what you think is a dome and it is an animal, it may rise under you or roll. Learn the difference. It is in the warmth — the animals are warmer than the ground, though only just — and in the breathing, which is slightly faster than the breathing of the domes, and in the smell, which is animal under the green.

**The light, and finding your way**

There are no shadows. Understand what this means for your eyes: there is no depth. A dome ten strides away and a dome twenty strides away are the same shape at slightly different brightnesses. The ground does not tell you where it rises or falls; a hollow and a hummock look alike. You will misjudge every step for the first several days, and after that you will misjudge only most of them. Walk slowly, use the pole, and accept that you will fall.

There is no sun. There is no landmark — no peak, no tree, no river, no coast. The horizon is too far and too low to be useful and is usually swallowed by cloud anyway. Visibility is a few dozen strides in the good hours and a few strides in the dim. Sound comes from everywhere and nowhere. If you set out from a point and walk in what you believe is a straight line, you will not return to that point, and you will not know which way it is.

This is the most dangerous single feature of the tableland, and the animals are not troubled by it only because they have nowhere they need to be.

A compass should work; the rock is far below but it is still there, and there is nothing in the mat to interfere. Bring two. Learn to travel by bearing and count your steps, which is dull and tiring and the only thing that will get you back. Keep a written log of every bearing and every count, in a waterproof notebook with a pencil, and update it at every rest. If your instruments include anything that can record a track without satellites, bring it, and bring the means to keep it charged, and bring the compass anyway.

Do not rely on marking your trail. The mat closes over footprints in hours. Anything you push into it will be grown around and then taken. Flags on poles will stand for a while — bring some, bright ones, and use them to mark camp and the way back to camp — but they are for a day's range, not a journey.

The people who were here before you solved this problem, and their solution is still lying on the ground. The stones are the only thing the tableland cannot digest, and the lines of stones go somewhere, and if you are lost and find one, you have found a road. Follow the line. It may take you somewhere you did not intend, but it will take you somewhere with a direction, and that is more than the mat will give you.

Do not move the stones. Do not take one. It is tempting; they are the only cold thing here, the only heavy thing, the only thing with an edge, and after weeks of softness the hand wants something to grip. Leave them. They are a road and a message and a grave marker, possibly all three, and they are the only guide any traveler after you will have.

If you want to make your own marks, bring your own stone. Small, heavy, cold — a pocket of pebbles from your own world. Set them down where you camp, where you turn, where you need to find your way back. Nothing else will stay. Whoever came before understood that, and carried weight they did not need to carry, so that something would.

Bring a light. Bring a very good one, and more power for it than you think you need, and a backup. The dim hours are genuinely dark, and you will need to see your feet, and you will need to see the green in the channels, and you will need, sometimes, just to see something with an edge.

**Water, food, fire**

There is water everywhere and you should treat all of it. It has been sitting in the tissue of a living thing, warm, still, full of a fine green suspension and whatever microbial life belongs to a compost heap the size of a continent. Filter it. Boil it or treat it. Do not drink from the channels untreated even when you are very thirsty, which you will be — the air is thin and you will be sweating in the damp without noticing.

The water is likely very low in minerals; it has never touched stone. You will need salt, and not just salt — the full range of electrolytes your body loses in sweat and cannot replace from anything here. Bring more than you think. Bring it in a form that will survive being wet. A deficiency in salt looks, in its early stages, very like altitude sickness and very like simple fatigue, and you will not distinguish them.

Assume nothing here is food. The mat may be edible; the grazers eat it, and they are built something like you, with mouths and guts. But you do not know what it does to a body that is not theirs, and you do not know what lives in the yellow layer, and there is no one to ask. Bring everything you will eat. This is the great logistical problem of the tableland: weeks of food on a soft surface in thin air. Choose the densest, lightest food you can and carry too much of it. Appetite fails at altitude; bring things you will actually want to eat when you do not want to eat.

You cannot make fire. There is no wood, no dry grass, no stone to strike, nothing that will burn. Everything is wet living plant or wet dead plant. Bring a stove and fuel, enough for every hot meal and every treated liter you will need, and protect the fuel from the wet. If your stove fails you will eat cold and drink what your filter and chemicals can manage, so bring both.

The warmth from below will keep you from ever being cold. It will not cook anything.

**Sleeping**

The ground is warm and soft and it is, in truth, very good to sleep on. You will not need insulation beneath you; the mat provides it, at the temperature of a body. You will not need a warm bag; a light sheet or bivy is enough in the dimmer hours. In this one respect the place is kind.

But the ground is alive and wet and inhabited. You will wake with things on you — the fringed slugs that climb the domes for condensation will climb you for the same reason, and they glow faintly, and it is unpleasant to wake in the dark under a dozen small wet lamps. The moth-things will hover over your face. The burrowers pass beneath you and you will feel them, a slow ridge moving under your back. None of this is harmful. All of it is hard to sleep through, at first.

Sleep on a dome, never in a channel; the air is better and the water does not gather. Put down a groundsheet that breathes, or you will wake in a pool of your own condensation. Bring a light net or a bivy with mesh, not against biting things — there seem to be none — but simply to keep the slugs off your face. Elevate your feet and leave them bare to the air.

Anchoring anything is difficult. Stakes hold poorly in the mat and pull out; long ones hold better. The pole helps. Some travelers do without a shelter entirely, since the rain is warm and the cold is absent, and simply lie down under a sheet. This is not unreasonable. It is the wet, again, that decides it: a shelter is not for warmth here but for one dry-ish space to open your sealed bags in.

**Time, and the mind**

There is a gray and a darker gray, and that is all the day and night you will get. No sunrise, no shadows lengthening, no stars. Your body will lose the thread of time within days. Bring a clock that does not depend on anything here, and follow it — eat when it says to eat, sleep when it says to sleep, rise when it says to rise — even when you do not feel like it, especially then. The alternative is a slow drift into sleeping at all hours and eating at none and not noticing.

The quiet is not silence and that makes it worse. There is a constant seep and drip and settle, and the occasional cropping sound of a grazer somewhere in the pearl, and once or twice a day the single low note of the thing in the cloud, arriving from inside your own head. Voices do not carry properly. If you travel with anyone, stay within sight, which is a few dozen strides, and agree on signals that do not rely on hearing where a sound came from. Losing a companion here is not a matter of shouting for them.

Most travelers who come back from the tableland describe not fear but a flattening. There is nothing to react to. The stimuli are so slow and so uniform — the same pearl, the same warmth, the same soft give under the feet, the same green light — that after a week the mind begins to quiet in a way that is pleasant and then in a way that is not. Motivation goes. Care goes. The discipline of the clock and the log and the skin check and the sealed bags, which is what keeps you alive here, is exactly the kind of small effortful task that stops seeming to matter. Know this before you go. Set your routines before you arrive, write them down, and do them because the list says to, not because you feel their necessity, because you will not feel it.

Bring something with hard edges for the mind. A book that has nothing to do with this place. A task. If you draw, draw. If you write, write. The people who made the slab cut regular lines into it in a place where nothing else is regular, and you will understand why once you have been here long enough.

---

**The marks**

You may find the stones, the slab, the walls. You may find other things — there is no reason to think those are the only marks left, only that they are the ones that survived the mat.

Treat them the way you would treat any grave you did not dig. Do not move the stones. Do not cut into the slab or scrape the dark green from its lines to read them; the water has been in those lines for longer than your language has existed, and whatever is under it is not yours. Walk between the walls if you like; the burrowers do. Sit with your back against a stone in the evening; the person who set it there did the same, or someone did.

If you can record, record. Sketch the lines on the slab. Note the bearing of the stone-roads and how far they run. This is the only knowledge that will leave this place, and there is very little of it.

---

**Leaving**

Know how you will leave before you arrive, and know it in a way that does not depend on finding your way back to a specific point on the mat, because you will not find it. If your crossing requires a fixed location, mark that location with stone, your own stone, cold and heavy, in a pattern you will recognize, and take a bearing from it every time you walk away and log every step. If your crossing can happen from anywhere, then leave when your body tells you to and not when your curiosity does. The altitude is patient. The wet is patient. The place is very, very patient, and it will let you stay long past the point where staying is wise, because it has no way of telling you otherwise and no interest in doing so.

Check your skin before you go. Whatever has started growing on you — and something will have — is easier to treat where there are dry places.

And leave the stones.
