**A Preparation Guide for the Overfilled Summit**

---

**First, what you are agreeing to.**

You are not going to a place. You are going inside a process. The distinction matters because you will prepare differently for each, and if you prepare for a place — a swim, a cave dive, a high-altitude scramble — you will bring the wrong things and, worse, the wrong posture. The basin on the mountain is not a location that has weather. It is weather, all the way down, with a floor.

Think of it as standing inside the working of a mind that has one idea and cannot stop having it. The idea is a building. The mind is made of warm water and it thinks in stone, loudly, and it has not learned to be careful about where you are standing. Nothing there wants to hurt you. Nothing there is capable of noticing you well enough to want anything about you at all. This is the correct frame: you are a visitor to an act of remembering, and the remembering is bigger than you and does not stop.

If you can hold that frame, the rest of the preparation is detail. If you cannot, no equipment will help.

---

**On breathing.**

The account says your lungs do something that is "not quite breathing and not quite not." Take this seriously and take it in both directions.

It means the place permits you. You will not drown in the ordinary way. Whatever makes the water stand up over the crater like a lens also makes it tolerable to a body, and this seems to be a property of the water itself rather than anything you bring. So you do not need to solve the problem of breathing.

But you also do not know what the permission costs, or whether it can be withdrawn, or whether it is uniform across the basin. So arrive with whatever supplementary air your own worlds have given you the means to carry, and treat it as a cushion rather than a plan. Test the ambient breathing immediately on arrival, within reach of one of the old stones, before you move anywhere. If your body does the not-quite thing, good; note how it feels so that you will recognize any change. If your body does not do it, leave. Do not explore a place that has declined to let you breathe.

Be aware that any mask or full-face apparatus will interfere with the single most important survival sense you have there, which is the warmth of the water against your face. More on that below. If you must cover your face, cover it partially. Cheeks and forehead need to stay bare to the water.

---

**On the cold and the heat.**

There are three temperatures and you need to keep them distinct in your body.

The bedrock — the real mountain, the old worn stone — is altitude-cold, and it will draw heat from any part of you that rests on it. The water is warm, roughly body-warm, and will keep you comfortable in a way that is deceptive because comfort is not what the warmth is for. And the fresh stone, the stuff the water has just made, is hot. It was liquid a minute ago. It ticks as it cools. It will burn bare skin on contact, and it is also sharp enough to open you to the bone along any edge, so you have two reasons not to touch it and both are sufficient.

Dress accordingly: something that covers the skin fully, resists cutting and abrasion, and tolerates brief contact with hot surfaces. Heavy gloves for the hands — you will want to brace against things, and everything you brace against will be either freezing bedrock or fresh glass. Boots with hard soles and closed toes; the floor is young and every edge is a cut edge, and the grit that falls continuously out of the water hides the smaller ridges until you step on them. Kneepads, if you have them. You will kneel more than you expect, because the low places near the originals are where you will spend your time.

Now the third rule: warmth is attention. Where the water is warmest, it is about to build. This is not a metaphor and not a tendency; it is the single reliable warning you will get. Your face is your thermometer. Practice, before you go, noticing small changes in water temperature against your cheeks — in a bath, in the sea, anywhere. You want the reflex of *warmth on the face means step aside* to be faster than thought, because thought is slow and the stone comes in the space of a breath.

Understand what happens if you fail. You do not get pushed. You get included. You would be part of the wall for thirty heartbeats, and then the wall would disperse, and whether you would disperse with it or be left standing, or something in between, is not recorded, and you should not be the one who finds out.

---

**On the sound.**

This is the part that people underestimate, because sound seems like a nuisance rather than a hazard.

It is not a nuisance. It is the environment. The water is saturated with it — grinding, tearing, a bell-depth boom, the cracking of stone made and unmade faster than stone should permit — and it never gaps, because it is not the sound of what is happening but of what is about to happen, a half-second early, always. You will feel it in your teeth and sternum and the bones behind your eyes. There is no getting used to it. There is only enduring it.

You will be tempted to bring ear protection. Do not. The roar is also your second warning system: a ridge slams into being exactly where, a moment earlier, you heard it slam into being. Heat tells you the water is considering a spot; sound tells you it has decided. Between them you have roughly a heartbeat's notice. Plug your ears and you halve your warning. Worse, you cut yourself off from the one change of key that matters — the moment the roar drops to a single sustained note, which means the water is building something large and slow and you have time to get well clear and watch.

So you will be inside the organ casing with nothing between you and the pipes. Prepare for what that does to a person. Constant pressure on the ribs makes breathing shallow; you will have to consciously breathe deeper, or do whatever the not-quite-breathing equivalent is. Constant noise with no gap erodes something in the mind faster than you would think — the account says that after a while you cannot reconstruct what silence was, and that is a real loss, not a figure of speech. Expect irritability, then a kind of flattening, then a strange calm that is not calm but exhaustion wearing calm's face. Do not trust the calm. It is the point at which you stop stepping aside fast enough.

Decide your time limit before you arrive, and make it shorter than you want. An hour is long there. Half that is reasonable for a first visit. Bring some way to count that does not depend on your judgment — a mechanical timer, a set of beads, your own pulse if you trust yourself to keep counting under the roar. When the limit arrives, leave. The shrine will not finish while you watch. It never finishes.

---

**On seeing.**

Visibility is ten body-lengths, in dim green-brown light, through water thick with falling grit. Beyond that the world is suspended sediment and the memory of shapes. This is not a place where you will use your eyes for much beyond your immediate footing and the nearest warning cloud.

Any light you bring will be swallowed within a few paces and will mostly illuminate the grit. It may still be worth having for close work — reading the surface of an original, finding the little bowl — but do not expect it to open the space up. The murk is structural. It is what the water looks like when it is thinking.

Protect your eyes from the grit if you can do so without covering your cheeks. Goggles, not a mask. The grit is fine and sharp and it settles on everything, including you.

Learn to see darkening. A cloud of the murk thickens ahead of you, gathers, takes on a heaviness, and then it sets. The thickening is visible before the heat reaches you and before the roar sharpens, if you are looking at the right spot. You will not always be looking at the right spot. But the habit of scanning for local darkening, the way one scans a sky for the anvil of a storm, gives you a third, unreliable warning to add to the two reliable ones.

---

**On direction.**

Up does not work. The account is explicit: rise toward the lens's surface and you find yourself approaching the crater floor from a new angle, as though the basin were the inside of a sphere and every direction eventually became down. Do not spend your limited time and attention testing this. Accept it. You cannot leave by swimming out, and you cannot orient by the surface, because there is functionally no surface you can reach.

The mountain is the only fixed reference. The crater floor, the broken lip of dark rock, the bedrock underneath the falling grit — these stay where they are, mostly. Even they are rewritten at the edges, growing spurs and losing them, so do not trust the lip as a boundary. Trust the originals.

The originals are the old stones: the dressed block with the chiseled groove, the step with the foot-hollowed edge, the little bowl cut into bedrock. The water does not build over them. It makes its copies at a respectful distance, and around each true relic there is a small clearing in the storm. These clearings are your rooms. Everything you do in the basin should be done from one clearing or on a deliberate, short, fast transit to the next. Find the first one immediately on arrival. Learn the others as you go. Never be more than a few paces from one when the roar drops key.

Think about a line. A physical tether tied to an original and paid out as you move gives you a way back that does not depend on sight or sense of direction, both of which the place degrades. But think about it carefully, because stone forming across your line will either sever it or fix it, and if it fixes it you are attached to a wall for thirty heartbeats and then to a dispersing cloud. Any line you bring must be one you can cut instantly, one-handed, with a blade kept where you can reach it without looking. If you cannot promise yourself that, do not bring the line; better to be lost near a relic than anchored to a phantom.

Your way out is your own way — whatever brought you across. It will almost certainly work from the same clearing you arrived in and may not work from anywhere else, given how the geometry folds. Do not wander so far that you cannot return to your arrival point in the time you have left. Mark it if you can. Do not mark it with anything the water might study.

---

**On what the water studies.**

This is the consideration that has no precedent in ordinary travel and that you must think about alone before you go.

The water has seen buildings. It has learned the grammar of steps and arches and doorframes from the bones of a shrine that stood on this summit before the sea came, and it is practicing, and it is getting better. The account says the arches are becoming truer as you watch, as though it is remembering rather than inventing.

It learns from what is present. Everything that is present.

You do not know whether it can learn a body. You do not know whether standing still in one place for long enough constitutes an offering. You do not know whether the gear you set down on a relic, or the line you leave paid out across the floor, becomes part of the vocabulary. But you know that it copies what it has studied, and you know it never builds over the originals but builds copies near them, and you should ask yourself what it means to be a new shape in a room that draws the same house over and over.

So: keep moving, gently, within your clearing. Do not set anything down and walk away from it. Take everything you brought. Do not leave a mark, a cairn, a token in the bowl. The bowl is full of warm water and there is nothing in it, and it should stay that way, both because it is old and someone once knelt there and because you do not want to teach a restless mind that bowls hold things.

And do not take anything. Not a fragment of the fresh stone — it will not survive the trip, it dissolves on its own schedule, and if it does not dissolve you have a hot glass shard that may or may not remember where it came from. Not a piece of an original — those are the only stable things in the basin, the only reason the water leaves clearings, and removing one may remove the clearing.

---

**On the living things.**

The black filaments on the oldest stones are the only softness there. A dozen tufts in the whole visible basin, streaming in the currents that the making and unmaking throws off. You will feel grateful to them out of all proportion, the account says, and it is right, and you should let yourself feel that. Do not touch them. They have found the one way to live in a place that will not hold still, and you do not know how fragile that way is.

There is one animal. You will see a paleness at the edge of vision — long, low, unhurried — and then the same paleness on the other side of you at the same moment, and ahead, and behind. One animal, several places. It is eyeless. It steers by the roar, reading the sound-shapes of stone that does not exist yet. It has learned to live inside prophecy.

Leave it alone entirely. Do not approach it, do not try to attract it, and above all do not follow it, because it is in several *heres* and you are only in one, and where it goes may not be somewhere a single-located body can go. Watch it, though, when it passes close. Watch how it moves when the water is about to build — how early it shifts, how little it hurries. It is the only thing in the basin that knows what it is doing. You can learn something from its timing that you cannot learn from any guide.

---

**On the shrine.**

At some point the roar will change key. It will drop, become a single sustained note that you feel in your knees, and at the center of the basin a cloud will gather and not slam into stone but hang, thickening, considering. Its warmth will reach you from twenty paces off.

Get to a clearing and stay there. This is the safest window you will have, because the water's attention is concentrated in one place and the small, sudden building elsewhere seems to slacken. It is also the reason you came, though you may not have known it.

The shapes come out in order. Foundation, walls, a rank of narrow openings that would be windows anywhere else. Pillars. A lintel too wide for anyone to have made, made in the manner of someone. It is building the whole shrine from the memory of the bones you are standing among, and it is almost right, almost exactly right, and you will want it to finish.

It does not finish. The note begins to descend, and you will hear — a half-second early — what is coming, and the walls will fog and the pillars soften and the whole thing will settle back into the dark with a sound like an enormous weight set down very gently. Then there is one breath of quiet. The only quiet there is.

Prepare for how that feels. It is not a spectacle. It is watching something try to say a name it has almost remembered, and fail, and begin again. People who have been there describe the grief as arriving late, after they have left, in a quiet room, when the silence they could not reconstruct in the basin comes back and they realize what the water does not have. You may want to have someone to talk to afterward, or somewhere to be that is small and still and has nothing in it that is being made.

And note the breath of quiet for practical reasons as well. It is the one gap. If your way out requires a moment without concussion — if the crossing is sensitive to pressure, or you need to hear yourself think for one second — that is your moment. It is short. You will need to be already in position, already decided, hand already on whatever you use. You cannot decide during the quiet. You can only act in it.

---

**A short list, for the pocket.**

Cover all skin; gloves, boots, knees. Face bare at the cheeks. Goggles, not a mask.

Supplementary air as a cushion, not a plan. Test breathing on arrival, within reach of an original.

No ear protection. Ever.

Warmth on the face: step aside. Roar sharpens where you are: step aside. Local darkening ahead: watch it.

Find a clearing first. Live in clearings. Transit between them fast and short.

Line only if you can cut it instantly without looking. Otherwise none.

Take nothing. Leave nothing. Put nothing in the bowl.

Do not touch the filaments. Do not follow the animal. Watch how the animal moves.

When the roar drops to one note, go to a clearing and stay. Watch. Be ready to leave in the breath of quiet after.

Time limit decided before arrival, counted by something other than your judgment. Leave when it arrives, whatever the water is building.

---

**Last.**

You will stand there with the roar pressing your ribs and the dark thick around you and, if you have prepared, you will be all right. You will step aside when you need to, you will stay near the old stones, you will watch the shrine almost happen. And you will leave, because you set a limit and you are the kind of traveler who keeps them.

But understand that the basin does not need you to be there and will not remember that you were. It is not a place that is visited. It is a place that is occurring, and will go on occurring, throwing up a doorframe and losing it, a flight of steps to nowhere, the arch a little truer each time. The bones of the shrine lie in the low places under the falling grit. The little bowl is full of warm water. There is nothing in it.

You will carry the silence you could not find there back to wherever you keep silence. That is the only thing you are permitted to take, and it is the thing the water, for all its fluency, cannot make.
