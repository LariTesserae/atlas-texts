**Before you go down**

Understand what the place is before you commit to the stair: not a cave, not a mine, not a ruin. A working district built inside a wound in the ground that is still hot from being made. Everything about your preparation follows from three facts. The air is hot and near saturation. The surfaces are new and therefore sharp. And it is somebody's home and workplace, and you are a guest in it. Most visitors prepare for the first fact and are hurt by the second and embarrassed by the third.

Do not go alone. Not because the ward is hostile — it isn't — but because the two ways it hurts people are quiet ones. Heat takes you sideways: you feel fine, then you feel a little stupid, then you are sitting down without deciding to. And a slip on wet brick against a wall of fresh glass-rock is a long cut, not a scrape. Arrange, if you can, to go down with one of the scrapers or a pump-hand starting a shift. They will not guide you, exactly, but they walk the gantries every day and you can follow their feet. Fail that, at least tell someone at the top of the stair that you are going, and when to expect you.

**The heat, which is the first thing**

Do not think of the heat as weather. Think of it as immersion. The description is exact: by the twentieth landing it is a medium, and you should treat entering it the way you would treat entering warm water — as something that will take your body's temperature away from you and make its own arrangements.

The specific danger is not the dry hot corners but the wet middle. Air that is hot *and* nearly saturated with steam does not accept your sweat. You will sweat, copiously, and it will do nothing; it will simply sit on you and mix with the drizzle. Your body's one real cooling mechanism is switched off the moment you step off the stair. This means you cannot rely on the usual signals. You will not feel yourself overheating in the way you would in a dry desert; you will feel heavy, and then irritable, and then vague. Give yourself a hard limit before you descend — an hour for a first visit is generous, forty minutes is wiser — and hold to it with a watch, not with how you feel. The boiler sighs every eleven minutes. Four sighs is forty-four minutes. Leave on the fourth if you have no watch.

Time your visit. The ward has a dry hour at dawn, when the city above draws its water and the seep slackens and the brick pales. The workers notice it because the paddles ring instead of thud. You should notice it because it is the only period when the air will take a little sweat off you. Arrive at the foot of the stair as the grates go grey. Do not go at night for a first visit; night is when it rains.

Drink before you go down, a great deal, and carry water — but understand that water alone is not enough here. You will lose salt with the sweat and you will lose it fast, and the ward is full of salt, and almost none of it is the kind you can eat. The white and pale-green fur on the copper pipes is copper salt, and it will make you very sick. The crust in the ceramic pans on the eastern wall is the saltworks' product and probably edible, but it is somebody's yield and you should not put your hand in it. Bring your own — salted food, or salt tablets, or whatever you use — and take it on the way down, before you are thirsty.

Then plan the ascent as the actual hazard. Everyone plans for going down; the stair is twenty-odd landings and you will climb them spent, in clothes soaked through, with the iron rail warm in your palm, and the heat does not let go of you until the eleventh landing going up either. Rest on the landings. Do not be proud. The cold above, when it comes, will hit you like a slap, and wet clothing in cool air will chill you within minutes; have a dry layer waiting at the top or in a sealed bag on your back.

**The stone, which is the second thing**

Nothing in the ward has been worn smooth by anything but hands and feet. The rock is glass-skinned and fractured into planes, and the fracture edges are as sharp as the day the dyke cooled. A fall against a wall here is a cut the length of your forearm. Fresh spall lies at the base of the walls like black sugar, and it is exactly as pleasant to kneel in as broken bottles.

Wear gloves. Thin ones, because of the heat, but tough — leather or a good synthetic — because you will reach out to steady yourself on something without thinking, and what you reach for will be either faceted rock, or a crystal-furred pipe, or a hot pipe. Do not grab pipes for balance at all until you can tell them apart, and you cannot tell them apart by looking in that light. The return pipes are cool and grow the fur; the steam and feed pipes are hot and bare. The furred ones will cut you and the bare ones will burn you. Steady yourself on the gantry rails, which are iron and wet and merely warm, or on brick.

Wear boots with thick soles and good tread. Sandals are what the locals wear, but they have years of feet that know every brick. You do not. The soles matter for two reasons: the brick is wet and mineral-slick everywhere, and the stone floor in the hotter vaults is warm enough to feel through a thin shoe. If you feel heat coming up through your sole, you are standing somewhere you should not stand still.

Long sleeves and long trousers, despite the heat, in the thinnest tough fabric you have. This is the one place where the heat argues against your safety and you should side with safety. A cut here is not the disaster it might be — nothing rots, and the ward's people will know how to dress it — but you would rather not find out.

Watch the walls. A slab the size of a door comes loose every few weeks, and behind it is a pale clean wound. Learn to see the pale wounds; do not linger beneath a fresh one, and do not stand under any stretch of wall where a pipe has been recently rerouted with a jointed elbow, because that reroute is somebody's note to themselves that this bit of wall is unfinished business. When the rock cracks — and it will, once or twice while you are there, a sharp report from inside the walls — do what everyone else does. Stop. Look toward it. Wait. Then go on. Do not be the one person moving when the ward is still.

**The wet**

It rains, hot, and it drizzles always. Cover your head. A brimmed hat of something that will not sag when soaked — waxed cloth, treated felt, a light helmet if you have one and it will not cook you. The drizzle off the pipes is mineral-heavy and warm and it wants your scalp and the back of your neck and your eyes. Bring eye protection — not tinted, the ward is dark enough — and accept that whatever you wear over your eyes will fog. Carry a cloth and wipe it often. Under the largest trusses, where the condensation is thickest, you may prefer to take the glasses off and squint.

Assume everything you carry will be wet through within the first quarter hour. Paper wilts and ink runs; if you want to take notes, use pencil on treated paper or wax tablets, or trust your memory. Anything electronic should be in a sealed bag and should stay in it; the heat and the saturated air will kill it, and if the air does not, the mineral rain will grow a crust on its contacts within the day. Cameras fog instantly on the lens and stay fogged. If you want a picture of the ward, you will need to warm the camera to the air's temperature first, which takes longer than you expect and may cook it anyway. It is not, honestly, a place that photographs. It is a place of shapes in brown light and it is better carried out in the head.

Metal you bring down will begin to streak and dull within hours. Do not wear jewellery you care about, and do not bring a good knife.

**The salt**

The crystal growth is the thing visitors most want to touch and most should not. The fringes on the pipe undersides are delicate as frost and cut like frost does not. They are also, much of them, copper compounds, and a cut from a copper-salt crystal will sting and swell and go a bad colour. Look. Do not touch. If you must handle a piece, take one that a scraper has already knocked into a trough, and ask first, and wash your hands after — in your own water, not the cistern's.

The translucent glaze at the far pinched end of the fissure, where the salt has grown over the black facets like ice over a broken window, is worth walking to. It holds the light. Stand there a while. Do not put your hand on it; beneath the glaze the rock is as sharp as everywhere else, and the glaze itself is not solid enough to protect you from it.

You will find, on your own skin, by the end of the visit, a fine grit of salt where your sweat dried in the drier corners. This is normal. It is also a good sign that you have been in the drier corners too long.

**The light**

Bring a lamp, but think about how you use it. The ward's people have eyes adjusted to fifteen paces of brown light and forty paces of shape. A bright white beam swung across a gantry will blind a scraper on a ladder, and a scraper blinded on a ladder above a floor of black spall is a serious thing. Keep your lamp low — pointed at your own feet — and dim if you can dim it, and red if you have red. Better still, arrive and stand for five minutes at the foot of the stair with the lamp off, and let your eyes open. You will see more than you expect. The fog glows where the lamps are lost in it. The glaze on the western wall gives back its not-light. The grates, if it is day, are four or five grey squares very high up, and once you have found them you will keep finding them, because they are the only thing in the ward that is the colour of the world you came from.

Do not go near the western wall, low down, where the red-brown glaze is. The people who work there have a pallor you will learn to recognise, and whatever gives them that pallor, you do not need to acquire in one visit. Look at it from the lowest gantry, from a few yards off, which is where the description's narrator stands and which is close enough. If your skin begins to feel tight and papery, you are too close; the air an inch from that rock is dry enough to crisp a moth.

**The engines**

Know where the boiler's relief valve vents before you go anywhere near the eastern vault. Every eleven minutes it exhales, and steam is not like hot air; it carries heat into you faster than anything else in the ward and a plume across a hand or a face is a scald in a second. The pump-hands know exactly where it goes and stand accordingly. Stand where they stand. Better, stand behind them.

The pumps you will feel in the brick before you hear them. Do not touch them and do not touch any cable run. The ward is fed electricity from above and it is wet everywhere and the insulation is old, and the people who live there know which runs to avoid by a knowledge you do not have.

Do not trust a ladder rung or a gantry plank by its look. Iron under a coat of salt can be sound or can be lace; the coat hides which. Test with weight before you commit, and where a scraper has gone before you, go where they went.

**The absence**

You should know in advance that nothing will move that a person or an engine did not move. No insect, no rat, no bird through the grates. People find this harder than they expect. The mind is used to a background of small living motion and its absence reads, after a while, as something watching. It is not. It is simply that life declined this place. Knowing that in advance helps.

There is green, in a few places — a felted moss along the steadiest drip-lines, and the fig under the largest grate. The scrapers leave the moss alone and so should you; it is not a rule, nobody made it, and that is exactly why breaking it would be wrong. Do not touch it and do not take a piece. The fig is forty feet up, rooted in the old city's foundations, hanging down into the ward's sky, and you can stand beneath it and look. If you find one of its fallen fruits dried to a stone on a gantry, leave it where it is. If someone gives you one, take it, and understand what you have been given.

**The people**

They will not be unfriendly and they will not be curious. They will be working. The ward is quiet because the air carries sound close, so speak low, and speak only when you need to. Do not stand in the scrapers' way on the gantries; they walk them with paddles and troughs and they do not have room for you. Do not sit on the rim of a pan or the lid of a cistern.

The water in the cisterns is the product. It is distilled, it is pure, and it is precious to the works above, and you should not drink it uninvited. If you are offered some, accept — it is very good, and being offered it means something. Carry your own otherwise.

If you want to bring something, think about what the ward does not have. Nothing ripens here; nothing is cold; nothing is fresh. Cold fruit, if you can keep it cold down twenty landings, is a wonder in a place where fig-fruit falls hard as stone. News from above is welcome. Anything from further away than above is more welcome still. Do not bring fire, for any reason, not a match, not a lamp with a flame. There has never been a fire in the ward and they would like to keep it so, and the air is dry enough in the corners to make one out of very little.

Some of them sleep in the mid-level vaults behind ceramic screens, through the day, and work the night. If you go at the dawn hour as advised, you will be arriving as they finish and others start. Keep your voice down near the screens.

**What to do while you are there**

Go to the foot of the stair and stop, and let your eyes open, and listen until you have heard the sigh once and know what it is.

Walk the eastern wall first, where the rock is hot but not dangerous and the pans are, and watch a woman rake salt out of a pan that was brine an hour ago. Walk under the lattice where the pipes cross the void and look up until you cannot tell which run is nearest. Go to the pinched end and see the glaze holding the light. Find the grates. Find the fig. Find the moss and see how green it is in a place that has no other green.

Then, if you have the time and the strength, go to the lowest gantry at the western end and stand a few yards off the glazed rock, and wait for the sigh, and feel the drizzle tick on the iron over your head, and understand what the description's narrator understood — that this is a place still in the act of happening, and you are standing in the middle of the happening, in a town that has grown into the wound because the wound was the one place where its particular life could take.

Then go up. Slowly. Rest on the landings. Change into the dry layer at the top and drink and eat salt and sit down somewhere cold for longer than you think you need to.

**After**

You will be salt-crusted and heat-tired for the rest of the day, and you will sleep hard. Rinse everything you wore; the mineral in it will stiffen the cloth otherwise and eat at the seams. Check yourself for cuts you did not notice — the sharp things in the ward cut cleanly and the heat numbs, and people come up with a slice on the forearm they cannot account for. Check your lips and the skin of your hands for cracking from the dry corners. Check that your ears have stopped listening for the sigh.

They will not have, quite, for a day or so. That is the ward's souvenir, more than any dried fig. A place that quiet, that full of process, sets a rhythm in you — the double thud you felt through the brick, the eleven-minute exhalation — and you will find yourself, in the ordinary world above, waiting for something very large to sigh in its sleep.
