**Preparing for the Hollow of Blades**

Start by being honest about what this place is. Strip away the cairns and the ochre and the leaning fins for a moment and look at the plain facts the description gives you: a bowl at the very top of a mountain, above the last living thing, in air so dry your lips crack in an hour, under a sky that presses down, where the cold at dusk is described as *total*. Everything you pack, every decision about timing, follows from taking those facts seriously. The beauty of the place is real, but it is the kind of beauty that belongs to environments that do not care whether you leave.

**Altitude is the first problem, and it is the one that kills people quietly.**

Life stopped several hundred body-lengths below the bowl—call it a thousand to fifteen hundred feet—and stopped completely. On most worlds that resembles ours, that puts you well above anywhere an unacclimatized body wants to be. You will be breathing air with perhaps half the oxygen you are used to, maybe less. If you step through from a sea-level home directly to the gap in the rim, you will feel fine for the first hour, slightly euphoric even, and then the headache will arrive, and then the nausea, and by evening you may be in real trouble with no way down that doesn't involve threading a maze in the dark.

So do not arrive at the top. Arrive low—in whatever valley the afternoon fog comes from—and walk up over days. Three at minimum, five is better. Sleep low, climb high, come back down to sleep. If your means of travel lets you choose an arrival point on this world, choose one at the treeline and be patient. If it doesn't, spend time somewhere else at altitude first, on any world, until your blood has adjusted. Acclimatization transfers. Bring the medication if you have access to it—acetazolamide or its equivalent—and know the signs of the two swellings, lung and brain, and treat the appearance of either as an order to descend immediately, fog or no fog, dusk or no dusk. A wet cough, a stumbling walk, confusion about which way the gap is: these are not things to sleep on.

Drink far more than you feel like drinking. The air will pull water out of you with every breath and you will not sweat visibly, so you will not notice. Carry all of it. Do not plan on the pool. There is a finger's depth of black water under a lens of ice at the low point of the bowl, and it has been receiving a paper-thin sheet of melt for longer than anything else there; breaking that lens to fill a bottle would be a small violence, and would gain you a mouthful. Bring a stove and fuel that works in the cold and melt ice if you must, but expect to be carrying a heavy pack up a steep approach, and know that the gap in the rim is barely wider than your shoulders. A pack that sticks out past your body will not fit. Either pack narrow and tall, or plan to take it off and pass it through ahead of you.

**Your eyes are the second problem, and this one is not quiet at all.**

Within a minute of arriving your eyes narrow to slits and do not open until dark. That is not a poetic exaggeration; that is what light from above and light reflected from below does at altitude. Ultraviolet is intense up here, the air filters almost none of it, and snow and ice bounce most of it back up into your face. Ordinary sunglasses are not sufficient. You want glacier glasses, the darkest category made, with side shields or a wrap so tight that no light leaks in from the edges. Bring two pairs, because losing one is a genuine emergency. Bring goggles as well for the fog and the rime, when glasses will bead over and become useless.

Snow blindness does not hurt at the time. It hurts eight hours later, at night, like sand ground into both eyes, and it leaves you unable to open them for a day or two. In a bowl full of blades that cut gloves, with a shoulder-wide exit somewhere in the maze, that is a very bad position to be in. Take the glasses off only under the fog or after dark, and even then be sparing.

Cover skin the same way. High-factor sunscreen, reapplied, including under the chin and inside the nostrils—reflected light burns the places direct light never reaches. A brimmed hat under a hood. Lip balm with sun protection, reapplied constantly, because the cracking begins within the hour and the sun finds the cracks. Wear a thin layer over everything even in the noon warmth; there is no warm color in this place except one rust stain on a wall, and there is no warmth in the light either, only intensity.

**The blades and the sand.**

The fins are old snow half-turned to glass, sun-cured and sharpened by sublimation to edges that will open a glove. You will be squeezing between them sideways with the ice against your back. So: gloves with leather or a tough synthetic on the palm and the back of the hand, not knit wool, not thin fleece. A shell layer that is abrasion resistant—not the thin, light fabric that packs down small and tears on the first edge. Think of moving through a room full of upright, rippled glass, and dress for it. A cut on the hand at this altitude in this cold heals slowly and bleeds in a way that matters.

The pillars are a different hazard. They heat in the sun—you can hear them tick as they expand—and their flanks are pitted and fluted. They will not cut you but they will scrape, and the sand at their feet is sharp-edged mica, glittering, a coarse gray grit that will get into every seam of your boots and your gloves and act like an abrasive. Gaiters help. Boots should be stiff, insulated, and already broken in, with a sole that grips both rock and old ice; carry microspikes or light crampons for the icier passages and for the pool margin if you go near it, but be aware that spikes will also want to catch on the sand and on the base of the blades. Move slowly. There is no reason to move quickly here and every reason not to.

Understand the blades' rhythm. They lean toward the midday sun, all at the same angle, and their bases thin through the bright season until once or twice an hour one tips and settles against its neighbor with a sound like a plate set down. That is a chest-high slab of dense ice moving. It does not shatter and it does not fall far, but if you are the neighbor it leans against, you will know it. Look at the base of any blade before you press yourself against it. If the base is hollowed to a stem, or if it is already leaning further than the others, give it room. Late in the bright season, when the fins are honed thinnest and most of them are near falling, the bowl is more dangerous than it looks and much more dangerous than it sounds.

**The cold, and the day's shape.**

There is a rhythm to the day and you should let it set your schedule. Morning: beads of condensation on the shaded side of every pillar, frost by mid-morning, gone by noon. Midday: the flat white light, the shadows shallow, the ochre marks and the blades' tilt all pointing at the same sun. Early afternoon: a thread of melt at the foot of the south-facing ice, the weep on the east wall running, the pool ticking. Later afternoon: the fog comes up out of the valley and pours over the rim.

Plan to be in the bowl in the morning and out—or committed to staying—by the time the fog arrives. The fog is the thing that will get you lost. Inside it, pillars and blades become identical gray uprights fading to white at three paces, and the sounds close in. There are two hundred pillars and a thousand narrow paths that dead-end where three fins lean together, and the gap you came in by is a slot in a rim that looks the same all the way round. Do not try to navigate in the fog. Sit down against a pillar, put your goggles on, put on every layer you brought, and wait for it to burn off from above. It will, usually within an hour or two, and the rime it leaves on every blade is one of the finer things you will see anywhere.

Before the fog, while you can still see, fix the position of the gap. Mark it in a way you can trust: note which pillar stands nearest inside it, note the cairn or two you can pick out with certainty, take a bearing if you have a compass and confirm the compass works on this world. Some travelers lay a thin cord from the gap to wherever they intend to sit; if you do this, take it up again when you leave. Someone left cord in the niche in the shelter and it has rotted to fibers, and the place does not need more of it.

Then the cold. The description says it plainly: by dusk the cold is total. The sand crusts. At this altitude with this dry air, night temperatures will fall far below freezing and there is nothing—no cloud, no moisture, no vegetation—to hold any warmth in. If you intend to be there after dark, you need a real high-altitude sleeping system: a bag rated far colder than you think you need, an insulated pad or two, a bivouac shelter or a small tent that fits on a swept floor the size of the one against the west wall. Down insulation works well here because the air is so dry. Hands and feet will be the problem; bring chemical warmers, bring spare socks and glove liners kept inside your jacket, and do not let yourself fall asleep cold expecting to warm up. You won't.

If you do not intend to stay the night, set a turnaround time and keep it. The band of copper along the rim at dusk is short. After it there is starlight, and the ice gives back enough of it that you can thread the blades hand on stone, hand on ice—but that is an emergency method, not a plan, and your hands will be the price.

**About the people who were here.**

There is a shelter against the west wall and a niche with worked quartz flakes and a cake of dried ochre. There are eleven cairns. There are faint bars and dots of rust-brown on the sun-facing pillars at the height of a face, placed where the morning condensation would slowly wash them away.

You will feel a pull, standing among all that, to add something. The place is full of standing things and someone long ago answered that fullness by standing more things up. Decide before you go whether you will do the same. My own instinct is that you should not—that the cairns you can identify with certainty are only eleven, and the ones you can't are perhaps a dozen, and adding a twelfth certain one changes a ratio that has been stable for a very long time. But this is your decision, and the place will not object either way. What you should not do is touch the ochre marks, or scrape the stain from under the weep, or take the quartz flakes from the niche, or sleep in the shelter and leave anything behind. The marks are being used up on purpose, a little every morning. Let them be used up on their schedule, not yours.

Use the shelter if you need it. That is what it is for, and its open side faces the pillars, which is a good direction to face. But sweep it when you leave as you found it swept.

**What to expect from yourself.**

The silence will be nearly complete, and you will hear your own pulse. You will be short of breath doing nothing. Your eyes, behind the glass, will be doing something strange with the whites and grays and the one poured blue, and by the second hour you will find yourself glancing sideways at pillars because they are exactly the height and width of a person standing still. This is normal. It is what the place is. It is not, I think, hostile; there is nothing in the account of any presence beyond mineral processes and old marks. But an ordinary human alone at altitude in total silence among two hundred standing shapes will produce figures at the corner of the eye entirely on their own, and you should know that in advance so that you can note it and go on.

Go with someone if you can. Not because the bowl is dangerous in any dramatic way—it isn't—but because everything that goes wrong up here goes wrong slowly and looks, from the inside, like nothing much. A companion notices the stumbling walk. A companion notices that you took your glasses off twenty minutes ago and forgot. A companion can find the gap when you can't.

And leave time. Not to do anything—there is nothing to do—but to sit against a pillar in the morning with the frost forming beside your shoulder and watch the light move the shadows a hand's breadth across the sand, and hear one blade somewhere behind you set itself down against another, and understand that the place has been doing exactly this for a very long time and will keep doing it after you have gone back through the gap and down to where things breathe. That is what you came for. Everything above is just so that you get to have it, and get to leave.
