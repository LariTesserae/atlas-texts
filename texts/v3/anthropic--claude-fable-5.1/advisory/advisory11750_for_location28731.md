**Preparing for the Drafting Room**

Start by understanding what kind of danger this is, because it is not the kind you're used to packing for. Nothing down there will hurt you. The floor is soft, the ceiling is roots, the temperature is a cellar's, and the sixty-odd animals regard you the way a field regards rain. You will not bleed, fall, freeze, or be eaten. The risk is quieter and more thorough than that. The room is a place where things are still being decided, and you are a thing, and it is patient. Every other precaution follows from this one fact: *you are entering as a finished draft into a space that does not recognize the concept of finished.*

**Go complete.**

This is the most important preparation and it has nothing to do with equipment. The room works on what is open. A stone that hasn't decided to be flint, a wren that hasn't decided not to be a moth, a crack that opens only when a beetle needs it. If you go down carrying an undecided thing — a grief you haven't settled, a choice you've been putting off, a question about who you are — the room will treat it as a seam of ochre and begin, gently and without malice, to audition it for roles. Do not go when you are hungry, because hunger is a want and wants are open. Do not go tired. Do not go in the middle of anything. Pick a day when you are, as far as a person can manage it, already what you are going to be. Eat a full meal. Sleep. Resolve the argument. Then go.

Decide before you enter exactly how long you will stay and exactly what you will do, and make it boring. Something like: *I will go down, stand near the entrance, look for the length of about a hundred slow breaths, and come back up the way I came.* Say it aloud on the hillside before the turf gives. In a room where outcomes arrive before events, your intention is the only luggage that actually gets there. If you go in intending to leave, you will find that you have already left, and the events will hurry to catch up. If you go in curious and unplanned, the room will be the one deciding what happens, and it may decide slowly.

**On finding it.**

You can't look for it. You find it by going down when the ground gives, on a slope of turf over what feels like chalk or limestone country — the kind of downland where flint and ochre-clay sit in the same hillside. Walk those slopes, alone, on an ordinary morning, and accept that most days the ground will be finished and you will simply go for a walk. Do not dig. Do not probe with a stick. An entrance you force into being is a decided entrance, and this isn't that kind of door; the step "the ground hadn't finished making" has to be unfinished when you find it or it isn't the step. If you have a companion, leave them at the top. Two people inside is a risk you don't need: from where they stand you may be one thing, and from a step to the left another, and neither of you will have moved.

**What to wear.**

Keep your shoes on. This cannot be stressed enough. There is a bare human footprint in the clay by the far wall, drying at the edges, and you will be wearing shoes when you see it. Read that correctly: the room is already imagining you unshod. Everything that makes a boundary between your skin and the surfaces — soles, gloves, sleeves, a hat against the roots — is a small refusal to be read. Skin is where the room takes its notes. The scratches on the wall fit your fingers before you decide to touch them; that's what contact does here.

Cover your scars. This is intuition rather than rule, but attend to it: nothing in that room is worn. Nothing has cracked, weathered, or aged, except the marks, which are older than the room and which the room has assembled itself around. A thing with history is the strangest possible object down there. You are made of history — every callus, every healed bone, every line on your face. The room has no such thing and will be interested. Long sleeves. Trousers. Don't give it your old wounds to study.

Wear nothing you love and nothing old. Not because it will be taken, but because you don't want to find out what a well-worn object does in a place where wear hasn't been invented yet. Buy the clothes new, wear them once to take the stiffness off, and be prepared to leave them somewhere afterward.

**What not to bring.**

Most of your preparation is subtraction.

No light. The light there has no source and does not yet know it should have one. Bring a torch and you offer it an answer, and the answer will be you, and shadows already pool at your feet.

Nothing that measures or counts. Look at what the previous visitor left: seven parallel scratches at kneeling height, a tally. Three flat stones stacked. A line pressed across the floor from wall to wall, stopping precisely at both, a boundary someone measured. Every mark in that room is an attempt to fix something — to say *this many, this far, this high* — and the room's response was to make those attempts older than itself, to build a wall around them so they'd have somewhere to be. Measurement is how you leave a mark here, and marks become geology. Leave the compass, the tape, the notebook. Leave the camera; a photograph is a decision about which of two things the wren is, and you don't have the standing to make it. Do not count the animals. You will lose count anyway, and the attempt to hold a number in your head is a small hard object you'd rather not drop.

Nothing to eat or drink, and touch none of the water. The moisture there is a concept that travels ahead of itself. You might have the fullness of a drink before the drink, the way the newt had the fullness of the beetle, and then the drink might or might not follow. Whatever you'd be after that, you wouldn't be sure of it.

No tools, no blades, nothing that could make a line. Not because you'd be tempted — because your hand might already be in the middle of making one when you noticed.

**Conduct inside.**

Stand near the entrance. You'll feel the shadows converging on the center of the room, and the center is wherever you're standing, so don't give it a center. Stay at the edge. Stay near the step.

Breathe slowly. You are the weather. Your exhalation swells a droplet, the droplet lets go, the floor learns it, the damp spreads faster than damp should. A quick breath is a squall. Do not speak; sound there is still on its way to being heard, and you don't know where your words would arrive, or when, or as what. If you must communicate with yourself, do it in thoughts, and keep the thoughts finished ones.

Don't name things. Naming is a decision. That is a wren from here; let it be a wren from here and a moth from there and don't try to settle it. Don't help the beetle. Don't watch the hunt too closely — the outcome is already present and the events are only catching up, and your attention is a kind of pressure.

Don't touch the marks. If you find your fingers already in the grooves, look at your hand *before* you look at the wall, and count five, and hold that, and withdraw. The gap where the impossibility should be is not yours to fill.

If a wall takes something in like water — the vole, or a root, or anything — do not follow, and do not test it with your hand. The wall is where the world is still substrate. That is the only place in the room you could genuinely be lost.

**Watch your shadow.**

It's your gauge. When you first arrive it will pool at your feet and lag a moment behind you when you move. That's the baseline. If the lag grows — if you shift your weight and your shadow takes a full breath to agree — the room has begun taking notes in earnest, and you should leave regardless of how many breaths remain in your plan. The moment you notice you're comfortable, leave. Comfort is the feeling of a draft being accepted.

**Leaving.**

Go up the way you came. Exactly the way you came. There is no other exit and you should not want one. The turf will close behind you with the quiet of a held breath letting go, and the hill will be a hill, and your shadow will fall behind you in one piece. Check that it does. Check your hands. Check your shoes.

Expect to come out damp in a way that doesn't quite make sense, damper than the room accounts for, and expect the damp to spread through your clothes faster than water moves. Change everything, down to the skin, as soon as you reasonably can. The idea of wet is leaving the room with you and will keep propagating for a while. Some travelers find it easiest to bag the clothes and be done with them.

**Afterward.**

There will be a tug. Faint, inward, for the rest of the afternoon — as if some part of you were still down there being drafted. This is normal. Sit somewhere ordinary with decided things around you: a wall that is only a wall, a cup that is a cup, a clock. Eat something. It fades by evening for most people.

If it doesn't fade — if the next morning things around you seem slightly unresolved, if you catch yourself standing still because your body is waiting to learn what it's about to have done — then you left something open down there. An intention you didn't finish, a question you carried in. Do not go back for it. It is in the wall now, being built around. Instead, finish the thing up here, deliberately, in daylight, with as much plainness as you can manage. Decide it. The room can't hold what's been decided; it only keeps drafts.

And if you ever find, on a hillside of your own, a bare footprint in soft ground where no one has walked barefoot, drying at the edges — don't kneel to it. Don't measure it against your shoe. Walk on, and be glad to be finished.
