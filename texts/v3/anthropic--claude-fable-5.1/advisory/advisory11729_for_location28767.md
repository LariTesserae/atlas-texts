**How to prepare for the city in the hole**

Start by accepting one thing: this is not a place you visit at noon. Everything else follows from that.

---

**Read the plain before you read the city**

The description tells you the plain gives no warning, and that's true of the eye but not of the feet. You will feel the tremor through your soles long before you see the edge. Treat that as your first instrument. When the ground starts to lean on you from below, slow down. When you start to hear it in the air, slow down more. Sinkhole edges in weathered limestone are undercut and rounded; there is no lip to trip over, just stone that stops. Approach the last hundred paces the way you would approach a cliff in fog, because the glare will make the plain and the sky the same color and your depth perception will be poor.

Carry a compass or some equivalent, and the moment you find the edge, mark it — a cairn, a scratch, a stake, something. The stair that lets you out comes up "some distance" from where you first looked over. You will emerge from it half-deafened, squinting, and wrung out, onto a plain with no features at all, and you'll want to know which way is back. Before you descend, walk the rim, find where the stair tops out, and note the bearing from there to your route home.

---

**Water is the whole budget**

There is none. Not a seep, not a cistern, not rain in living memory. Everything you drink, you carry down that stair yourself and carry the empties back up.

Assume this is a hot, dry, reflective environment: a white bowl under a white sky, stone that radiates heat back at you from every side, dry air that pulls moisture out of you without your ever feeling sweat. Budget something like four to six liters a day for a person doing light activity, more if you climb a lot of stairs. That is heavy. Water is what sets the length of your stay, not curiosity. A realistic visit for one person carrying their own supply is two nights, maybe three. Don't stretch it.

The resin is offered as "the only thing here that is like drinking." Be careful with that sentence. A fingernail's worth that makes the mouth not want water for a day is not hydration — it's a suppressant. Your body still loses the water; you just stop noticing. For someone who has adapted over a lifetime, in a place with nothing else, that may be a workable trade. For a visitor, it is a way to become dangerously dehydrated while feeling fine. If you try it, try it on the last day, in a tiny amount, and keep drinking anyway. Note also that a pulled stem smells of hot pepper — the plant contains something irritant, and you have no idea what the resin does to a body that didn't grow up with it.

---

**Sound: what it will do to you**

This is a place where grit dances on the paving and a person cannot stand at the focus. That is not "loud" in the way a festival is loud. That's a sound pressure level that damages hearing in minutes, and a low-frequency component that acts on the body directly — chest, gut, inner ear, eyes. People at the bottom walk "as though through deep water" because their balance organs are being shaken. Expect nausea, a sense of unreality, pressure headache, the teeth-ache the text mentions, and possibly panic — the body reads unbroken infrasound as threat.

So:

- Bring proper hearing protection, doubled: deep-fit foam or molded earplugs *and* over-ear muffs. This is not optional and it is not sufficient. It will save your hearing for the morning and dusk hours and reduce noon from catastrophic to merely unbearable. It will not stop what comes through the bones of your skull and the wall of your chest.
- Do not go below the first gallery between mid-morning and mid-afternoon. Do not go to the floor at noon at all. The locals who walk the floor at midday have decades of adaptation and no choice. You have neither.
- Anyone with a heart condition, a seizure disorder, migraine, vertigo, a recent ear infection, or who is pregnant should stay on the plain at noon, and probably shouldn't descend at all. The chest pressure is not a metaphor.
- Plan for tinnitus and dulled hearing for days afterward. The text is honest about this — at the top, hearing "will seem thin and unconvincing." Don't schedule anything that requires sharp ears or quick judgment right after you climb out. Don't travel alone if you can help it.

Also think about what noise does to *thinking*. At the volumes described, you will not be able to reason well. Make all your decisions — where to sleep, when to leave, how much water is left — in the quiet hours, and then stick to them.

---

**Light**

Everything is white and everything reflects. This is functionally a snowfield at altitude with the sun overhead. You need dark glasses with side coverage, or you should copy the locals and wear a woven strip across the eyes with a slit. The slit is actually the better tool — a pinhole sharpens depth perception in glare, which matters on stairs without rails. Bring both.

Cover your skin. Reflected light burns the underside of the chin, the nostrils, the inside of the ears. Wear a brimmed hat, long light sleeves, and something to shade the neck. Lip protection. Sunscreen if you have it, reapplied often, because it will sweat and wipe off in the dry heat.

The bottom of the hole is in shadow until noon and again after mid-afternoon. Use that. The shaded side is where the locals sleep. It's also cooler and, crucially, quieter — the stems on the shaded wall wake later and sleep earlier.

---

**The stairs and the stone**

Spirals with no rails, cut into limestone worn to soap. Wear shoes with real grip and ankle support. Keep one hand free at all times; carry your load on your back, not in your arms. Descend in the cool, when your balance is intact. If you must move during the loud hours, move along the walls, seated on the steps if you have to, and never with your eyes on the far side of the hole.

The grit that collects in the doorway corners is powdered lime. Fine calcium carbonate dust is mostly a nuisance, but if any of it is caustic — and a stem's root cup is "powdered lime" that smells of pepper — you don't want it in your lungs or eyes. A cloth over the mouth and nose during the day costs nothing.

---

**A schedule that works**

Arrive at the rim in the late afternoon. Sit at the top and listen to the noon-chord decay from above, where it's merely enormous rather than disabling. Watch the shadow climb the west wall.

Descend at dusk, when the stems slump and quiet and the people come out with their knives. This is the hour the city is most itself and most bearable. Stay out of their way; watch from a gallery, not from the floor. Do not step among the stems around the focus — some of them house beetles, the locals can tell which and you can't, and cutting or trampling a beetle stem is the one clear taboo the text gives you.

Spend the night. Ask — with hands, with a gesture at a doorway and a questioning look — before you sleep in a room. They're swept, which means they're kept, which means they're someone's responsibility even if they're nobody's home. Take the shaded side. Prop something across the open doorway if you have it; you won't have an amber pane, but a blanket dampens a little. Sleep with the earplugs in. The city is "almost silent" at night — the west wall mutters, the tubes whine, your own blood roars — and an hour before dawn a single stem on the eastern lip begins to breathe, and then another.

Be on the stair, climbing, before mid-morning. Feel the sound drain out of you step by step. Reach the plain while you can still hear a wind.

If you have the water, do this twice.

---

**Meeting the two dozen**

There is no speech here, ever, so don't arrive expecting to explain yourself. Learn a handful of clear gestures before you go: *may I*, *thank you*, *water*, *where*, *sleep*, *I will not touch*. Keep them small and slow; their language is quick and small, made for glare, but yours will be read as a stranger's, and slow is more forgiving than fast. A slate and chalk is better than paper — legible in glare, wipeable, doesn't blow away.

Bring nothing to trade that they can't use. They own knives, spindles, and eye-strips. A good flaked blade or a small copper hook might be welcome; food that needs no water to prepare might be; anything else is clutter in a place that has spent a very long time removing clutter. Don't offer water unless you have a surplus, and if you do, offer it — it may be the most generous thing anyone has ever handed them.

Don't expect hospitality in the sense you know it. Two dozen people in a city for a thousand, seen one at a time, who go about their work in a noise that would break you: their attention is on the stems. You are weather to them, a rumor. That's fine. Take it as permission to be quiet.

---

**Things not to do**

Don't cut a stem. Don't pull one to see the root cup. Don't block a gallery or stack your gear in a doorway — the arches are tuned, the columns are spaced to throw tone, and the whole city is an instrument that keeps its plants alive. Muffle a corner and stems die there. You would not walk into someone's field and salt a row.

Don't stand on the floor at noon to see what it's like. The text tells you what it's like: it folds the knees. You will fall, and you will fall among the stems, and you will not be able to hear anyone coming to help.

Don't leave anything. Not a wrapper, not a stake, not a trace in the grit. They sweep every morning. Whatever you drop, someone will carry up a very long stair.

---

**What to expect from yourself afterward**

You will come up onto the plain feeling scoured. Your hearing will be muffled and ringing, your balance a little off for a day, your chest oddly tender. The quiet will feel wrong — too thin, unpersuasive. That passes. What tends to stay is the memory of standing in a gallery at dusk with the whole bowl going amber and forty thousand tubes settling into their cool shapes with a sound like a very high, very distant crowd falling silent, and a dark point on every branching tip catching the last light and doing nothing at all.

Carry your water. Cover your eyes. Leave before noon. The city will go on being lit and loud without you, and it does not need you to understand it — only to not get in its way.
