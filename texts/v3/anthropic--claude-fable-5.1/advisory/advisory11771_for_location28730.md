**Preparing for the Finished Plateau**

---

**First, understand what kind of place you are going to.**

Most dangerous places announce themselves. A glacier looks like a glacier. A desert looks like a desert. This place looks like a very quiet field on a cool afternoon, and it is trying to kill you gently, by omission, the entire time you are standing on it.

The fundamental fact is altitude. Not altitude as a mountaineer knows it—a thing you climb into over days, acclimatizing, sleeping low—but altitude as a starting condition. You arrive at the top. There is no valley below to retreat into, no base camp, no lower. The single most important treatment for every altitude illness ever recorded is *descend*, and here the word means nothing. Your only descent is the way you came in. Hold that in your head above everything else: the exit is not just your way home, it is your medicine, and the distance between you and it is the distance between you and the ground.

The warmth from below has tricked the temperature into tolerable. It has not tricked the pressure. The air is thin enough that the sky goes purple at noon, which puts you somewhere in the territory where an unacclimatized human has, at best, hours of clear thinking before the headache, the nausea, the slow stupidity begin—and the stupidity is the part that gets people, because it is the stupidity that decides the headache isn't so bad and the structures are so interesting and another hour won't hurt.

So: this is a short visit. A day, ideally. Two if you are disciplined and equipped. Plan it like a dive, with a hard turnaround time set before you arrive, written on the back of your hand, obeyed without discussion.

---

**The body**

*Oxygen.* Bring it. Not as an emergency measure but as the plan. A portable concentrator will struggle at this pressure; compressed cylinders with a demand regulator are more reliable. Budget more than you think you need, because you will be breathing hard against the thin air even at rest, and because you will want to walk, and walking here is work. If you cannot bring oxygen, keep the visit to a few hours, stay within sight of your exit, and treat every mild symptom as the end of the trip.

Bring a pulse oximeter and check it every half hour whether you feel fine or not. Set a number—say, a saturation below eighty percent for more than a few minutes—at which you leave, no argument, no photograph first. Acetazolamide taken the day before can blunt the worst of it if you tolerate the drug. Dexamethasone in your kit for the case where somebody's judgment or coordination visibly goes.

*Dryness.* The air is thin and it is cold at night and it holds almost no water. Every breath you exhale carries moisture out of you into an atmosphere that will not give it back. You will dehydrate faster than at any low place of the same temperature and you will not feel thirsty in proportion. Drink on a schedule. Bring all your water; the plateau's entire hydrological cycle is a film of frost thinner than breath on a window that vanishes the instant the sun sees it. You could scrape a plinth's shadow side in the last hour before dawn and get a damp fingertip. That is a curiosity, not a supply. Lip balm, saline spray for the nose, and expect nosebleeds.

*Sun.* A dark sky means the air is not scattering light, which means it is not filtering it either. What reaches you is close to raw. Ultraviolet will be ferocious, and the shadows, with nothing in the air to soften them, will be nearly black—your eyes will be hauling between glare and pitch with every glance. Wraparound glasses with side shields, the darkest you own. Cover skin. Hat with a brim and a neck flap. Sunburn here in an hour would be a bad kind of burn.

*Cold.* The ground is cold—colder than the air, noticeably, so that kneeling in the flour feels like kneeling on a freezer floor. The air by day is spring-cool; by night it drops below freezing, or the frost could not form. Dress in layers you can adjust and bring a sleeping pad with a very high insulation rating if you intend to lie down at all, because the ground will pull heat out of you all night and the thin air will not put any back.

*The flour.* This is the hazard that is particular to the place. It is fine enough to run through your fingers with almost no weight, which means it is fine enough to hang in air and fine enough to reach the bottom of your lungs. You will be breathing hard. You will be breathing it. Whatever those grains are—and nobody knows—they retain preferences, they sort themselves, they move toward and away from one another. You do not want a substance with habits in your alveoli. A properly fitted respirator or high-grade particulate mask under your oxygen delivery, sealed goggles rather than glasses if the air is moving at all, and a scarf or buff over the mouth when you take the mask off to eat or drink. Wipe your hands before you touch your face. Do not lick your fingers. The metallic smell on your skin is the material telling you it is more than dust.

---

**The kit**

Beyond the above, and trying to think through a day on the surface:

- Radios, one per person, tested before you arrive. Sound does not travel here. A shout from thirty meters is a whisper; a whistle is nothing. If you and a companion are separated by a hundred meters and a low ridge, you are separated absolutely except by radio or line of sight.
- A tether. Two people walking, connected by ten meters of cord, is not paranoid. The ground reshapes itself; the person behind can lose the person ahead behind a wall that was not there a minute ago.
- Brightly colored gear. Everything here is grey-fawn. You want to be the one thing that is not.
- A sextant or a star chart appropriate to the sky you'll be under, and a reliable timepiece. There is no satellite navigation. A compass may or may not behave—the flour smells like metal and covers a continent, and it would be unwise to trust a needle over a sun. Learn where the sun will be at each hour before you go.
- Stakes and a mallet. Whether the flour will respect a stake you place is unknown—it respects the old fixed things, but you are not old, and you did not make anything harder than mountains. Test it. Plant a stake, watch for an hour, see whether the bare ring forms or whether the material simply flows over it. If it flows over, your markers are worthless and you must navigate purely by the fixed objects and the sky.
- A small spray bottle of water. The flour draws back from frost and leaves the rock bare for the rest of the day. Whether it draws back from liquid water is the single most useful experiment you can run in your first hour. If it does, a misted line around your sitting place will keep the ground from creeping in; a damp cloth over a pack will keep it clean; a wet fingertip will clear a patch of rock to check for a groove. Water will sublimate off fast in this air, so it is a temporary tool, but a good one.
- Knee pads. You will kneel to look at things and the ground is hard, cold, and glassy beneath the flour.
- Headlamp with a red mode. You should not be walking in the dark, but you should be able to see your camp.
- Notebook and pencil. Not a device. Devices fail; also, you will want to draw the structures, and drawing forces you to actually see them rather than just stare.
- More food than a day requires. Appetite dies at altitude and you must eat anyway; the body is burning fuel to breathe.
- Nothing you cannot afford to leave behind. If a fall or a fog of exhaustion means dropping a pack and walking to the exit, drop it.

---

**Arrival**

You will step into a silence so complete that your own pulse is a sound in the room. Give yourself the first ten minutes to just stand. Let the headache of the pressure change settle. Check your oximeter. Look up—the sky will be a color you have not seen from the ground before, deepening straight overhead almost to indigo, paling to a thin milk at the horizon where the air runs out. Look down. Nothing will appear to be happening.

Then look at one spot in the middle distance and count sixty seconds without moving your eyes.

It is important to do this at the start, before fatigue, so that you know for certain what the ground is doing and you do not spend the rest of the day half-believing you imagined it. The ridge you fixed on will have shifted or gone. A dark stain will have relocated. Somewhere in the corner of your vision the flour will be smoking, that hand's-width haze rising and falling like something exhaling. Once you have seen it you will not stop seeing it, and that is correct. This is not a plain. It is a slow boil that has cooled to the temperature of a spring morning, and you are standing on the surface of it.

Locate your exit against something fixed. If you have arrived within sight of a plinth or column, note the bearing and distance to it and to your exit point exactly, in writing. If you have arrived on open flour with no fixed object visible, you are already in a worse position than you should be: the ground around your entry point will be entirely different by evening. Plant your test stake now. Spray your water line now. Watch what the flour does with them. Your day's radius depends on the answer.

---

**Walking**

Move slowly. Not because there is anything to hurry away from but because at this pressure every fast step costs you oxygen you cannot recover, and because the surface is deceptive underfoot. The flour is weightless and fine and gives no purchase; beneath it, wherever the underlying rock shows, it is dark glass, smooth past any natural stone, and frost-slick in the mornings. The assembling structures are a hand high with edges "sharp as if cast"—not enough to injure, exactly, but exactly enough to catch a boot at the wrong moment when your balance is already compromised by thin air. Watch your feet. Lift them.

Do not cross the structures if you can go around. Partly this is courtesy to a thing that is, in its dim way, trying to do something. Partly it is that you do not know what happens when a construction is disrupted mid-assembly—whether the material simply slumps, or whether it reorganizes around the intrusion, or whether the dispersing grains carry some small preference now for the thing that broke them. The flat animal passes through the structures at the moment they collapse, and gets larger. You do not want to be the one who collapses them.

Navigate fixed object to fixed object where you can, and never lose sight of the last one until you have the next in view. Where you cannot—and you may walk for hours with nothing on the horizon—navigate by the sun and by counting paces, and accept that your count will be inaccurate because you will be stepping around things that were not there when you set out. Keep the day's range small. The plateau is not going to reward you for distance. It is the same for a hundred kilometers in every direction; whatever you will learn about it, you can learn within an hour's walk of the way home.

If you find a groove—a hand wide, a finger deep, straight as a rule, exposed by a bare patch—do not follow it. This is the hardest advice on the list because it will be the most tempting thing you see. The longest anyone has traced ran two hundred and eleven kilometers without bending. It goes nowhere you have the oxygen to reach. Kneel, look at it, photograph it, note its bearing and where it meets another if it does. Then get up and turn back toward the fixed thing you can still see.

---

**Camp, if you must**

Do not camp on the open flour. The material moves, it will bury a low tent by morning or build a wall through it, and the flat animal moves at night across exactly this surface, straight through whatever is on it, heavy enough to press a meter-wide smear that lasts hours. You have no idea whether it would notice you and no reason to find out.

Camp in the bare ring around a plinth. The flour keeps half a meter clear of the fixed objects, everywhere, always, as if they are still saying something it can hear. That ring of dark glassy rock is the only stable ground on the continent. It is cold, it is hard, and your sleeping pad will earn its keep, but nothing will build on you in the night and—this is a guess, but a reasoned one—whatever the animal is, it seems to be of the same substance as the flour, and the flour does not come here.

Two things about the ring. First, the crust. On the shadow side, in rings that follow where the morning shade falls, there will be a black-green film thinner than paper that has been growing since before the plateau finished flattening. It takes a century to grow a centimeter. Do not sit on it, do not lean your pack on it, do not touch it, do not scrape the frost off it in the morning to wet your lips. Set up on the sun side of the ring where nothing grows and leave the shadow side to whatever has spent ten thousand years earning it. Second, the frost. It comes in the last hour before dawn on the shadow side. It is grey and looks like fur. When the sun reaches it it will go directly to vapor with a sound you will not hear. If you want to see it, wake early. If you touch it, use the back of a gloved hand and only on bare rock, never on crust.

If the fixed object you find is the dish—the shallow bowl four meters across set flush in the rock—do not step into it. It is level to a tenth of a millimeter, it is polished smooth, and in the frost hour it is a lens. You would slide, and you would mar something that has read the sky every morning since before your species had a word for morning.

Do not walk after dark. The structures collapse in the dark unseen and rise unseen, the footing changes without warning, and the only thing here that profits from the turbulence is out on the surface, moving in reorganizations rather than steps, no eyes, the same color as everything. Stay in the ring. Watch the stars, which with no air to blur them will be a more violent and crowded sky than you have stood under before. Check your oximeter by red light. Sleep if you can; you probably won't much.

---

**The flour, up close**

You will want to touch it. Do, once, with a gloved hand, and understand what you are feeling: cold, nearly weightless, a metallic smell that will stay on the leather. Let it run out and watch whether the grains you dropped rejoin the surface at random or go somewhere. They will go somewhere. That is the whole of the place in a palmful.

Do not fill a bag with it to take home. This is not superstition. You would be carrying a substance that sorts itself by a property nobody has identified, that builds and unbuilds according to preferences worn down to reflex, that avoids water and old stone and gathers everywhere else. A bag of it in a lowland room with normal air pressure and normal humidity is an experiment you would be running in your house.

Watch the structures. This is why you came, and it is worth coming for. Pick one patch of ground a courtyard wide and give it an hour—an hour set on a timer, because you will lose the hour otherwise. You will see it raise itself into concentric walls, or a spiral, or ranked ridges too regular for wind, hand-high and clean-edged, and you will feel with absolute certainty that it is about to become something. Then it will lose its conviction. The edges soften, the walls pour back into the plain, and a hundred meters off another one has already started. Draw them. You will never see the same form twice, and drawing them is the only way to hold the shape long enough to notice that it was almost, but never quite, a plan.

Late in the day, when the light goes long and amber, they throw shadows for the first time and for a few minutes the whole plain looks inhabited—a settlement, a field of instruments, work in progress. That is the moment to be sitting still with your back against a plinth. It does not last. Be in your ring before it ends.

---

**Things not to take, and things not to do**

Loose objects surface in the flour: a hinge that has never rusted, a lens the size of a fist that shows the same view a few seconds late. They circulate. The same lens was found twice, eighty kilometers apart, a decade between. Do not pocket them. Whatever system moves a lens across a continent over ten years is a system still running, and you would be removing a part from it. Look, photograph, set it down where it was. If you look through the lens, do not look long; a view a few seconds behind your own eyes is disorienting in a way that compounds badly with hypoxia, and you may find yourself stepping toward where something was rather than where it is.

Do not dig. The grooves are in the rock, under the flour, and they are older than the flattening. You will want to expose one and follow it with your hands. Clear what the frost or your spray bottle clears and no more. The material draws back from water on its own terms; it should not be forced back with a trowel.
