# Preparing for the Tableland

Start with the thing that will govern everything else: you are going somewhere that has no light and cannot have any. Not "dark." Not "unlit." Light does not occur there. This is a different category from any darkness you have experienced, and almost every instinct you own — every reflex trained by a lifetime of a lit world — will be wrong. Preparation for this place is mostly a matter of un-training yourself, and then of protecting a body that was not built for constant wet, thin air, and unending noise.

Think of it less as a trip and more as a short dive. You go in, you have a limited window before the environment starts to take you apart, and you come out. Plan the visit on that basis.

---

## I. Before you go: learn to be blind

Nothing else you do matters if you have not done this.

**Spend real time blindfolded.** Days, not hours, and not sitting still. Walk your house. Walk outside. Cook a simple meal. Find things you have dropped. Learn what it feels like to move through a space with your hands and feet and ears leading and your eyes contributing nothing. You will bang your head, and you should, here, where the consequences are a bruise, rather than there, where they are a hot pipe or a cliff.

Better still, seek out people who live without sight and ask them to teach you. There is a body of real technique — cane work, the way you shuffle rather than step, how you keep your weight on the back foot until the front foot has confirmed ground, how you trail a wall with the back of your hand instead of the palm so you don't jam your fingers into something. Some blind people navigate by making sharp clicks with the tongue and listening to the returns. Learn to do this. You will be walking into a forest full of animals that do exactly this, and you will need every scrap of it.

**Break the reaching habit.** In darkness, your hand goes to your pocket for your phone, for a light, before your brain catches up. There, this reflex is worse than useless — it is a way of dying, because the half-second you spend groping for a light that will never work is a half-second you weren't feeling the ground in front of you. Practice noticing the reflex and letting it pass.

**Understand that closing your eyes will make no difference,** and that this will bother you more than you expect. In ordinary darkness you still get the faint swimming grey of your own visual system idling; you can press on your eyelids and see color. There, nothing. The visual cortex receives no input of any kind. Some travelers find this a flat, uncanny pressure behind the face; others find that after a day or so the sense of "looking" simply quiets and they stop noticing. You cannot rehearse this. Just know it is coming and that it isn't damage.

## II. What not to bring

- No flashlight, headlamp, lantern, glow stick, or candle. They will not work, and worse, they will tempt you to keep trying.
- No screens. No phone, no camera, no GPS unit, no anything with a display. Even if the electronics survived the wet — they won't — you cannot read them.
- No matches, or only one, for the experience of feeling a flame you can't see. It is worth doing once. It is disturbing.
- Nothing scented. Soap, sunscreen, deodorant, insect repellent. Smell is one of the four senses you'll have left, and there are animals there whose entire lives run on heat and chemical traces. Don't paint yourself with a signal.
- No cotton. It will be saturated within the hour and it will stay that way and it will rot against your skin.
- No paper. Ink runs, and you can't read it anyway.

## III. What to bring

**A long stick.** Longer than a walking cane — chest height at least — smooth, with a rounded tip. This is your first sense. It sweeps the ground before your feet, finds trunks before your face does, tells you when the "soft" of the mat changes to the "hard" of engine ground or the "ring" of something hollow. It is also what you push into the water before you put a foot in. Practice with it until it stops being a thing you hold and starts being the front of your body.

**Cord. A great deal of cord.** More than you think. Bright colors are irrelevant; what matters is that you can feel it and that it doesn't rot in a day. You will run a hand line from your arrival point outward, tied trunk to trunk at hand height, and you will follow it back. You will mark things with knots — a system you have decided on beforehand and practiced until you can read it by touch: one knot for the way home, two for water, three for a burn hazard. Cord is also how you stay attached to a companion, if you bring one.

**A tactile watch.** Mechanical, with raised markers you can read by feeling the hands, or a Braille watch. This is not a luxury. There is no hour there. There is no dawn to tell you a day has passed, no evening to tell you to rest. Your body will lose the thread of time within a single sleep and you will not know whether you have been there six hours or thirty. The watch is the only thing that will tell you when to sleep, when to eat, when to turn back. Wind it. Check it more often than feels reasonable.

**Gloves — and a decision about them.** You need bare fingertips to read the world: the spiral ridges of bark, the joints in cut stone, the shelled ridges cemented to the iron. But the pipes are hot enough to burn, the rust is sharp, the shell colonies will open your palm like coral. Many travelers settle on one gloved hand and one bare, or gloves with the fingertips cut away, and accept the trade. Whatever you choose, bring a spare pair, because the first will be soaked and abraded quickly.

**Footwear that drains.** Your feet will be wet for the entire visit. There is no version of this where they are not. Boots that hold water will macerate your skin into something that peels off in sheets. What you want is a shoe that protects the sole from rust and shell and lets water leave as fast as it enters — a sandal with a hard sole, a water shoe, a canyon boot. Bring antifungal powder and use it whenever you rest against warm metal, which is the only place you will ever get partway dry.

**Wool and synthetics** in layers you can shed. The air is warm, but you will be standing in wind at the plateau's edge, and stillness in saturated air is its own kind of problem — see the section on heat.

**Musician's earplugs.** Not the foam kind that block everything. You need your hearing; it is your long-range sense, the only one that works past arm's length. But the roar is constant, and near an engine it is loud enough that a few days of it will leave you permanently deafened to the frequencies you most need. Filtered plugs that cut the volume but preserve detail let you stay longer. Take them out when you need to listen carefully. Put them back in when you sleep.

**Water treatment that does not depend on sight.** Chemical tablets, or a squeeze filter. There is water everywhere and it is full of living things. You can also heat water: fill a metal bottle and wedge it against a pipe. It will be hot in a while. This is far safer than a stove.

**If you must bring a stove,** bring one you can light and monitor entirely by ear and feel — a pressure stove with a pump and a hiss. Understand that its flame will be invisible and that you will locate it only by heat on your face and the sound of it. Never leave it lit and step away. Most travelers find that heating things against the engine pipes is enough and leave the stove behind.

**All your food.** Do not eat anything there. The animals are not afraid and are easy to catch and you know nothing about their chemistry. The moss on the iron smells of pennies and you should assume it is exactly what it smells like.

**A wound kit built for wet.** Burn dressings. Antiseptic. Waterproof tape in quantity. A course of broad antibiotics if you can get them, because a cut in a warm saturated environment that never dries is a cut that will infect. A current tetanus vaccination — this is a world made of rust. Blister care for feet. Something for headaches, which you will have, from the altitude, from the noise, from the strain of a mind trying to see.

**Something for your hands to do.** A worry stone, a string of beads, a length of cord to tie and untie. When you sit still in that place, your body will want to do something with the absence, and your hands are the part of you that can.

## IV. The body, and what the place does to it

**Altitude.** The air is thin. You feel it as breath coming faster than the exertion warrants, and later as a headache and a queasiness that is easy to blame on the noise or the dark. Acclimatize before you go if your route allows a staging point at height. Move slowly from the moment you arrive. Rest often. If your head throbs and your stomach turns and rest doesn't fix it, that is your body telling you to leave, and you should listen.

**Heat.** This is counterintuitive. The place is warm, not hot — but the air is saturated. Sweat cannot evaporate into air that is already running with water. Your body's cooling system is disabled. Add exertion, add the thin air making everything harder, and you can overheat in an environment that feels merely muggy. Signs: you feel your heart going too fast for what you're doing; you stop being able to think in straight lines; your skin is hot to your own touch. Stop. Sit. Drink. Move away from the engine if you're near one. Pace is everything here. The narrator of the description walks for a very long time along a curving wall; you should not.

**Wet skin.** Every fold of your body will be wet for as long as you are there — armpits, groin, between the toes, under any strap. This becomes chafing in hours and open skin in a day or two. Loosen straps. Change what you can. When you rest with your back against a warm engine, open your clothing and let the heat work on you; it is the closest to drying you will get. Some travelers strip entirely at rest stops. There is no one to see.

**Hearing.** Covered above, but worth repeating: the roar is not a backdrop. It is a slow injury. Plan for it.

**Burns and cuts.** You will find pipes with your face and your hands before you find them with your stick, because they run at head height and you are looking down for the ground. Move with one hand raised and open in front of you, palm out, as if feeling for a curtain. When your hand touches something warm and rough, stop moving entirely and map it before you go on. The shell colonies on the iron are reef-sharp; read them with the backs of your fingers, not the pads.

## V. Moving through it

**The forest is the easy part.** The trunks are close and cool and you turn your shoulders between them. Trail your hand along one, step, find the next. The ground gives and you will trip on the roots that are pipes and the pipes that are roots. Fall well — hands out, chin down — and get up slowly.

**The trunks tell you where you are.** They lean, all of them in a given stretch, toward the nearest engine. Put your hand flat against a trunk and feel which way it leans; that is where the heat is, the hard ground, the noise, the life. If you want the engine, follow the lean. If you want to get away from the engine, walk against it. This is your compass. Do not trust a magnetic one; you are on a tableland full of iron.

**Water tells you down.** Kneel and put your hand in any moving water and feel which way it goes. The plateau drains, slowly, toward its edges. This is important for the next thing.

**The edge is the thing that will kill you.** The ground stops cleanly. There is no rail, no change in vegetation you can count on, nothing but a shift in the sound — the roar in front of you goes soft and hollow — and a change in the air, which comes up warmer and wetter into your face. Learn those two signs before you go, by having someone describe them to you and by imagining them until you are sure you'd know them. Then, there, the rule is absolute: if the sound ahead of you opens up, or the wind starts coming from below, stop where you are, get low, and turn around. Do not go closer to feel it. Do not try to reach the lip. You cannot see how close you are and you never will. There is something exhaling down there. Let it.

**The engines are the landmarks and the destinations.** You come on them by the roar resolving into a single stroke and the ground going hard. Then the wall: warm, wet, curved, alive under your palm with the slow rise and fall inside. This is where most travelers spend most of their time, and it is the right choice. The wall gives you a fixed reference in a place with none. Sit with your back against it and nothing can come at you from behind. The heat lets you partly dry. The pipes let you heat water. And the tremor coming up through your spine is, frankly, the point of the visit.

Stay on the hard ground. The moat around the engine is warm and full of things that bump your ankles and slide away, and larger things that surface with a sound like a bucket coming out of a well. Probe with the stick before you step. Do not put your hand in the water for any reason.

**Do not try to walk from one engine to another.** They are a day or two apart. A day or two of blind travel through saturated forest at altitude, with the edge somewhere out there, is not an ordinary human's journey. The pipes connect them and you could follow a pipe — but the pipes are hot, they dip and rise, the forest has grown into them, and you would be committing to a march you cannot see the end of in a place that does not do ends. One engine is enough. It is more than enough.

**If you are with someone, tie yourselves together.** A few meters of cord, waist to waist. You will not be able to hear each other over the roar at anything more than arm's length, and a person three steps away in that place is a person who is gone. Agree on touch signals before you go — a hand on the shoulder means stop, two squeezes means go on, a hand pulled down means get low. Agree on what you'll do if the cord goes slack. And know this: tapping on a pipe with a stone carries along the metal for a long way. If you are separated, find a pipe and tap. Slow, in threes. Listen for an answer. It is the closest thing the tableland has to a telegraph.

## VI. The animals

They are everywhere and they are not afraid of you. This is not because they are friendly. It is because fear of the strange is something you learn from being looked at, and nothing here has ever been looked at. They will bump into you, climb across you, lift their heads toward your warmth, and mostly move on.
