**Preparing for the Low Country**

Start by understanding what kind of danger this is. Nothing here hunts people. Nothing here has ever seen a person, and almost nothing here can see at all. The place will not attack you. It will simply continue, at full volume, in every direction, and the risks are the ones that come from being a soft, warm, hearing, sighted, upright animal set down inside a machine built for something else entirely. You will be hurt, if you are hurt, by a wet step, by your own ears, by the cold, by breathing, or by standing still too long near a ledge. Prepare for those, in that order.

---

**Your ears are the first thing to protect and the first thing you will resent protecting.**

There is no gap in the sound. Waterfall noise you can habituate to; this is millions of aimed voices at every frequency, and the mid-band alone is almost certainly loud enough to do permanent damage in hours. Wear deep-seated foam plugs *and* over-ear muffs, both, from before you descend the first step. Test the fit at home; a plug that works out in a dry room will work out faster with condensation running down your neck.

Understand what the protection will and won't do. It will save your hearing. It will not stop the low pressure, which you will feel in your chest and your breastbone regardless, or the shrilling above hearing, which you will know about because your teeth and the small bones of your face will tell you. Those two come through everything. Accept them now. They are the part of the place you cannot refuse.

Understand too what you lose. Everything here navigates by sound. With your ears sealed you are the deafest thing in the basin, and you are already nearly the blindest. You will be moving through a country that is constantly describing itself in a language you have shut out, and you have shut it out because it would deafen you. That trade is correct. Make it anyway.

At the very end — if you want the moment the description offers, hand on the wall — lift the muffs for a count of ten and keep the plugs in. That is enough. Longer than that is not courage; it is just hearing loss.

---

**Footing will kill you before anything with legs does.**

The whole country is a descent over wet rounded limestone, and the flat places are not floors so much as accumulations. The drift of droppings and shed skin and fallen bodies lies deep in every sink, and you have no way of knowing whether it is a hand's depth over stone or a body's depth over a crack that goes down to the rivers. Pools drain into holes. The floor of one sink is the roof of another.

Wear boots with the most aggressive tread you own and gaiters sealed over them. Carry a stiff probing pole — a trekking pole is barely adequate; something longer and stronger is better — and test the drift ahead of every step in any place you can't see stone. Keep your weight back. Move as if on ice, because the limestone under the wet is not far off ice.

Wear a helmet with a chin strap. Things fall from the ceilings constantly — the dead, the young, the weak picked off by wall-walkers, and occasionally stone. Most of it is small and soft. Not all of it.

For the actual steps between sinks: rope, a harness, and the ability to rig a simple rappel and climb back up. Practice this before you go, in the wet, in the dark, in gloves. You will be doing it exhausted and cold on the way out, which is the only direction that matters.

Do not enter the half-roofed galleries. Do not go down toward the sound of the rivers. There is something large in the lowest levels, known only by the way the roar bends around it, and you do not need to learn anything more about it than that. Stay in the open sinks nearest your entry. This is a region the size of a country; you are not going to see it, and trying is how people don't come back.

---

**Breathe through a filter, cover your eyes, cover all of your skin.**

The air is full. A fine rain of dust and skin and microscopic life drifts constantly down from the ceilings, and the drifts underfoot are, functionally, guano beds — the kind of substrate that on any world grows the fungal spores that settle in human lungs and announce themselves weeks later. There will be ammonia in the low places. There will be things you have no name for.

A full-face respirator with P100-class filters solves three problems at once: it filters what you breathe, it seals your eyes against the constant soft collisions of flying bodies, and it keeps the wet off your face. Goggles alone will fog instantly in air this saturated; the airflow of a full mask helps. Bring spare filters sealed dry. Do not take the mask off for any reason while you are below the rim — not to eat, not to drink, not to speak, because speaking is impossible anyway.

Cover everything else. Waterproof shell over insulating layers, hood up, thick gloves, no gap at wrist or ankle or neck. You will be walking into a continuous soft impact of things that are leathery, chitinous, feathered, wet, and occasionally have mouths. Nothing is likely to bite you on purpose. Some things will bite you because you are there.

---

**You are warm, and warmth is how the ledge-predators find their food.**

The long soft hundred-footed things lie along the ledges and taste the air for heat. You are almost certainly the warmest object of your size in the basin, and unlike the fliers you are not hanging above them; you are walking past at their level. They are patient. They do not need to chase.

So: do not lean on walls. Do not sit on ledges. Do not rest against anything. Keep your body in the middle of the open floor when you stop, and do not stop for long. Cold will push you to want shelter against the stone, out of the drip. Refuse that. The stone is where they are.

The wall-walking crabs pick weak fliers off their perches. To a crab, a human standing still against a wall is a large weak flier. Move.

---

**Cold and wet are the slow danger, and they are the ones that will actually make you leave.**

It never rains and everything is soaked. Moisture condenses on you as fast as it condenses on the stone. There is no sun, no dry place, no way to warm up below the rim. Hypothermia here is not dramatic; it is a gradual loss of coordination on exactly the terrain where coordination is the thing keeping you alive.

Dress for cold-water conditions, not for a cave. Bring a complete change of dry clothes in a sealed bag and leave it at the rim, not in your pack. Bring hot liquid in an insulated flask and leave that at the rim too, because you will not be able to drink it below. Set your turnaround by your energy, not by your clock: when you have used half of what you have, go up, because the climb out is harder than the climb down and you will be doing it colder.

Do not drink the water there. It has run through everything.

---

**Time and light: bring your own, because the place has neither.**

Midday is brown dusk and the rest is night, and the cloud never moves, so there is no way to read the hour from anything around you. Wear a watch you can see without light and that will survive immersion, and write your exit time on a card in a pocket you can reach.

There is a right window. The fliers leave toward evening — hours of them pouring up through the cloud — and while they are gone the floor comes alive: the crawlers move most, the pools boil, the ledge-predators come down. Then toward dawn they all return, and the return is worse than the leaving: the whole sky of the sink filling with descending bodies. You want neither. Enter after the dawn return has fully settled and the roar has closed over again. Leave long before the pitch begins to drop toward evening. You will not hear the pitch drop through your muffs, but you will feel it change in your chest, and by then you should already be climbing.

For light, carry a headlamp on its dimmest setting, ideally red, and two backups sealed against water. Bright light will disturb fliers into mass movement and will also make you the most visible object in a country where some things lure prey with light. Use what you need for your feet and no more. After a while your eyes will give up looking for anything else and begin to find the faint blue-green glow of the animals themselves, in threads down the water, in constellations on the ceiling. That is the place's own lighting. It is not enough to walk by. It is enough to see by, once you stop asking it to be more.

---

**You cannot navigate here the way you navigate anywhere else.**

There are no landmarks. Every sink looks like the last one: pale walls, lost floor, grey and slightly luminous with wet. There is no sun, no stars, no satellites in whatever sky is above the cloud. The sound that maps the country for its inhabitants is exactly the thing you have sealed out of your head.

So do what cavers do. Fix a line at your entry point and pay it out behind you; never let go of it, never go past its end. Mark your turns with retroreflective tape that shows only in your own light — not glow-markers, which will vanish among the animals' own glow. A compass may work; do not trust it alone. Stay within the first sink, or the first two, and treat the far wall of the second as the edge of the world.

If you go with someone, tether to them. Speech is impossible, so agree in advance on a small vocabulary of touch signals and light signals: stop, come, up, danger, out. Practice them in the dark at home. You will be relieved you did.

---

**When the roof falls.**

Somewhere in the basin on any given day, ten thousand years of water finishes its work and a ceiling lets go. You will feel it as a single deep note through your feet, and then something you will not expect: for a few seconds, the roar stops. Everything in the country goes still to listen.

When that happens, stop where you are. Cover your head with your arms. Do not run — there is nowhere to run to, and running is how you go into a crack. Wait for the roar to come back. It will, slightly rearranged. Then check the air for dust, check your line, and begin heading up, because you were probably going to anyway and now you have a reason.

---

**What it will do to your mind, and how to hold on.**

The cold gets into you, then the wet, then the noise, and the noise goes deepest. After some time you will find there is no room left in your head for words. This is not a metaphor. The continuous pressure and the shrilling and the mid-band chorus occupy the part of you that narrates, and it goes quiet, and something in you starts to experience the place as the place experiences itself: not as chaos but as a country announcing itself, seeing itself by sound, full in every direction.

This is the thing worth coming for. It is also a state in which you will make poor decisions about your feet and your time. So anchor yourself with tasks that don't need words: count steps, count breaths, run the line through your glove. Check the watch even when you don't want to. The card in your pocket with the time on it is for the version of you that will not be able to think the sentence "I should leave now." Write it clearly.

Do not sleep. Do not sit. Do not let the place become comfortable, because it will not become comfortable; it will become familiar, which is different and more dangerous.

---

**Afterwards.**

Strip and bag everything at the rim. Change into the dry clothes. Drink the hot thing. Sit in the quiet for a while before you try to talk, because your first attempts to speak will come out wrong, too loud and too flat, and your ears will be reporting a silence so total it feels like a pressure of its own.

Decontaminate your gear as if it has been through a guano cave, because it has. Watch your lungs for a month: cough, fever, tightness. Watch any bite for swelling. Write down what you saw as soon as the words come back, because they will come back slowly and the first hours of memory are the ones the place keeps.

---

**A last thing.**

The description says you will not stay, and that this is not a rule but simply what the place is like. Believe it. Everything here has been in its niche since before the stone above it fell; there is no gap for you and none will open. You are not an intruder, exactly, because the place has no concept of intrusion. You are just a warm, slow, temporary object passing through a system that is complete without you.

Before you go up: stand once in the middle of a sink, well away from the walls, and put your gloved hand out to the nearest shelf of the air-coral and feel it click under your fingers like a run of small stones. Then take the hand back. Then look up at the ceiling, where the wall is breathing, and feel through your boots and your sternum the country hearing itself.

Then climb. The steps are soft and grey and they only go one way, and up is the way that leads home.
