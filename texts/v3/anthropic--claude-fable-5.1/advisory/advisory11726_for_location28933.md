Begin, as the description does, with the altitude — but not because it's the worst thing. It's the thing that stacks with everything else.

**The body, first**

You are going to a place that is high, thin-aired, and at the same time warm and saturated. Those two conditions almost never coexist, and your body has no ready script for them. At altitude you breathe faster and lose water through your lungs; in a warm fog your sweat does not evaporate and you overheat without noticing because you never feel dry. The pan-quarters especially — steam off boiling brine, engines, no wind — will behave like a sauna at the elevation of a mountain pass. Expect a headache the first two days. Expect to be thirsty in a way that does not seem to match how much you're sweating. Drink more than feels reasonable and keep drinking.

Do not go straight up the switchback road in one day. Sleep partway. If there is a waystation below the cloud-line — and there will be, because the carts of ash and salt and fertilizer come down and the carts of grain and timber go up, and drivers rest — spend a night there. When you hear the roar an hour below the cloud and think it's wind, you'll have been warned that it isn't, and you'll still think it's wind. That's fine. Let your ears adjust before your lungs have to.

The sun is the other altitude problem, and it's a trap, because you will be under cloud almost constantly and assume you are shaded. You are not. Thin air lets the ultraviolet through and the cloud scatters it rather than stopping it, and then the white crust on every surface throws it back up at you from below. People burn here without ever seeing a shadow. Cover your neck, the backs of your hands, the underside of your chin and nose. Locals will have skin like tanned hide or they will have skin that is never exposed at all; either way they've stopped thinking about it, and won't warn you.

**The water, which is the real hazard**

Everything else here is uncomfortable. The water can actually hurt you.

It is alkaline in the range of lye. Not so strong it will burn on contact — you'll be able to dip a finger and pull it out — but strong enough that skin left wet with it goes slippery and soapy-feeling, and that soapy feeling *is* the burn. Alkali dissolves the fat in skin. It keeps working after you've dried off. Pan-workers have hands like cracked plaster for this reason. The eyes are the danger: a splash in the eye, or wiping your face with a hand you rested on a wet rail, will take the surface off the cornea if you don't flush it fast and long. The rule is simple and you must actually follow it: never touch your face with a hand that has touched anything outdoors. Wear glasses or goggles near the channels and the pans. If you feel that slick, soapy sensation anywhere on your skin, flush it with clean water for far longer than seems necessary, and do not rub.

Carry a small sealed bottle of vinegar or a paper of citric acid. A weak acid neutralizes a weak alkali and will stop a splash from continuing to work. This will also make you popular; see below.

The condensation is a subtler version of the same thing. It's not pure water — the description is explicit that it carries mineral, which is why the walls are white. It's carrying aerosol from the pans and the flats. It's mild. Over a week it will make your skin tight and your lips split and any small cut sting and refuse to close. Over a lifetime it makes the pan-workers' hands. Keep a fat-based barrier on your hands and face — the locals will almost certainly use rendered bird fat for exactly this, and you should buy some and use it, because it will be better than anything you bring. Grease your lips. Grease the inside of your nostrils, which will otherwise crack from the soda dust.

Drinking water: ask, before you drink anything. The city must have a source — a soda-works produces vast amounts of clean condensate as a byproduct, and there will be cisterns and collectors on the cooler side of the pans where the steam recondenses as something nearly distilled. That is the water to drink. Do not drink from anything that has touched the flats. Do not trust that a channel looks clear; the red is bacteria but the clear is soda.

**Air**

The fog in the pan-quarters is mildly caustic and full of fine soda ash. Short exposure is unpleasant; long exposure is what gives the pan-workers their cough. For a visit of days or weeks you will be fine, but wear a damp cloth over your mouth in the ash sheds and the pan-houses and anywhere the steam is thick enough to taste. The flavour will be soapy and mineral. You'll get used to that faster than you expect.

The smell is another matter. Sulfur from the vents, ammonia from the roosting cliffs, rot from the rendering sheds, soda over all of it. It will be overwhelming for two or three days and then it will vanish, not because it's gone but because you've stopped registering it. Do not eat much the first day. Do not try to fight it with perfume or scented anything; that just adds a layer.

The guano is the specific medical concern. Where bat and bird droppings accumulate in warm damp places, fungal spores accumulate with them, and breathing those in bulk causes lung infections that can be serious. The roosting cliffs during scraping season, the bat fissures, the reinforced roofs of the rendering sheds — cover your mouth with something wet and don't linger. Do not enter the bat cracks at all. There is nothing in them you need.

Bird populations this dense carry disease among themselves constantly. Don't handle live birds, don't handle dead birds, don't handle eggs, don't let the small dark mammals come near your hands — anything that lives on carrion and dropped chicks in a population this size is exposed to everything, and it will bite. The bats are a rabies risk in any world. When one lands on you at dusk, and one will, let it leave.

If you can vaccinate against anything before you go, do it against influenza and tetanus; the iron here is all rust and every scratch will be dirty.

**Footing**

The crust is the shore and the shore is the crust, and the crust is not always solid. Evaporite terraces build outward as thin shells and some of them are hollow underneath, or sitting over warm water. The pale ground near a vent may be a lid. Walk where locals walk. Their paths will be worn a slightly different white from the surrounding crust — smoother, greyer — and they will go around places that look perfectly sound to you. Follow them.

Everything wet is slick, and everything is wet. The rock is coated in a film of mineral and bacterial slime and bird droppings. The stairs of the old districts have been rounded off by accretion until they're more like a series of shallow bowls. Wear boots with deep soft-rubber soles — not leather, which the alkali will destroy at the seams in a fortnight, and not hard rubber, which skates. Bring a second pair.

Do not lean on railings. Iron railings are rust holding the shape of iron. Also, they may be birds.

Near the vents, the ground is hot enough to cook you, and you cannot tell from looking which patch of pale crust is warm and which will take the skin off your palm. The pans are set directly over the heat; the area around a pan is a gradient from warm to lethal. Stay behind whatever the pan-workers stay behind.

**What to wear and carry**

Nothing that rots, nothing that rusts, nothing that needs to stay dry.

Cotton goes to pulp in this humidity within days and the alkali finishes it. Leather stiffens, cracks, and comes apart at the stitching. Wool and heavy synthetics are what work; wool in particular tolerates being wet forever, resists the alkali better than plant fibre, and doesn't smell worse than everything around it. Wear loose layers you can shed in the pan-quarters and add on the outer plateau, where above the cloud-line the thin air can go cold fast.

Every metal fitting on your gear should be brass, bronze, or plastic. Steel zippers will seize. Steel buckles will weep rust onto everything they touch. Any tool you bring should be one you're willing to leave behind orange.

Anything electronic will die. Not eventually — quickly. Salt-laden humidity gets into every seam. If you need to record anything, use pencil on paper you've waxed or oiled, and even then expect the pages to soften. Paper money and documents should be sealed in something impermeable and kept against your body.

A dry bag, or several. Not for keeping things dry — nothing will stay dry — but for keeping the worst of the mineral off the things you care about.

Goggles. A brimmed hat with a chin cord, because the birds will land on it and the flies will find your eyes under it. Fine mesh for your face at the waterlines, if you want to be able to open your mouth outdoors. The flies do not bite but they are a *pelt*, a hand's width deep, and when they lift they go into your nose and mouth and ears. Locals eat indoors, or with their backs to a wall and a hand over the plate. Do the same.

Earplugs for sleeping, the first several nights. The noise is not sharp — it's a steady grey roar with the engine-thump under it — and after a week you'll sleep through it and wake if it stops. But the first nights it will keep you at the surface.

**Trade**

Nothing grows here. Read that again. Every scrap of grain, every vegetable, every piece of fruit, every plank of timber, every hide, every length of good iron, comes up the mountain in a cart. The city exports soda ash, salt, fertilizer, bird oil, feathers, pigment, and lime, and it imports everything else.

So: fresh food is the single most welcome thing you can carry. Oranges, apples, onions, anything acid or crisp. A traveller who arrives with a bag of lemons will find doors opening. Vinegar, as mentioned, is both medicine and gift. Good steel — a knife, a file, a pair of shears that hasn't yet begun to rust — is worth a great deal to people whose tools are in permanent replacement. Leather goods, oiled and new. Cloth that isn't already grey.

Don't bring salt, soap, or glass. Don't bring pink or red pigment. These are what they make.

Whatever currency the low country uses will probably be accepted, but it will be soft with damp within a day. Ask what the pan-workers are paid in and try to hold that.

**How to behave**

The most important thing to understand is that nothing here is remarkable to the people who live here, and your remarking on it will mark you as someone from below who has heard the stories.

Do not call the birds a wonder. Do not stand at the shore gaping at a million flamingoes while a pan-worker is trying to get past you to his shift. Do not ask whether time has stopped. Do not ask whether the birds are souls. Do not suggest the white stone is a spell. The people of the plateau have heard all of this from low-country visitors for generations and find it faintly humiliating, the way anyone does when their livelihood is turned into someone else's mysticism. The birds are livestock, more or less. The water is the mine. The crust is weather.

The pace is the other thing. Everything here runs at a steady rate and has for centuries, and people move at that rate. There is no rush hour. There is no urgency in the pan-houses because the pans have never stopped and never will. If you are in a hurry, you will be conspicuous and mildly resented, the way a person who runs through a library is. Slow down before you arrive. Speak at the pace people speak to you. If you ask a question and the answer takes a while, wait.

Do not interfere with the birds even to be kind. A chick fallen from a nest is going to feed a small dark mammal, and the mammal will feed a beetle, and the rendering sheds will get whatever's left, and all of that is someone's income. Eggs on a ledge belong to the ledge's owner in some sense you may not immediately grasp; don't touch them. If a bird is on your door handle, wait. It will move.

Do not go into the pan-houses, the rendering sheds, or onto the scraping cliffs without being taken. These are workplaces and dangerous ones and your presence is a hazard to people who know exactly where to put their feet and are not accounting for someone who doesn't.

Expect a certain incuriosity about wherever you've come from. Not rudeness — just a settled sense that whatever is happening below the cloud-line doesn't change anything up here and isn't likely to. People will ask about the price of iron and grain. They may not ask about anything else.

**What it does to your head**

This is the part the practical lists miss.

You will lose the horizon. Things at a hundred paces are silhouettes and things at three hundred are gone, and the light is a shadowless grey-gold that comes from everywhere at once, and the only clocks are the bats at dusk and the shift-bells if the city has them. You will not know which way is north. You will not know what time it is. You will follow a pipe furred white on the outside, or a line of worn grey path, or the sound of a particular engine, and that will be how you navigate. Learn the sounds early — which pan-house thumps at which rhythm, where the gulls are loudest, which direction the waders' grumble comes from. Locals steer by ear without knowing they do it.

Some people find this profoundly soothing after a few days. The place has reached its level. Nothing is going to happen. The birds will be on the rail tomorrow and the pans will boil and the flies will lift and settle. There's a kind of rest available inside the roar that is hard to find in cities where things change.

Other people find it suffocating, and it comes on around the fourth or fifth day: the sense that you are inside something that has finished happening and will never let you see an edge. No sky. No dry stone. No silence. No shadows. No new thing. If you feel this starting, walk to the outer rim of the plateau where the cloud thins, get above it if you can, and look at the low country for an hour. The hard white sun will hurt after the fog. Let it. Then go back down into the roar.

Do not stay long enough to find out which kind of person you are, unless you already know.

**Leaving**

When you go back down the switchback, you'll notice about an hour below the cloud-line that the roar has become wind, and then a while after that, that it's stopped, and the silence will feel like a pressure change. Your skin will take a week to stop feeling tight. Your clothes will smell of soda for months and there is no washing it out; the mineral is in the fibre. Whatever iron you brought will be red. Whatever leather you brought will be gone.

The birds will still be on the rail. That's the thing you'll find yourself thinking about, later, somewhere dry. Not the millions of them. The one on the rail, that moved over, and moved back.
