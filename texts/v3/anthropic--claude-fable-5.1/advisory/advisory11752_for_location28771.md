**Preparing to visit the hollow**

Start by understanding what kind of visit is possible. The description says a kneeling person could put their head and shoulders into the room, and this is true in the sense that the geometry allows it. It is not true in any sense that matters. The room is on the scale of a dinner plate. The city climbs its walls. If you put your head into it, your ears will be in the tiers, your hair will be across the bridges, and the moving wet wall will be a centimetre from your cheek. You will have entered the way the shrew enters, except that you are roughly two thousand times its mass and cannot leave in forty drips.

So the first thing to accept is that you are not going in. You are going to the threshold of a thing and looking at it from outside. Most of your preparation follows from that.

---

**The fen**

Before you get to the tree you have to get to the tree, and the fen is the larger part of the trip. It is wide and grey and cold, and it is autumn turning to winter, and the alder stands in black water. Expect to be in that water to the knee for hours, and kneeling in it at the end. Waders are the minimum; a drysuit under them is not excessive. The cold here is not dramatic, it is patient, and a person who has been kneeling in fen water for forty minutes with their face in a freezing hollow will find that their judgement has quietly left before their fingers have. Set a turnaround time before you go in and treat it as a drip you have no say over.

Sphagnum does not bear weight the way ground does. Move slowly. Probe with a stick. A closed-cell foam pad to kneel on will crush the moss at the alder's base, and you should feel bad about that, and use it anyway, because the alternative is your own knees going numb against roots.

Bring nothing scented. Nothing that rustles. Tie back your hair.

---

**Finding it**

Nothing about the outside suggests it. This is stated plainly and you should read it as a warning rather than a flourish. There are many alders standing in black water with sphagnum on their lower trunks and sedge leaning against them. The split faces north, away from anything, and it is already half-curtained with liverwort. From two metres it will look like bark.

You may not find it. Plan for a day in which you look closely at the bases of a great many trees, and let that be a real day, not a failed one. Go around each alder on the north side. Look for a burl — a swelling — with a vertical seam. Look for liverwort growing where it has no obvious reason to be, a fringe that is greener and wetter than its surroundings, because it is drinking sap. If you find it, do not touch the curtain. Sit back. Let your breathing settle. You have arrived somewhere that has never had a visitor your size, and the next hour is about becoming as little as you can.

---

**You are weather**

The room is very near freezing and everything in it happens slowly. Your body is at thirty-seven degrees. Every breath you exhale is saturated with warm water. If you breathe into the hollow, the air in it will fog, then that fog will freeze on the pale cut walls, then your next breath will melt it, and the drops that were hanging for a long time under the overhang will fall early, and the skin of ice on the cistern will thin, and the temperature of the pool will lift by some fraction of a degree.

This is not trivial. Nearly everything in the room is asleep, and it is asleep because it is cold, and it is autumn. A springtail warmed into motion in the second month of winter does not have a spring to wake into. It has a few hours of activity and then nothing to eat and then the cold again. The frog is, as the description says, technically dead, and the arrangement it has made with death is thermal. The most harm you can do here is not crushing anything. It is warming it.

So: breathe away. Turn your head to exhale over your shoulder, or bring a length of flexible tubing and breathe out through it into the fen behind you. Practise this before you go. It feels ridiculous and it is the single most important thing you will do.

Keep your face out of the opening. A hand's width back is not enough; your face radiates. Look in from the side at an angle, or better, don't look in directly at all — a small angled mirror on a handle, held at the liverwort's edge, will show you the tiers without your putting anything warm across the threshold. A dental mirror works. A cold one, kept in the outer pocket, not against your body.

---

**Light**

There is no light in there, and your eyes will have to do what they can. Sit in the fen for twenty minutes before you approach, in the dark, letting them open. Do not use white light at any point. A red light on its lowest setting, shielded so it falls only where you point it, held well back and used in short intervals, is the most you should allow yourself, and even that you should ration.

Two things about what you will see. The fungus thread along the seam is described as not actually luminous, only pale enough that the eye insists on it. In near-total darkness after twenty minutes' adaptation, you may find you can see it without any lamp at all — a white line, a blur on the oldest plaster, the road laid across it. This is worth the wait. It is the only thing in the room that will show itself to you unasked.

The other: the builders' plaster is marked all over with a repeating dent, which is a mouth. Bring a hand lens, ten power, and look at that. It is the closest you will come to touching anything.

---

**Time**

The drip is the clock. One every eleven seconds from the ceiling into the pool. Count in drips, not minutes. The shrew — a native, a thing this room has adapted to — is gone in forty. You should try not to be present for more than a hundred, and you should count them, because the cold and the dark and the strangeness will otherwise dissolve your sense of duration completely and you will find you have been kneeling there with your knees dead for an hour.

Come at night, or in the long dusk that passes for afternoon. Noon here is a rumour, and daylight will not help you see inside anyway; what it will do is warm the fen and bring the water up, and the fen's cycle — swelling through the night, the pool taking the lowest tier — is something you should not add to.

If you can make more than one visit, make several short ones rather than one long one. Come on different nights. The city climbs a step and leaves one behind; the ice on the cistern forms and a builder stands on it until it holes. You will see more of the room's life across three visits of thirty drips than in one of a hundred and fifty, and you will have warmed it less.

---

**What you will and won't see**

Be prepared for the room to look empty. The city is sparse, with a great deal of nothing in it. The fauna are mostly invisible — a texture on the wood, a drift like spilled seed, a grey button that is a slug. The builders move like cold syrup and may not move at all while you are there. What you are likely to see is dark, wet, pale walls, a thread of white, and a smeared arrangement of small shapes on the wall that could be plaster or could be grain.

If that is all you see, you have seen it. Do not use more light to see more. Do not lean in. The description is a thing that was assembled over weeks by something with a great deal more patience than you can bring on a single cold night, and you are not owed the whole of it.

Listen instead. The sound just below the drip — the hush of a full theatre before anything has happened — is real, and you can hear it if you are still and your own breathing is going out through the tube behind you. It is the most any human will get of the room's interior. Give it the last twenty drips of your budget.

---

**The shrew**

It comes in under the liverwort curtain with the fen water on its fur. That is: it comes in through the same opening you are kneeling in front of. It will not be deterred by you. It is a hot fast thing that has never encountered anything like you and will treat you as terrain.

If you hear it — and you may not — do not move. Do not shine anything. Let it pass over your hand if that is where it goes. What it does inside is not for you to interrupt; the city's spareness is, at least partly, the shrew's work, and the builders repair the runnel in the morning. If you frighten it at the threshold, it may go in faster and hungrier, or it may not go in at all and take something else in the fen, and you have no way of knowing which of those the room is built for.

---

**Do not**

Do not touch the liverwort. It is the door and it is drinking.

Do not touch the raw wood. It is a wound, and every surface is a cut surface, and your skin carries things.

Do not remove the fungus. They built a road over it.

Do not repair anything. If the shrew has broken the lip of the runnel and the water is running where it should not, that is theirs to move back.

Do not take anything. Not a fibre of the cord. Not a flake of plaster. Not one of the thousands of mites you cannot see and might carry away in the pile of a glove.

Do not leave anything. A hair. A fibre from your sleeve. A drop of your own sweat, which is warm and salt and a different chemistry entirely from the alder's.

Do not feed anything, or wake anything, or try to help the frog.

---

**Leaving**

Back away the way you came, on your knees until you are clear of the alder's roots, and then stand slowly. Your legs will not work well. Wait for them.

Look back once. The tree will look exactly as it did before you found it: an alder in black water, sphagnum on its ankles, sedge leaning against it. You will not be able to see the split from where you stand. That is the correct last image.

---

**What to expect afterward**

You will have knelt in freezing water in the dark for something under twenty minutes and seen, probably, a pale smear and a wet wall and perhaps a white thread, and heard a hush. It will not feel like enough. It is meant not to.

The room will close. Callus tissue is already beading the edges of the split; in years the alder will fold new wood over the wound and there will be no room, only a scar in a burl, and inside it — perhaps — galleries again, and whatever the builders do when they have no sky. You are visiting during the brief interval in which a sleep has a door. You do not get to see it well. You get to have been there, and to have been, for a hundred drips, the largest weather it ever had, and to have left it a fraction of a degree warmer than you found it and no worse than that.

If you can manage no worse than that, you have prepared adequately.
