**Before you go: understand what kind of place this is**

It is not a ruin and it is not a cave, though it will try to convince you of both. It is a construction site the size of a city, underground, that has been running long enough to develop weather and short enough that nothing in it has ever been repaired. That combination is unusual and it governs everything about how you should prepare. Ruins are dangerous because things are failing. Caves are dangerous because things are indifferent. This place is dangerous because things are *working* — pumps, fans, trucks, cutting machines, a curing schedule — and none of them were designed with a wandering visitor in mind. The worksite hazards are real and immediate. The cave hazards are real and slow. You have to carry both sets of habits at once.

Assume the temperature is somewhere between a refrigerator and a cellar, nine or ten degrees, and that it never changes except near the pump stations and the new pours. Assume one hundred percent humidity. Assume you will be damp within an hour and wet within four, no matter what you wear, because the air itself is wet and the ceiling rains. Plan for the cold as if it were a long day in a boat, not a day in the snow: the danger is not frostbite, it is the slow theft of heat through wet cloth over many hours, the kind you don't notice until your hands stop working properly.

---

**Clothing and footwear**

Wool or synthetic base layers, never cotton. Cotton in this environment becomes a cold poultice against your skin and stays that way. Over the base layer, an insulating mid-layer you can remove near the pump stations, because standing between two of them is genuinely a few degrees warmer and you will sweat under your shell if you don't vent. Over that, a hard-shell waterproof with a hood that fits under a helmet — the rain at K-43 falls forty meters and by the time a drop reaches you it stings, and the drift moves, so you will be under it without meaning to be.

Boots: waterproof, ankle-supporting, with a hard toe and an aggressive sole. The floor has a permanent film that never dries and the sole compound matters more than it usually does. The flat parts are merely slick; the hazards are the lake margins, where fine grey silt over concrete behaves like wet clay on glass, and the lime crust, which looks like dry footing and shatters into a paste. Bring a second pair of socks in a sealed bag and change them at your halfway point regardless of how your feet feel. Bring gaiters if you have them.

Gloves, two pairs: a thin working pair and a waterproof insulated pair. You will want to touch the piers, and you should, but understand that fresh concrete and the water that runs off it are strongly alkaline. Skin contact with the wet green-grey surfaces in the newest quarters, or with the cloudy runoff pooling beneath them, will not hurt at first and will hurt later. Wash your hands in flume water, which is clean and cold, not in standing water near the pours. Keep your gloves on when you lean on anything you haven't looked at.

A hard hat is not optional. It is a construction site with conveyors overhead carrying broken slab and rebar of a gauge no one recognizes. Things fall. A high-visibility vest, ideally in orange to match the column stencils, will do more to keep you safe than anything else you carry, because it makes you legible to a mixer driver at two hundred meters in a hall where the only other things that are bright are the lamps.

---

**Light**

The lamps are islands. This is the single most important thing about the geography. Between islands you are in real darkness, and the islands are far enough apart that you cannot see the details of the next one — only its glow. You will spend most of your walking time in the dark, navigating toward brown-gold light and listening to your own boots.

Carry a headlamp with a strong beam and a dim mode, and a second light of a different type — a handheld with a tight throw — for picking out column numbers, which are stenciled at eye level and only at eye level, so that if you approach a pier from the wrong face you will walk around it in the dark before you find the number. Carry spare batteries for both, and carry them somewhere warm on your body, because cold drains them. Carry a third light, a small one, that you never use, so that you know it is there.

The fog banks matter. When the fans change speed and lift a sheet of mist off a cold lake and push it down the columns, the lamps dim one after another as it passes through them. If you are between islands when this happens, your destination will disappear. Do not walk toward where it was. Stop, put your hand on the nearest column, and wait. The fog passes at walking speed and the lamp will come back. If you keep moving you will drift, and drifting in a hexagonal grid thirty paces on a side puts you somewhere you did not intend with disorienting efficiency.

---

**Navigation**

Learn the grid before you do anything else. Columns carry a letter and a number. Letters run along one axis and numbers along the other, and the grid extends in every direction farther than any map shows, because the map is redrawn weekly and the excavation adds a few meters a day. Find the pump station at H-5 and photograph or copy the map on its wall, and write the date on your copy, and understand that your copy is already slightly wrong.

Then find your exit and memorize the route to it as a sequence of column numbers, not as a picture. Pictures fail here because every island looks like every other island. The shafts are not exits — they are vertical drops full of falling water and grates at the bottom. The exit is wherever the mixer trucks come from. Watch a convoy arrive and note the axis they travel along. That is the way out, and it is also the way in for anything heavy.

Count columns as you walk. Thirty paces between piers is a rhythm you can hold in your body, and if you keep a running tally you can always place yourself within one column of where you think you are. Check the stencil at every fifth column. If a stencil does not match your count, stop and reconcile before you go on. The hall is regular enough that a small error compounds quietly.

Carry a compass and see if it works. It may not — there is a lot of rebar. Carry a length of bright cord for marking your route through any section where the lamps are sparse, and take it up again when you leave; the crews will not appreciate finding it.

---

**Water and the six-hour rule**

Before you descend, learn what the surface weather was six hours ago and what it will be for the next six. This is not a curiosity. It is the tide table.

A storm on the surface at noon arrives underground at six as a change in the entire character of the place: the shafts turn white with falling water, the channels rise, the pumps come up in sequence and the pitch of the district drops into your chest. The lake at K-43 spreads across floor that was dry an hour before. The flumes, which were fast and clear, become fast and full and cannot be crossed except at the grated bridges. If you are somewhere low when this happens you will have to move, and you will have to move in the right direction. Know where the low bays are. They are where the lakes are, and where the silt is, and where the pale stalks sprouted from a seed that came down with the last storm. Do not linger in them if rain is due.

The flumes deserve their own rule: never step into one. The water is clear enough to look shallow and it is not, and it moves faster than it appears, and it is at the temperature of a mountain stream. A flume takes a person to a grate. Cross only at the crew bridges. The heron walks the flume edges because the heron weighs a kilogram and has a heron's feet.

The sump at the far end — the black basin where the channels converge and the pumps draw — has a rail, and you may go to the rail, but you should not go alone and you should not lean. The surface trembles from the intake. There are stories about what moves in it that is not the intake. You do not need to test them. The pumps will not stop for you.

Bring your own drinking water. The flume water is probably fine and you will be tempted, but it drains a surface you have not seen and passes through a district still leaching lime, and there is no reason to find out.

---

**Sound and the horns**

Wear hearing protection near the pump stations. Do not wear it anywhere else. Your ears are how you will find the face, the batch plant, the trucks, and the horns, and the horns are the closest thing the district has to a language.

Two long and one short from one station, and a lower answer from a kilometer off. Nobody will explain the code to you unprompted, and it does sound like animals calling, and you will find yourself listening for it. Ask a crew member. Do not guess. Do not ever imitate one. Some horn sequences almost certainly mean *a pour is coming* or *the face is blasting* or *the channels are rising*, and if you are standing in a bay when one of those goes and you don't know it, the lag between the signal and the event is the only margin you have.

The noise also means you will not hear small things. You will not hear a person walking toward you. You will not hear the heron until you are on it. You will not hear a mixer truck's engine over the pumps until its headlights hit the column beside you. Get in the habit of stepping fully behind a pier when you see a sweep of light, not beside it.

---

**Trucks, conveyors, and the face**

The mixer convoys are the brightest thing that happens. For a few seconds as they pass, the hall reveals its actual size — ranks of piers vanishing into a distance you had been underestimating — and the temptation is to stand and watch. Watch from behind a column. The drivers are not looking for you.

Conveyors run along the ceiling carrying the spoil back from the face in a slow black river. Do not walk directly beneath them for long stretches. What they carry is broken slab and rebar, and rebar of an unfamiliar gauge is still rebar. Wear your hard hat even in quarters that feel finished.

The face itself — the grinding under everything, the machines the size of ships hidden in their own dust — you may want to see. It is the one place where the second rule of this world is visible: the drills cutting through older concrete, older brick, a curve in a wall that suggests someone with different ideas about weight. If you go, wear a respirator rated for fine particulate. Cutting concrete makes silica dust, and the older material, whatever it is, makes more. Stay well back. Stay behind the crew, and be aware that the crew regards what they are cutting as a materials question and only a materials question, and there are reasons for that which you should respect. You can ask what the old stuff is. You will be told it cuts easier and fouls the aggregate. Take the answer. Do not ask twice, and do not stand in front of anyone and say the word *arch*.

---

**The crews**

This is a workplace. Everyone in it has a job and you do not. The thing that will make your visit go well is being obviously, visibly not a problem: high-vis, hard hat, out of the way, moving predictably, standing where you can be seen.

Someone painted a small orange ring around a coin-sized fern growing sideways out of a tie-hole. That tells you something about the people here that the horns do not. They are not indifferent to the place. They notice what grows under the lamps. If you find a ring, stay outside it. If you find something growing that has no ring, do not touch it, and if you feel moved to, tell someone, and let them decide whether it gets one.

Bring something to share that is warm — a thermos of something — and it will get you further than any question.

---

**The life**

You will probably not see the heron. Most days no one does. If you do, at column M-19 or elsewhere along the flumes, do not approach it. It has already seen you. It is the largest thing living here that is not a person, and it has found a way to live in a place mixed on a Tuesday, and there is a real chance it is the reason a certain kind of person keeps coming back down. Do not feed it. Do not photograph it with a flash.

The moss under the lamps is a map of the lighting and it is also, in the six-month quarters, the first thing here that looks like it belongs. Look at it. Do not scrape it. The three pale stalks in the silt at the lake's edge will not survive the next rise; you can know that and still not be the thing that ends them.

There are cats, reportedly. Cats are fine. If a cat follows you it is because you are warm.

---

**Time**

Bring a watch, and set it to surface time, and do the six-hour arithmetic on paper. The day exists here only as a faint grey pillar in the shafts, and it will not keep you oriented. What will keep you oriented is the pour schedule and the storm lag and the horns, none of which run on a clock you know. Decide before you descend how long you are staying and where you will be when that time is up, by column number, and keep to it. The district is designed to make you underestimate its extent and it is very good at it.

Do not sleep down here on your first visit. If you must rest, do it in a lit island near a pump station, on the warm side, with your back to a pier, and do not lie down on the floor. If you are drawn — and you will be — toward the new quarters where the shrouded columns still give off the heat of their own chemistry, and you find that a plywood-wrapped pier is the only warm thing for a kilometer that is not a motor, you can put your palm against it. You can lean on it a while. Do not sleep against it. It is inside an active pour zone, and things happen there in the middle of the night that are not scheduled for your benefit.

---

**Mindset**

Here is the part that is hard to pack for. The place is halfway to being what it imitates. It was built to be a basin and it is becoming a cave, on purpose and by accident at the same time, and the first stalactites are already forming at the outfall of every drip, each the size of a grain of rice. You will notice this and it will do something to your sense of scale — not the spatial scale, which you'll adjust to within an hour, but the temporal one. You are standing in a landform at the moment of its formation. The lime frost is the oldest thing here and it is a few weeks old. The oldest thing under here is being cut up and screened.

Some people find that exhilarating and some find it unbearable, and you will not know which you are until you are two kilometers in with the horns calling to each other in the dark. Prepare for either. Carry one small object that is definitively from your own world and your own time, something with wear on it, something rounded by hands. There is no rounding here. You may want to hold something that has been held.

And go slowly. Not because the place is fragile — nothing here is fragile except the moss and the fern and the three stalks — but because it is being made faster than it can be understood, and the only way to see it at all is to walk between the islands at the speed of your own feet, counting columns, listening for the rain to start walking north, putting your hand on each pier to learn whether it is cold, which means it is finished, or warm, which means it is not.
