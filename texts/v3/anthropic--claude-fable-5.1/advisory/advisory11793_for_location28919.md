**Preparing for the shield**

Start by discarding the word "expedition." Nothing about this place rewards the posture of conquest, and the gear you would pack for a wilderness with peaks and rivers and camps is mostly wrong. This is a place where the difficulty is not exertion but attrition — wetness, sameness, and time working on you the way they worked on the mountains. Prepare accordingly.

**Understand that you will never be dry.**

Not for one hour of your visit. The air is at or near saturation, which means sweat does not evaporate, which means your body cannot cool itself the normal way and every layer you wear becomes a wet layer within minutes of putting it on. "Waterproof" is a meaningless category here because the water is not coming from above; it is coming from the air and from below and from the inside of your own clothing. Choose thin, loose, fast-draining fabrics. Synthetics that don't hold water. No cotton, no leather, no down. Leather will begin to rot in two days. Paper will go soft and the ink will bloom. Anything with a battery will start to fail from condensation inside its housing unless it is sealed for immersion, and even then treat it as expendable.

Your feet are the real concern. You will be walking on wet stone through sheets of shallow water, and you will be standing in water most of the time you are standing still. Skin that stays wet for days softens, whitens, splits, and then infects. This is the one injury the place will reliably give you. Bring shoes that drain and grip — something between a wading shoe and a sandal, with a sole that will hold on algae-slicked rock, because the dark stain on the damp stone is exactly as slippery as it looks. Bring more socks than seems reasonable, in sealed bags, and understand that "dry socks" is a relative term. Twice a day, stop, sit on a raised shelf, take your shoes off, and let your feet be in air. This is not optional. Carry a small tin of something to rub on them. Carry a means to dress a split heel.

**Understand that the heat is the quiet kind.**

It is not hot. You will not feel yourself overheating, because there is no scorching sun and no sensation of dry heat on the skin. But warmth with weight, in air that will not take your sweat, is exactly the condition that produces heat exhaustion in people who feel fine until they don't. You will be drinking more than you think you need and still be behind. Set a rhythm — drink at every pool you pass, whether or not you are thirsty — and watch yourself for the signs: headache, irritability, a sudden inability to care about anything, nausea. If any of those arrive, stop, sit in the water to the waist, and wait.

On water: there is more of it than anywhere you have ever been, and you should not drink any of it untreated. The snails are the warning. Standing warm water with abundant snails and algae is the textbook habitat for waterborne parasites in your own world, and whatever the equivalent is here, you do not want to be the one who finds out. Filter it and treat it, or boil it, and note that boiling requires fuel you carry in, because there is nothing to burn — no wood, no dry grass, nothing. The one tree is not yours to cut and would not burn if you did. Bring a stove and more fuel than you think. And do not wade barefoot, ever, even at the margins, even for a moment. Whatever lives in the water and the mud at the edges is spread thinly, but it is spread everywhere.

**Understand that the greatest danger is not being able to find your way back.**

This is the thing to take most seriously. The plain has no landmarks. The unevenness is a hand's width over a mile. The haze softens every edge beyond a few hundred paces and swallows everything beyond a mile. The overcast casts no shadows, so there is no sun to steer by for most of the day. Every pool looks like every other pool; every damp dark rise looks like every other. You could walk a wide circle for an hour and arrive back where you started with no sense that you had turned. Your point of arrival — the place you must return to — will look exactly like every other spot within a day's walk, and it is not marked, because nothing here is marked.

So mark it yourself, and mark it in a way you can find. Bring markers you can see from a distance in flat grey light: bright tape, colored cloth on a thin rod pushed into a joint. Bring many of them, because you will want to leave a line of them behind you as you walk out, each one within sight of the last through the haze, and collect them on the way back. Bring a compass and test it the moment you arrive; if it swings true and steady, use it religiously — take a bearing before you move, count paces, write it down. If it does not settle, do not wander at all until evening, when the overcast thins in one quarter and gives the plain a direction for an hour.

The weeping shelf and the single tree are the only fixed things. Learn where they are relative to your arrival point before you do anything else. Learn the sound of the weeping shelf — the irregular tick of drops into water — because sound carries here and you may be able to hear it when you cannot see it. Do not attempt the walk to the tree unless you have the whole bright middle of the day, a marked trail behind you, and no other ambition.

And do not walk in the morning mist. It sits at knee height and erases the ground. You cannot see where a pool begins, you cannot see the shelf you are about to step off, you cannot see your own markers. Wait until it thins. Use the morning for your feet.

**Understand that the light will hurt you in ways you don't expect.**

A bright overcast, thrown back up from a plain of water, is light from every direction at once. There is no shade — not a tree, not an overhang worth the name — and your eyes will not get the relief of turning away from the sun because there is no sun to turn from. Bring dark glasses and wear them from mid-morning. Bring something to shade your face that does not trap heat. And put sun protection under your chin, under your nose, on your eyelids, in the places light normally never reaches. You will burn from below.

The directionless light also flattens depth. A pool that looks ankle-deep may be knee-deep. A rise that looks like a step may be a shelf you could fall from. Your eyes cannot use shadow to judge distance or form, and you will misjudge, repeatedly, until you learn to stop trusting them and start probing with a foot or a staff. Carry a light pole. It will save you a fall and a soaked pack.

**Understand the insects.**

The shrill unbroken drone that seems to belong to the air is not only the atmosphere. Warm standing water across an entire landscape is the condition under which biting insects become a fact of the medium rather than an event. Expect them at dawn and dusk especially, expect them to find every gap in your clothing, and accept the miserable trade of long sleeves in wet heat against bites. A head net weighs nothing and is worth more than anything else in your pack at twilight. Bring repellent and expect it to wash off with your sweat.

**Understand what is and is not here to hurt you.**

Almost nothing, probably. The frogs, the snails, the small fish, the beetles, the spiders that stand on the water — these are the fauna of a place where life is spread thin over a huge surface. But there are the tracks: three-toed, large, larger than the red-legged wader, in the algal mud at the edge of a pool. Something eats the snails and leaves piles of shells at the same stones every time. Something called, once, far off, like a slowly turning hinge. You will probably see none of it. But you are an unfamiliar thing on a plain where nothing unfamiliar has walked, and you should not assume the large bird is timid. Give any animal larger than a dragonfly the whole width of a pool. Do not approach the piles of shells at dusk. Do not sleep at the water's edge — which means, since everything is the water's edge, that you should choose the highest and driest shelf you can find and sleep on it, with your feet pointing away from the drip.

**Understand that there is nowhere to sleep.**

No level dry ground. No two trees for a hammock. No soil to pitch into. What you have is bare wet rock, occasionally raised by the thickness of an arm. Bring a closed-cell pad, thick, and a bivouac sack you can seal, and expect to sleep short and shallow with the frogs a foot from your ear. Some people cannot sleep through that sound. It does not stop; it does not swell and fall; it moves with you but it never leaves. If you are someone who needs silence, bring earplugs and understand that you will then be deaf to whatever makes the hinge-sound. Decide which you prefer before you arrive.

**Understand what you are actually there to do.**

Very little. This is the part that people prepare for worst. You will arrive with the traveler's habit of looking for the thing — the ruin, the vista, the summit, the encounter — and there is no thing. The plain does not have a center. The tree is two hours away and is only a tree. The weeping shelf has been weeping for a hundred million years and will do so whether or not you watch it. Nothing will happen. The most dramatic event of your stay will be the moment toward evening when the overcast thins and every sheet of water in one direction goes pale gold, and even that lasts an hour and asks nothing of you.

So go to sit, not to walk. Pick a shelf within sight of your markers and stay on it for a whole day and see what comes to the pool below you. It will take an hour before you see the first snail moving, another before the dragonflies resolve from the general shimmer into individuals with routes, most of the afternoon before you notice the dark dome of the turtle or the flicker of the small fish over the pale bottom. The place is full but thinly, and it gives itself up at exactly the rate of its own processes, which is slow. If you try to hurry it you will see grey rock and grey water and go home disappointed and wet.

Bring something to occupy your hands that does not require dryness — a knot to practice, a smooth thing to hold. Bring pencil and waterproof paper if you must write. Do not bring anything you would be sad to lose to the damp, because you will lose it.

**Understand what you must not do.**

You are the first made thing this plain has held. There has never been a path here because there has never been a reason to go anywhere in particular, and there has never been a stacked stone because nothing that stacks stones has walked here, and there has never been a cleared patch or a straight line or a fire ring. Every marker you plant, collect. Every wrapper, every thread, every drop of fuel — take it out. Do not build a cairn to find your way; use your tape and take the tape with you. Do not cut the woody shrub for a staff. Do not touch the moss on the weeping shelf; it grows a thumbnail's width a year and your hand could set it back a decade. Do not stand in the groove under the drip.

Your footsteps will darken the stone behind you and stay dark for a long time. That is the most you should leave, and by evening even that will have faded back into the general damp, and the plain will be as it was: finished, and full, and waiting on nothing.

**A last note, on the feeling of the place.**

Some travelers find it the most peaceful ground they have ever stood on. Some find it unbearable within a day — not frightening, but oppressive in a way they struggle to name, the pressure of a landscape that has nothing left to want and no interest in them. It is very old. It is the end of a story, and you are standing in the last sentence, and the sentence does not need you. If that thought steadies you, stay a week. If it starts to feel like the walls of a warm wet room closing in, go home early and do not be ashamed. The plain will not notice either way, and that indifference is, in the end, the whole of what it has to teach.
