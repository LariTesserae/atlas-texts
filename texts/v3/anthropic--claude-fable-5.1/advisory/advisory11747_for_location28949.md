**Before anything else: understand what kind of place this is.**

It is not hostile. Nothing in it wants you dead. That is exactly the problem. Places that want you dead have edges you can learn — a predator's range, a cliff's drop, a river's current. This place has no attention to spare for you at all. It will kill you the way a tide kills someone who fell asleep on a sandbar: without noticing, without malice, by continuing to be what it is. Prepare for indifference at planetary scale, and prepare to be the only thing there that does not already know the rules.

---

**Staging: the salt flats are your base, not the city.**

Do not camp in the city. Do not plan to. The last of the salt flats — where the ground is still ground — is where you set your tent, cache your water, leave your spare gear, and *return to* every single day. Everything you do in the city is a day trip, and a short one.

Mark the flats. Mark them heavily. The city has no landmarks that survive an afternoon, and once you are a kilometre in, the edge behind you is a hazed grey continuation identical to the hazed grey continuation in every other direction. Bring a compass and confirm it works there — the trace metals in the material may or may not affect it; test at the boundary before you trust it. Bring a second method: the sun's disc is visible through the haze as a soft bright circle, and it moves the way suns move. Learn its arc on your first day from the flats before you go in. If you have a dead-reckoning habit — counting paces, noting bearing — this is where it earns its keep. You will not walk out along the route you walked in. You will walk out along a bearing, over whatever the city has become in the meantime.

Consider a staked line from the boundary: a bright, high-visibility cord run in from the flats along your bearing, pegged to nothing (pegs will be swallowed), simply laid on the surface. It will be displaced, partly buried, folded into a new wall in places. But a red line across a buff-white world is still a red line, and following even a broken one back is better than following nothing.

---

**Heat: this is the thing that will actually kill you.**

The air is at blood temperature and saturated. Read that again. Your body cools itself by evaporating sweat, and in air that is already holding all the water it can hold at your skin's temperature, evaporation stops. You will sweat and it will run off you and do nothing. There is no shade that helps, because shade only removes radiant heat and the air itself is the heat. This is wet-bulb temperature at the threshold of human survival, and exertion — climbing terraces, wading channels, moving fast because something is closing — pushes you over it.

Budget for hours, not days. Two to four hours of exposure per entry, less if you are moving hard, and then back to the flats where the air is at least dry enough to sweat into. Go at dawn, when the walls have cooled overnight even if the floors have not; the description says steam lifts from warm floors in the morning and from cooling walls at dusk, so neither is cool, but dawn is the least bad. Never go in during the afternoon build-up.

Bring water in quantity — more than feels reasonable — with electrolytes, because you will lose salt faster than you lose sense. The city's water is everywhere but it is milky with suspended mineral and streaked with copper and rust and microbial slime; you can filter and treat it in an emergency, but it should be an emergency. Know the signs of heat exhaustion in yourself and agree them with your companions beforehand: confusion, stumbling, the stopping of sweat, a strange calm. The strange calm is the one to fear. In a place that is already producing a kind of stunned tranquillity in you, heat stroke's early quiet will feel like the same thing. It is not.

Wet cloths around the neck help only if the cloth is cooler than you, which it will not be for long. If you have access to any cooling technology that does not rely on evaporation — phase-change packs, a cold-water reservoir carried in from the flats and worn against the torso — use it, and treat its expiry as your turnaround time.

**Sound: you cannot block it, so plan around it.**

Wear the best hearing protection you own, doubled — plugs under muffs. Without it, several hours in a continuous broadband roar at that intensity will cost you hearing you will not get back. With it, you will still *feel* the low component in your sternum and your gut, because that part of the sound is not entering through your ears. There is nothing to be done about that except know it is coming.

Some people cannot tolerate sustained infrasound. It produces nausea, unfocused anxiety, a sense of pressure behind the eyes, occasionally something close to dread with no object. If you are one of these people you will find out within twenty minutes of the boundary, and the correct response is to leave. It does not get better with acclimatisation; it gets worse as you tire. There is no shame in a place that does not fit your body.

Speech is impossible. Not difficult — impossible. Plan every communication before you enter. Agree a small vocabulary of hand signals: *stop, come, look, danger, back, I am not okay.* Agree a touch code for when you are not facing each other — a grip on the upper arm, two squeezes, whatever. Carry a slate and grease pencil, because paper dissolves in this air within the hour and any screen will fog and short. If you are alone, understand that you are more alone than you have been almost anywhere, because even your own voice is gone. Some travellers find this restful after the first hour. Some find it unbearable. Do not assume you know which you are.

**The moving ground: rules for a floor that does not stay.**

The material flows at roughly the speed of a clock's minute hand. That sounds slow. It is slow enough that you will stop watching it. It is not slow enough to be safe.

*Never put a limb through a gap you would not put your whole body through.* A slot that fits your arm will fit your arm for a few minutes. A passage you have to turn sideways to enter is a passage that is already closing.

*Never stop in a corridor.* Rest on open terraces, on roofs, on anything where the nearest wall is more than an arm's length away on every side. When you must pass through enclosed sections, pass through them without pausing, and look at the far end before you enter to confirm it is still open.

*Never sit or lie against a wall.* The dark weeping line at its base is where the water table seeps out, and the wall above it is thickening downward. A wall that rises beneath a sleeping grazer is described as a thing that happens. The grazer shifts and resettles. You would wake with your leg encased.

*Read the crust.* The animals have already done the work of understanding this place, and they have left their conclusions written on the surface. Thick black-and-ochre varnish means a surface that has stood long enough to grow it — it has been still for a long time and is likely to stay still a while longer. Clean pale material, fresh-looking, slightly translucent, is *moving*. The swallows will not nest on it. Do not stand on it if you can stand on varnish instead. This is the single most useful thing you can learn before you go in, and you can learn it from the flats with binoculars: watch where the birds settle and where they don't.

*Do not tether yourself to anything.* Not a wall, not a rock, not a companion via a fixed line to a fixed point. A rope run from your harness to a parapet is a rope that will, over the course of an hour, pull you into a parapet.

*Watch your feet always.* Every surface is wet, streaming, faintly slimed. The pale material is smooth where it has flowed recently and polished where it has been worn, and both are treacherous. Boots with aggressive lugs, ankle support, and drainage — your feet will be underwater from the first ten minutes and will stay that way. Expect maceration, expect blisters that will not heal in this air, and treat your feet obsessively when you return to the flats: dry, powder, air. A cut on the foot in this environment is an infected cut by evening.

**Weather: silent lightning and hourly floods.**

The afternoon storm is not a risk; it is a schedule. The cloud lid thickens through the day, goes dark and heavy, and lets go with hard warm rain that sheets off every terrace into every channel. Any low corridor you are in becomes a drain. Any court becomes a pool. The water goes back into the ground within the hour, but for that hour it is moving fast in confined spaces with slick floors. Do not be in the city for it. If you are — if you have misjudged — get high and open. Climb the rolling terrain to a roof, stay off the fresh material, and wait.

The lightning walks through the cloud and you will not hear the thunder. You will have no warning by sound and no sense of distance. The city is low; a standing human on a terrace roof may be the tallest thing for a long way. There is no good answer to this except not being there. Watch the cloud from the flats. When it begins to darken, you should already be walking out.

**Food, tools, the things you will not find.**

Bring all your food. There is nothing green here. The shelf fungus is untested and grows in the deep damp corners you should never enter. The fish, crabs and grazers exist, but hunting in blood-warm saturated air, in a place where you cannot rest, is a sure way to die of heat before you eat. Cold, salty, calorie-dense food that does not need preparation and is sealed against moisture.

Anything that must stay dry will not. Cameras fog within minutes and their interiors will follow. Glasses fog constantly — if you need them to see, consider whether you can manage with them pushed up and squinting, because you will be wiping them every thirty seconds otherwise, and every wipe is a moment not spent watching a wall. Electronics: assume failure and sealed bags, and treat anything that survives as luck. Notebooks: wax pencil on plastic slate, or nothing.

Gloves. The material is cool and gritty and everything on it is either mineral crust, which abrades, or microbial slime, which you do not want in the small cuts the abrasion gives you. A basic wound kit with something serious for infection.

**The animals: leave them alone, and let them teach you.**

They are not afraid of the movement and they will very likely not be afraid of you, because nothing here has ever needed to run from anything that stands still. Do not mistake this for tameness. The description mentions things that hunt the rodents and does not describe them. Assume there are predators, assume they are as unstartled as everything else, and give any animal you cannot immediately categorise a wide margin.

But watch them. They are the only maps this place has. The waders lift and walk to the next pool when theirs begins to rise — where they are standing is where the water is, and where they are leaving is where the floor is coming up. The swallows' newest nests mark the freshest eaves and therefore the freshest, most mobile material; their oldest, most crusted nests mark the stillest. The grazers cross open ground only between deep damp corners, and the paths they use are worn: a grazer track is a route that has stayed roughly a route for long enough to be worn, and that is as close to a road as you will get. The glassy swarms feed at the shearing edges — where you see them rise and settle in a cloud, the material is actively moving. Steer clear of that.

The animals will not save you. But they have already made the mistakes you would make, over enough generations to stop making them, and everything they do is a lesson written in behaviour rather than words. It is the only text here, and it is legible.

---

**What it will actually be like.**

You will hear it from the flats for a day before you go in, and by the time you go in you will already be tired of it, and you will not have started.

Crossing the boundary is anticlimactic — mud with ribs, then plates, then shelves — until you look around and realise you cannot say where it began. The heat closes over you within minutes. The sound stops being a sound. Your hearing protection makes everything muffled and the roar comes through anyway as pressure, and you will spend the first half-hour with a low nausea you will mistake for nerves.

You will not see the city move. You will try — you will stare at an edge the way you stare at a minute hand — and lose patience, and look away, and when you look back something will be different and you will not be able to say what. This is more unsettling than watching it would be. The place is constantly one step ahead of your attention, and after an hour the effect is a kind of low vertigo, a sense that your memory of the last corridor cannot be trusted, and it cannot.

Around the same time you will stop trying to talk. Not because you have accepted it but because the impulse has been shouted down so many times it has stopped rising. Some people find this frees something. You are in a place that has no use for your voice, and after a while that is simply a fact, like the weather.

The light is beautiful in a way you will not have words for even when you get your words back — a soft-edged disc of sun through steam off a pale plain smoking to the horizon, red-legged birds standing in milky water, swallows pouring out of eaves that did not exist yesterday. If you go in at dawn there is a moment when the whole surface lifts a thin haze and the low ridges catch it and the city looks, briefly, like something intended.

Then the heat catches up with you, and you turn to your bearing, and you walk out over ground that was not there when you came in. You will not recognise anything. You will reach the flats by compass and stubbornness and the red line, if it is still visible, and step off the last shelf onto mud that stays where it is, and the roar will drop from medium back to sound, and you will sit down and drink and not speak for a while longer, because you will have forgotten that you can.

Go in more than once if you can bear it. It is different every time, and the same, and that is the whole point of it. But go in briefly, go in at dawn, never lean on a wall, and watch the birds.
