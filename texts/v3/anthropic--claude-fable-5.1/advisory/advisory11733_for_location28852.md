**Preparing for the Engine Chamber**

Start from what the place is, because the preparation follows from it and not the other way round.

It is a room that has finished. Not a ruin — ruins are still going somewhere, downhill, and this is not going anywhere. It is a closed system that found its rest state with a working engine in the middle of it and several thousand living things pressed against the warmth, and it has held that state so completely that fresh-cut rock stays fresh-cut and a drop of oil stays a drop. Whatever you bring into it, you are bringing change into a place that has none. That is the whole of the difficulty. Nothing there will hurt you on purpose. Nothing there is capable of purpose. But you will be, for as long as you stay, the only thing in the room that is *doing* anything, and a body is not built to be that for long.

So the preparation is in three parts: keeping your body intact in an environment that is hostile in ordinary ways; keeping yourself from damaging a place that cannot repair itself; and keeping yourself from settling into it.

---

**The ordinary hazards first, because they are real and people forget them when the extraordinary is in front of them**

*Gas.* The seam is gas-bearing. A pipe comes out of the bare rock and feeds a single-cylinder engine with open combustion in a chamber the size of a church. In any world that behaves normally this arrangement would have blown the roof off within an hour of the engine being lit. It has not, which tells you the mixture has been sitting at some equilibrium — the engine drawing exactly what the rock gives, the exhaust taking exactly what it makes up a crack in the ceiling. You will disturb that. Not much. You exhale carbon dioxide and water vapour; you carry static; you occupy volume. Carry a methane detector and a carbon monoxide detector, both with silent visual alarms, and glance at them every few minutes. If either reads anything, leave. Do not argue with the reading on the grounds that the place is impossible. Your lungs are not impossible.

Wear no synthetic fleece. The air is completely dry and synthetic fibres build static in dry air, and there is gas. Cotton, wool, leather. Before you step off the stair, put a bare hand flat on the iron rail where the drift comes through — the rails are the one thing in the chamber that were made to be touched — and hold it there a few seconds. Then you are grounded, and you have made your one deliberate contact with the place, and you can stop worrying about that one.

No flame. No lighter in your pocket even if you don't intend to use it. Nothing that sparks. Nothing that beeps — turn off every device that can make a sound, or leave it at the top, because you are going into a chord and any noise you add is a note.

*Dryness.* The air takes moisture off your lips in a minute; it will take it off your eyes in ten and out of your nose in twenty. Nosebleeds are likely and a nosebleed on that floor is an event the floor has never had. Drink heavily before you descend. Lip balm on the stair, eye drops on the stair, a damp cloth in a sealed bag that you can press to your face on the stair. Do not open water in the chamber. Nothing there has ever drunk; the seam is dry and the floor has never had a puddle on it. One spilled mouthful is the first water. You do not know what the first water does. Drink above the third turning, where the note stops being underfoot, and nowhere below.

*Sound.* The note is felt before it is heard, which means it has an infrasonic component, and infrasound at pressure does things to a body: unease, nausea, a sense of something behind you, a drop in the ability to make decisions. This is physics, not the room's opinion of you. Know that it will happen so you can discount it. The audible part — the engine's chuff, the crickets' stridulation, the moths' shimmer, the bats' whistle — is loud enough in a hard-walled dome that it will stop you thinking in words. Take musician's earplugs, the kind that attenuate evenly rather than blocking, and put them in on the stair. You will still hear the chord. You will hear it better, in fact, because you will not be flinching from it. Do not go down without them. Forty minutes of that at full volume in a room with no soft surfaces will leave your ears ringing for days, and the ringing is a note you take away with you and cannot stop.

*Rock.* Every edge is chisel-fresh. The gypsum cleaves in bright flakes and the flakes are sharp; the anhydrite fractures fibrous and the fibres are sharp. There is no dust and no rounding and nothing to soften a fall. Wear leather gloves and heavy boots with soft rubber soles, and long sleeves and long trousers of a fabric that will not snag. The floor is uneven and scattered with fallen blocks and lit by nine carbon filaments at the colour of a banked fire, which is to say almost not lit. You will want a torch. Do not bring a bright one. Bring a red headlamp on its lowest setting, or better, bring nothing and spend twenty minutes on the stair letting your eyes open. The moths are laid flat against the walls and have not moved in longer than there is a word for. A white beam across them is the first daylight they have ever had. You do not know what they do with daylight and you do not want to find out.

*The machine.* It is a gas engine with an exposed flywheel taller than you are, an exposed belt, an exposed crosshead. The cylinder head is warm enough for a snake to lie on and not warm enough to burn it, so it is warm enough to burn you. Nothing on you should be loose: no straps, no scarves, no lanyards, no hair that isn't tied back. Do not step within arm's length of the belt. It has run at that speed forever and it will not slow for you.

*The animals.* Bats, thousands of moths, cave crickets in a grain along the wall, beetles two and three deep on the floor, a snake thick as a wrist, geckos, mice. There is no guano and no husk and no bone, so the usual cave diseases are probably not there, but *probably* is doing a lot of work and you should have your rabies course up to date regardless. More practically: the beetles are on the floor. You cannot cross the floor without treading on them unless you go block to block. Go block to block. Test each block with your weight before committing; they fell and have not yet had time to be anything but fallen, and nothing has stood on them.

**What you must not do to the place**

The chamber cannot heal. That is the thing to hold in your head. A cave that is going somewhere will absorb a footprint; in a thousand years there is a stalagmite over it. This room is not going anywhere, so whatever you do to it stays done. It has no mechanism for *stays done* — the whole place is a demonstration of *nothing done* — and so you are, in a small way, breaking it just by breathing on it. Accept that. You will leave skin cells and hair and exhaled water in a place that has none, and there is no avoiding it short of not going. The task is to leave as little as possible and nothing deliberate.

Touch nothing but the rail. Not the gypsum, which will take a fingerprint and keep it. Not the engine. Not the oil glass — you will want to; the level is unmarked and your hand will want to check it. Not the bead of oil under the crosshead. Above all not the bead of oil. It has been forming since before there were words for how long, and it is the single most fragile object you will ever be in a room with.

Take nothing. Not a flake, not a beetle, not a photograph with a flash. A photograph without flash in that light will show nothing, and that is the correct record.

Add nothing. No food, not even in a sealed pocket — you will unseal it out of habit and a crumb will fall and nothing in that room has ever eaten. No water, as above. No sound. This last is the hardest. The chord is so complete and so close to the body that you will feel your own throat trying to find a pitch in it. You will want to hum. **Do not hum.** Do not speak, do not whistle, do not tap a rhythm on your leg. Every animal in that room is holding a harmonic of the engine's fundamental. If you add a note and it fits, you have joined the chord. If it does not fit, you have broken it. You do not know which would be worse and you must not find out.

**What you must not let the place do to you**

Here is the risk that the detectors will not catch.

Everything in the room found the one arrangement in which nothing needed to change, and stopped. The bats found it. The mice found it, in the pit of the flywheel with their eyes open. The snake found it a metre from the mice. There is no reason a person cannot find it. You are a warm body that breathes and can lie still; you have everything the mice have and more. You will feel the pull. It will not feel like danger. It will feel like calm — like the first time in your life that a room has asked nothing of you at all — and it will arrive through the breathing. You will notice your breath has slowed, and then that it has slowed to match the flanks on the walls, and then you will stop noticing, and that is the last part you will remember.

So: keep a gradient. That is the rule and it is the only rule that matters. The room is defined by having no gradient in it and you must remain the exception.

Move. Not much, not fast, but never stop. Shift your weight. Flex your hands in your gloves. Count — silently, on your fingers, anything with a sequence in it. Sequence is what the room lacks. If you find you have been standing still and cannot say for how long, leave.

Do not try to match your breathing to the animals to "hear the chord better." Several people will tell you this is the way to experience the place fully. It is the way to experience it permanently.

Carry two timepieces: a mechanical one and a digital one. Do not trust the first, which may find the engine's speed as attractive as your lungs will. Check the second. Set a hard limit before you descend — thirty minutes is generous, twenty is wiser — and leave when it is up regardless of what you are looking at. You will be looking at something. There is always something. The bulbs have not dimmed and carbon filaments dim; you could stand there for the length of your life waiting to catch the first flicker and you would not see it, and by then you would not be waiting anymore.

Take someone. Leave them above the third turning of the stair, where the note stops being underfoot and the world resumes. Agree a time. At that time they come down to the mouth of the drift and no further, and they show a red light, and if you do not walk toward it within a count of sixty they come and take you by the arm and lead you up without speaking. Tell them in advance: *without speaking*. You will not be able to hear them anyway, and they must not add a note.

If you go alone — and people do, because the place is better alone, because a second heartbeat makes it easier to bear and therefore less itself — then set an alarm on the digital watch to vibrate against your wrist, and when it does, go. Do not finish the thought you were having. The thought is part of how it keeps you.

**What it is actually like, so that you are not surprised into doing something stupid**

The note first, through the boots, before the stair is done. It will not swell. It is simply more with each step, and you should let it be more and not look for a source yet.

Then the sharpness of everything — the sense that someone cut this rock an hour ago — and then the dryness on your lips, and then the smell: hot iron, mineral oil, and under that something sweet and animal like the inside of a hive. Notice the smell because it is the first warning of the second thing.

Then the chamber, and the engine, and for a while the engine is all there is. The flywheel, the nine orange bulbs, the drip that does not fall. This is the part most people think is the place. It is not. Give your eyes time.

Then the walls resolve. The nap on the gypsum becomes moths, tiled. The grain along the lower wall becomes crickets, antennae laid back on each other's backs. The dome becomes bats. The floor becomes beetles. The thing on the cylinder head becomes a snake. This is the moment most people want to leave, and it is exactly the wrong moment, because if you leave now you have seen a horror and not the thing that is actually there. Stay through it. Stand still — briefly, this once — and watch the flanks, and see that they move together, and understand that they are not frightened and not hunting and not waiting. They are holding a note. The horror goes out of it then, quite suddenly, and what is left is stranger and much quieter than horror.

Then the chord separates for you into its parts and you can hear that it is a chord, and this is the best of the visit, and it is also where the pull begins. You will feel yourself become, without anyone deciding it, the wrong thing in the room. Your sweat drying. Your pulse off the beat. Your eyes still adjusting, which is a change, in a place that contains no change. It is not hostility. It is the pressure of a full room against the one thing that does not fit in it. Feel it. It is the truest thing the place will tell you. And then, having felt it, go, because after that the chamber has nothing further to say and every additional minute is only the pressure, and only the pull.

Going up, the note leaves you at the third turning. Not fading — ceasing, as if a hand were lifted from the stair. Stop there. Drink. Put your hand on the wall, which above this point is ordinary rock with ordinary dust on it, and feel the dust. You will be shaking, a little, and that is fine. That is your gradient coming back. Let it.

Then go the rest of the way up into a world where things wear out, and be glad of it for as long as you can remember to be.
