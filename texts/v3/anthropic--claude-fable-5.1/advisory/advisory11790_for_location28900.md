**Preparing for the Hollow**

Start by accepting what kind of place this is. It is not dangerous in the way a mountain or a city is dangerous, and you will be tempted, on that account, to treat it lightly. Don't. It is a small, slow, closed system that has reached terms with itself over a very long time, and the main risk you pose is to it, not the other way around. Most of what follows is about how to be there without being a wound in it.

**Your body will be damp the whole time. Plan for that first.**

The temperature drops a coat's worth on the way down and stays there, and the air holds moisture the way a cellar does. You will not get rained on. You will simply never be dry. Everything that touches you — your collar, your cuffs, your hair, the strap of your bag — will take on a faint clinging weight within the first hour, and it will not give it back until you climb out.

So: wool, not cotton. Cotton in this air becomes a cold rag against the skin and stays that way. Wool warms wet. Bring more of it than you think a short stay needs, because nothing you take off will dry while you are there. An outer layer of waxed cloth or oilskin against the drip from the eaves and the beading from every leaf you brush. Buttons and ties rather than zips; metal zips will seize with fine rust in two days, and plastic ones grow slick and stiff in the cold.

Your feet need the most thought. The paths are firm and glossy, like an old wooden floor — they grip a smooth sole well and shed a lugged one, and a lugged boot tears the soft edges where the path meets the cushion. Rubber boots with a plain sole are right. Inside them, thick wool socks, and at night — this matters — take the boots off, dry your feet as best you can with a cloth you keep in a sealed bag for the purpose, and put on the one pair of socks you have not let get wet. Skin that is never dry softens and splits and is slow to close; small cuts from the stem edges will not heal while you are there, so cover them. A little powder for the feet is not vanity.

Keep everything you need to stay dry in oiled or sealed bags: your dry socks, your food, your notebook, any small mechanism you care about. Metal here beads on contact with the air and rusts as far as it is going to rust. Oil your knife before you go down and again each evening. A watch will fog from the inside. Anything electrical will run for a while and then not, and there is no fixing it there; bring a small light because the nights are truly dark, but expect to lose it, and do not rely on it.

If you have a chest that reacts to mould or spores, think hard before you go. Below the living surface the mat is centuries of dead stem going spongy, and when a path is cut back or the retting troughs are turned there is a rich, sour breath from it. The people are used to it. You may not be.

**The ground is not ground.**

Nobody has reached the bottom of the mat. In the center, where the pump house stands, it is soft enough that the engine's every stroke moves through it and into your feet from the far rim. Treat every step off a kept path the way you would treat a bog: not as a surface but as a suggestion. It will hold you — the cushions spring back, the liverwort is slick but solid — but you cannot know where a soft place lies, and if you go through, there is nothing under you but more of the same, wet, for a depth no one can name.

Beyond the safety of it, there is the courtesy. The kept paths have been trodden into that floor-like firmness over a century or more. Where you leave them and cross a cushion, you leave a print that stays, and the place closes it at a finger's width a day. Six steps off the path is a mark someone will look at for a month. Stay on the paths. When paths fork, take the one that shows wear; the other may be being let go, deliberately.

Do not step on the pipe casings, though they look like great roots and look like they would hold you. Do not lean on the frames that hold the nets. Do not hang anything from them to dry. The nets are the water, and the water is everything.

**Clean yourself before you go down.**

Scrape your boots. Brush your coat. Empty your pockets of seeds, crumbs, burrs, the small grit that any traveler carries between worlds. This hollow is a handful of plant kinds living on their own dead, in balance with a fog and a pump, and it has almost no animals — some snails, a beetle, a cat that is now a story. You do not know what a foreign seed does in a mat that has never had to compete, or what a mouse in your baggage would do to a place with nothing to eat it. Bring no living thing. Bring no food that smells strongly or crumbles. Eat over a cloth and take the cloth out with you.

Do not pick a leaf. It seems a small thing. There are no surfaces here that are not leaf.

**Arrive in daylight, and learn the clock.**

Come down the rim in the afternoon, when the mist has been gone for hours and you can see the whole bowl and the paths through it. Never try to enter in the mist. Two hours before dawn it rises out of the mat and by first light it fills the hollow to the lip like water, and inside it you cannot see the trough you are about to step into or the beam that is about to come down. If you wake in it, stay where you are. Listen to the engine — it sounds closer in the fog, and it is the one fixed point — and wait. It thins from the top.

Then, when the sun clears the rim, be somewhere you can see across. There is about an hour of long low light and hanging drops when every leaf in the hollow turns, slowly, toward the east, and the whole place leans. You can watch it if you are still. This is the reason to have stayed the night. Do not talk during it. Do not walk during it. You will be able to feel the roofs lift with the first warmth, and the walls of whatever building you slept in will begin the long creaking that goes on all day, a ship at anchor. By noon the hollow has leaned back. By evening the roofs have settled. The mist is coming again.

One full turn of this — afternoon, night, dawn, morning — is the right length of a visit. The hollow is two hundred paces across. There is nothing to explore that you cannot see from the rim. What there is, is the cycle, and you should see it once entire and then go, up over the eastern rim with the fibre if they will let you ride the handcart, or on foot at dusk so you can turn at the top and see the chimney's thread of steam be the last lit thing.

**Sleeping there.**

You will be offered, or will find, a place inside one of the woven buildings. The light inside is green and even; the walls sweat. Put your groundsheet down first, then your bedding, and expect the underside of the groundsheet to be beaded through by morning. Expect a drip. The roofs shed most of the condensation to the eaves, but not all, and it will find you. Sleep with your face covered.

Then there is the pulse. One stroke every four or five seconds, all night, the soft heavy knock coming up through the mat into your spine. Some travelers cannot sleep the first night. Some sleep better than they have in years. Nearly everyone finds, afterward, that they have brought it home with them for a while — a phantom beat in the floor of a quiet room. There is nothing to prepare for this except to know it is coming.

**The people.**

You will see one at a time. Someone at the nets. Someone up a ladder trimming pale shoots off the beam. Someone with a curved blade walking a path edge. They do not call to one another across the distances and they will not call to you. This is not coldness. They know where each other are by the work that has been done — the net rehung, the path cut, the beam trimmed — and if you want to be known, that is how: not by announcing yourself but by being seen to do no harm, and after a day perhaps by carrying something, or holding a ladder, or laying cut stems onto a path where you are shown.

Do not offer to help with the engine, the nets, or the blade until you are asked, and do not be hurt if you are not asked. Do not trim anything. Do not straighten anything. Do not ask whether the engine was built this way or repaired into it. You will want to; the question sits in the mind like a stone in a shoe once you have seen the cam lobes that are burls and the connecting pin that is a knot. The answer you will get, if you get one, is that it runs, and that is a true answer and the only useful one. Let the categories go. You are not there to sort it.

If you want to bring something, bring what the hollow cannot grow. Files and whetstones — the blades are steel, the burls are finished by hand, and there is no iron here that is not already spoken for. Salt, perhaps. Good needles. Nothing that grows. Nothing that eats. Ask before leaving it; there may be a place where such things go.

**The works.**

The engine is slow and this makes it seem safe. It is not. A flywheel that turns once every few seconds still weighs what it weighs, and a beam coming down at the end of its stroke will not notice you. Keep a body's length from any moving part unless you have been placed nearer by someone who tends it. The pump house floor near the rising main is the softest ground in the hollow.

The retting troughs at the east side are full of stem rotting in pumped water, and the smell is honest and the surface is not something to lean over. Do not drink from them. Drink from the nets or from the rising main if you are shown where. The condensed water is as clean as water gets.

The rail line runs iron rails on stem sleepers up over the rim. Do not walk on the sleepers, which are living and take the weight of the cart but not the tread of feet, and do not walk in front of the handcart on the downgrade.

There is fire in the pump house — the chimney's mouth is blackened, the exhalation is steam — but there is fire nowhere else that you will see, and you should make none. Every wall is fuel. No pipe, no candle, no flame of any kind outside the place where flame already lives.

**What it will be like.**

Small. Quieter than you expect, then not quiet at all once your ears open to it: the pulse, the drip in irregular time, a chain somewhere, a shutter, the roofs working. Cold in a way that gets into the joints rather than the skin. Green in every direction and every kind of green, from the black-green of wet liverwort to the pale new shoots on the beam. You will feel, after a few hours, the particular sensation of being somewhere inhabited but not occupied — that the place is busy in every part of itself and none of that business is for you or about you, and that it will go on being trimmed and fed and pumped and rotted into thread exactly the same whether you stand there or not. Some travelers find this a great peace. Some find it very hard, the closedness of the loop, the sense of a thing that needs nothing. Know which you are likely to be, and if you are the second kind, stay one night anyway, for the leaning at dawn, and then leave without feeling you have failed something.

Take a pencil, not a pen; ink runs and paper goes limp but a pencil will still write on it. Write down the sound of the engine while you are there, because the words for it will not come back to you once you are out in the dry country and the ground under you has stopped breathing.
