**First, understand what you are actually visiting**

Strip the wonder off it for a moment and look at the physical facts, because they are what will kill you if anything does.

This is a cold, deep, alpine lake at 2,000 metres, with a bottom temperature a few degrees above freezing in every season and a surface that in the warmest month is still cold enough to hurt. It has a soft, nearly continuous ceiling a hand's breadth under the surface that you cannot reliably break through from below. It is acoustically hostile for roughly half of every daylight hour. It contains a community of an unknown species who build on a timescale of centuries and have, as far as anyone can tell, never met a person who arrived from outside.

Everything else — the green light, the hall of pillars, the domes that breathe — is real and worth the trip. But you should plan the trip as a cold-water overhead-environment dive at altitude, into an inhabited place, and only *then* let yourself think about how beautiful it is.

---

**Getting to the rim**

The lake is a day's walk above the last trees. That means a day's walk above the last firewood, the last windbreak, and the last easy water you didn't have to melt or filter. The granite bowl will be exposed to wind constantly — the description says wind is the *only* weather, which is not the same as saying there is little of it. Expect cold nights even in high summer, sun that burns fast through thin air, and no shade anywhere.

Plan for a minimum of three nights on the rim: one to recover from the approach and let your body settle to the altitude, at least one full day at the lake, one to pack out. You will be carrying diving equipment up a mountain. Be realistic about that. Every kilogram you bring to the rim you bring back down. If you can arrange a base camp lower, where there is fuel and shelter, and make the final ascent with only what the water requires, do that.

Bring a stove and all your fuel. Bring a tent that can take wind from every direction, and rock to anchor it, because there will be no soil to stake into. Bring more warm clothing than you think you need for *after* you get out of the water; the hour after a cold dive at altitude, standing on windblown granite in a wet drysuit, is the most dangerous hour of the whole visit.

Two thousand metres is not extreme, but it is enough to give some people headaches, poor sleep, and reduced judgement, and it changes the arithmetic of diving. Your dive computer must be set for altitude. Your no-decompression limits will be shorter than the depth suggests. If you are not already comfortable with altitude diving theory, learn it before you leave, not on the rim.

---

**The cold, which is the real problem**

Four degrees at the bottom. Perhaps eight to twelve at the surface in late summer. This is not wetsuit water for any useful length of time. You want a drysuit with serious undergarments, a thick hood, and dry gloves — and you should accept that even so, your hands will be the first thing to fail. Cold hands cannot manage buckles, cannot signal well, cannot work a valve. Plan every dive so that nothing critical depends on fine dexterity after the first twenty minutes.

Cold shock on entry is real. Get your face wet at the edge before you commit. Breathe deliberately. Do not enter the water already tired or already cold from the wind.

Assume forty minutes is a long dive here, and that two dives in a day is plenty. The temptation will be to stay — you will want to stay — and the cold will make the wanting feel like a reason. It is not. Set a hard time before you go down and honour it. Have a warm, wind-sheltered place ready to change in when you come up, with dry clothes laid out and hot water already made.

---

**The ceiling**

This is the thing I would drill into anyone going: **you cannot count on surfacing where you are.**

The plant's plates lie a hand's width under the surface across the whole green part of the basin. They are soft, they are described as a second ceiling, and a diving bird parts them — but a bird has a spear-shaped head and knows where the seams are, and it enters from above where it can see them. From below, in an emergency, with cold hands, you may push up into a yielding green mass that does not open. Treat the plant's canopy exactly as you would treat the roof of a cave.

That means: enter and exit at the margins, where the columns thin and the bottom is bare granite sloping up to open air. Know where those margins are before you go deep. Carry a reel and lay a line from the shore into the town if you are going to swim any distance among the columns; visibility is excellent, but the pillars all look alike and the light changes as the afternoon comes on. Carry two lights. Keep the granite edge in your mind as the only real surface.

Do not, under any circumstances, try to get air by ascending under the plates and breathing whatever gap you can force. If you need air and you are in the middle of the town, the nearest surface is the shore, not up.

---

**Breathing, and the chambers**

You will bring your own gas, obviously. The question is what kind of rig.

Open-circuit scuba — an ordinary tank and regulator — exhales a stream of bubbles that rise and pool under the plates and, if you are anywhere near a chamber, may find their way into a dome that a family spent a summer filling with oxygen and now will have your nitrogen and carbon dioxide in it. Beyond the rudeness, there is the noise: in a basin that hears everything, a regulator is a shout. If you have access to a rebreather and the competence to use it in cold water, it is the better choice here. If not, go open-circuit, stay clear of the buildings, and accept that you are announcing yourself with every breath.

About the chambers themselves: **do not enter one on your first visit, and do not assume the air in them is breathable for you.**

They belong to someone. The mouth at the floor, the bell of still green air, the sweating walls — this is a dwelling, or a workshop, or a place of rest during the loud hours. Swimming up into one uninvited is walking into a stranger's bedroom, and you will be breathing their air and leaving your own.

Even if you are somehow invited, be careful. The plant leaks oxygen into a sealed dome. Nothing in the account says what *else* is in there. If the gas is mostly oxygen and the chamber sits at any real depth, the partial pressure could be high enough to cause convulsions within minutes — and convulsing in a bubble under a lake is fatal. Carry an oxygen analyser. If you ever put your face into chamber air, test it first, stay minutes not hours, and keep your regulator in your hand. The inhabitants may breathe it comfortably. You are not them.

---

**The noise**

Nothing prepares you for this properly, so at least know it is coming.

In the morning it is the char drumming — a throb you feel in your chest more than hear. In the afternoon the plant vents, and the whole basin turns into a frying pan. It builds for hours. It does not stop for you. Water carries sound so well that there will be no direction to it, no source; it will simply be *everywhere*, and you will not be able to hear your own exhaust or your buddy's rattle or anything else. Signals must be visual or by touch. Agree on them before you go in. Keep your buddy within arm's reach in the loud hours, or — better — do not be in the water in the loud hours at all.

The inhabitants go inside in the afternoon. Take the hint. Dive in the morning, when the light is coming in slant and green and the drumming is a low companionable thing, and be out on the granite by the time the fry begins to rise.

Occasionally a column shifts and the whole lake groans like a ship. It is not an earthquake. It is not aimed at you. Hold still and let it pass.

---

**Moving through the town**

There are paths. They are worn into the root-mat by centuries of feet — actual feet, walking, which tells you the builders move along the floor at least some of the time. There is a lot of open water between the columns and between the buildings, and that spacing is not an accident; the account says so twice.

Match it. Stay in the open water. Keep a column's width between yourself and any structure. Do not rest your hand on a wall to steady yourself, do not fin along a seam, do not settle onto the root-mat outside a chamber mouth. The pale fresh wounds where scoring was done this year are living tissue under management — a young arch being taught to bend — and a careless fin against one could undo a decade.

The root-mat gives underfoot and springs back. If you must touch down, do it on an existing path, gently, and think about how much you weigh with your gear. Better to hang neutral in the middle water, where the char hang, and simply look.

Do not take anything. Not a snail, not a fragment of softened column, not a bone from the shore. There is almost nothing to build with here, and everything that exists is accounted for.

---

**The animals**

They are not afraid of you, which is unnerving in its own way. Char will drift within touching distance. Newts on the arches will not blink at you. The eels in the dead columns come out at dusk and you will not be there at dusk, because you will be on the shore getting warm.

The bird is the one to watch. It comes through the ceiling once or twice a day, a stone with a spear on it, and works the basin with its eyes open. It has probably never seen a thing your size and shape underwater. It is probably not interested in you. Do not be between it and the char. Do not be directly under the spot where it enters. If you see the plates part above you, hold still and watch it go by.

The amphipods and snails grazing the columns are harmless and constant. You will hear them before you notice them.

---

**The people, if they are people**

Nobody knows what they are. They work stone and bone, they lash cord from plant fibre, they persuade stems into arches across a hundred years, and they spend the loud afternoons under domes of their own oxygen. They may be mammalian, in which case they are astonishing to survive that cold; they may be something closer to the newts. They may have language you can hear. They may communicate in ways that are lost in the drumming.

What you can be sure of is that they will know you are there the moment you enter the water, because the basin hears everything, and that they have been doing what they do for a very long time without visitors.

So arrive the way the lake arrives at things: slowly, from the edge. Go in at the granite margin, in the morning. Move a little way into the columns and stop. Hang in the open, in the light, where you can be seen from anywhere. Do nothing. Let the drumming go on around you. If nothing approaches, that is a complete first visit; come out, get warm, try again the next morning. If something does approach, let it come the last distance. Keep your hands visible and empty. Do not follow it home.

Bring nothing to give. A gift of metal or plastic into a place built entirely of one living material is not a kindness; it is litter with a story attached.

And hold in your mind, the whole time, that the spacing between the columns is the spacing between the buildings is the spacing between the people. Whatever else this town has decided, it has decided how close things should be. Respect the distance. The most generous thing an outsider can do here is take up very little room and leave very little behind.

---

**Timing**

Late summer. The surface is as warm as it gets — which is not warm — and the ice is months off. Winter is out of the question: the surface freezes, the plates are locked under it, the ice grinds at the margins where you would enter, and the sound coming down through the water is described as fierce and metallic. Spring may still have snow on the rim and meltwater in the shallows. Early autumn is possible but you are gambling on the first freeze.

Give yourself several days at the lake. One dive will overwhelm you. The place only starts to make sense on the second or third morning, when the drumming has become familiar and you can begin to see the difference between an old arch and a new one, a living column and a softening dead one, a path and the bare mat beside it.

---

**What to carry (a summary)**

For the mountain: wind-resistant tent, rock anchors, stove and full fuel, water filter, sun protection, the warmest dry clothes you can justify carrying, a first-aid kit that includes something for altitude headache and something for cold injury.

For the water: drysuit and thick undergarments, hood, dry gloves, boots suitable for both fins and standing on granite; a rebreather if you can, otherwise open-circuit with a cold-water-rated regulator and a redundant second stage; altitude-capable dive computer; two lights; reel and line; a slate for writing, because there will be no talking; an oxygen analyser; a knife that you carry and do not use; a surface marker you will *not* deploy inside the plant, because it would foul the plates.

For yourself: a hard rule about when to come out. A companion. Patience of the kind the place itself has, or as much of it as a warm-blooded animal with a finite air supply can manage.

---

**What it will be like**

You will come down off the granite into water that takes your breath, and the light will turn green, and after the first few metres the sound will arrive — the throb in your chest first, and under it the endless sigh of the grazers, and you will realise that the silence of the surface was a lie the lake tells to anyone who doesn't look under.

Then the columns. Far apart, farther than you expect, so that you keep drifting toward one and finding another twenty strides beyond it, and the floor between them soft and lit and empty. Then a shape among them that is not a column: an arch that took a hundred years, a dome with a low dark mouth. You will want to go in. Don't.

You will hang in the open and the char will come and look at you with their pale blunt faces and go back to their drumming. Something the length of your forearm will watch you from a crook in the arch and not move. The light will shift as a plate above you lifts and settles. And you will understand, in your body before your head, that this place is not waiting for you. It found its distance long before you came and it will hold that distance long after. You are the diver bird: something that came through the ceiling, that the lake has room for, that it will let pass.

Be that. Come through, take nothing, go back up. Get warm. Sit on the rim in the wind and watch the dark disc of water give nothing away, and know what is frying softly beneath it in the sun.
