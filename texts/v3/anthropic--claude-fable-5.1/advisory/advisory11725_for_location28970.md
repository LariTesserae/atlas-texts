# Preparing for the Bright Basin

## First, understand what kind of place this is

It is not hostile. Nothing here will hunt you, poison you on contact, or collapse on your head. But it is a place that will quietly wear you down along three axes you may not think to defend: your eyes, your sense of time, and your footing. Almost everything below follows from those three, plus one more — that you are walking through someone's infrastructure, and they are not there to tell you the rules.

Treat it as high-altitude glacier travel crossed with a walk through a stranger's water treatment plant, in a world where nothing has ever learned to be afraid of you.

## Your eyes

This is the thing to take most seriously, because the description undersells it. Light "from everywhere at once" with no shadows is the visual condition of a whiteout, and whiteouts hurt people not by blinding them but by removing depth. Without shadow you cannot tell whether the pale rock ahead is level, a step up, a step down, or the lip of a fracture with a fern crown drawn over it like a lid. The eye needs contrast to build a floor under your feet, and this place gives it almost none.

Add altitude — "cold bright altitude," the narrator says — and a white sky that is almost certainly scattering ultraviolet from the whole hemisphere, and you have the recipe for photokeratitis: snow blindness. It does not require snow. It requires a bright diffuse sky and a few hours of unprotected exposure, and the pain arrives late, after the damage. The narrator's "your eyes give up" is a pupil at maximum constriction for a whole waking cycle, which produces headache, nausea, and a dullness of judgment that feels like calm.

Bring:

- Glacier glasses or wraparound sunglasses with side shields, dark (category 4 if you can), and a second pair. Losing your only pair here is a genuine emergency.
- An eye mask for sleep. It does not get dark. Ever.
- Lubricating drops. The air is humid but you will be squinting and blinking less, and the light itself dries the surface of the eye.
- A hat with a brim, though it does less than you'd hope — the light comes from below as much as above.
- Sunscreen for skin, high SPF, applied to the undersides of your chin and nose and ears. Reflected UV burns the places the sun never usually finds.

Then learn to walk differently. Use a trekking pole not for balance but as a probe: tap ahead of every step in vegetated ground. You will feel edges you cannot see.

## Your footing

The rock is new, and new rock is a blade. Fracture faces that have never been rained on keep the edge of a broken plate. A slip here is not a scrape; it is a laceration, and possibly one dusted with mineral grit that has not yet decided what it is.

- Gloves at all times when hands are near rock. Leather or reinforced synthetic, not thin liners.
- Boots with stiff, thick soles. The kind of sharp that cuts a hand will also work through soft rubber over a day.
- Gaiters or tough trousers. The ferns are gentle; what the ferns hide is not.
- A wound kit heavier than you would normally carry: irrigation syringe, plenty of gauze, closure strips, a tourniquet you know how to use. Nothing here can help you.

Understand also that the ground is moving. A hairline crack becomes a fingertip's width in two cycles. The ground rings underfoot. This is a landscape mid-lift, and however slow the tilt, slabs under tension release. Do not camp in the seams between slabs, however inviting the shelter. Do not camp under overhanging fracture faces. Choose the middle of a slab, or the top of a cushion-plant mound if you can bear to — the plants are dense enough to hold you, and they are at least not rock. If you feel the ring underfoot change pitch, move.

## Time

The light does not change. There is no sun to cross the sky, no dusk, no stars. The only clock the basin keeps is the cool dip when the water beads, and that lasts an hour. Left alone in this, a human body drifts. You will sleep too little or too much, eat at the wrong intervals, and — this is the real danger — lose track of how long you have been here. The narrator stood still for a long time. The narrator says "you do not stay" as though it were a decision that had to be made deliberately, against something.

- Bring a timepiece that does not need the sky and does not need to be dry. Two, ideally.
- Set a departure time before you enter. Write it down. When it arrives, go, regardless of how much you feel you have not yet understood.
- Keep a log of cycles. Mark each condensation dip. Count them. Trust the count over your sense of things.
- Eat and sleep on a schedule, by the clock, not by hunger or tiredness, which the constant light will scramble.

The place also does something with its stillness. There is no wind, no sound, no motion but your own. Anechoic silence is known to produce disquiet in people within an hour — you hear your own blood, your joints, the tick of your jaw. Some find it peaceful. Some find it unbearable. You will not know which you are until you are in it, so bring something that makes a small sound you control: a wind-up music box, a set of beads, a book to read aloud even though the words will stop a meter from your mouth.

## Water

There is water here and none of it is easy. It never rains. The only water is the condensation that comes once a cycle, films every surface, pools fingernail-deep for an hour, and is gone.

Carry in as much as you can bear. Then, to extend it:

- Bring a large sheet of smooth, non-absorbent material — a tarp, a groundsheet, a sheet of thin plastic — and rig it during the warm part of the cycle, tilted toward a container, so that when the dip comes it beads and runs. Fabric will not do; it holds the water and the plants will take it back. You need something that sheds.
- Bring a cloth you can wring, and use it on the fern fronds during the dip. It is slow and undignified and it works.
- Bring a filter and a means to boil. The water smells mineral and metallic because it has been sitting on fracture faces that have never been leached. It may be fine. It may carry dissolved metals in quantities a body should not have for long. Filter, boil, and do not make it your only source for more than a few days.

Do not take water from the column footings. The temptation is enormous — it is the one place in the basin where water stands — but that water is the reason the whole place exists. It is being collected by someone, for something, and the pipe leaves the footing on its slow crawl toward whatever is downhill. Taking from it may be theft, may be pollution, may be nothing at all, and you have no way to know. Leave it.

Understand too that cold humid air lies about thirst. You will not feel dry. You will lose water anyway, through breath at altitude and through the constant slight effort of a body squinting and tensed. Drink by the clock, like everything else.

## Cold, damp, and altitude

Cold, not freezing — the water beads, it does not frost. But cold enough that every cycle every surface you own will be wet. Your sleeping bag, your clothes, your food, your instruments.

- Everything in dry bags. Everything. Then a second layer for electronics and paper.
- Synthetic insulation, not down, which dies in damp.
- A shelter that vents condensation from inside as well as sheds it from outside; you will be breathing a cycle's worth of moisture into it.
- Layers you can peel during the bright part and put back for the dip. The change is only a degree or two in the air, but it is enough to chill wet cloth on skin.
- If you come from low ground, spend time acclimatizing before you cross the ridge. Breathlessness, headache, and a dull persistent nausea will present themselves here as the light's fault, and you will not think to treat them as altitude until it is worse.

## The machines

Twenty grey columns, finned, each four stories tall, each fed a thin wire from the line that crosses the basin, each draining into a glass-sheathed pipe. They do not hum. They vibrate below hearing.

Treat them as you would treat live electrical infrastructure that you do not understand, because that is what they are.

- Do not climb them. The wire that enters the top carries something. The fins stay wet longest of anything in the basin. Wet metal and unknown current is not a thing to test with a body.
- Do not stand at the footing for long. The narrator felt "a pressure on the eardrum, a sense of something enormous turning very slowly." Infrasound at that intensity — enough to feel through a hand on the metal — is known to produce in humans a specific and unpleasant cluster of effects: unease, nausea, a feeling of being watched, a sense of dread without object. If you feel any of these near a column, it is not the place speaking to you. It is the machine. Walk away from it and the feeling will lift.
- Do not open, cut, tap, or lean on the pipes. They carry the product. The trestles that carry them over fractures are not bridges for you.
- Do not touch the wires or the masts. Obviously.
- Do observe them. They are the only things here that tell you which way is down. Every pipe runs toward the notch in the lower ridge. If you are lost — and you can be, in a basin without shadows or sun — find a pipe and follow it, and you will reach the place where they all converge, which is the one landmark that cannot move. Do not go through the notch. Whatever is down there is the factory, and you have no invitation.

The columns are also the only large structures that break the visual field. Use them for navigation. Count them from the ridge before you descend, note which is nearest which wall, and you have a rough grid to walk by.

## Sound and separation

Sound dies here. Your voice goes a short distance into the brightness and stops. This is not poetry; it is a description of a still-air environment full of soft absorbent vegetation with no reflecting surfaces at useful angles. Shouting will not reach a companion fifty meters away. A whistle will do a little better. A referee's whistle, held long, will do better still, but do not count on it.

If you travel with others:

- Keep line of sight always. Agree on hand signals before you enter.
- Bring a mirror or a bright cloth for visual signaling; there is no shortage of light to flash.
- Agree that anyone separated goes to the nearest column and waits. The columns are visible from everywhere.
- Do not rely on radio without testing it first. The wires overhead carry something, and you do not know what it does to a signal.

If you travel alone, understand that no one will hear you fall.

## Food, and the living things

Bring every calorie you will eat. There is nothing to hunt, nothing to gather that you can trust. The plants have never been eaten by anything — there are no insects, no grazers — which means they have never needed to be edible, and also means they have never needed to be palatable. Some may be harmless. You have no way to know which, and the mineral-rich new-rock soil they grow in can concentrate metals in leaf tissue. Do not experiment.

The bird did not move when touched. The snail may or may not have been alive. Whatever lives here has never met a thing that moves fast, and has no fear reflex. You could pick the bird up. Do not. It is possibly the only one, and you do not know what it is for.

Also consider what you bring in. You are a warm animal covered in microbes, carrying seeds in your boot treads and pollen in your hair, entering a place with no animals and no insects and a plant community that has never had to compete with anything that arrived on a foot. Brush your boots before you cross the ridge. Shake out your clothes. It is not enough, but it is what you can do. And when you leave, brush them again; spores from a place this wet and this green will want to travel with you.

## What to leave at home

A camera that cannot handle full-scene overexposure; you will get white frames. Anything that needs a shadow to read — sundials, obviously, but also your habit of judging time by light. Down. Cotton. Fear of the dark; there is none. Curiosity about the notch in the ridge; leave that too.

## What the place will actually do to you

You will spend the first hour squinting and the second hour walking too fast, because it looks flat and open and safe. Then you will stumble once, over an edge you did not see, and slow down for good. You will find the silence very large. You will reach the first column and put your hand on it and feel the turning and take your hand off quickly, and a minute later you will not be able to say why you felt uneasy. You will rig your tarp badly the first cycle and get half a cup and rig it better the second and get a liter. You will lose track of cycles by the third if you have not been counting.

And you will stand still. That is the thing the narrator warns about without warning about it. The basin is built to make you stand still — bright, silent, windless, full to the brim with slow growth and slow drinking and slow making. The stillness is not a mood. It is the physics of the place, and it will settle over you the way the water does, once a cycle, a little more each time.

Set the clock before you enter. When it goes, cross the second ridge with the brightness on your back, and do not turn around more than once.
