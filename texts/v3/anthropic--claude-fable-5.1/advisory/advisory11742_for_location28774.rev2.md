**Before anything else: decide how long you are going to stay, and decide it here, where the air is thick enough to think in.**

This is the single most important preparation, and it has to be done before you arrive, because the plateau will take the decision away from you once you are on it. Not by force. By patience. Everything there happens at a pace that makes waiting feel like the correct response, and the air is thin enough that waiting will also feel like the only response you have the energy for. You will sit down on a sill to watch the dust fall and you will not notice that you have been there long enough for the frost to have moved. Set a duration. Write it down. Then halve it.

## The body

Take the height seriously, and take it as a medical problem first, because it is one.

The air is thin enough to hurt. Read that as: you will be hypoxic from the moment you arrive, and hypoxia does not announce itself as suffering. It announces itself as contentment, slowness, a pleasant reluctance to bother. It makes you stupid in a way that feels wise. On this plateau, where stupidity and slowness are the ambient conditions, you will not be able to tell where the place ends and your failing brain begins. Bring supplemental oxygen and use it on a schedule, not when you feel you need it—you will not feel you need it. If you can acclimatize in a high place beforehand, do. If you have access to the drugs that help with altitude, take them starting the day before. Know the signs of fluid on the lungs and swelling in the brain: a wet cough, a headache that will not shift, staggering, confusion. If any of these appear, you leave. Not soon. Then.

The cold is the second problem, and it is a strange one. The air is not cold—there is too little of it to be cold with any conviction—so you will not feel the cold as cold. You will feel it as tiredness, and later as a kind of thickness in the hands. The heat leaves you by conduction, through whatever you touch, and everything you might touch is stone that has been waiting a very long time to be warmed. Do not sit on the bare stone. Do not lean against a wall. Do not lay your hand on a sill to steady yourself and leave it there. Bring a closed-cell pad and sit on that, if you sit at all. Insulate your feet from the ground as if you were on ice, because as far as your body is concerned you are. Wear layers meant for still, dry cold rather than wind—the wind is there but it does not seem to touch anything, and it is the stillness that will hollow you out.

There is no water. None. Not a drop to be found, not frost that will melt, not dew, not a seep. The frost comes away as a powder and the powder does not fall, and you should not try to eat it, drink it, or breathe it. Carry every drop you will drink, and carry more than you think, because at this height you lose water simply by breathing—litres of it a day, exhaled into air that takes it and gives nothing back. Your lips will split. Your nose will bleed. Your eyes will feel like they have sand in them. Bring lip balm, saline, and a scarf to breathe through. Drink on a schedule from a bottle you keep inside your clothing, because outside your clothing it will become something you cannot drink.

Do not plan to cook, or to light anything. There is not enough air for fire. Bring food that needs no preparation and no water, high in fat and sugar, and eat it whether or not you are hungry. You will not be hungry.

Protect your eyes and skin from the sun as though it were fierce, even though it will look weak. The dimness is a lie of some kind—the light arrives spent, the description says, and perhaps it does—but there is nothing between you and the sun to take out what burns, and you should assume the worst. Dark goggles. Full cover. A brimmed hat. Your eyes will want to adjust to the dimness and open wide, and that is exactly when you want them shielded.

## The light, and the dark

Every surface is lit from one side and black on the other, and the black is complete. A shadow there is not a dim place. It is a place where seeing stops. If you step into one, you will not be able to see your own feet, the ground, a drop, a step, or the wall you are about to walk into. Carry a strong handheld light. It will work—light goes where it is pointed there, and does so with unusual honesty—but it will not fill anything. It will show you a bright spot and nothing around the spot. Move slowly. Test the ground with a pole before you put weight on it, especially anywhere near a threshold, because the thresholds are worn into dips and the dips are always on the shadowed side.

Do not use shadows to keep time. The sun does not move, or moves without travelling, and once in a while the shadows are somewhere else. If you need to know where you are relative to the sun, look at the sun. It is small and hard and has no glare and you will be able to find it. Note its direction against the rim when you arrive. It is the only fixed thing you will have, and it is not fully fixed.

Bring a watch that winds. Not one that relies on the sun, or on a signal, or on a battery you expect to behave. Set your turnaround by the watch. The plateau has no days, only a condition, and you will lose all sense of how long you have been there within the first hour. Check the watch more often than feels reasonable and write the time down each time in a notebook, with a pencil—ink fails in the cold and the dryness. The notebook is not for memory. It is for evidence, later, of how much time you lost.

## Sound

You will not hear your own footsteps. You will hear them from somewhere else, a little late, and sometimes what arrives is not yours. Know this before you go so that the first time it happens you recognize it rather than turn around.

Do not use sound for anything. Do not call out to a companion. Do not whistle. Do not clap to test an echo. Nothing you make will arrive where you want it to, and it may arrive where you do not. If you are travelling with someone—and you should be—agree on hand signals beforehand, and agree that you will never be more than an arm's length apart. Rope yourselves together. This is not overcaution. The streets change width. A companion at the far end of a street may be at the far end of a shorter street when you look again, and you want to be attached to them when that happens.

Carry a small mirror for signalling across distances. Light travels there honestly. Sound does not.

And when your footsteps arrive from behind you, and stop when you stop, and then there is one more: do not turn around to look for it. Note it in the book. Keep walking.

## Doors

There are rules here, and they are rules about doors.

Do not stand in a doorway. This is the one thing you should treat as absolute. The pale animal stands in doorways, and something hunts it, and the hunting is done by shortening the street until the doorway at the end arrives. You do not want to be the thing in the frame when the street closes. Cross thresholds without pausing in them. Do not rest in them, do not shelter in them from the dust, do not use them to frame a view.

You will not enter buildings through their doors. You cannot—every door opens onto the plateau, from the inside. Instead you will be walking, and then you will be inside, and there will be a door in the middle of a wall. When this happens, leave. Immediately, through that door, without crossing to the far wall to see how far it is. The room is larger than it looks, the far wall has a habit of arriving, and the dust on the floor has never been walked on and you will not be the one to change that. You are not exploring. You are in something that has a way out, and you are taking it.

Do not stand at the far end of a long straight street. Do not stand in the middle of a long straight street either, if you can help it; walk along the edge, near a wall—not touching it—so that if the street shortens you can step sideways out of its line. The hunting is done along the length. You want to be off the length.

If you see the pale thing in a door, do not approach it. Do not photograph it, if you have brought something to photograph with—it will take time, and you will be standing still in a street looking down its length at a doorway, which is precisely the posture you have been told not to hold. Look at it once. Note the two shadows. Then move on and do not look back to see if it has moved. It has.

## Navigation

You cannot navigate here, and the sooner you accept that the safer you will be.

Your feet leave no mark in the dust. Your landmarks are impermanent—a tower may be where a gap was, a crossroads may have grown a street. The wind, if it is wind, moves the dust in sheets that will cover anything small you set down, and the dust is the city's own stone, and what you leave may not be there when you return, or may be there and be stone. Chalk is pointless. Cairns are pointless. Painted marks may work or may be scoured to nothing by the time you look for them.

What you can do:

Stay near the rim. The rim is the one edge that does not appear to move, and the cloud-floor beyond it is unmistakable. Keep it in sight at all times. Do not go so far in that you lose it. If you want to see the towers, look at them from the rim. If you want to see the crossroads, choose one within a few hundred paces of the edge.

Fix the sun against the rim when you arrive, and check that bearing every time you look at your watch. If the sun has moved without travelling, note it, and treat every bearing you have taken since the last check as suspect.

Lay a line. Carry as much light cord as you can bear and pay it out behind you from a fixed point at the rim, weighted at intervals. It may not survive. But a cord is the one marker that is continuous, and a broken cord tells you something a missing cairn does not.

Count paces, and write the counts down. Not because the distances will be the same on the way back—they will not—but because a count that has changed is information, and you want information more than you want comfort.

If you are lost, walk toward the sun's fixed direction until you reach the rim, then follow the rim. It is very long. It is also the only path that goes somewhere.

## Things not to touch

The frost. Its powder does not fall, and you do not know what it does when it is inside you. It has a tide, and where it has been the stone is paler; do not walk on the paler stone if you can avoid it, and do not walk on the stone that is waiting for it either.

The grey-green crust on the north faces. It may be alive in some minimal sense and it may not, and it favours shade and avoids frost and spreads along the grain, and none of that tells you what it would do on skin or in a lung. Do not scrape it. Do not breathe near it. If you want to see the pattern that looks like writing from below and like a map from above, use a lens, from a distance, and understand that you will not be able to read it, and that if you could it would probably be a map of somewhere you are standing.

The dark disc at the bottom of the basin. It holds a reflection, and the sky in it is bright. You are a traveller between worlds and you will feel this in your chest as a way. It may be one. It may not. It is at the bottom of the one place on the plateau where the erosion works downward, which is to say it is at the bottom of the one place that is a hole. Look at it from the rim of the bowl. Do not walk the rings down. Do not step on it. If you intend to go through, that is a different visit, and a different preparation, and you should make it with your eyes open and your affairs in order. This document is for coming back.

## The mind

You will feel watched, and you will feel that the watching is not hostile, only very old and very unhurried, and that is more or less accurate. You will feel that the city is a memory of a city, or a city that has not yet been remembered. You will feel grief without an object. You will want to sit down.

Prepare for this the way you prepare for cold: by knowing it is coming and by having a schedule that does not depend on how you feel. Move on the watch. Eat on the watch. Take oxygen on the watch. Speak to your companion by hand on the watch, even if only to confirm that you are both still there and both still counting. The plateau's danger is not that it will do something to you. It is that it will do nothing, at length, and let you do nothing with it, and the air will make nothing feel like enough.

Bring one thing that is loud and bright and fast and entirely yours—a photograph, a piece of music you can hear through bone if not through air, a taste that is sharp. Use it when you notice that you have not noticed anything for a while.

---

## The deadline

The cloud is rising.

Slowly, the way everything there is slow, but the floor of the world is coming up toward the plateau, and when it reaches the rim the plateau will have weather again, and moisture, and the frost will do something it has not done in a length of time not available to thought, and the city will no longer be the settled form. You do not want to be there for that. You do not know how long it will take. It may be centuries. It may, given how time behaves there, be an afternoon.

Watch the cloud. Mark its height against the rim when you arrive—a particular ledge, a particular tower. If it has risen visibly by your first check, halve your time again. If it has risen visibly by your second, leave.

---

## Leaving

Do your leaving at the rim, in the sun, in the open, with the cloud-floor in front of you. Not in a room. Not in a doorway. Not in shadow. Not in a street. Not in the bowl. Wherever your method of departure takes you from, make sure it is a place that is only stone and sky and nothing framed, nothing enclosed, nothing that has a far end.

Your footsteps will arrive after you have gone. That is all right. Let the city keep them. It has room.
