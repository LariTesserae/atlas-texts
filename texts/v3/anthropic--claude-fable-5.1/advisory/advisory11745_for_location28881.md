# Preparing for the Saddle

## First, understand what kind of place this is

This is not a dangerous place in the way a glacier or a volcano is dangerous. Nothing on the shelf wants to hurt you, and nothing on it will chase you. The danger is almost entirely one of tempo. The shelf does one thing, slowly by the standards of a landslide and very quickly by the standards of a mountain, and the whole art of visiting it is knowing exactly how fast that is and staying just ahead of it.

Everything that follows comes down to this: **the ground you are standing on is being replaced from below, continuously, and it will accept you as readily as it accepts a newt.** It is not malicious. It has no opinion. That is precisely the problem — you cannot argue with it, so you have to keep moving.

Read the hare into your bones before you go. The upper half of a hare, cast at a run, on the fourth terrace. Whatever happened to it happened while it was doing something ordinary.

---

## The approach

The saddle is high, above the tree line, and remote — fifty kilometers of sparse country in any direction. Prepare for it as you would for any serious alpine day: acclimatize beforehand if you're coming from low ground, carry more water than you think you need (see below on why), and bring full cold-weather kit for the approach and descent even though the shelf itself is warm. The transition between the two is where people get hurt.

The northern scree is honest scree. Loose, sharp, frost-split. Boots with real ankle support and a stiff sole, gaiters, gloves for the inevitable hand-placements on rock that will cut you. Trekking poles help on the ascent and are nearly essential on the way down, when the scree will be wet with dark silty runoff from the shelf and behaves worse than it looks.

Watch the two peaks. Their faces are described as cracked and pale, and they lean over the saddle. Whatever the shelf is doing to the local air — warming it, saturating it — is also happening to old rock that spent millennia adapted to cold and dry. Freeze-thaw cycling at the boundary between warm wet shelf and cold old mountain may be more aggressive than it used to be. Do not linger beneath the steep faces. Do not camp under them. Treat rockfall as a live possibility, especially at night and in the early morning.

---

## The line

You will reach a point where the scree simply stops and the rock becomes dark, smooth, and warm. It is described as clean as a tidemark. Stop here. Genuinely stop, on the old side of it, and let your body adjust.

This is your last uncomplicated ground. Take off your pack here. Eat here. If you are going to sleep at all, sleep here or further back — not one step forward.

Establish this as your base and do not let anything you own drift across the line unattended. A pack set down on the shelf for forty minutes is a pack with a rock foot. A tripod left through lunch is a tripod forever. Everything you carry onto the shelf stays in your hands or on your body, and you check yourself on that discipline every few minutes because the place is fascinating and you will forget.

## Footwear and the hour rule

The new stone is soft as chalk for roughly its first hour, and then it is stone. The film of water on the surface goes cloudy and settles into rock and a new film slides over it. This is happening everywhere on the shelf at once, at slightly different rates — faster toward the highest, central terraces, slower toward the older northern edge where lichen has begun to take hold.

Practically: **do not stand still.** Not for a conversation, not for a photograph, not to watch the lip of the terrace thicken — though you will want to, and you should, kneeling, briefly, then rising and shifting your weight. Shift your feet every minute or two as a habit. Lift each boot and put it down somewhere else. You'll feel the beginnings of adhesion as a slight tackiness, a reluctance, and that is your signal that you have already waited too long.

Your boots will scale up. The mineral water wets them, the load precipitates, and within an hour the crust is genuinely hard. Bring a stiff brush or a blunt knife and scrape your soles and welts every time you cross back to the old rock. Do this before the crust sets, not after, or you will be carrying stone home on your feet and your boots will be heavier and stiffer every day. The same goes for pole tips, the hem of your trousers, anything that touches the wet.

Smooth-soled footwear is a mistake. The dark rock is wet and glass-smooth where it is fresh and you will slide. Good lugs, and even then, expect to move like someone on a wet boat deck.

The lower terraces hold thin sheets of standing water full of newts. You will step in these. Waterproof boots, and step carefully — the newts do not move until you are nearly on them.

## Water and air

This is the wettest dry place you will ever visit. Mist beads on everything, your clothes will be damp within minutes, water runs off in every direction, and none of it is safe to drink. It carries the dissolved mountain — silica, lime, iron, and whatever else the old rock is made of, which up here is anyone's guess. Some of that will be metals you do not want. **Carry all your drinking water in.** Filters will clog with silt and will not remove dissolved minerals. Do not fill a bottle from the runoff to "boil later"; boiling concentrates it.

The smell is described as something like a cave and something like a struck match. That second note is likely sulfur compounds. In open air on a windy ridge this would be nothing, but the saddle sits under a permanent cloud lid and the wind has to go around. The air on the shelf is probably not well mixed. If you get a headache, if your eyes sting more than mist would explain, if you feel unusually tired or slow-witted, walk back to the old scree and sit in moving air until it passes. Do not push through it. Do not descend into any hollows or low pockets on the shelf where heavier gases could sit.

Everything electronic and optical will fog. Bring lens cloths and expect to use them constantly. Silica-rich water leaves a haze on glass that does not wipe off — keep camera lenses capped when not shooting, and accept that whatever you use on the shelf will come away with a permanent film.

## The sound

The hum is not subtle. It comes up through your boots and sits in your back teeth. If you have metal dental work, you may feel it more than others do; this is not dangerous but it is disconcerting and worth knowing in advance. Over an hour, most people stop noticing the hum and start noticing its absence when they step off.

The cracks are another matter. They are sharp — like a branch breaking in hard frost — and they come without pattern from anywhere on the shelf. You cannot predict them and you will flinch at every one for the first hour. Soft foam earplugs take the edge off without deadening you to the rest of what's happening, and you should bring them and use them if you plan to be on the shelf for more than a few hours. Sudden loud reports over many hours are wearing in a way that sneaks up on you.

The account says the pattern starts to feel like breathing and then stops feeling like anything you can name. Take that seriously as a description of a mental state, not just a nice sentence. Extended time in a space with no directional light, no sunset, constant low sound and irregular sharp sound, and no reliable visual horizon does things to attention and time-sense. Wear a watch with an alarm. Set it. When it goes off, walk back to the line, sit down on old rock, drink water, and check that you still know what time it is and where your feet are.

---

## Light and time

Under the cloud, the light is dim and directionless at noon and becomes simply *less* toward evening. There is no sunset, only a dusk that goes on and then is night. Your body will not track the day correctly. Bring a headlamp and spare batteries; bring the watch; decide before you go up how long you will stay and hold to it.

The lenses on the three posts are the last things to hold light, from no clear source. This is beautiful and it will draw you toward them in the dusk. Don't go. Navigating the terraces in the dark, on wet rock, with a soft new layer forming underfoot, is how someone ends up as the second hare.

---

## The objects

The spindle, the glass ring, the three lensed posts. They are cool. They do not hum. They are being swallowed by the stone they seem to be producing, and the best available guess is that they are pegs rather than engine — the things that hold the process in place rather than the process itself.

Do not touch them. Not out of superstition, but because nobody knows what they are pegging down, and the account of a tent held by pegs should be read to its conclusion: pull a peg and something moves. The shelf is already redirecting the wind of two valleys and drawing their moisture into a permanent cloud. That is a great deal of atmosphere already committed to whatever this is. You do not want to be the person who finds out what the uncommitted version looks like.

The lenses show a flat absence — not a reflection, not the sky. People will tell you to look into them. You may. Do not look for long, and do not look through them in the sense of trying to see what is on the other side. There is no evidence that this is harmful. There is also no evidence about anything to do with them, and "no evidence" is not the same as "safe."

## The animals

Leave them alone. That should go without saying, but the shelf is so crowded with life at an altitude that usually has none that people forget the normal rules.

The choughs roost along the terrace lips at night because the lips hold heat. If you are near the shelf at dawn — and you should try to be, once — stay back on the old rock and let them lift. Do not approach the roost in the dark. Hundreds of birds taking off at once from ground level around your feet, in near-blackness, on wet stone, is not something you want to be inside.

The heron takes newts and does not care about you. Give it distance; it is the only one of its kind up here and it has clearly worked something out that you haven't.

Watch the fox. It comes to the edge, onto the old scree, and does not cross the line. It has been watching this longer than you have and it has made a decision. You are not obliged to agree with a fox, but you should at least know that one animal that could go onto the shelf, chooses not to.

## What you leave

Every step you take on the fresh terraces is a record. Not a metaphor — a record, sharp as the moment it was made, sealed under the next layer and the next, a meter down by next season, untouched by wind or weather for as long as the mountain stands. Your boot-prints will outlast every other thing you have ever done.

Decide before you go up how you feel about that. Some people find it unbearable and cannot walk more than a few meters in. Some find it the only reason to go. Neither is wrong, but you should find out which you are before you are standing on the second terrace with the choughs' feet all around you and the hum in your teeth.

If you intend to leave a deliberate mark, know the material limits. Paper smears — the water gets to it first. Anything organic will be a shape, not a thing. Metal, glass, and ceramic hold; a coin or a ring set on the highest terrace is gone into the stone within the hour and will be there, presumably, when the peaks are gone. Scratching into the fresh surface works only in the first hour, and only with something harder than chalk, and then only if the next layer arrives before the water softens your marks — the timing is not fully in your control. A hand outline scratched at the right moment will last. A hand outline scratched at the wrong moment will be a shallow blur, and you will not know which you got.

Leave nothing that you would be ashamed to have found. The shelf does not forget and it does not edit.

## Overnight

Do not sleep on the shelf. This is the one absolute rule.

If you want to stay the night — and hearing the shelf climb in the dark is by all accounts worth it — camp on the old scree well back from the line, away from the peak faces, on the north side where the runoff channels can be seen and avoided. Expect your tent to be damp in the morning from the mist that spills off the shelf. Expect the ground under you to stay cold while the air over you is warm and wet; this is a strange combination for the body and you will sleep worse than you think. Expect the cracks to be more frequent and to wake you.

Watch for the warm runoff meeting cold old rock at the margins. In the night the edges of the shelf may glaze, or the wet scree may ice where the warmth doesn't reach. Test every step in the dark.

---

## Coming down

The warm shelf and the cold mountain do not blend. You will step off ground that is body-temperature and wet into wind that has come off the high peaks, and you will be soaked to the skin from hours in the mist. This is the most dangerous moment of the whole visit. Change into dry layers at the line before you descend. Have them in a dry bag inside your pack. Do not underestimate how cold you will get once the shelf's heat is behind you and the wind finds you on the scree.

Scrape your boots. Clean your poles. Look at your pack straps and your trouser hems for grey crust and get it off while it's still chalk.

Then go down. The runoff makes the northern scree treacherous — dark, silty, slick over sharp rock. Poles, deliberate steps, no hurry.

---

## The part nobody can prepare you for

There is no why. Nobody has offered one. The shelf is building more shelf, not toward a wall or a vessel or anything with a shape, just higher and wider as though healing a wound the mountain never had. The objects may be the whole apparatus or only the part that happened to land here. It may stop when the saddle is level. It may not.

Most places you visit as a traveler give you something to hold — a history, a purpose, a story you can carry away. This one gives you a fossil bed in which nothing is old, a hare that is still running, a hum that does not stop, and a set of your own footprints going down into the dark at a meter a season. You will come away with the smell of warm minerals in your clothes and a haze on your lenses and a feeling that is hard to put into words, which the account itself notices: the pattern that stops feeling like anything you can name.

Give yourself a day at low altitude afterward before you go on to anywhere else. Not because you will be harmed. Because you will want to sit still for a while, on ground that isn't going anywhere, and let that be enough.
