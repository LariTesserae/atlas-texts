**Before anything else: understand what kind of place this is**

It is not a lake with a town in it. It is a single organism that has, over centuries, been persuaded into rooms, by a small number of people who have been persuaded by it into a certain pace. Everything about your visit should follow from that. The plant does not hurry, the builders do not hurry, and the dangers here are almost all the dangers of a human hurrying in cold water under a ceiling. If you go, you go slow, you go far apart from things, and you leave when the cold tells you to, which will be sooner than you want.

The other thing to fix in your mind before you set out: this place has found its distance and is holding it. A dozen strides between columns. Thirty chambers where there were once more. There is no sign that anyone there regards the shrinking as a loss. You are an addition. Even a careful one is a change to a thing that stopped changing before anyone was watching. Go, if you go, as briefly and lightly as you can, and be ready for the possibility that the right amount of visiting is a day on the rim and an hour in the water.

**Getting there and staying alive on the shore**

Two thousand meters is not extreme altitude, but it is enough that a lowlander will feel it, and the things you are planning to do in the water — hold your breath, get cold, exert yourself — all get worse with thinner air. Spend a night or two higher than you live before you go, if the route allows it. Do not arrive at the rim and go straight in.

The trees are a day's walk downslope. That means there is no wood at the lake: no fire from local fuel, no windbreak but boulders, nothing to rig a shelter to. Carry a stove and more fuel than you think you need, because rewarming after cold water is the whole game and hot water is how you do it. Carry a tent that can take wind, because wind is the only weather they have and it will have the whole bowl to run in. Pitch in the lee of the biggest boulders you can find, away from the shore, and expect frost at night in any season — there is old snow on that shore in late summer, which tells you what the nights are like.

Sun at that height, off water and old snow, is fierce. Bring glacier glasses and cover your skin. Do not wear sunscreen into the water — this is a small closed alpine basin with one organism in it and no reason to think it has ever met an oxybenzone molecule. A hood and a suit will cover most of you anyway. What skin is exposed, accept burning or shade with cloth.

Water: the lake is drinkable in the way high lakes are, clear and cold and nearly sterile, but it is full of snails and one enormous plant, so filter or boil. Take it from the bare granite shallows, not from among the columns. Do not wash in it. Do not put soap in it. Do your latrine business well back from the rim, in the granite, and pack it out, because there is no soil here to take it and the lake is downhill of everything.

Go in late summer. The ice will be off, the surface will be as warm as it ever gets, which is still cold enough to make your teeth ache, and the days will be long. Do not go in winter. The plates make a ceiling a hand's breadth under the water; add ice on top and there is no surfacing anywhere but the bare shallows, and the sound of that ice coming down through the water is described as fierce and metallic, and you do not want to be under it when it splits.

**The water: cold is the thing that will kill you**

Everything that follows is secondary to this. The bottom is a few degrees above freezing in every season. The surface in late summer is perhaps eight or ten degrees, cold enough to hurt. This is not water you can be in for long, and it is not water you can be in at all without a plan for how you get warm again.

Cold shock comes first: the gasp reflex when your face goes in, the racing heart, the minute or two when your breathing will not obey you. Enter from the bare granite shallows, slowly, face last, and wait it out before you go anywhere. Never let the first shock happen under the plates.

Then the slow theft. In water this cold you have perhaps forty-five minutes in a very good wetsuit before your hands stop working, and your hands are what part the ceiling, find the chamber mouths, work your line. You will not notice them going. Set a time before you enter, tell your buddy, and get out when it comes regardless of what you are looking at.

Gear: a thick open-cell freediving suit, seven millimeters or more, two-piece with an integrated hood, plus gloves and socks of the same, plus a weight belt to counter all that neoprene. This is a cold-water freediving kit and it is miserable to put on and it is the right thing. A drysuit is warmer and you may want one for lying on the surface at the edge looking down, but it is nearly impossible to freedive properly in, and freediving is what this place is scaled for.

About scuba: consider it honestly and then probably don't. The plates make this an overhead environment, which is to say a cave dive, with everything that implies about training, redundant air, guidelines, and the rule of thirds. Your bubbles would pool under the ceiling in silver sheets and you would be a roaring, clanking thing in a place whose inhabitants breathe by swimming home. And you would have to carry the tanks a day above the trees. If you are cave-trained and determined, do it at the margins and with a line; otherwise leave it.

Have a rewarming station ready on shore before you go in: dry clothes laid out, sleeping bag open, stove lit, water heating. When you come out you will be clumsy and stupid and cold and you must be able to strip and dress and drink hot sugar water without thinking. Get out of the wind. Get into the bag.
