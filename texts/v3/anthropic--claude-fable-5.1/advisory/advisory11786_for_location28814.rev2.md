# Preparing for the Plateau

Start with the honest version: this is not a place that will try to kill you. It is a place that will kill you incidentally, without noticing, the way a floor kills a dropped cup. Nothing there is hostile. Almost everything there is indifferent in a way that produces the same outcomes as hostility. Prepare accordingly.

I'll go through this roughly in the order the place will hit you.

---

## Before you go: your body

**The air.** You are going somewhere higher than most things have any business being, and you will arrive without having climbed. This is the single most likely way for the visit to end badly in the first two days. Ordinary altitude sickness — headache, nausea, sleeplessness, the sense that your thoughts are arriving a half-second late — is nearly certain. The dangerous forms (fluid in the lungs, fluid in the brain) are possible and can kill inside a day.

If you have any control over your arrival point, arrive on the slope, not the top. The tableland rises so gradually that a person walking up it does not know they are climbing; use that. Take three or four days to walk the last of it. Your body will acclimatize without you having to think about it, and you'll reach the level ground already half-adjusted. If you must arrive on the top itself, plan to do almost nothing for the first forty-eight hours except lie down, drink, and monitor yourself. Bring acetazolamide if your world has it, and start it a day before you leave. Bring dexamethasone as an emergency measure and know the signs that call for it. Know that the only real treatment for serious altitude illness is *going down*, and on this plateau, "down" is days away in every direction. That is worth sitting with.

Be aerobically fit before you leave. Not strong — fit. You will be breathing fast the whole time, and everything will cost more than it should.

**The warmth.** This is the one mercy of the place, and it changes the whole calculation. At that height you would expect to be fighting cold; instead the ground is warm as a sleeping animal and the air, though thin, is mild. You are not going to freeze. You *are* going to be wet — constantly, totally — and wet skin in moving air loses heat, so don't dress for a beach. But you can leave the expedition-grade insulation at home and bring, instead, layers of wool or synthetic that keep some warmth when soaked. No cotton. No down; it will be a wet lump within an hour and stay that way.

## The sound: this is the real preparation

Read this section twice.

The plateau is a landscape-sized organ, tens of thousands of shafts each ringing its own note, driven by a wind that never settles. The sound does not live in the ears. It lives in the sternum, the sinuses, the teeth. People who live there go deaf young and do not consider it a loss. Believe them. You will *not* get used to it in the sense of it becoming comfortable; you will get used to it in the sense that your hearing will degrade until it bothers you less.

**Bring the best hearing protection that exists in your world, and double it.** Deep-insertion foam plugs under over-ear muffs rated for industrial noise. Bring spares of the plugs — many spares, in waterproof containers — because they will get wet and lose their seal and fall out and roll into a shaft. Put the protection on before you can hear the plateau, and take it off only in the deepest part of a shelter, and then only briefly.

Understand what the protection will and won't do. It will save the hearing you have left when you go home. It will *not* stop the low end. The pressure component — the moaning, pulsing, subsonic part — passes straight through plugs and through your ribcage. Expect a set of symptoms that you may not immediately connect to the sound: nausea that isn't altitude, a sourceless anxiety, chest tightness, a feeling that the ground is moving when it is not, fatigue out of proportion to what you have done. This is your body being played like one of the shafts. Some people are more susceptible than others; you will not know which you are until you are standing in it. Give yourself permission to have a bad reaction and to leave early because of it.

**Plan to be functionally deaf the whole time.** With protection in, you will not hear a companion shout from two paces away. Without it, you will not hear them either. So:

- Work out a hand-signal vocabulary with your companions *before* you arrive. Not a full language — a working set. Stop. Come. Danger ahead. Hole. I'm hurt. Water. Go down. Look. Yes. No. Practice until it's reflex.
- Bring writing surfaces that work wet. Wax tablets, slate and soft stone, waterproof paper with a pencil (not ink). Tether them to yourself.
- Learn to talk the way the locals talk — a hard forced sound from the base of the throat, meant to be felt in the chest by someone pressed against you, not heard. It works at arm's length with hands on each other's shoulders.
- Expect that when you get home, the silence will be its own problem for a few days. You will hear the plateau in every quiet room. Tinnitus is very likely; some of it may be permanent. Decide now whether the trip is worth that, because it probably is a real cost.

There is a stranger thing to prepare for. Once in a day or so, the wind falls, and for a few seconds the sound drops to a hum. People who have been there describe the moment as almost violent — the ears ring with the absence, the body lurches as though a wall it was leaning on has been removed. Don't be standing on the edge of anything when it happens. Don't take the protection off to "hear the silence." It's not silence, and the wind comes back without warning.

## The wind

There is no lee. Write that on your hand. Whatever you shelter behind, the wind will come round it within a few breaths and find you.

**Silhouette.** Anything that stands up gets torn out; that is why nothing on the plateau is taller than a knee, and it applies to you. Learn to move low. Learn to crawl without embarrassment, because there will be hours when it is the only way to move at all. When the downdrafts come — you'll see them coming, a widening ring of flattened green rushing toward you — drop flat and spread out. Don't crouch; a crouched body is a sail with a high centre of gravity. Lie down and become part of the mat.

**Clothing and gear.** Nothing that catches air. No cloaks, ponchos, capes, loose hoods, wide brims, scarves that trail, pack flaps, dangling straps. Everything you wear should be snug and fastened, and everything you carry should be inside something closed and strapped tight to your back. Tie long hair down or cut it. A hat will be gone in the first minute; use a close-fitting cap tied under the chin, or nothing.

**Your eyes.** The wind carries grit, torn stalks, hard little fruits the size of a fingernail, and water — all at speed, all from every direction. Sealed goggles are mandatory, not optional. Bring two pairs. Bring something to wipe them that isn't also soaked. Don't wear contact lenses; grit under a lens at altitude in a whiteout is a genuine emergency. If you need glasses, wear them under the goggles and tether them.

**Your things.** Nothing set down stays where it was set. Everything you own is either in your hands, tied to your body, or gone. Water bottles, tools, notebooks, food, the goggles you took off for a second — tethered, every one, on short cords. The spirals that lift loose stalks a hundred feet into the air will happily take a pack. They will take a small person, briefly. Stay heavy.

**Rope.** In a normal broken landscape I'd say rope up. Here I'm less sure. A long line between two people is a thing for the wind to grab and swing, and a line that snaps taut when your partner is blown sideways can pull you off your feet or, worse, toward a shaft. If you rope, keep it short — two or three body-lengths — and be ready to release it. Practice the release blind.

## The shafts: the thing that will actually kill you

Tens of thousands of vertical holes, wrist-width to house-width, polished smooth as the inside of a shell, of unknown depth, hidden in the green. This is a crevasse field disguised as a meadow, and the meadow moves.

**The mat lies.** It grows right to the lip of a shaft and sometimes over it. A sheet of mat that has been torn up somewhere and dropped here may be lying across a mouth with nothing under it but air. And because the mat is a mattress over stone, you cannot feel the difference between solid ground and a bridge of runners until it gives. Assume every step is a question.

**Probe.** Carry a stout pole, a body-length long, and use it like a blind person's cane — ahead and to both sides, hard, before each step in any ground you haven't walked already. A pole long enough to span a wrist-width hole will not save you from a house-width one, but house-width ones are usually visible, and the mid-sized ones — the ones just wider than your shoulders — are what the probe is for. In cloud, when visibility is an arm's length, do not move at all if you can help it. If you must, crawl, probing with your hands, and go very slowly.

**Do not travel alone.** A person who goes into a shaft alone is not found. A person who goes in with a companion nearby has some chance, if the shaft is narrow enough to jam in, if the companion has a line, if. The walls are smooth; there is nothing to grab. Bring a light line anyway, with knots in it, and a small anchor that can be wedged.

**Do not go down into an unoccupied shaft to explore.** The air at the bottom of some of them is not air you want. They breathe out warm sulphurous exhalations from time to time, and whatever is down there smells like a volcano's stomach. The people live ten feet down, on platforms, in shafts they have chosen over generations; they are not doing this in random holes. Follow their choice, not your curiosity.

**Don't drop anything down one.** Practically, because it's gone. Socially, because the shafts are people's houses, and you don't know which ones.

## The wet

Everything is damp all the time. There is no standing water and every surface is beaded with it. Rain comes sideways and is gone in five minutes and it does not matter, because the cloud was already soaking you.

**Feet first.** Over days, constantly wet skin breaks down. Between the toes, under the arms, in the groin, wherever skin touches skin. Trench foot is a real risk and it is disabling — on a plateau days across, a person who cannot walk is a person who is in serious trouble. Bring more socks than seems reasonable, in individual waterproof bags, and change them twice a day. Bring an antifungal powder and use it before you need it. Whenever you are in a shelter — and here is the one gift of the warm rock — take your boots off and put your bare feet on the stone. It dries them faster than anything you could carry. Locals do this without thinking.

**Skin generally.** Chafing where wet cloth moves against wet skin, all day, in wind. Wear close-fitting base layers that don't bunch. Bring a lubricant for the places that rub. Bring lip protection; wet lips in constant wind still crack.

**Sun.** The plateau is inside a cloud more often than under one, and you will still burn. Altitude and thin cloud are a bad combination for skin and worse for eyes — another reason the goggles must be tinted or at least sealed. Sunscreen will wash off within the hour; reapply anyway, or cover.

**Your gear.** Assume it will all be wet always. Paper that isn't waterproof will be pulp. Electronics — if your world has them and they function there — will die. Anything that rusts, will. Anything that needs to be dry to work is a liability. Wax, oiled leather, stone, bone, fibre, glass: these are the materials that survive the plateau, and the locals arrived at that list the hard way.

## Food, water, fire

**Fire is not available above ground.** There is no wood. The only thing that grows is the mat, and it holds water like felt and will not burn. The wind would take any flame you made regardless. In a shelter, ten feet down, a small stove *might* work — but you are in a confined space in a shaft that occasionally exhales sulphur, and burning anything in there is a decision to make carefully, with the mouth uncovered. Most travelers should plan to eat cold for the duration.

**Bring food.** All of it. The plateau offers a small hard sour fruit that the locals live on; it is filling and apparently safe, and it tastes like exactly what it is. Try it, accept it if offered, but don't plan your calories around it. Bring dense cold-eatable food that survives wet: fats, hard cheese, cured things, dried fruit, nuts, chocolate. You will burn more than you expect — altitude, wind, cold-wet skin, the constant low physical stress of the sound.

**Water is everywhere and nowhere.** Nothing pools; it all drains into the stone. But a bowl left at the bottom of a shelter fills overnight from the drip, and the mat can be squeezed like a sponge. Bring a wide-mouthed container you can leave out, and filter or treat what you collect. The condensation itself is probably clean. What it picks up running off the mat and the rock — and what the sulphurous exhalations leave behind — is less certain.

## Finding your way, and finding your way out

Here is a problem specific to this place: **there are no landmarks that last.** The mat is rearranged every season. Cairns get swallowed. The horizon is a convex line of identical green. In cloud, you cannot see your own hand. And whatever direction-finding tools you rely on at home may not behave here.

- **Do not travel in cloud.** When the plateau closes in, stop, lie down, and wait. It may lift in minutes or hours. Moving blind across a field of hidden shafts is not travel; it is a lottery.
- **Use the still moments.** The once-a-day pause when the wind falls and the cloud hangs is the only time the plateau shows you its full extent. Stand up — carefully — and *look*. Take bearings on anything that isn't mat: a boulder smoothed to an egg, a pale-lipped shaft mouth larger than the rest, a dark hump that might be a shelter. These persist. The green between them does not.
- **Mark your arrival point the way the locals mark a home**: a ring of stacked stones around something fixed. Not a cairn — a ring, flat and heavy. Choose a large, distinctive shaft nearby as your true reference, because the ring may be under mat when you return. Pace and record the distance and direction from that shaft to your point, on wax, tethered to your body.
- **Ask the people.** They know where things are in a way you cannot learn in a week. A guide who will walk you a day toward the next group is worth whatever you can offer.

## The people

A few hundred, across a space that takes days to cross, in groups of a dozen or two, living on stone platforms in the warm dark with the note of their shaft humming through everything they touch. They are deaf, or nearly. They speak with their hands and with a forced sound felt in the chest. They have stone, bone, fibre, sour fruit, water from the drip, and heat from the rock, and nothing else.

**Approaching.** Come into view from the front and at a distance, slowly, low, hands visible and empty. You cannot announce yourself with sound; a person appearing out of the cloud at the mouth of someone's shaft is a shock. Stop at the ring of stones and wait to be seen. Do not step inside the ring uninvited. Do not touch the rag on the pole, whatever it is for; nobody remembers, and that is exactly why you leave it alone.

**Speaking.** They will read your hands better than you read theirs. Be patient, be literal, point at things. If someone puts a hand flat on the stone and looks at you, do the same — they are inviting you to feel what they feel, and it is the closest thing the place has to a greeting. Learn their signs faster than you teach yours.

**Giving.** Think hard about this. They have never had metal. A single steel knife is, to them, a technology from another age, and you can imagine the ways a gift like that reshapes a group of twenty people who have been in balance with each other and the rock for a very long time. My honest advice: bring fibre, bring needles of bone or hard wood, bring dry cordage, bring food. Things that fit the life. If you bring metal, bring enough that no one person owns the only piece, or bring none.

**Being.** They live in the warm dark at the bottom of a hole under a waterfall of sound, and they are not miserable. An old woman twisting fibre in the mouth of her shaft, out of the worst of the wind, with the note of the rock coming up through her spine — this is most of what their built world is, and it is a life. Don't pity it in front of them. They can see faces even if they can't hear tone.

## The animals

Rare enough that each one is an event, and none of them, as far as anyone knows, dangerous to you.

The grey fliers live in the cloud and come down with it. If you are lying flat in a whiteout and feel the wind drop a beat and something pass over — that's one. Stay still. They have never been known to take anything from the surface. Their feathers turn up in the mat, long and stiff and sky-coloured; the locals may value them, so ask before you pocket one.

Blind many-legged things eat the mat from beneath, and something quick and dark eats them. Shake out your bedding. Check inside your boots. Neither is known to bite, but you're a soft warm thing lying on their food.

The broad slow grazer, if it exists, is reported once every few years and does not linger. If you see one, you are lucky, and you should not approach; anything that survives on this plateau by being low and heavy is heavier than you.

## Your mind

I've left this for last because it is the hardest to prepare for and the most important to have thought about.

You will be in a place that gives your eyes nothing to hold and your ears too much. The green goes to a horizon that curves. There are three things in all that distance. The sound comes up through your feet and lives in your chest and never stops. Then the cloud closes and there is nothing at all, an arm's-length of white in every direction, and the sound is still there. Then it opens and the nothing is enormous again.

People describe feeling immense and feeling like nothing at the same moment, with the wind pushing them from side to side. That's a fair description of what a nervous system does when it is starved of visual anchors and flooded with pressure. It comes out as: sudden weeping, sourceless dread, a flat grey exhaustion that no amount of rest fixes, irritability with companions that is out of all proportion, an urge to lie down and not get up, an urge to walk in a straight line just to be moving. None of that means you are weak. It means you are a mammal on a plateau that was not built with mammals in mind.

- **Go with someone.** Not for safety only. For scale. A human face at arm's length is the only thing in that landscape that is the right size.
- **Set a duration before you leave, and keep it.** Days, not weeks. The place does not get easier with time; your defences get worse. Decide in advance what would make you leave early, write it down, and honour it when the moment comes, because in the moment you will not be thinking clearly.
- **Spend time in the shelters.** Ten feet down, the wind is a roar overhead like being under a waterfall, and the rock is warm, and it is dark, and the note of the shaft is a single steady thing instead of ten thousand shifting ones. It is the closest the plateau comes to rest. Take it when it's offered.
- **Bring one small thing that is entirely yours.** Something with a texture. Something that smells of home. You will need to hold it.
- **Use the still moments for yourself, too.** When the wind falls and the mat lies still and the beads of water stop shivering — that is the plateau as it actually is, a flat warm stone at the top of the world with nothing on it. It is worth having seen. It is what you came for. Then the downdraft hits somewhere far off and the green goes flat in a ring and the sound comes up again, and you put your goggles back on.

## The short version, for the pack

Acclimatize on the slope. Double hearing protection, many spares. Sealed goggles, two pairs. Snug clothing, no cotton, no down, nothing that catches wind. Probe pole. Short rope, practiced release. Everything tethered. Socks in waterproof bags, antifungal powder, feet on warm stone whenever possible. Cold food for the whole trip. Wide bowl for the drip; treat the water. Wax tablets and a hand vocabulary worked out in advance. A ring of stones at your arrival point and a bearing to a big shaft, recorded. Gifts that fit the life. A companion. A leaving date you don't argue with.

And this: you will come home with the sound still in you, and quiet rooms will feel wrong for a while, and you will find yourself putting your palm flat on any warm stone you pass and waiting for it to hum. It won't. That is the plateau's one souvenir, and it does not wear off quickly.
