**Before anything else: the air will try to kill you, and it will do it politely.**

Read the description again and pay attention to the temperatures. The floor is the temperature of a hand. The water is a bath left a while. The drizzle "arrives on the skin as a temperature." Nothing evaporates. Put those together and you have air at or near body temperature at full saturation, and that is not a discomfort — it is a physiological wall. A human body walking generates a hundred-odd watts of heat and disposes of most of it by sweating, and sweat only works if it can leave. Here it cannot. You will sweat, and the sweat will sit on you under the drizzle-film, and your core temperature will climb at a slow fixed rate for as long as you stay, with nothing anywhere cooler than you to shed it into. The floor is your skin's temperature. The water is your skin's temperature. The animals are warmer than you.

The animals are fine because they were made for this. You were not. The honest number is that unprotected, moderately active humans last a few hours in these conditions before heat illness begins, and the onset is quiet — a headache, a thickness in the thinking, a sense that the place is more oppressive than it was an hour ago, which you will attribute to the place. Do not attribute it to the place. Attribute it to your blood.

So the first preparation is active cooling, and it has to be the kind that does not depend on evaporation. Phase-change vests, the sort with packs that melt at a set temperature, buy you a few hours per charge; bring more charges than you think you'll need, sealed, and understand they will not refreeze here. A powered cooling garment with a heat sink you can carry is better if you have one. Failing both, cut your visit to what an uncooled body can afford — call it ninety minutes of slow walking, with the exit in sight the whole time — and treat that as a hard clock, not a suggestion. Move slowly on purpose. Every step you don't take is heat you don't make.

Drink. The well water is probably fine — alkaline, mineral, degassed, faintly of warm ceramic; the kettle-scale along the channels tells you it's mostly dissolved carbonate, and nothing that could sicken you seems to live in it — but drink it from a well-housing, where it has just come up, not from a collecting channel, and not from anywhere near the brownish slick that pools behind the housings. Drinking warm water does nothing to cool you, but you will be losing fluid steadily and invisibly and you'll want to replace it. Bring electrolytes. Do not bring anything you expect to keep cold.

**Second: the floor, and your feet on it.**

The surface is very slightly wet everywhere and will stay that way. Your feet will be wet within a minute of arriving and will not dry until you leave. Warm and wet is the recipe for maceration: the skin softens, whitens, splits along the folds, and then it blisters and the blisters open. It takes hours, not days, in conditions like these. A barrier — thick petroleum jelly or a proper anti-maceration cream — on the feet and between the toes and everywhere else skin meets skin, before you step through, and a plan to reapply. The same for the groin, the underarms, anywhere fabric will rub on damp skin.

Footwear needs a hard sole. The flash along the seams is described as a millimeter or two of overspill sharp enough to catch a fingernail, and it runs everywhere, and the plate corners are crisp and unrounded. Soft soles will be cut. A fall will put your hands and knees on those edges. Wear gloves — thin, tough, wet-tolerant — and think about knee protection if you're clumsy or expect to kneel to look at the troughs. Any cut here happens in warm, wet, alien conditions with a biofilm nearby you know nothing about; carry a way to clean and seal wounds and treat any break in the skin as more serious than it looks.

Grip is uncertain. The film does not bead, which suggests it wets the ceramic completely; that could mean a thin tacky layer or a thin lubricating one. Test it in the first meters. Look at the drainage gradient — the water moves in one direction only — and be aware that the surface is very slightly tilted everywhere and your inner ear will not tell you so.

Depth perception is going to be poor. There are no shadows, the floor and sky are the same grey, and the only contrast is the brown-grey of the animals. The lip of a trough, the apron of a well, the step up onto a housing — all of these will be nearly invisible until your foot finds them. Walk as if you were in fog, because functionally you are.

**Third: the animals.**

They are mild. They tolerate touch. They look at nothing. You will be tempted to treat them as scenery, because that is what the description does, and for most of the visit that will be right. Two exceptions.

The first is the shrug — the slow wave of weight that passes through the crowd for a kilometer at a time. If you are in a lane between two packed bodies of hog-sized animals when one of these passes, the lane closes, briefly, on you. Nothing in it is malicious. It is simply a great deal of warm mass shifting in one direction, and you are a small soft thing standing in the gap. Stay in the wider lanes and on the well aprons, where there is room, and if you feel the crowd begin to move, get to a housing and put your back to it. The housings are waist-high and the animals do not stand on them.

The second is what the place does with anything that lies down. The rendering grilles are in the lanes and the aprons, wider than a body, and an animal that goes down near one is gone "within a period that is not long," with nothing seen to take it. You are a warm body in a lane. You do not know whether the mechanism distinguishes. Do not lie down. Do not sit down for long. If heat or exhaustion puts you on the floor, get onto a housing before you rest, above the grilles, and this is one more reason to have the exit close and the clock short. It is a real possibility that the place counts you as one of its population the moment you arrive, and its population is held constant to a rounding error, and you have no idea what its correction looks like.

Beyond that: the animals' skin has a bloom of moisture and their breath is steam and you are going to be touching them, brushing past them, breathing what they breathe. Their biology is unknown. Assume nothing about pathogens either way. A mask will be miserable in this humidity and probably useless; the realistic precaution is to keep your hands away from your face, wash — in well water — before you eat or drink, and accept a small unknown risk as part of the price. Do not eat the paste. It smells sweet and is warm and looks like set custard and the place is a closed loop with rendering grilles in it; you can work out what the paste most likely is. Even set aside what it's made of — it's formulated for them, not for you.

**Fourth: getting back.**

This is the part where travelers die who did not die of the heat. There is no edge. There are no landmarks. The provinces differ — plate shapes, trough profiles, the pitch of the pump — but they repeat, and the haze closes at twenty kilometers and travels with you. If you walk out of sight of your exit you will not find it again by looking. You will find a well housing that looks like the one near it, in a province that looks like that province, and you will be wrong.

Mark the exit before you take a single step away from it, and mark it with something that survives this place. Chalk and paint will not: the film is constant and the drizzle is constant. Nothing you scratch into the ceramic will show; the floor is denser than anything you carry. What works is an object — heavy, bright, waterproof, not the colour of anything here — set on top of the nearest well housing, above the animals' heads, where it can be seen over the crowd. Bring two, in case. Better still, run a line. A few hundred meters of light cord tied off at the exit point and paid out as you walk is unglamorous and it is the single most reliable thing you can do. The animals will step over it or stand on it; it will still be there. Do not go past the end of it.

If you do lose the exit, the drainage gradient is the only directional information the place gives. Water moves one way. Remember which way it was moving relative to your exit before you leave it, and check it constantly.

**Fifth: time, and your head.**

Bring a watch, set an alarm, and obey the alarm. The light does not change, the animals do not sleep, and nothing here will tell you that an hour has passed except your own tiredness, which you will misread as the place's weight. You will lose track of duration faster than you think possible. The heat clock and the alarm clock should agree, and both should be shorter than you want.

Recording will be difficult. Every lens fogs the instant it enters this air and stays fogged. Warm the camera to ambient in a sealed bag before exposing it, if you must have images; otherwise accept that this is a place to be looked at with eyes and remembered. Paper turns to pulp. Whatever notes you take, take them on something that tolerates being wet.

And the pump. It is felt, not heard — a pressure in the sternum, fixed, unending. Some people tolerate infrasound and some people find after an hour that they are nauseated and anxious and cannot say why. If you are the second kind, you will not know until you are there. Take the unease as data and leave earlier rather than later. Earplugs do nothing for it.

What the place will do to you, past the physical, is this. It has no purpose it will show you, and it has no end you can reach, and the only sound is several million things breathing in no rhythm. The mind reaches for meaning and finds a thermostat. That is disorienting in a way that isn't quite fear — closer to the feeling of standing too long in a very large empty building, except the building is full and the fullness is the emptiness. Bring your own purpose. Know before you cross what you are going there to look at — the flash on the seams, the moiré of trough and well grids, the wave passing through the herd — and look at it, and go home. Travelers who go in "to see it" without a question in hand tend to walk further than they meant to, because there is always more of the same and it is never quite enough.

**A short list, then.**

Active cooling and spare charges. A hard timer. Barrier cream everywhere skin meets wet. Hard-soled boots, gloves, knee protection. Electrolytes and a way to fill a bottle from a well grille without touching the channels. Wound kit. Two bright heavy markers and a spool of cord. Waterproof notes. Nothing you need to keep dry, nothing you need to keep cold, nothing with a lens you can't stand to lose.

Do not go alone if you can help it; a second person can watch the shrug coming while you look at the floor. Do not sleep there. Do not lie down. Do not eat anything. Do not pass the end of your line.

**What it will be like.**

You will step through into warmth that is instantly total, and the first breath will feel like breathing bathwater, and within a minute your clothes will be wet through and stay that way. The light will be everywhere and nowhere. You'll hear nothing at first, then the drip, then the breathing — a sound like a shore very far off that never resolves into waves — and then, when you stop listening for anything, the pump, low in your chest, patient. The animals will be nearer than you expected and will not look at you. If you put a hand on one it will be warm and damp and will give under the palm, and there will be a slow pulse under the skin, and it will breathe out, and the breath will go up into the grey.

Then you will look down at the seam you are standing over and see the mold flash on it, still sharp, and understand that nothing here has ever been worn, and that you are the most perishable thing in twenty kilometers in any direction. Take that seriously. Look at what you came to look at. Follow your cord back. Leave before the place has any reason to notice that its count is off by one.
