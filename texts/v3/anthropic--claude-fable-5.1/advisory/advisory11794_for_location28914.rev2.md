Take the description at its word about the altitude, and then take it more seriously than that, because it is the thing that will hurt you first and it will hurt you before you have even seen the water.

**The rim comes before the sea**

You arrive on the outside of a mountain, in snow, at three and a half thousand metres. Your body does not care that the destination is warm. Spend three days on the outer face before you go over the top — longer if you have never slept above two thousand metres. Headache, nausea, waking at night gasping: these are ordinary and they pass, but you must not go into the water while you have them. Everything that follows — the breath-holding, the dusk, the confinement — is a thing you do with a clear head or not at all. If the headache does not pass, go back down. The city has waited centuries; it can wait for you.

Crossing the rim you go from snow to steam in a few hundred metres of scree. The inner face is black glass rubble, slick with condensation and dusted with yellow where the vents breathe, and it will cut through soft boots and open your palms if you fall. Wear hard soles and gloves for the descent. Expect fog. Expect never to see the far side of the caldera and to see the sky only as a brighter grey overhead; the air is cold, the water is warm, and the whole basin lives under its own permanent weather. The sun is up there, fierce and thin, and it burns straight through the fog. Cover your skin. Zinc on your face. You will not feel it happening until evening.

Sleep high. Not on the shore, and not — however tempting — on a raft over the city. At night the air stills, an inversion settles over the warm water, and the gas that the vents have been exhaling all day pools in the fog at the surface. Heavy gas, invisible, odourless once the sulphur has dulled your nose. People die on the shores of ordinary volcanic lakes this way, and this is not an ordinary one. Make your camp on the inner slope, well above the water, where there is moving air, and go down each morning. Treat the water's surface over the city as a place to be in with a regulator in your mouth or not at all.

**You cannot breathe anything here except what you carry**

This is the second fact, and it shapes the whole visit. The people of the city hold their breath for a long time and then, as you get older, apparently do not need to. You will not learn this. You will be a person who needs air every few seconds, in a place where the only air is either at the fog-choked surface or trapped in bells that are filled with the mountain's own breath.

Be clear about the bells. They are beautiful and you will be invited into them and you should go. But the air inside is vent gas — carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulphide, whatever else the fissures are giving up — with whatever oxygen has leaked in through the rim over the years. It "tastes of eggs and iron" to people whose bodies are made for it. To you it is a closed room with no oxygen and a poison in it. You will enter a bell, rise into its air-space, take your regulator out to be polite, and be unconscious before you understand what has happened. Do not take the regulator out indoors. Not to speak, not to eat, not to sleep. If they offer you food, accept it with your hands and eat it later on the shore. If they offer you a place to sleep, decline gently and go back up. You are a guest in a city every room of which would kill you, and the courtesy you owe them is to not die in their hall.

So: you dive. Bring gas. Tanks are heavy, and heavier at altitude when you are carrying them over a rim in snow. A small compressor is worth its weight if you are staying more than a few days, but its intake must be on the outer face, in clean air, well away from the steam; a compressor drinking vent gas fills your tanks with the same thing that fills the bells. Have every tank's contents tested before it goes on your back. If you cannot test, fill only from the far side of the mountain.

Learn altitude decompression before you go. Surface pressure here is two-thirds of sea level, and every table you know is wrong. The city sits on a shelf, and most of what you want to see is shallow — but the description says the dusk begins at forty metres, and the deep channels are below that, and you are not going there. Set yourself a hard limit near twenty and hold it.

Your equipment will suffer. Hydrogen sulphide blackens brass in hours and eats at rubber over days; silica comes out of the water onto anything that stays in it. Bring two full sets of regulators and service kits for both. Rinse everything in fresh snowmelt the moment it comes out of the water, before the film sets — once it sets it is stone and you will not get it off. Do not leave gear in the water overnight. After a week your mask lens will have a haze on it you cannot polish away and your zips will be black and rough and your wetsuit will crackle faintly when you fold it. This is the place happening to you. Budget for it.

**The water itself**

It is body-warm. That sounds like comfort and is actually a problem, because you cannot dump heat into water that is the same temperature as you are. Every stroke you take is heat you have to breathe out. Swim hard for ten minutes and you will feel your pulse in your temples and a floating nausea that is not the altitude. The locals move slowly and you had assumed it was a cultural matter; it is also physics. Move at their pace. Rest often. Drink before you go in and drink when you come out, because you are sweating the whole time and the water hides it from you.

The heat is not even. Near a vent it goes from bath-warm to scalding in the length of your arm, and in the milk you will not see the shimmer that would warn you in clear water. Learn to read it on your face — the one part of you not covered — and never put a hand into a crack or under a lobe of rock to feel for a hold. If you must touch the floor, touch the tops of the pillows, which are cooled, and touch them lightly.

The floor is glass. Nothing has worn it. The pillows have a crust like burnt bread and where that crust has split, the edges are as sharp as a knapped blade, which is exactly what the people make from them. You will cut yourself. Everyone who goes in this water cuts themselves. Wear the thickest gloves you can still feel through — kevlar over neoprene — and hard-soled boots and something over your knees and shins. A full suit is a heat trap but a thin abrasion-resistant skin over your whole body is not optional. Then accept that you will still be cut, and treat every cut, however small, the moment you are on shore: fresh water, disinfectant, close it, cover it, and watch it. Warm mineral water thick with sulphur-eating bacteria is an ideal place to grow an infection and a poor place to fight one. Bring more antibiotic than you think you need.

The water is salty and it is dense. You will float higher than you are used to and you will need more weight than you have ever carried. Do not bring lead up a mountain; fill a pouch with black rock from the shore scree — from the shore, not from the city — and dump it when you leave.

Your eyes will sting if the mask leaks, and the water will fog your sight even after you clear it, because it is not clear water. Your ears will get infected — warm mineral water does this to almost everyone within days — so dry them after every dive and put drying drops in whether or not they hurt. Your skin will go slick and then tight and then start to split at the knuckles and the corners of the mouth. Limit yourself to a few hours in the water a day, however much you want more, and grease yourself when you are out.

Do not drink it. There is no fresh water below the surface and the only fresh water anywhere is the snowmelt on the outer face and the few streams that make it over the rim. Carry every drop. Carry more than you would at sea level, because the air on the slope is thin and dry and takes water from you even while you sleep.

**Seeing nothing**

You will never see the city. Understand that before you go, so that you do not spend your visit waiting for it.

A metre down it is bright and white and you can see your hand. Ten metres down the light is a pearl held to a lamp and you can see a few body-lengths. Where the columns begin, the weed closes in and you can see the pillar you are touching and the shape of the person next to you and not much else. There is no vista. There is no moment where the forest of columns opens out and you take it in. There is only the next pillar under your glove, ridged where the lashings were, and a wall of weed moving in a current you cannot feel, and the pale curve of a bell's underside passing over you like a moon.

A torch is nearly useless. Light in milk scatters straight back into your eyes. You will use it to read your gauges and for nothing else. And do not carry it into the city lit: these people live their whole lives in a steady grey dusk, and their eyes are made for it, and a beam of white light in that dusk is, to them, roughly what a shout is. It hurts. It is rude. Keep it off.

A compass will lie to you. The floor is fresh basalt, iron-rich, still cooling, and it does whatever it likes to a needle. A guide line back to your entry point is sensible and you should use one, but you must retrieve it every time, because a line left in this water for a season becomes a stone line and is there forever, cutting through somebody's garden. Better than either: a guide. The city is built so that "everyone is always close enough to reach," and that is the navigation system. Someone will take your wrist. Let them. Do not pull away, do not signal that you are fine, do not swim off to look at something. You will lose them in two strokes and you will be alone in the grey with no idea which way is out and a limited amount of air, and there is no way to call for help in a language you do not have.

Prepare, also, for what this does to your mind. Warm water, no horizon, no far sight, no light change from noon to evening, a thicket pressing in on every side, and a sound-world of ticking rock and fine rain. It is close to a sensory-deprivation tank with hazards in it. Some people find it holy. Some find it unbearable within the hour. You will not know which you are until you are in it. Tell your guide, before you go down, the sign for *up*, and use it early rather than late.

**Sound and speech**

You will be the loudest thing in the city.

Every breath you take is a rattle and every exhale is a burst of bubbles, and in water that carries sound well and a place where a raised voice hurts, you are a machine that raises its voice every four seconds. There is nothing to be done about this except to be aware of it and to breathe slowly. A rebreather, if you have one and know it well, would be a kindness to your hosts and would let you hear the place properly; but do not bring one you are not intimate with, because the failure modes of a rebreather in warm, sulphur-heavy water at altitude are not something to learn on site.

Their language is made with the mouth closed, and carries beautifully in water. Notice that a regulator in your mouth leaves you exactly one form of speech: humming. You cannot learn their language in a fortnight but you can, in a fortnight, learn to make four or five of its sounds through your nose and throat with the mouthpiece in — greeting, thanks, *I do not understand*, *help*. Practise these on the shore with whoever is willing to teach you. They will be badly made and everyone will know it and it will still be the difference between being a piece of noisy equipment and being a slow child.

Your dive hand-signals mean nothing here. Before you go down, agree a small set of gestures with your guide — up, stop, hurt, air — and use nothing else. They will mostly communicate with you by touch, by pressure on your arm, by turning you. This is normal for them. Do not read it as pushing.

**How to behave**

Nothing here is ever lost. That is the rule of the place and it should be the rule of your conduct.

Tether everything to your body. Every clip, every tool, every spare. A thing that slips from your hand does not go away; it settles onto the floor and begins, that same day, to turn to stone, and in a hundred years it is still there, exactly as it fell, a little heavier, part of somebody's wall. You cannot litter here and have it forgiven by wind and water. Every object you bring into the city is a thing you are proposing to add to it permanently. Bring few, and bring nothing you could not stand to see become architecture.

For the same reason, take nothing. The dropped basket is still someone's basket. The rope furred with grey is on its way to being a column. There is no such thing here as a loose stone that belongs to nobody; there is only the city, at various stages of thickening.

Do not touch the columns more than you must for balance, and when you must, touch them as you would a person's shoulder. Some of them are people. The dead are lashed into frames and become pillars, and you will not be able to tell which pillars, and they will not tell you, and you should not ask. Do not knock on the walls of the bells to hear them ring, however much you want to. They ring like bells because they are the oldest and most patient things in the city and people sleep inside them.

Do not ask about the breathing. Do not stare at the blue along an elder's ribs or count how long someone has been down. You will surface constantly, like a small child, and you will be treated somewhat as one, and the graceful thing is to accept that. Children here are not lesser; they are just early.

Go slowly in everything. Fast is dangerous — you overheat, you cut yourself, you lose your guide. Fast is rude — it is noise and turbulence in a place built on stillness. And fast is pointless, because nothing you are there to see will show itself to a person moving quickly. The people think in decades. You have perhaps two weeks. Do not try to close that gap by hurrying; you cannot. Close it by sitting still against a column with your regulator hissing and your hand on the grey ridges and letting the place come to your fingertips, which is the only way it comes to anyone.

If you bring gifts, bring things that will still be good in three hundred years, because that is how long they will be there. Not metal — it goes black and then goes away. Not anything that shatters. Good cord of a kind they do not have, perhaps. A well-made knot they do not know, taught rather than given. Something small and hard and honest that can be lashed into a frame and become part of a wall and not embarrass you when it does.

**The mountain**

Every few years, without warning, the floor opens somewhere on the western side and a new pillow the size of a hall comes up glowing under the milk. You will probably not be there for it. If you are, the water will go cloudy — cloudier — and warm, and the ticking of the rock will quicken, and the fine rain of vent bubbles will thicken into a hiss, and the faint high whine that you can hear at night with your ear to a column will climb. The people will move. Move with them, at once, without asking where. They have been reading this mountain for generations and you have been reading it for a week.

Stay off the western side altogether. Stay off any floor that is glossy and dark and unfurred, because it is new and it is hot. If you ever feel the water warm suddenly around you for no reason you can see, go up. Not to the surface — remember the surface is its own hazard — but up and away, toward the shelf and the shore, and get out, and sit in the fog and shake for a while, and be glad.

**Leaving**

Coming out of thirty-eight-degree water into five-degree fog at altitude is a shock you will not be ready for the first time. Have warm, dry things at the exit point, waiting, and get into them before you do anything else. People get hypothermic on the shore of a warm sea here. It is one of the place's small jokes.

Then rinse everything. Every strap, every buckle, your hair, your skin. What you do not rinse off will set by morning. Look at your gloves after a week: faintly rough, faintly grey, a little stiffer than they were. Look at your own fingernails.

And then go back over the rim into the snow and the wind, where things wear down and are taken away, and notice that for a moment it feels wrong — profligate, careless, a world that lets go of its past. The people of the city think so. You will understand them a little, then, and that is the most you can reasonably bring back, because you cannot bring back anything else. Everything you might have carried out is, by design, already becoming part of the floor.
