# How to Prepare for the Room That Breathes

Read the description again before you go, and notice what it does not say. It does not say how you get out. It says "you come to it from above, which is the only way anyone has ever come to it," and then it talks about the stair going down, and then it never mentions the stair again except to tell you it has become a tree. Everything that follows is about arriving and staying. Nobody has written the part about leaving.

That is the part you are preparing for. The rest is easy — or rather, the rest is difficult in ways that are mostly about attitude, and attitude can be adjusted on the spot. The climb back up a slick, root-eaten stair in the dark, soaked to the skin, with your core temperature a degree above where it should be and a column of forty thousand bats coming up the shaft toward you: that cannot be adjusted on the spot. That has to be arranged beforehand.

So, in order of what will actually hurt you.

---

## 1. Heat. This is the real danger, and it does not feel like one.

The chamber is at roughly body temperature and the air is saturated. Sit with that for a moment. Your body cools itself by evaporating sweat. In air that is already holding all the water it can, sweat does not evaporate; it simply runs. You will be pouring with it and none of it will be doing anything. Every surface in the room sweats, the description says, and it is being literal, and you will be one of the surfaces.

At rest, in that heat, an ordinary person is fine for a few hours. Moving — climbing, stepping over roots, crouching to look at things, walking the slope to the west and back — you are generating heat you cannot get rid of. The first signs are not dramatic. You feel heavy. You stop being curious. You sit down and it feels like wisdom rather than symptom. Then a headache, then nausea, then the stair looks a very long way up.

Prepare accordingly:

- **Arrive already hydrated**, and carry more water than seems reasonable — three liters at minimum, with electrolytes in it, not plain. You will not want to drink; the air is so wet you will feel drowned already. Drink anyway, on a schedule, not on thirst. Salt snacks help.
- **Do not plan to exert.** The place is telling you this in every paragraph. Move at its pace. Go down slowly, sit, look, and let the room come to you. If you find yourself wanting to explore the western end and the flooded passages, that is exactly the impulse to distrust — see section 3.
- **Time-limit yourself.** Two hours in the chamber is a long visit. Three is a lot. Set a physical alarm — a mechanical or dive-rated watch, not your phone, which will be dead (section 6) — and when it goes off, start up regardless of what the frogs are doing.
- **Know the difference between the fog cooling you and the fog not cooling you.** When the chamber breathes and everything drips, it will feel like relief. It is not. Condensing water releases heat into the air; the fog event is, physically, a warm event. The drenching feels like rain because you are primed to feel rain as relief. Enjoy it. Do not count on it.
- **Do not go if you cannot do the surface part.** The plain above is glare and laterite and no shade, and you will cross it twice, and the second time you will already be spent. Hat, sun protection, and water for the surface — kept separate from your chamber water, cached at the rim in shade under the ferns.

---

## 2. The stair, and getting back up it.

Seven meters, roughly — four times a person's height. Cut into the eastern wall. Shallow, uneven treads "worn into a slick trough," gone in places, replaced in places by living fig root. The wood is warm, the stone is wet, and after every fog event the whole thing has just been rained on.

Going down is not the problem. Gravity is on your side and you are fresh and it is daylight. Coming up, you are tired, overheated, soaked, possibly in failing light, and every tread is polished. The roots that have "pried the steps apart and then, growing fat, replaced them" are good news for handholds and bad news for footing — cambium under a film of water has roughly the friction of soap.

- **Fix a handline before you descend.** Bring twenty meters of static rope and the knowledge to anchor it. There is thorn scrub at the rim and probably rock; find something that will hold a falling person, test it, and run the line down the eastern wall alongside the stair. Do not trust the fig alone as an anchor — you don't know what it's rooted in. If you are not comfortable rigging a line and using it, bring someone who is, or don't go. This is not a place to learn.
- **The rim is soft.** That collar of ferns "so green they look wet" is growing in the one damp place on a dry plain, which means the edge is undercut and crumbly. Approach the lip on your belly the first time. Rig from well back.
- **Shoes**: sticky rubber, low profile, ankle-covering. Not heavy boots — you want to feel the tread. Not sandals — see centipede, below. Some people swear by felt soles for wet limestone; they are worse on root. A good approach shoe is the compromise.
- **Gloves** for the climb, thin ones with grip. You'll take them off in the chamber.
- **Come up in daylight.** This is the single most important scheduling decision. Sunset in the tropics is abrupt — "quite suddenly" — and it is followed within minutes by the bats leaving, and they leave *up the shaft*, in "a column so dense it blots the stars," for ten minutes. You do not want to be on the stair for that. Not because they will hurt you — they will flow around you — but because forty thousand small animals streaming past your face while you cling to wet root in the dark is a good way to let go. If you want to see the exodus, watch it from the floor, from the eastern side, well clear of the shaft, with something over your head.

If you intend to stay the night, you are making a different trip, and the rest of this guide assumes you are not. But see section 8.

## 3. Air. Go west with a monitor or not at all.

The smell is "faintly sulfurous," which is either the bats or the fissures or both. The heat "comes from below." There are "low and flooded passages" at the western end, and the chamber "breathes" from them — warm air pulsing up from the geothermal system in a rhythm "of a large slow body."

Geothermally warmed caves put out gas. Carbon dioxide is the one to fear: heavier than air, odorless, pools in low places, and the western end is the low place. Hydrogen sulfide is the one you can smell at low concentration and stop smelling at dangerous concentration, which makes your nose worthless as an instrument. The description mentions the sulfur smell only "faintly" and at the surface, which is reassuring, and mentions no dizziness or headache in a long stay, which is more reassuring. But the writer stayed on the dry eastern side and sat at the basin on the northern wall, and never went into the dark.

- **Bring a multi-gas monitor** — the small clip-on kind that cavers and sewer workers wear, reading CO₂, H₂S, and O₂. Clip it low on your body, not at your collar; gas pools at the floor, and you will be crouching.
- **Treat the western third as off-limits** unless the monitor is quiet. The flooded passages where the salamanders live are absolutely off-limits. They are low, flooded, dark, and the source of whatever comes up when the room breathes. Nothing in there is for you.
- **During a breath, watch the monitor.** The pulse of warm air from below is exactly when gas concentration would spike. If it climbs during a breath and doesn't fall after, leave, up the stair, without discussing it.
- **The guano hill** on the western floor is a histoplasmosis risk — the fungus that lives in bat droppings and infects lungs when its spores are inhaled. Wet guano is less dusty than dry, and this guano is very wet, which helps. But do not walk on it, do not dig in it, do not stand over it and breathe. An N95 or better in a dry bag, put on if you go anywhere near the western end. If you have a compromised immune system, do not go into this chamber at all; the bats alone are reason enough.

## 4. The animals. Look. Do not touch. Wash before you eat.

The place is wall-to-wall with small lives and almost none of them are dangerous, but the ones that are, are, and there is no way to see everything before you put your hand on it.

**The centipede** — "the length of a forearm," hunting the guano hill by feel — is the one real medical hazard in the room. Big tropical centipedes deliver a bite that is agonizing for hours, sometimes days, and can cause swelling, fever, and in rare cases worse. They hunt at night and in the dark, which is to say they hunt in the western end all day. Keep your feet in shoes and your hands where you can see them. Do not put your hand down on anything — root, stone, wall — that you have not looked at first. Do not sit in the dark.

**The millipedes** — hand-length, glossy dark red — are harmless unless handled, at which point they secrete a defensive fluid that stains skin brown for days, burns, and in the eyes can do real damage. Some tropical species' secretions contain cyanide compounds. They are beautiful and they are slow and you will want to pick one up. Don't.

**The wolf spider** the size of a hand will bite if pressed, and it will hurt, and you will live. The main danger is that it will be on the fig root you are reaching for.

**The frogs** are small and colored like leaves, which usually means not aposematic, which usually means not very toxic. But you don't know, and many tropical frogs carry skin secretions that are unpleasant on mucous membranes. There are thousands; you will touch one by accident. When you do, don't touch your face until you've rinsed your hands with your own water.

**The snails** are the ones nobody warns you about. Large tropical land snails, and the slime they leave on every leaf, can carry rat lungworm, a parasite that causes a form of meningitis if ingested. It is caught by eating raw snails or things snails have crawled over. You are not going to eat a snail. But you might rest a hand on a begonia leaf and later eat a sandwich. Don't eat in the chamber at all. Eat at the top, after washing.

**The bats** will not touch you and you must not touch them. They are a colony of tens of thousands sleeping in the ceiling; a bright light or a loud noise will start them shifting, and a stampede in daylight costs them enormously. If one falls — they do — leave it. A grounded bat is one of the few ways rabies moves to humans. It is also simply not yours to help.

**The salamanders**, pale and eyeless, are cave animals that will die of your skin oils, your sunscreen, your insect repellent. Look at them in the pools. Do not put anything in the pools.

**The water in the cups and the basin** is full of frogs and whatever the frogs are eating and the runoff from a century of guano. Do not drink it. Do not wash a wound in it. Bring your own.

Bring a small first aid kit with a strong antihistamine, a serious painkiller, sterile wound wash, and tape. If the centipede gets you, the treatment is heat (hot water immersion, which you have in abundance at the northern wall), painkillers, and getting out.

## 5. Light. Bring red, use little, wait.

The room is dim, not dark, until the western end, where it becomes dark, and then at the far wall the fungus glows — "enough to see the outline of your hand." Your eyes will adapt over twenty or thirty minutes and the "thousand shades" of dimness will resolve, exactly as promised. Every time you turn on a white light you reset that clock, and you spike the bats, and you bleach the fungus glow out of your own retinas for the next half hour.

- **Headlamp with a red mode and a low setting**, plus a backup, both in waterproof housings. Use the red for footing on the stair and for nothing else if you can help it.
- **Do not aim any light at the ceiling.** Ever.
- **Do not aim any light at the far wall.** Let it come to you.
- **Do not use a flash.** See section 6 about whether a camera is even worth bringing.

Give yourself the half hour. Sit on the dry eastern side near where the stair comes down, in the fig's shadow, and wait until you can see the colonnade of roots and the platter-leaved begonias and the iridescent shiver of the club-moss without effort. Then start looking at what's alive. You will see nothing for a while and then you will see everything.

## 6. Things that will be destroyed.

Saturated air at body temperature destroys electronics. Not slowly. Your phone will fog internally within twenty minutes, and the fog will condense on the board, and it will die or behave strangely, and either way it is not coming up the stair as a working object. A camera is the same unless it is a sealed dive housing — and then the lens fogs from outside every time the room breathes.

- **Leave the phone at the rim, in a dry bag, in the shade.** Bring a mechanical watch or a cheap dive watch for time.
- **If you must record, use pencil on waterproof notepaper.** Ink runs. Ordinary paper turns to felt.
- **If you must photograph, accept you will get three good frames and a dead camera**, and take them early, before the first fog.
- **Anything you value that is made of leather, untreated wood, or natural fiber** will come out of the chamber changed. Watch straps rot. Book bindings swell.

Your clothing will be wringing wet within ten minutes, from sweat and from the room, and will stay that way. Wear thin synthetics — long sleeves and long trousers, despite the heat, because mites and because you will brush against a hundred things. Bring a complete dry change and leave it at the rim. Changing into dry clothes on the laterite plain at four in the afternoon is the closest thing to luxury this trip offers.

Your skin, after several hours wet, will start to go soft and white at the pressure points. Not dangerous in a day. Uncomfortable. Dry your feet at the top.

## 7. What not to bring, and what not to do.

- **No scent.** No sunscreen on your hands (put it on before you cross the plain, then wipe them), no repellent, no perfume, nothing that goes into the pools with you or onto the leaves. The room's chemistry is closed and the animals in it are sensitive to a degree you cannot intuit.
- **No food in the chamber.** For you and for them.
- **Nothing collected.** Not a snail shell, not a frond, not a chip from the low wall. It should not need saying. It needs saying because the calcite figure in the niche is beautiful and someone will want a piece of it.
- **Do not touch the figure.** For its sake, obviously. Also: the snails have not touched it. Everything else in this room is coated in the passage of a thousand radulae, and that one object is clean. Whatever the reason — the calcite, the height, something in the mineral — you are not going to improve it with your hand.
- **Do not cut, pull, or lever the fig roots** to make the stair easier. They *are* the stair now. Damage the cambium and in ten years there is no way down.
- **Do not stand on the low wall.** It is the snails' road, and it is knee-high, mossed, and rounded, and you will fall off it onto something.
- **Do not go alone if you can avoid it — and if you cannot, tell someone where you are and when you'll be out.** The stair is the only exit. A turned ankle at the bottom of it is a serious situation, not an inconvenience.

## 8. What it will actually be like, and how to be ready for that.

Everything above is about not getting hurt. This is about not wasting the trip.

You will go down the stair fast, because that is how people go down stairs, and you will arrive at the bottom breathing hard and sweating and already looking for the next thing. The place will not give you the next thing. The place gives you the same thing, over and over, slowly, and the whole visit consists of learning to receive it.

So: when you reach the bottom, stop. Find the dry patch on the eastern side under the fig. Sit on a root you've looked at first. Put the water bottle where you can reach it. Then do nothing for half an hour — not looking for anything, just letting your eyes adjust and your breathing slow and your body stop fighting the heat. The description says you will find yourself "slowing your feet until you are moving at the pace of the place." That is true, but it happens faster if you start slow.
