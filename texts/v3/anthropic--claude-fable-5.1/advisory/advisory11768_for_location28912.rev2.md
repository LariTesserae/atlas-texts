**Preparing for the plateau**

Start from what the place actually does to a body. Strip away the strangeness and it is: high, cold, wet, glaring, silent, slippery, sharp-edged, and structurally unfinished at its margins. Any one of those will hurt an unprepared person. The uncanny parts—the fox, the lagging reflection, the vanishing grating—are more interesting to think about, but they will not be what kills you. Exposure, a fall, and getting lost are what will kill you, in that order, with the uncanny parts mostly serving to make the ordinary hazards worse.

So prepare for it first as a mountain-summit industrial site, and second as a place that does not want to be understood.

---

**The body**

You come up through cloud onto a plateau that sweats. The air is high and cold; the machinery is warm; every surface is beaded, and a fine wet haze comes and goes on a cycle you cannot predict. This is the hypothermia scenario mountaineers know well—not a blizzard but a long damp chill that soaks through layers while you are distracted by something else. Dress as you would for a cold, wet, windless summit: wicking base layer, insulating mid-layer, a shell that is genuinely waterproof rather than nominally so, and spare dry layers sealed in bags. The absence of wind will fool you into underdressing. Don't let it.

Gloves. Not optional. The description says the edges of the I-beams are fresh enough to take skin, and the whole plateau is built at a density where pipes are within reach on every side. You will touch things by accident constantly. Wear leather or a durable synthetic that will turn a slice, and carry a lacerations-first medical kit: pressure dressings, adhesive closures, a tourniquet you know how to use. Fresh-poured concrete is also caustic—if you kneel on damp slab or put a bare hand on the dark, uncured sections, wash it off. You'll need your own water for that, which brings us to—

Bring every drop of water you will drink. Everything on the plateau goes into the plant; the standing pools, the drips, the haze—it all drains back. Do not drink any of it. Not because it will obviously poison you, but because you have no idea what a liquid becomes after it has cycled through a system whose business is turning mountain into decking. Carry more water than seems reasonable. The air is cool and you will not notice how much you are sweating.

Eyes. The sky is evenly lit horizon to horizon, and there are no shadows to give your eyes a rest. Treat this as snow-glare without the snow. Wear dark, wraparound eye protection, and consider a second, lighter pair in case the first makes the shadowless ground too hard to read. You will get a headache anyway. Bring something for it.

Footwear with aggressive tread and a solid ankle. Wet grating, wet diamond plate, wet rolled steel, and stairs that do not agree on how many steps they contain. Assume you will stumble several times an hour. Assume some of those stumbles will be near an edge. A helmet is not absurd here—conveyor bridges cross overhead at three and four levels, and things drip from them, and you will be looking at your feet a great deal.

Ozone and wet cement are in the air. A light respirator or even a simple mask over mouth and nose will help with the throat irritation on a long visit. Not critical. Nice to have.

**The eyes, again, and the ears**

This is where the ordinary hazards start to tangle with the strange ones.

The light has no direction and casts no useful shadow. Your visual system uses shadow-length and shadow-direction constantly, below the level of awareness, to judge how far away things are and which way is level. Here it gets nothing. On top of that there is no ambient sound to give you distance—no roar from the machinery, no wind, no echo you can trust. The result is that your depth perception is broken in a way you will not be able to feel. You will reach for a handrail that is a step further than it looks. You will step off a platform that is a step nearer. The red water tank an hour away will suddenly be dripping on your head.

You cannot fix this. You can only work around it. Move slowly. Test every edge and every stair with a foot before committing weight. Keep one hand on a rail whenever there is a rail, and treat the rail as truth when your eyes disagree with it. Use touch as your primary sense for anything within arm's reach and treat sight as advisory. The plateau is dense enough that you can nearly always be touching something; make that a habit.

The silence will get to you in a different way. Large machinery that makes no noise is deeply wrong to a human nervous system, and the pressure of that wrongness accumulates. Talk to yourself. Out loud, plainly, narrating what you are doing. It will sound thin and strange in that dead air, and you may feel it being drawn away from you, but it keeps the part of your mind that tracks *I am a person doing a thing* engaged. If the plant is collecting sound, let it have yours; it's a cheap price for staying oriented.

---

**Navigation**

Here is the central problem: you cannot go back the way you came. The description is explicit. The grating you first stepped onto is not findable. A shed with no door stands where the path should meet the plateau. Do not plan your visit as an out-and-back. Plan it as a traverse through a place that closes behind you.

Landmarks fail—the water tank does not approach. Counting fails—stairs disagree with themselves. Compass may fail near the transformer yards, and even if it doesn't, north is not obviously meaningful on ground that is being extruded. The sky gives no bearing. Dead reckoning fails because distances do not resolve.

What does not fail, as far as the description shows, is *flow*. The conveyors run one direction, slowly, carrying gray wet blocks of future plateau toward the forming edge. That is a gradient, and a gradient is a compass. Downstream is the growing edge—rebar over cloud, soft slab, no railings. Upstream is wherever the material comes from, which by the traveler's own guess means wherever the mountain is being fed in. If there is a way down to true rock that isn't a rappel off the shear face, it is most likely upstream. Follow the belts. Where they vanish into sheds, walk around the sheds and pick up the belt on the other side rather than going in. If a belt enters a wall and does not exit—and it may—choose another belt.

Do not go into the sheds at all if you can avoid it. They are windowless, large enough to hold ships, and the one described door onto an interior loops back onto itself with you absent from the room. Whatever happens inside them is not for you.

Mark your route anyway, knowing the marks may not survive. Chalk on curing concrete, tape on rails, whatever you have. Do it not because you expect to follow the marks back, but because when you find one of your own marks ahead of you instead of behind you, that is information you want. The plateau reorganizes. Your marks are a way of catching it doing so.

Carry a long line if you know how to use one—static rope, several hundred meters, and the hardware to anchor it. Not to tether yourself to the entry (the entry will not be there) but because the only guaranteed exit from a plateau perched over open air is *down*, and down here means over the shear-face lip and through a gap of still air to the girders, and from the girders to the receding gray rock. That is a serious descent through a space the description says has no wind where wind should be. If you cannot do it safely, you should not be on the plateau without a companion who can, and possibly not at all.

---

**Time**

The light does not change. Ever. Your only clock is your body and whatever you carry. Carry a mechanical watch—not a phone, not anything with a screen whose numbers might behave like the gauge on the boiler and change under your gaze. Wind it before you step onto the grating. Set a hard total limit for your presence—I would say six hours—and hold to it regardless of how close you feel to understanding something. The traveler in the description was there long enough for their legs to give out. That is too long.

Do not sleep there. Under no circumstances. The animals do not flee and do not feed; they stand, or perch, or lie, and they are described as held in suspension, constituent material. Stillness is what they have in common. Whatever mechanism converts an inhabitant into a component, I would bet it works on things that stop moving. Keep moving. Eat standing up. If you must rest, rest sitting with your back to something and your hands doing something, and get up before you want to.

---

**Conduct**

Some rules, in the spirit of cataloguing the local weather.

Do not count. Not the stairs, not the leaves, not the pigeons, not the foxes. Counting is how the place snags you—you count eleven leaves and then you must check the next tree, and the next, and now you are walking the courtyard grid instead of leaving. Go up and down stairs with a hand on the rail and your attention on the feel of the landing under your foot, not the number. Arrive early or late; stumble; keep going.

Do not stare. The gauge needle moves when looked at directly. The reflection lags when watched. The word stops being a word. Glance, register, look away. If your own reflection in a black rectangle of standing water is late by half a second, you have learned something and you do not need to learn how much later it can get.

Do not touch the animals. Do not feed them, photograph them at length, or try to resolve whether the fox is three foxes. Give them the same courtesy you would give a load-bearing wall. Step around. If one is standing in the passage you need and does not move, use a different passage; there are always more passages than there should be.

Do not open doors. If you must, do not let one close behind you, and do not open one from the far side to see if the room is the same.

Do not walk out onto the forming edge. Soft slab that takes a bootprint is slab that may not take your weight. Plywood and shoring over nothing. Look from the last hard surface. The heron can stand there; you cannot.

Do not touch the transformer yards, cable trays, or anything with ceramic insulators, no matter how calmly the goats stand among them. The plant is powered and the power is real even if the sound isn't.

Leave as little as you can. You will leave breath, sweat, skin, footprints in the damp; you cannot help that. But don't drop wrappers, don't spit, don't relieve yourself into a drain if you can hold it until you are below the cloud again. It's partly superstition and partly the sober recognition that a place whose one activity is intake does not need to be encouraged.

---

**A canary**

Before you step onto the first grating, pick up a stone from the mountain. A real one, weathered, gray, from below the cloud where there is still rock. Put it in a pocket you can reach. Take it out every hour and look at it—briefly—and feel it.

If it is still a stone, you have time. If it has become something else, or you cannot find it, or you find two, leave. Not toward the entry. Down.

---

**On the guess**

The traveler at the end offers a theory: the mountain is feedstock, the plateau is product, the product extends at a centimeter a day to meet everything. Whether or not it is true, prepare as though it is—not because you should fear it, but because it tells you what kind of place you are visiting. You are not a guest in a building. You are standing on a process. The process is not hostile. It is not friendly. It has tolerances, and you are within them until you aren't.

The only real protection an ordinary human has here is the one the animals lack: you can decide to leave, and you can act on it. That decision gets harder the longer you stay, and the light will never tell you it is time. So decide before you go up. Write the limit on your hand. When the watch says so, stop cataloguing, stop counting, stop wondering what it is for, turn upstream along the belts, and go find the mountain.
