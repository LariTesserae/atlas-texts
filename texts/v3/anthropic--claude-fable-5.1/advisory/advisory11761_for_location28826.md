**Preparing for the Drowned Terraces**

*A guide for someone with a body, a limited air supply, and a way home*

---

**First: understand what kind of place this is, because it will try to tell you it is several other kinds.**

From the surface it looks like a marsh. It is not a marsh. It is open water, deep water, with a lid on it.

From under the canopy it looks like a forest. It is not a forest in any sense that matters to you. Nothing lives in it that could help you or harm you. It offers no shelter, no food, no landmarks that repeat, and no floor.

From among the walls it looks like a city, and here your instincts will be most dangerous, because a city is a thing built to be walked through, and this was, once — by people the height of people, stooping through doorways the height of doorways — but it is now a flooded overhead environment of unknown depth, in total darkness, in water near freezing, with a ceiling of vegetation between you and the sky that will hold your body down with the same patience it uses to pull the walls apart.

Strip the beauty away for a moment and name it in the language of divers: **cold-water, low-visibility, high-entanglement diving over a bottomless structure, with overhead sections, no surface support, and no possibility of rest at the surface.** Each of those clauses is, on its own, something people train for years to do safely. Here they are all true at once. That does not mean you should not go. It means you should go the way you would approach anything that has killed better-prepared people than you: slowly, in stages, with redundancy, and with a hard rule about when you turn around.

---

**The arrival problem: the surface is a trap.**

Wherever you appear, you will want to appear on the surface, because that is where the light is and because you are a land animal and will want to see the place before you go into it. Understand what that means. The rafts of weed will hold you for the length of one breath and then you will go through, into cold water, into a tangle of blades that lie over one another like wet slate and close above your head. This is precisely the way kelp kills swimmers on your own world: not by pulling them down, but by lying on top of them when they try to come up.

So: **do not arrive on the surface unprepared to be immediately underwater.** Arrive already dressed for immersion, already breathing from a regulator or with one in hand, mask on, with a cutting tool clipped where your dominant hand can reach it without looking. If you have any control over your point of arrival, arrive *below* the canopy — at ten or fifteen meters, among the upper stipes, where you can orient and ascend on your own terms — or arrive on the surface at the edge of a raft rather than its middle. If you can't control it, assume the worst.

There is nowhere to rest on the surface. No islands, no rocks, nothing standing. A person lying flat and still might be half-supported by the weed for a while, but you will be lying in cold water regardless, in fog, under a grey sky, with the swell lifting and dropping you once a minute in a motion that will make most people ill within an hour. A small inflatable raft or float — not a boat, nothing with a motor, the weed would foul a propeller in seconds — is worth having as a place to put your head above water while you sort gear or wait out a moment of panic. Do not plan to *travel* on the surface. You cannot paddle through this. You cannot walk on it. The only way across the Drowned Terraces is beneath them.

Which means the surface is for one thing only: leaving.

---

**Cold.**

The water is cold everywhere. The description says so again and again and you should believe it more than you believe most things. Assume something between two and six degrees Celsius among the stipes — the temperature of a Scandinavian fjord in winter — and colder in the rooms, and colder still in the cisterns, where the water is stratified and does not mix and has a "cold with weight," which is the exact phrase a person uses when they have put a hand into water that is denser and nearer freezing than the water around it.

You will need a **drysuit**, not a wetsuit, with heavy undergarments — the thickest you can move in. Dry gloves with liners, because your hands are your only sense down here and cold hands stop being able to work a reel or a clip or a knife within minutes. A thick hood. If you have access to heated undergarments and can power them, bring them. Cold water robs judgment before it robs strength; you will make your stupidest decision on the Terraces when you are chilled and don't know it yet. Plan your dives short. Plan them shorter than that.

The air above is cold too, and wet, and foggy. When you break the canopy you will be wet-headed in cold wind with nowhere to stand. Have your way home ready to trigger from the water.

---

**Breathing and depth.**

There is no bottom. This is the single most important physical fact about the place and it is the one you will most want to forget. "Drop a stone from a platform edge and there will be another platform below it. And below that." The tops of the walls sit perhaps fifteen to twenty-five meters down — the stipes rise fifty to eighty feet through the dark to reach the canopy — and from there the platforms step down, and the terraces step down, and the cisterns cut down through three or four layers of building to something below the beginning. You could be at forty meters, sixty, more, and nothing in your surroundings would tell you, because a wall at sixty meters looks exactly like a wall at twenty in the beam of a torch.

**Wear a depth gauge and a computer and look at them constantly.** Set a hard maximum depth before you enter the water — thirty meters is generous for an ordinary human on air — and treat it as a wall you cannot pass. Nitrogen narcosis at depth in cold darkness feels like calm; it feels like the place is finally making sense; it feels like *one more platform*. That feeling is the drug. Turn around.

Bring more gas than you need and then bring redundancy. Two independent cylinders with two independent regulators, at minimum. Regulators that are rated for cold water and will not free-flow at three degrees. If you are going anywhere near a doorway, consider **sidemount** configuration — cylinders under your arms rather than on your back — because the lintels are low enough that "anyone going through would have stooped," and a back-mounted twinset will not stoop. Better still, if you are not already trained in overhead diving, don't go through a doorway at all. More on that below.

And remember that if you go deep and stay long you will owe decompression time on the way up, and you will owe it *hanging in the dark among the stipes, cold, motionless, with the canopy above you*. That is a hard place to spend twenty minutes. Plan your profile so that you owe nothing.

---

**Darkness and finding your way.**

Below the first body-length, light halves and halves again. By the wall tops it is "a memory of green." Below that, black. In the rooms, a black "you can lean on." Your torch is the only light that has ever entered most of these spaces, and when it fails you will be in a darkness more complete than any you have experienced, at depth, in the cold, in a maze.

**Three lights minimum.** A primary with hours of burn time, two backups clipped where you can find them blind. Test them in the water before you descend past the canopy.

Navigation here is not like navigation anywhere else. The stipes are so thick "you cannot move in a straight line." The walls run out for miles and meet other walls at corners and the pattern changes gradually, "the way a language changes across a country," so that nothing repeats and nothing stands out. There is no bottom to follow. There is no wall to keep on your left. The current is faint but real and will drift you without your noticing. **Without a physical line back to your point of entry, you will not find it again.** Say that to yourself until you believe it.

So: **mark your stipe.** The one you came down. Clip a strobe to it just below the canopy — a bright one, on a lanyard, high enough to see from below. Then run a guideline from it, on a reel, and never let go of the reel. Every meter you travel, you pay out line; every meter you return, you take it in. If you branch, you tie off and mark the branch with an arrow pointing home. This is cave-diving discipline and it is the only discipline that works in a place with no landmarks.

Do not trust the light to lead you up. The green comes back "first as a rumor," and in the shedding season, or in fog, or late in the day, the rumor may not come at all. Trust your bubbles — they always go up, even in the rooms — and trust your gauge, and trust the stipe, which always leads to the canopy because that is the whole reason it exists.

---

**The weed, and what it holds onto.**

The stipes are wrist-thick, smooth, cold, slightly yielding, and they will touch your shoulders as you pass. The temptation is to use them: to pull yourself along, to steady yourself, to grab one when you lose buoyancy. Resist it. Each stipe is anchored by a holdfast that has "felt its way into every joint and is slowly, over lifetimes, prising the courses apart." A stipe is rooted in loose stone. Haul on it and you may bring down the block it grips, onto you or onto the line you are following. Move through them the way the description says — "as through a crowd that is not looking at you" — with your hands to yourself.

**Entanglement is the thing most likely to kill you here.** Fronds of red weed, the fine filamentous growth that lies on everything like fur, shed blades drifting down from the canopy, and above all the canopy itself when you try to surface through it. Streamline everything. No dangling gauges, no loose fin straps, no snorkel. Practice — in a pool, in a kelp bed at home, somewhere safe — the discipline of *stopping when caught*. Struggling wraps you tighter. Stop. Breathe. Find where you are held. Cut or unwind. A line cutter you can use one-handed without looking, a second one on the other side of your body, and a real knife somewhere. Practice with cold, gloved hands.

When you ascend, come up your stipe slowly, and when you reach the underside of the canopy, **do not push straight up through it.** Look for a gap. Failing that, reach up, part the blades with your hands, get your head through and then your shoulders, and clear your regulator only when your face is in air. People die a body-length from the surface in kelp because they exhale, push up, and find a lid.

One more thing about the walls that is worth knowing with your hands: the last courses on top of every wall are the worst-built. "Hurried, undressed stones piled on the good work beneath them like a sentence that trails off." The top of a wall — exactly where you will reach to steady yourself or pull yourself over — is the loosest stone in the place. Don't.

And the pale lumpy crusts of stony algae cemented to the rock: they are sharp, the way coral rock is sharp. Another reason for gloves.

---

**The rooms. Be honest with yourself.**

The rooms are the heart of the description and they are where an ordinary human should almost certainly not go.

Consider what they are. A doorway you find *by feel*, low enough to stoop. A roof that has "held, mostly." A floor of silt so deep and untouched that a single fin-kick "gives up a cloud that takes an hour to settle." Water that is stiller and colder and more used-up than the water outside, with no oxygen in it, breathing "the slow chemistry of the silt." Total darkness. No stipes to follow because there is nothing for them to grow toward.

This is a flooded cave with a compromised ceiling and a silt floor. On your own world, that combination — overhead, silt-out, zero visibility, cold — is responsible for the deaths of trained, certified, experienced cave divers every year. If you are not one of those, **stay outside.** Hover in the doorway if you must, shine your light in, look at the mounds the mats have built over centuries, and then back away without touching the floor.

If you are trained, and you go in anyway: line in from the doorway, every time, tied off outside to something that isn't a stipe. Perfect trim, perfect buoyancy, frog kick only, stay high off the silt. Assume the ceiling can move. Do not go past the first room. Turn at a third of your gas, not half. And when the water clouds — because it will, no matter how careful you are — close your eyes, put your hand on the line, and follow it out. That is what it is there for.

One hazard the description hints at without stating: in rooms where the roof has held completely, gas from the mats may have collected against the ceiling over centuries. If your light finds a silver surface above you in a room — a pocket of trapped gas — **do not breathe it.** It is not air. It is very likely methane and hydrogen sulfide, and a single breath could kill you where you float. Keep your regulator in your mouth from the moment you go under the canopy to the moment your face is in the grey sky.

---

**The cisterns.**

Square shafts sunk through three or four layers of building. Stratified water that "does not mix." Mats thick on the walls. Silt at the bottom "not disturbed since the day the last person drew from it."

The description invites you to put your arm in. You may. But understand that the layered cold you feel is a sign of water that has not exchanged with the outside in a very long time — possibly saltier, possibly sulfurous, certainly without oxygen.
