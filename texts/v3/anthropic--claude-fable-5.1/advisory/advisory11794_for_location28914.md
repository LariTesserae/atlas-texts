**Preparing for the milk sea**

Read the description twice and notice what it does to you: it makes you want to be there, in the warm grey among the columns. Hold that feeling, because it is the reason to go, and then set it aside, because almost everything that will hurt you in this place is hidden inside the things that make it beautiful. The warmth, the stillness, the air kept in bells, the patient coating of every surface — each is a hazard wearing the face of a comfort. Prepare accordingly.

---

**1. It is a mountain first**

Before it is a sea, before it is a city, it is three and a half thousand metres of altitude, and the rim you cross to reach it is higher still. Treat the approach as a mountaineering trip, because it is one.

Do not go up fast. Spend at least two nights around 2,500 metres and another at 3,000 before you cross the rim. Learn what altitude sickness feels like in yourself — the specific headache, the nausea, the shortness of breath lying down — so that you can recognise it later when it matters more. Carry acetazolamide if you tolerate it. Know the signs of fluid on the lungs and swelling in the brain and understand that the only treatment is descent, and that from the caldera floor "descent" means going *up* over the rim first, which is a cruel arrangement. If someone in your party is deteriorating, that climb has to happen while they can still do it.

The inner faces of the rim steam. That means fumaroles, hot ground, sulphur crusts that look like solid rock and are not, and gas pooling in hollows on still nights. Sleep on the rim high and in the wind, never in a dip. Descend the inner slope in daylight only, testing every step, and expect your boots to suffer.

The sun at this height is thin and vicious. Off the white surface of the sea it comes at you from below as well as above. Bring proper glacier glasses and use them from the moment you top the rim. Sunburn on the underside of your chin and inside your nostrils is a real thing here. Cover everything.

And it is cold. The water is warm; the air is not. A night at 3,500 metres beside a steaming sea is damp, windy, and well below freezing on the exposed rock. The single most dangerous transition you will make, over and over, is climbing out of bath-warm water into that air with your body soaked and your core already stressed. Have dry clothes and a windproof shell waiting at the exit point, in a sealed bag, every time. Have a hot drink ready. Do not linger wet on the shore admiring the steam. People die of hypothermia beside hot springs.

---

**2. The water is not water as you know it**

It is salt, silica, lime, dissolved gas, and heat. Each is a separate problem.

*Heat.* Most of the sea is body-warm; parts are hotter; near vents it will scald. The insidious thing about body-warm water is that you cannot cool yourself in it. Swimming is work; work makes heat; the heat has nowhere to go. You will sweat and not notice, and dehydrate faster than you would in a desert, and the first you will know of it is confusion and cramp, which are also the first signs of several other things that kill divers. Drink far more than feels reasonable — before, during, after. Fresh water does not exist in the sea; it comes down from the snow on the rim in streams, and you should establish exactly where those are and how much you can carry before you plan a single dive. Learn the vent map from the locals and respect it absolutely. Keep a bare hand out in front of you when moving into any unfamiliar current so that it, and not your face, discovers the hot water first.

Do not bring a wetsuit for warmth. You will cook. Bring a thin full-body skin — lycra, or a light Kevlar-blend if you can find one — for one reason only: the floor is glass.

*The rock.* This is fresh basalt, cooled fast, with a rind of natural glass that has never been touched by sand or weather. Every edge is a blade. The description says it will open a hand; believe that literally. Gloves are not optional — cut-resistant, full-fingered. Boots with thick soles and covered ankles. Knee and elbow protection is not excessive. And then understand that you *will* still get cut, because you will be moving by touch in the dusk, and prepare for what comes after: this is warm, mineral-rich water full of vent bacteria of kinds no one has catalogued. A small cut here is a serious wound. Bring far more antiseptic and dressings than seems proportionate, an oral antibiotic you know how to use, and the discipline to clean and cover every scratch the moment you surface. Watch for redness spreading. Ear infections are all but guaranteed for anyone who spends days in warm water; bring drying drops (the alcohol-and-vinegar kind) and use them after every exit.

*The chemistry.* The sea tastes of eggs and iron — hydrogen sulphide, iron, probably a fair bit of carbon dioxide dissolved and coming off as those threads of bubbles. In the open water, moving, this is mostly a matter of stinging eyes and a flat metallic taste. Keep your mask sealed. Do not swallow. Rinse your eyes with fresh water after. If you have any skin condition, expect it to flare; the locals' skin has had generations to make peace with this water and yours has had none.

*Buoyancy.* It is salty and heavy with dissolved solids. You will float much higher than you expect and will need more weight than any table tells you. Any depth gauge calibrated for seawater or fresh water will be wrong here. Test everything in the shallows before you go anywhere.

**3. Breathing, which is the whole problem**

Here is the thing to fix in your mind: the people of this city are not doing what you will be doing. They hold their breath for lengths of time that should not be possible, and the old ones do not surface at all, and there is a bluish cast along their ribs that you have been told not to ask about. Whatever is happening to them is not available to you. You are a land animal visiting a people who are, slowly and without fuss, ceasing to be one. Nothing about their way of living in the water is a model for yours.

This has consequences.

*The bells are not air.* Read the description again: the vent breathes gas, the dome traps the gas, the gas fills the room. That is not atmosphere leaking down from the surface. That is volcanic exhalation — carbon dioxide, sulphides, perhaps methane, perhaps some fraction of something you can use, perhaps not. The people sleep and cook in it; their bodies apparently tolerate what yours may not. A stale, warm room that smells of eggs and iron is exactly what a lethal atmosphere smells like, and a room with very little oxygen and no carbon dioxide smells like nothing at all and simply makes you sit down and not get up. Never enter a bell and breathe its air without testing it. Carry an oxygen cell and a hydrogen-sulphide monitor — small, cheap, and the single most important items in your kit. Until you have tested a particular bell, keep your own regulator in your mouth inside it. Never carry a flame into one. Do not sleep in one unless you have tested it that night and will trust the reading with your life, and even then sleep with your cylinder beside you and a companion who has agreed to wake you.

You will find this awkward. The bells are the heart of the city's indoor life, the place where people sit close and talk in the closed-mouth language, and you will be the guest who sits among them breathing from a tank and cannot join the conversation. Accept it. This is not a place where you get to belong.

*Diving at altitude.* You will need compressed air or something like it — you cannot free-dive a city that lives at ten to forty metres, and no amount of training will get you close to what a child here does casually. But diving at 3,500 metres is not diving at sea level. The air at the surface is about two-thirds as thick, and your body's tolerance for the nitrogen it has absorbed is set against that thin air, not against the pressure you are used to surfacing into. The practical rule of thumb is that every dive here counts as roughly half again as deep for the purpose of decompression: twenty metres behaves like thirty; the deep end of the city at forty behaves like sixty. Bring a dive computer with a true altitude mode and check that its range covers 3,500 metres — many stop at 3,000. If you do not know how to use altitude tables, learn before you come, or do not come. Stay in the upper city on the shelf. Keep dives short and shallow and surface intervals long. Two modest dives a day is plenty; three is asking for something.

And then the thing nobody remembers: leaving means climbing. Going over the rim after diving is the same as flying after diving — you ascend into thinner air with nitrogen still in your tissues and it comes out of solution where it should not. Plan at least a full day, preferably two, of no diving before you begin the climb out. Build this into the schedule at the start and do not let the beauty of the last day talk you out of it.
