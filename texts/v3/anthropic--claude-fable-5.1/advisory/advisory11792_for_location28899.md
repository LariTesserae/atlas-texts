# Preparing for the Warm Plateau

## First, understand what you are visiting

You are not visiting a place so much as a moment. Everything the description tells you about this ground — the unrounded edges, the water that has not yet found a channel, the fist-sized moss, the birds who have come for a warmth that is leaving — says that this is a landscape perhaps two to five years into its existence, and that the particular arrangement of things you will see there has a shelf life measured in a handful of seasons. If you go back later, you will find something else. Prepare for it the way you would prepare to see an eclipse or a spawning run: something that will be there when you arrive and that will not wait for you.

The second thing to understand is that nothing about this place is dramatic in the way that would make you careful. There is no lava, no eruption, no wall of ice. It is grey and wet and quiet, and it will kill you gently, through a fall you didn't see coming, a night that got colder than you had gear for, or a slow suffocation you mistook for the fatigue of altitude. The dangers here are the sort you have to think about in advance because nothing in the moment will announce them.

## The air

You will be very high. The description does not give a number, but cloud at knee height on a plateau with older snow-capped mountains visible *below* the rim suggests you are somewhere above four thousand meters and possibly well above that. An ordinary body cannot simply appear at that altitude and function. You will be short of breath, your head will ache, you will not sleep properly, and your judgment — the one faculty you most need on ground like this — will be the first thing to degrade.

If you have any control over your point of arrival, arrive low. Come in on the older mountains beyond the rim, or on the slopes below the lip of new rock, and spend two or three days ascending. If you must arrive on the plateau itself, plan to do very little for the first twenty-four hours: set up shelter close to where you came in, drink, breathe, and watch. Do not walk into the interior on your first day. You will be tempted, because the interior is where the birds are and the birds are what you came to see, and because the ground looks flat and simple. It is neither.

Learn the warning signs before you go. A headache that worsens rather than improves, nausea, a cough that produces frothy or pink sputum, confusion, an inability to walk a straight line — any of these mean you descend, immediately, by whatever route you came, and you do not argue with yourself about it. There is no one here to make that decision for you.

Carry a simple pulse oximeter if you can. Carry acetazolamide if you know how to use it. Carry dexamethasone and know that it is for emergencies only. These are not heroic measures; they are the ordinary kit of anyone who works high.

## The ground

You have probably never walked on rock like this. Fresh lava comes in two textures, and the description mentions both. The ropy kind — smooth, folded, looking like poured taffy — is the easier to walk on, and even that will have glassy crusts that shatter under a boot and drop you ankle-deep into a hollow full of edges. The blocky kind is worse: loose, sharp, unstable, every piece of it capable of turning under you and every edge capable of opening skin and fabric. People who work on young lava fields wear leather gloves not for warmth but because they know they will fall, and when you fall on this you put your hands down onto glass.

So: heavy boots with stiff soles and high ankles. The soles will be shredded; bring boots you are willing to ruin. Leather work gloves over your insulating gloves. Trousers with reinforced knees, or knee pads under them, because you will kneel to look at the moss and the rock will go through ordinary fabric in a week. Gaiters, both for the wet and to keep the edges off your shins.

Then the fissures. The gaps between blocks go down farther than you would want to test. Some of these are simple cracks. Some open onto the drained tubes that fed the flow, which can be many meters deep, with floors of shattered rock and walls you cannot climb. The surface over a tube can be thin enough to sound hollow under a footfall, and thin enough to break. Walk with a pole and use it: probe ahead of you across any surface that looks smooth, and listen. A dull thud is rock. A ring or a boom is a roof.

Never move across ground you cannot see. This sounds obvious until you are standing in cloud that comes and goes every few minutes and the next swell is *right there*. Wait. The cloud will lift. Watch the ground the whole time it is lifted, choose your line, and move only while you can see where your feet go. When it closes in again, stop. This will make you slow. Slow is correct here.

And the ground moves. Every few days some part of the plateau settles, and you have no way of predicting which part. Do not shelter under overhangs. Do not camp on a block that is bridging a gap, or in a depression that is obviously a subsidence. Choose flat, low-relief, ropy ground for sleeping, well away from the larger fissures, and accept that "safe" here is a relative term. When you hear the door closing in the distant part of the house, stop where you are and wait for the birds to resume before you do.

## The water

The wet is constant and it will get into everything. Down insulation is useless here — it will be soaked by condensation within a day and stay soaked. Everything you rely on for warmth should be synthetic. Bring a shelter that handles condensation well, with real ventilation, and expect to wake with the inside walls running anyway. Pack a small towel or sponge just for wiping the tent. Keep your sleeping bag in a dry bag whenever you are not in it.

The pools are the specific hazard. Black water on black rock, invisible except as a faint lighter patch reflecting the cloud, and in the morning skinned with ice that looks exactly like the frost on the rock around it. Some are a hand across. Some are the size of a room, and you do not know how deep. Falling into cold water at altitude in wind is a fast route to hypothermia, and there is nothing here to build a fire with. Probe every flat dark surface before you step on it. When the ice is on, be doubly suspicious of anything smooth.

Drinking water is plentiful but not necessarily clean. Rain and condensation are pure enough, but water that has been sitting on fresh volcanic rock can pick up fluoride, sulphate, and dissolved metals, and it can run acidic. Collect your water from the drips under overhangs rather than from the standing pools — it has spent the least time on the rock. Filter it, and if you have pH strips, test it before you drink much. Boil what you can. A liquid-fuel stove, not a canister one: canisters lose pressure in the cold and at altitude, though you may find, as one small mercy, that a canister set on the warm rock does better than it has any right to.

## The warmth, and what may come with it

The description mentions vapor rising at the warm spots and flies emerging from the cracks. What it does not mention is gas, and you should not assume there is none. Young lava fields breathe. They release carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide, sometimes hydrogen sulphide and hydrogen chloride, through exactly the kind of fissures that here are weeping water and feeding insects. The wind on the plateau is probably strong enough to disperse most of it most of the time, which may be why no smell is noted. But wind drops at night, and cold night air is dense, and carbon dioxide is denser still. It pools in low places. It has no smell.

So: do not sleep in a depression, however sheltered it looks. Do not sleep close to the strongest warm spots, however inviting the dry rock. If you can carry a small carbon dioxide alarm — the kind sold for confined-space work — carry it and keep it beside your head at night. If you cannot, learn the signs: a heaviness in the chest, rapid breathing that doesn't feel like altitude, a headache that arrives suddenly, tingling, a metallic taste. Any of these, especially at night or in still air, and you get up and move to higher, more open ground. Do not lie down to think about it.

You may notice a sulphur smell at some of the vents. That is not, by itself, an emergency, but it tells you the mountain is still exhaling, and you should give those places the respect you would give an open flue.

## The birds

Everything about how you behave near the colony should start from one fact: those birds are spending nothing they do not have to. They have flown a very long way. They are sitting still because sitting still is how they stay alive. A bird that you flush — that you frighten into the air — burns energy it may not be able to replace, and in this place, in this cold, that can be the difference. You could, without meaning to, kill a few hundred of them just by walking through.

Do not walk into the lattice. Stay at the edge where the ground goes cold, and be content with what you can see from there, which is everything. Approach slowly, low, and stop the moment you see heads turn toward you rather than toward the rock. If you want to photograph or watch closely, do it lying down at the margin and stay there for an hour before you move again. They will accept a still shape faster than a moving one.

When the birds go still all at once and hold it for a slow count of thirty, go still with them. Something is above you in the cloud that they know about and you do not. It is almost certainly not interested in you, but you do not know that, and in any case it is a courtesy: they are showing you how to live here, and the least you can do is pay attention.

The scavengers in the fissures will find you. They are small and probably not dangerous, but they will investigate your camp at night, and they will take anything you leave out. Hang or seal your food. Keep your boots inside the shelter. Do not feed them, even to get a look at them; you would be teaching them a lesson about people that the next visitor will pay for.

If you find a dead bird with a ring, look at it, and if you have a notebook, copy the numbers down as carefully as you can even though you cannot read them. Do not take the ring. Leave the body to the mouths it feeds.

## The green

You will be tempted to touch the moss. Do not. That fist-sized cushion may be a decade old and one of a few dozen founders for what will, in three centuries, be the vegetation of this entire plateau. The grey-green stain in the lee of the blocks is even more fragile — a lichen or a proto-moss with no thickness to speak of, which a boot heel removes completely and which will not come back in your lifetime. The lee of the larger blocks is also where you will want to shelter, because that is where the wind is broken. Look before you sit. Look before you put a pack down. Choose bare rock, and there is a great deal of bare rock to choose from.

The rosette you may or may not find: if you find it, leave it exactly as it is, and do not mark the spot in any way that would lead someone else to it.

## Finding your way

The cloud will take your horizon away for most of every day. On ground with no landmarks, no soil, no vegetation, and every block looking like every other block, this is genuinely disorienting, and people have walked in circles on lava fields in far better weather than this.

A compass may not help you. Fresh basalt is iron-rich and can hold strong local magnetic anomalies that will swing a needle ten or twenty degrees from one block to the next. Take readings on the move and average them, and do not trust any single bearing. Any positioning device you bring from your own world will be talking to satellites that are not there. Learn the ground while the cloud is up: note the direction and shape of the swells, note where the birds are relative to where you came in, note where the aircraft passes and where the sodium light rises in the evening — that light is the one fixed point in this world and it is always northeast. Navigate by that when you can see it, and by memory when you cannot.

If you must mark a route, use something you will carry back out. Bamboo wands with flagging, a few dozen of them, placed as you go in and collected as you come out. Do not build cairns. You would be the first person to put a made thing on this rock, and the point of this place is that no one has.

## Sleeping

The nights are the hardest part and the most beautiful. The cold will be deep — well below freezing — and the frost will fall on you like a dust. The rock underneath, though, is warm, and if you have chosen your site well you may find that a sleeping pad on warm rock is more comfortable than anything you expected. Use a pad with insulation anyway: warm is not the same as dry, and you will be lying on a surface that weeps. A vapor-barrier liner in your bag will keep your own moisture from wicking into the insulation; it will feel clammy and it will save you.

Bring more warmth than you think you need. At altitude, in wind, wet, an ordinary body loses heat faster than any table will tell you. Have a full spare set of dry base layers sealed in a dry bag that you do not open except to change into them for sleep. Put your damp day layers inside the bag with you if you want them warm in the morning; otherwise they will freeze.

Sleep with your alarm, your headlamp, and your boots within reach. If the ground moves in the night, you want to be able to get up and see.

## The engine and the light

These are not hazards, but they should change how you think about where you are.

You are on someone's world. There is an industrial facility a day or two to the northeast running all night, and aircraft crossing overhead on a schedule that suggests a regular route. This rock is new enough and high enough that it is of no use to anyone yet, but that does not mean no one is watching it. A fresh volcanic plateau of this size would, in most worlds, be something geologists have instruments on — a seismometer bolted to a block somewhere, a camera, a gas sensor, a satellite pass every few days. Do not be surprised if you find one. Do not touch it. Assume that an aircraft flying over on a clear day could see your shelter, and that a stranger camping on a hazardous volcanic site may be a matter of official interest to someone. You cannot read their writing. You may not be able to speak to them. Behave in a way that would look, from above, like someone who knows what they are doing and intends no harm.

Have an exit plan that does not depend on your own means of travel working. If you are stranded, the light in the northeast is where people are. Getting there means descending off the plateau — the lip you came up over is the only known route down — and crossing an unknown distance of ground you have not seen, at altitude, in cold, without resupply. It is possible. It would be very hard. Carry enough food for at least twice the time you intend to stay, and carry it in a form that can be eaten cold.

## What to bring, gathered

Stiff-soled boots you can destroy. Leather over-gloves. Reinforced knees or pads. Gaiters. Full synthetic insulation, more than seems reasonable, in layers. A hard shell that actually keeps out water. A vapor-barrier liner. A four-season shelter with good ventilation. An insulated sleeping pad. Sealed dry bags for everything. A liquid-fuel stove and fuel for melting and boiling. A water filter and pH strips. A trekking pole or two, used constantly. A first-aid kit heavy on wound care — closures, dressings, antiseptic, tape — because the rock will cut you. Altitude medication, understood. A pulse oximeter. A carbon dioxide alarm if you can get one. A headlamp and spare batteries kept warm. Sunglasses; the overcast does not stop the ultraviolet up here. Retrievable route markers. A notebook, kept dry. Food for twice your planned stay. Something to occupy your mind during the long hours when the cloud is down and there is nothing to do but listen to the drips.

Do not bring anything you intend to leave. Do not bring anything you intend to take.

## What it will be like

Grey. Wet. Quiet in a way that is made entirely of small sounds — drips, wind, the tick of cooling stone, the click of a beak. You will be cold and short of breath and slightly stupid from the altitude for most of your stay, and much of your time will be spent sitting still waiting for the cloud to lift, which it does, and then closes again. You will see less than you hoped and it will take longer than you planned.

And then the cloud will part, and the ground will fall away from you in swells to a rim you didn't know was there, and beyond the rim the old mountains with their snow and their real valleys, and you will understand that you are standing on something that is younger than your boots. You will see the lattice of birds go on farther than seemed possible, each one a body's length from the next, each one sitting in its small dark circle of bare rock while the frost falls around it. You will hear the engine cross above you and see the one straight line in the world unravel and drift east. And at the edge of night the sodium light will come up in the northeast and you will know exactly where you are, which is on the last piece of ground in that world that no one has walked on yet, watching the first tenants of an unfinished building eat their way through a warmth that will be gone before the moss has grown a hand's width.

Stay three or four days. That is enough to see the cycle turn twice, to hear the mountain move at least once, and to get out before the altitude and the wet wear you down to the point of making a mistake. Leave the way you came, over the lip that has not yet decided on a shape. Take your wands with you. Take the numbers off the ring, if you copied them. Take nothing else.
