## Preparing for the Plateau

Start from the premise that this place is not dangerous in any of the ways you have trained for, and is dangerous in three or four ways you probably haven't. Nothing there wants to eat you. Nothing there will chase you, poison you, or ambush you. The whole living population of the place, per square kilometer, weighs less than your pack. What the plateau does instead is very simple: it is high, it is cold, it is wet, it is blind for half of every day, and it does not end. Prepare for those four things and the rest is a matter of temperament.

---

### Before you go up

**Acclimatize.** The description doesn't give an altitude, but a sky that is "a harder blue than it should be" and a noon sun that "puts no heat into the stone" tells you what you need to know: you are well above the point where an unacclimatized body starts to fail. The treeline is your staging camp. Spend at least two nights there, better three. Go up slowly, and go up early in the day, so that you reach the first terraces while the morning is still clear and can see what you're walking into. If you have a preferred pharmaceutical for altitude, bring it; if you don't, bring aspirin at minimum, and know the signs of the headache that doesn't stop.

**Mark the trees.** The last trees are the only landmark on the approach that will not slowly change shape while you are gone. Everything above them is being built and unbuilt. Flag your ascent route heavily and redundantly from the treeline down, because you may come back to it a month after you meant to, from a direction you did not leave in. Leave a cache there: food, fuel, dry clothing. You will want dry clothing more than you can currently imagine.

**Provision for a month.** People go up for a week and come down after a month. This is stated flatly and you should treat it flatly. Whether this is because the place is vast and disorienting, or because something about it doesn't add up the way a normal landscape does, is not your problem to solve on the way in — your problem is that you need thirty days of food and stove fuel and you need to carry it, because there is nothing up there to eat and nothing up there to burn. Not one stick. The tallest plant on the plateau is ankle-high and half stone already.

**Tell someone your intended edge.** The plateau has edges — or an edge — where the terraces stop and the water falls into the fog. If you can, decide in advance which escarpment you're aiming for and when you expect to reach it. It's a fragile plan, but it gives whoever is waiting for you a direction.

### The body

**You will be wet for the entire duration.** Not soaked, not immersed — wet the way the stone is wet, a permanent film of it. Your boots will never dry. Your gloves will never dry. The fog is water, the walls are water, the floor of every tunnel is invisible ankle-deep water, and the air itself has "a kind of pressure" of dripping. Plan your clothing accordingly:

- Wool and synthetics only. Nothing that holds water and chills — no down, no cotton, nowhere near your skin.
- Footwear that drains and that you can walk in wet for days: something between a boot and a wading shoe, with an aggressive but *soft* sole. Travertine is polished smooth in the channels and coated with algal film around every pool; hard rubber and metal both skate on it. Neoprene socks under wool socks. Change and wring your socks every single time the fog lifts.
- **Trench foot is your most realistic injury.** After a week of feet that never dry, the skin starts to go. Every afternoon, when the wind clears the fog and the sun comes sideways for two or three hours, take your boots off, dry your feet, powder them, and let them see the light. This is not optional. Treat that afternoon window as the medical appointment it is.
- Gloves in layers: a thin waterproof shell you can touch walls through, and dry wool underneath in a sealed bag for camp.

**The lime is alkaline and it is on everything.** Days of contact with lime-saturated water will dry and crack the skin of your hands and lips faster than any ordinary wet environment. Bring a heavy barrier cream — the thick kind, lanolin or petrolatum — and use it every night. Bring a small bottle of vinegar or a bag of citric acid; more on this below, but a dilute rinse of your hands at the end of the day will neutralize what the water has left on you.

**Eyes.** Two threats. The first is glare: a hundred thousand square kilometers of pale wet stone, every pool a mirror, under a high sun. When the fog is off, the place is as bright as a snowfield. Bring proper dark glasses with side coverage, and a spare pair. The second is the water itself, which is hard enough that a splash in the eye will sting and leave a residue. Carry fresh water for rinsing.

**Breathing.** The fog "tastes faintly of chalk," which means you are inhaling a fine suspension of calcium carbonate for hours a day. It's not toxic. It is not nothing, either, over weeks. A buff or a light mask over the mouth and nose during the fog hours costs you very little and your lungs will thank you.

**Cold.** The stone is always the temperature of the air, the air is cold, and there is no dry ground anywhere. You will sleep on a wet surface every night. An insulated pad is the single most important piece of camp equipment you own — thick, closed-cell or a very good inflatable, and a second thin one under it as a sacrifice to the damp. Your sleeping bag must be synthetic. A tent is worth its weight for the wind alone, which comes every afternoon and does not always leave.

### Water

Here is the thing nobody tells you about a place made entirely of water: you can't easily drink it.

The pool water is not poisonous. It is saturated with dissolved lime, and drinking it is roughly like drinking the hardest well water you've ever tasted, times several. Small amounts are fine. A week of it will unsettle your gut and leave you feeling thirstier than when you started, and it will fur the inside of your bottles and your stove in days. A standard filter does nothing against dissolved minerals.

But notice what the description says about the rain: it "adds almost nothing to the water in the pools — the water in the pools comes from below." The rain and the fog are fresh. They came from the sky, not from the deep layer. So:

- **Harvest the fog and rain.** A tarp pitched at an angle, or better, a length of fine mesh strung on cord — a fog net — will collect clean, soft water through the fog hours and the evening drizzle. This is your primary supply. It will be cold and it will taste of nothing, which after the chalk will feel like a luxury.
- **Use pool water for washing, and drink it only when you must**, choosing the fast-running rust-colored channels over the still pools, since the standing water has had longer to saturate.
- **Carry a small quantity of vinegar or citric acid** and descale your cookware, bottles, and filter every few days. This is also what you will use on your zippers, your buckles, your camera lens, and your boot eyelets, all of which will grow a white crust and begin to seize. The plateau calcifies everything, including you. The description mentions spider silk, moss, bird droppings, and snail shells all turning to stone; your gear is not exempt. It won't happen in a night — "a hair's thickness in a season" — but the film that dries on a metal surface after a day in the fog is enough to stiffen a zipper, and after a week it's enough to stop one.

### The daily rhythm

The plateau has a schedule and you should adopt it entirely. Fighting it is how people get hurt.

**Dawn to mid-morning: clear.** This is when you move and when you look. Get up in the dark. Be on your feet at first light with your route for the day already chosen. Climb to a high point — a fallen tower, a terrace crest — and take your bearings before the pools start to breathe. Sketch what you see. Note the direction of the escarpment, if you can see it; note the tallest towers, since they are the only things that will still be visible above the fog when it comes. You have perhaps two to three hours of good visibility. Use them for distance.

**Mid-morning to early afternoon: fog.** "So dense you cannot see the wall you are touching." When it closes in, stop. Sit down. Eat. Do not attempt to make progress in the fog unless you are following a channel with a raised curb you can keep one hand on the entire way and you know exactly where it goes — and even then, know that channels migrate, flood, and undercut ledges. The fog is not the time to be near the edge of anything. If you are traveling with others, you are roped together during the fog, full stop. Not because anyone will fall far — most terraces are shoulder- or head-high — but because a person who steps three meters away from you in this fog is gone, and the lime-deadened air means you will not hear them call. Sound "travels ten meters and stops." Shouting is useless. Whistles carry a little further. A tug on a rope carries perfectly.

**Late afternoon: wind and light.** The wind tears the fog off and for two or three hours the whole city is lit sideways and you can see forty kilometers. This is your second travel window, and your only drying window. Priorities, in order: get your boots off and your feet in the sun; hang everything wet where the wind can get at it; take your second set of bearings and correct your morning's plan; then move, if there's time. Photograph now if you're going to. Do not let the light seduce you into walking until it fails. It fails quickly, and the rain comes behind it.

**Evening: rain and dark.** Soft rain, "like the fog decided to fall." Be camped before it starts. Choose your site with the collapses in mind: not under a terrace lip, not on a dome (they are cracked and they roof hollow chambers), not on the white sludge rim of a young pool, which will not hold you and which you will scar permanently. The best camps are on old, dry, grey-green stone in the lower districts, or in the mouth of a roofed channel where you have a wall at your back and the wind is cut — accepting that you will be dripped on all night and that the water on the floor is real even though you can't see it.

Sleep early. There is nothing to do in the dark here but listen to the dripping and, if you're unlucky, the falcon.

### Moving through the city

**Walk the streets, not the balustrades.** The channels — narrow, curbed, floors polished smooth — are the natural paths, and the water built them to be walked in, more or less. The terrace lips are another matter. They are "waterlogged past their strength" and they break off "with a sound like a wet cloth dropped on a floor." Do not stand on the rounded edges of terraces. Do not sit on them. Test any lip you must cross with a pole before you trust it with your weight.

**Poles.** Bring two, with rubber tips, not carbide. Carbide points skitter on travertine and chip it; rubber grips. Use them constantly in the tunnels to find the floor and probe for straws that have broken and re-cemented into "a lattice, a filigree." The description says the lattice doesn't break. Assume that's true of the old lattice and not the new.

**Beware the beautiful young stone.** The whitest, newest surfaces are the softest. The turquoise shallow pools have rims of sludge that "will be walls in three hundred years" — right now they are nothing, and a boot in them sinks and leaves a print that will be preserved for as long as the plateau exists. The old districts, ochre and grey-green and rust, are the sound ones.

**The deep pools.** Some read as black. You have no idea how deep they are, and the water is very cold, and the sides are lime-smooth. Don't wade into anything you can't see the bottom of. Don't wash in them. A fall into a deep pool alone, in the fog, in cold water, with lime-slick walls, is the plateau's one genuinely quick way to kill you.

**The tunnels.** Roofed channels where the terraces met overhead. Light comes from gaps above, "blue-white and moving as the fog passes." The water is ankle-deep, invisible, and dead still. The walls have never been rained on and the scallops are "sharp-edged, delicate, almost translucent." Two things: go slowly, because you cannot see the floor and every step you take sends ripples to the walls and back and that is the only way you'll know where the water is; and keep your head down, because the ceiling is hung with meter-long straws that are the finest and most fragile things on the plateau, and you will snap them with a careless movement, and they took a thousand years. When the pressure changes and every drop falls at once "like a handful of sand thrown on a floor," stand still. It's only water.

**The edge.** You will find it as the terraces step down and the last pool spills into fog and the sound of falling water goes on for a long time and then doesn't come back. In fog this edge is undetectable until you are on it. When the terraces start to trend steadily downward and the air begins to move up toward you — the afternoon wind rises from the heated flats *below* the escarpment, so an upslope wind is a sign you're approaching a drop — stop. Wait for the afternoon clearing. Look at it with your feet planted.

### Navigation

Nothing you bring up will map it. Travelers "come back down a week later or a month later talking about the same thing from different sides, and the accounts do not join." Whatever that means, the practical consequence is: **do not rely on anyone else's route, and do not expect your own to be consistent.**

What works:

- **The sun.** When you can see it. Note its bearing at dawn against the tallest towers.
- **The wind.** Afternoon wind comes up from below the escarpment. It is your most reliable pointer toward an edge — any edge.
- **The stone's color.** White and cream are young and high; grey-green and rust are old and low. Downhill and inward is older. If you want out, you want younger stone, and you want it in the direction the wind comes from.
- **The towers.** The only landmarks that stand above the fog. Name them yourself. Sketch their profiles. Do not trust that the one you named yesterday will look the same from the other side, but it is more than nothing.
- **Your own journal.** Write every evening. The walls are identical, the fog erases distance, and after four days you will not remember which cream-colored lane you came down without notes. Record time, weather, color of stone, direction of channels, what you saw. This is the only account of the plateau that will be true for you.

What doesn't work: cairns (they calcify into the city within a few seasons and become indistinguishable from it), flagging tape (wet, coated, gone), footprints (either preserved forever in sludge or nonexistent on hard stone), and shouted contact.

### The living things

You do not need to prepare for them. You need to prepare to not disturb them, because they are so few.

The flies are slow and harmless and rise in clouds when you step near a pool. Insect repellent is dead weight. The spiders web the scallops and are the only architects here other than the water; walk around their webs. The bobbing grey-brown bird that walks underwater — you'll see one every day or two and never two together, and you'll find you've started looking for it. It nests in hollows in the towers. Don't put your head in. The falcon you will hear once, "absurdly loud in the hush," and then never again, and it will unsettle you more than a wolf would somewhere else, because it will be the loudest thing you've heard in a week.

The snails in the tunnels are on the pale moss along the floor seams. Rice-grain shells. Watch your feet.

Bring nothing that could seed. Nothing lives above the ankle here because the lime seals over every seed before it roots — but you don't want to be the one who tests that.

---

### What you leave

Understand this before you set foot on the first terrace: **the plateau records you.** Everything that stays wet gets coated. A dropped glove is a fossil next year. A footprint in the young rim of a pool is a footprint in the wall three centuries from now. Human waste does not decompose here — there is no soil, no warmth, no bacteria to speak of beyond the algal films — it calcifies. Bird droppings in the tower rooms have "begun to turn to stone." Yours will too.

So: pack out everything. Everything. Carry your waste down. Carry your food scraps down. Do not drop, bury, or leave. There is no "leave no trace" here in the ordinary sense; there is only "leave a trace that lasts forever" or "leave nothing," and the second is the only one you can defend.
