**Preparing for the vault**

Start by getting one thing straight in your head, because everything else follows from it: this is not a cave. It is a machine room that has been left alone long enough to start behaving like a cave, and it has the hazards of both without the courtesies of either. A cave has been what it is for a long time and has settled into it; a machine room has someone who checks on it. This place has neither. Nobody is coming down that ladder behind you on any schedule you can count on, and the room has no interest in whether you get back up it.

So the first preparation is a decision about time. You are going to be in there for less than an hour, and you should know before you touch the top rung what your turnaround is. I would say forty minutes from the bottom of the ladder to your hand back on the bottom of the ladder, and I would say that having already accounted for the fact that you will want to stay longer. You will. The place is hypnotic in the way that slow things are. That is exactly why you set the number in advance.

---

**The heat is the thing that will actually hurt you**

Everything else on the list — the bats, the guano, the pipes — is a hazard you can see and step around. The heat is not. The air is somewhere around forty degrees and it is close to saturated, and what that means in practice is that your body's one real cooling mechanism has been switched off. You will sweat. The sweat will not evaporate. You will lose water and gain nothing for it. The description says your collar is dark before your boots find the last rung, and that is the room telling you, politely, that it is already ahead.

Prepare for this like you would prepare for a sauna you cannot leave quickly:

Drink before you go down, a lot, and cold. Drink in the room, on a timer, not when you feel thirsty — by the time you feel thirsty in that air you are behind. Bring more water than seems reasonable; two liters for a forty-minute visit is not excessive, and some of it should have salt in it. If you can pre-chill a towel and wear it around your neck under your collar, do. It buys you minutes.

Know the signs you will not want to admit to: your heart racing while you stand still; a headache that arrives from nowhere; the moment you notice you have *stopped* sweating, which feels like relief and is the opposite; and the subtler one, which is that your thoughts get slow and agreeable and you find yourself thinking that another ten minutes would be fine. If you brought a companion — and you should have — you each watch the other for the agreeable phase. It is the phase that kills people, because it feels like calm.

And understand that the climb out is part of the exposure, not the end of it. That ladder is inside the same column of heat you descended through, and you will be going up it with wet hands, wet boots, fogged lenses, and whatever reserve you have left. This is why the turnaround is forty minutes and not sixty. You leave the room with enough in you to climb, not enough to just barely climb.

**What is in the air**

Three things, and you need to handle all three because they do not care about each other.

The first is what the bats leave behind. Decades of guano in a warm, wet, dark room is close to the ideal condition for *Histoplasma*, and the spores are invisible, weightless, and stirred up by almost nothing — a footstep in the dry lee of the manifold, the wind of the colony leaving. Breathe them and in two or three weeks you will have something that starts as flu and can go on to be a great deal worse. This is not a maybe. Treat the floor near the manifold the way you would treat asbestos.

The second is ammonia. That sharp sweet note the description says you had taken for machinery — that is nitrogen coming off the guano, and in a dense free-tailed roost it can reach concentrations that burn eyes and airways and, at the top of the range, can drop a person. You will smell it, which is good; you stop smelling it after a while, which is not.

The third is what the air *isn't*. A blasted vault with a single ventilation duct, warm rot on the floor, and a few thousand small mammals breathing overhead is a room where the oxygen can drift downward and the carbon dioxide can drift up. Probably not to a dangerous degree — the duct is working, the swifts in it are proof — but probably is not a word you want doing much work underground.

So: a half-face respirator with combination cartridges, particulate at the highest rating plus a cartridge rated for ammonia. Not a paper mask. Not a bandana. Fit-test it before you go, because a respirator that leaks at the jaw is decoration. Sealed goggles, not safety glasses, because the ammonia will find your eyes otherwise, and because the drip from the ceiling is guano-water. Accept now that the goggles will fog, buy the best anti-fog treatment you can, and plan on wiping them constantly with something you will throw away afterward. If you can lay hands on a small gas meter that reads oxygen and ammonia, bring it and glance at it; if it shrieks, you are done, no discussion.

The trade-off you are making is real: a respirator and goggles in forty-degree wet air are miserable, they trap heat against your face, and they shorten how long you can stay. Good. Let them.

**What is on the surfaces**

Assume every surface below the lamp line is wet, and every wet surface is either slick, contaminated, or hot.

The flowstone is the beauty of the place and the worst footing in it — calcite rills under a film of warm water are as slick as ice and less honest about it. The slurry floor is uneven. The standing water may have rat in it, which means leptospirosis, and it certainly has everything that has dripped through the colony. Do not wade. Do not kneel. Do not sit, even when the heat makes you want to, especially then. Rubber boots to the knee with a proper tread. Nitrile gloves under work gloves, so you can peel the outer pair if they get fouled and still have a barrier.

The pipes are district heating mains, which means the water inside them is well above boiling and under pressure, and the cladding on them is scarred, which means there are places where the insulation has failed and the steel underneath is at whatever temperature the water is. You will not know which places by looking. Keep a hand's width from the cladding at all times. The failed seal at the far end is pouring hot water onto a hot pipe and making steam; that corner is not for you. The manifold with its cart-wheel valves is not for you either. Those wheels are not decorative and they are not stuck, and a person who turns one, even a little, out of curiosity or because it seemed like it might relieve something, has just changed the pressure in a system that runs under a city. Do not touch the machinery. Do not touch the transformer cabinet. Do not touch any metal while standing in water, which is all the time.

Wear a disposable coverall over your clothes and plan to take it off at the top of the ladder and never see it again. The smell will be in whatever you wear underneath for days regardless.

**The colony**

Free-tailed bats are not aggressive and they are not interested in you. They are also a rabies reservoir, and a bat that ends up on you — a pup that has lost its grip, an adult that gets confused in the lamplight — is a bat you must not handle barehanded and an event you must report to a doctor when you get out, because post-exposure treatment works only if you take it. If one lands on you, freeze, let your companion brush it off with a gloved hand or a hat, and note the time.

More important: do not disturb them. This is for their sake and yours. A colony spooked in daylight will drop pups, will break into flight in a closed room where the only way out is the shaft you came down, and will fill the air with everything that was on the ceiling. Use red light on the ceiling if you must look, and look briefly. Keep your voice under the pumps. Move like the room moves — which is to say, barely. The description gets this right: nothing here happens quickly, and you should not be the thing that does.

The floor under the roost is theirs too, in a different way. The seething you can see in the guano is a food web that has been running for decades, and the cockroaches will climb your boots if you stand still long enough. They are not dangerous. They are, for most people, the moment the place stops being interesting and starts being something to leave. Decide before you go down how you are going to feel about a cockroach on your leg, because you will not have time to decide when it happens. The answer is: brush it off, don't stamp, keep going.

---

**The sound and the dark**

The pumps are loud enough to hurt over time and to hide the other sounds you want. Foam earplugs, the kind that take the edge off rather than block everything, so you can still hear your companion and still hear the chitter. Agree on hand signals before you descend, because the respirators and the noise together mean you will not be talking. You need at least: *I'm fine. Look. Time. Now.*

Two of the four sodium lamps are out. That means half the room is inference, and your headlamp is not optional. Bring two lights per person, one on the head, one in a pocket, with fresh cells in both, and treat the headlamp failing as the same event as the timer running out. Do not rely on the room's own light; it was installed by people who expected nobody to be down there.

Your phone will not work, will fog, and will be ruined by the ammonia if it is out for long. Seal it in a bag and leave it in your pocket. If you want to remember the place, remember it.

---

**The choreography**

Go down in the late afternoon if you want to see the emergence, and understand that you are timing your forty minutes to end as the colony begins to leave — you do not want to be at the bottom of the shaft when a few thousand bats decide to go up it. Watch it start from the floor, then be on the ladder before the roar peaks, climbing against a tide that will part around you and will not, mostly, touch you. Or go down after full dark, when the ceiling is bare and sweating and you can see the straws in their ranks and the room is as quiet as it ever gets, and be gone before dawn brings them home.

Rest at the top of the shaft before you start, in the coolest air you can find. Go down slowly. At the bottom, stop, and give your goggles and your lungs and your nerve a minute to arrive. Then walk the open middle — the wide floor between one presence and the next — and stay there. The middle is where the room lets you be. Go to the flowstone between the pipes and crouch, don't kneel, and watch a terrace fill and spill. Look at the fern. Look at the ceiling once, with red light, and put the light down. Look at how the newest stone is exactly the width of one drop.

Drink. Check the time. Check your companion's eyes. When the timer says so, go, without the extra minute.

---

**After**

At the top: coverall off and bagged, outer gloves off and bagged, boots hosed before they go anywhere near a vehicle. Wash your face and hands before you touch your face. Shower as soon as there is a shower. Wash everything you wore in hot water, separately, and expect it to smell anyway.

For the next three weeks, notice how you feel. Fever, cough, tightness in the chest, a tiredness that doesn't fit what you did — go to a doctor and say the words *bat roost* and *histoplasmosis* in the first sentence, because it is not what they will guess. If a bat touched skin, you do not wait three weeks; you go the same day.

And then, some evening when you are somewhere cool and dry, you will find that you are thinking about the fern. The size of a hand, in a circle of light no wider than a dinner table, not growing and not dying, in a room that is slowly wrapping its own machinery in stone. That is the thing you went for. It is a place that is not finished, that has not learned it is exposed, and that is filling itself with life while the middle stays empty. You cannot stay in it. You can only visit, carefully, and leave before it notices you were there.
