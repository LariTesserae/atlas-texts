# Preparing for the shelf

## First, understand what you are preparing for

Most cold places are dry. That is what makes them survivable. Antarctica, high mountains, the winter interiors of continents — the air is cold and so it carries almost no water, and a person who dresses properly can stay dry, and a person who stays dry can stay warm. Almost everything the outdoor world knows about surviving cold is built on that single fact.

The shelf breaks it.

The air on the shelf is below freezing and it is saturated. Not humid — saturated, at the density of fog, everywhere, always. The rock beneath you is just above freezing and giving off steam. You will be standing in a cloud made of water that is a degree or two above zero, breathing air that is a degree or two below it, and every surface you touch will be wet, and nothing you carry will ever dry. Not your gloves, not your socks, not the inside of your sleeping bag, not your skin. You will arrive wet from the climb and you will leave wet and in between you will be wet.

Wet cold at zero degrees is more dangerous than dry cold at minus thirty. Water pulls heat out of a body twenty-five times faster than air does. Hypothermia here does not need a storm or a fall into a river. It needs you to stand still for an hour in the wrong clothes, which is exactly what the place invites you to do, because standing still and listening is the only way to understand it.

So: everything you bring, and everything you plan, should start from the question *how do I stay warm while permanently wet, in the dark, for weeks*. If your kit cannot answer that, do not go.

## The climb

You arrive from below. The last thousand metres are inside the cloud.

Assume the climb takes longer than any estimate. You will be moving over young basalt — sharp, unweathered, with edges that have never been rounded by anything — in visibility that drops to your own hands and then to nothing. There is no trail. Nothing has walked this way enough to make one, except the animals, and the animals came from somewhere and you may be able to find and follow their routes if you are patient. Look for polish. Look for places where the stone is worn faintly smoother than the stone beside it. Whatever wore it was heavy and went the same way many times, which means the way goes somewhere.

Wear leather or heavy synthetic on your hands from the moment you enter the cloud. The basalt has glass in it. You will put your hand down in the dark and you will not see what you are putting it on. A cut here will not dry, will not scab properly, and will sit in water for the rest of your stay. Knee protection too, and something over the shins. You will fall. Everyone falls.

The fog thickens as you climb and by the end you will not be able to see your feet. Do not use your light on the climb any more than you must; you want your eyes adjusted when you reach the top, and you want to arrive quietly. But a headlamp on the dimmest red setting, pointed at the ground directly ahead, is a reasonable compromise on the ascent. You are not yet among them. Once you level out, that changes.

You will know you have arrived when the ground stops rising and the sound arrives with it. Stop there. Sit down, put your back to a rock, and do nothing for as long as it takes for the roar to come apart into its parts. That may be an hour. Let it be an hour. You are learning the only language the place has.

## Clothing and warmth

Forget everything designed to keep you dry. It will fail within the first hour and then you will be wearing wet waterproofs, which are worse than wet wool.

Instead, dress to be wet and warm. That means:

**Wool against the skin, everywhere.** Wool is the only common material that holds meaningful warmth when saturated. Not merino base layers designed for hiking — heavier, denser wool, the kind fishermen wore. Two layers if you can move in them. Wool socks, several pairs, and rotate them not because they will dry but because a fresh wet sock is warmer than a compressed wet sock.

**A vapour barrier as the outermost layer, not the innermost.** Ordinary vapour barrier practice puts a waterproof layer against the skin to keep sweat out of insulation. Here, reverse the logic: your insulation is going to be wet regardless, so the point of the outer shell is windproofing and nothing else. There is wind on the shelf — the construction shelters from it, which means it exists — and wind moving over wet wool will kill you faster than anything else on this list. A simple, loose, fully windproof shell with no ambitions toward breathability.

**Nothing down. Nothing that loses loft when wet.** Synthetic fill if you must, and expect it to lose half its value. Closed-cell foam is your friend: it insulates when wet, does not absorb water, and can be cut into pads for sitting, sleeping, kneeling.

**Heat source.** You cannot carry enough fuel to stay warm for weeks, and a stove is a light and a noise and a smell in a place where you are trying to be none of those. Your heat source is the city. The mineral is warmer than the air. The rock near the springs is warmer still. The seal-things lie in the pools for a reason and you will end up doing something similar — not in the pools, never in the pools, but against the walls, in the lee, on the warm floors of the galleries with a foam pad between you and the stone. Sleep in the old districts where the construction is tallest and the heat has had the most stone to come up through. Sleep close to the walls. Sleep where the climbers are thickest, because they are also seeking warmth and they are better at finding it than you are.

**Sleeping.** A bag made of the heaviest wool blanket you can carry, or a synthetic bag you have already accepted will be sodden. Inside a bivvy sack, not a tent — a tent is a room, and you will want to hear. Expect to sleep badly and briefly and often. Expect to wake shivering and move to find warmer stone. This is normal here. It is what the animals do.

**Hands and feet.** These are where you will lose the fight if you lose it. Check them by touch every few hours — you will not be able to see them. Numb toes in wet boots at zero degrees is the beginning of trench foot, which will end your stay and possibly your feet. Wiggle constantly. Take boots off and press your feet against warm mineral whenever you stop. Carry a small tin of lanolin or heavy grease and coat your feet before you put wet socks on. It helps more than you would think.

## Light

You will want to bring a light. Bring one. Bring three, in fact, and batteries for all of them, and then understand that you will use them perhaps a dozen times in the entire visit, for a total of perhaps twenty minutes, and every one of those minutes will cost you something.

Light is the loudest thing on the shelf. Nothing here has ever seen the sun. The bats are blind by preference and the climbers may not have eyes at all, but something registers it — you will switch on a lamp and the sound will fall away in a widening ring around you, and the silence will last long after the light is off, and the place will take an hour to forget. Whether it forgets entirely, you will not know. The water runs differently near you. Perhaps it runs more differently after a light.

So the discipline is this: you go dark. Completely. From the moment you level out until the moment you descend. Lights are for a fall, a wound, a piece of kit you cannot find by touch, and for one deliberate look at the city, once, if you decide you cannot leave without having seen it — and you should think hard about whether that is a thing you need, or a thing you merely want.

This means preparation before you go, and the preparation is not optional. Spend nights blindfolded before you travel. Not one night — a week of them. Learn to dress, eat, pack, find things in your bag, and walk in an unfamiliar room with no vision at all. Learn what your hands know that your eyes have always overruled. Learn to keep everything you own in exactly the same place so that you can find it without looking, because if you set something down on the shelf in the wrong spot you will spend a very long time feeling for it among the climbers.

Your eyes will invent things. The description warns you and you will still be surprised by how convincing the inventions are — faces in the fog, movement at the edge of the black, a grey glow that is sometimes real and sometimes not. After a day or two you will stop believing them. That is the right response. The place is not to be seen. Let your eyes go idle and give the work to your ears and your hands.

## Sound

Do not bring earplugs, or if you bring them, do not use them except to sleep, and reconsider even then.

Your hearing is your only reliable distance sense on the shelf. The roar is disorienting for the first hours and then it becomes a map. The hiss tells you where the water runs and how much of it. The groan tells you where the pools are and how full. The click tells you which surfaces are covered and how thickly. The rush of a colony startling tells you where a tower is and how tall. And silence — a patch of silence in a place that is never silent — tells you that something has moved through, and you will learn to notice the shape of the quiet before you learn to guess what made it.

You will get tired in a way you have not been tired before. Continuous noise, even noise that becomes background, is work for the brain, and combined with the dark and the cold and the disrupted sleep it will wear you down faster than the physical exertion does. Plan for this. Plan for shorter days than you would spend anywhere else. Plan to be stupid by the end of the first week, and to make the kind of mistakes stupid people make — dropping things, mis-stepping, forgetting to check your feet.

And listen for the whistle. Nobody knows what makes it. It comes from above, in the fog, over the towers, and after it the bats sometimes fall. You are considerably larger than a bat, and probably it has never considered anything your size, and probably that is enough. But do not climb the towers, and do not stand on high open ground with nothing above you, and when you hear it, be somewhere with a roof.

## Footing

Three surfaces, all of them bad.

**The basalt** is sharp and wet. Treat it as you would sea-cliff rock at low tide: assume any edge will cut, any block may rock, any surface with water running over it is slicker than it feels. Test with your foot before you weight it. Move slowly. Slow is the pace of everything here anyway.

**The mineral** is worse. It is hard as porcelain and smooth as porcelain and wet, and where the film grows on it, it is wet porcelain with a coating of something like the slime inside a drainpipe. The seal-things have polished the floors of the lower galleries smoother still. Boots with soft, sticky rubber — climbing-shoe rubber, not hiking-boot rubber — will help. Nothing will help enough. You will go down, and you will put a hand out, and the hand will land on something living, and you will need not to flinch, because flinching is how you go down the second time.

**The seal-paths** are the exception. Where they haul themselves, nothing grows; the film is worn off and the mineral is dull rather than glazed. Their routes are the safest ground on the shelf and they are also, for reasons that go beyond safety, the only ground you should be walking on. Learn to find them by touch: they are a fraction rougher, a fraction less slick, and they are worn into shallow troughs that your foot will find if you let it. Follow them. Where they go, you can go. Where they do not go, ask yourself why.

Do not go into the pools. The seal-things are in the pools and they are heavy and they are packed and they do not know what you are, and beneath them is whatever takes the pups. The water is warm and you will be very cold and it will be the most tempting thing on the shelf. It is not for you.

## Water and food

**You cannot eat anything here.** Not the film — it lives on the chemistry of the emerging groundwater and that chemistry is almost certainly sulphur or iron or something less friendly, and the organisms that eat it have been adapting to it for thousands of years and you have not. Not the climbers, for the same reason one step removed. Not the bats. Not anything. Carry every calorie you will need for the entire stay, plus a margin, plus a second margin for the climb down, which will be harder than the climb up because you will be tired and cold and clumsy.

Cold and wet burn calories at a rate that will surprise you. Budget for four to five thousand a day and carry dense food — fat and sugar, things that can be eaten wet and cold and by touch without preparation. You will not cook. Cooking is a light and a smell and a noise.

**Do not drink the spring water.** It is full of dissolved minerals in the act of depositing themselves as walls, which means it is at or past saturation with whatever those minerals are, and that is before you consider the question of what was *written into it*. Nobody understands the mechanism. Nobody should be drinking the mechanism.

**You can, however, drink the fog.** The fog is steam — water that has evaporated from the pools and condensed in the cold air — and steam leaves its minerals behind when it rises. Condensate is close to distilled. Hang a wide cloth, wool or cotton, somewhere the fog moves, and wring it out into a container every few hours. It is slow and it is cold and the water will taste of wool and faintly of the rock. It will keep you alive and it means you do not have to carry three weeks of water up a thousand metres of cliff. Test it on yourself in small amounts the first day and pay attention to your gut.

Do not collect water from where it has run over the mineral or through a colony. Fog only, caught from the air.

## Air

The uplift is cooling and the water coming through it is warm, and the film lives on chemistry, and all of that together suggests the springs may be venting gas along with water. Carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulphide, possibly others. The shelf is open to the sky and the wind moves, so in the open you are probably fine. Inside the galleries, in hollows where the water ran and left cells behind, in low places near the springs where a heavy gas could pool — less certain.

Add to this the bats. Thousands upon thousands, roosting in enclosed hollows for thousands of years, and the ammonia off their guano will be considerable in the upper cells, and there is a long list of things that live in bat colonies that you do not want in you.

So: stay out of enclosed spaces you cannot leave in two steps. Do not sleep in a cell with only one opening. Do not go deep into the towers. If you smell rotten eggs, or if you stop smelling rotten eggs after you were smelling them a moment ago, leave the space immediately — hydrogen sulphide deadens the sense of it at exactly the concentrations where it begins to matter. If you notice your breathing quicken for no reason, or a headache, or a fizz in your thoughts, move uphill and into the wind. A small gas detector is worth its weight if you can get one that works by touch or by a sound you can feel through your hand. Not a light. Not a beep.

Cover your mouth and nose with wool when a colony startles overhead. What comes down with the wings is not only wings.

## The animals

They will be on you. Accept this now.

The climbers are on every surface and you are a surface. You are warm and you are wet and you have a film on you too, of sweat and grease and dead skin, and they will find it. They will be on your boots within minutes and on your legs within an hour and inside your clothes by the end of the first night, and they will click, and you will feel their many small feet, and there is nothing to be done about it except to decide not to mind. They are grazers. They are not equipped to bite you, or if they are, not seriously. Brush them off gently before you sleep and again when you wake and understand that you will never be clear of them. In time you will stop noticing, the way you stop noticing the hiss. You may even come to find their weight a small comfort — evidence that you are among something.

The bats are not interested in you but they are numerous beyond imagining and they startle as a body. Do not stand directly beneath a tower when you are new here. Ten thousand wings coming down past your face in the dark, with the smell that comes with them, will make you do something sudden, and sudden is dangerous on this footing. Later you will know the sound that precedes the rush and you will have time to turn your face away.

The pale predators are the ones to think about. Dog-sized, slow, and taking climbers off the walls with a sound the description calls tearing paper. You will hear this sound. It is distinctive and it will be close. What you cannot know is whether anything that takes climbers would consider you, and the honest answer is that nothing on this shelf has ever encountered a thing your shape, so nothing has an opinion yet. Probably you are too big. Probably you are too strange. Probably. Carry something — a stout stick of hardwood, a metre and a half long, is more useful than any weapon that makes noise or light, and it doubles as a probe for footing. Keep your back to a wall when you sleep. Keep your face and hands covered. And listen for the tearing to stop, because a predator that has stopped feeding has started paying attention to something.

The seal-things will not attack you. They will crush you, if you are between them and where they want to be, without ever knowing they have done it. Stay out of the galleries when they are moving. You will hear them coming — a heavy, wet, hauling sound — and when you hear it, get to the side, get behind a corner, get up onto something if the mineral will let you. Their paths are your paths but they have right of way.

The thing in the pools and the thing in the fog: stay out of the pools, stay off the heights. That is all that can be said.

## The rules

The description gives them and they bear repeating, because you will be cold and tired and in the dark, and it is under those conditions that people stop following rules.

**Do not touch the water.** Do not dam it, divert it, drink from it, wash in it, put your hand in it to see how warm it is. Every trickle is building something. Stop a trickle and the wall stops, permanently, and the fault is there forever. You are one warm body among several hundred million, and you have the singular capacity among all of them to leave a scar that lasts as long as the city. Do not.

**Step where the seal-things step.** Not only for footing. Because where they step nothing is building, and where nothing is building you cannot break anything.

**Leave nothing behind.** Not a wrapper, not a thread, not a drop of urine near a spring. Whatever is written into the water is reading its environment closely enough to build plumb walls to a tolerance no craftsman would bother with, and you have no idea what it makes of foreign chemistry. Carry everything out. Relieve yourself at the edge, over the cliff, downwind, far from any flow.

**Do not take anything.** You will want a piece of the mineral. It is pale grey and porcelain-smooth and warm and it grows at the speed of a fingernail and it would be the most remarkable thing on any shelf at home. Breaking it off leaves a wound that grows back wrong. Leave it.

**Do not try to help.** You will see — hear — a seal-pup taken, a colony fall, something slow being eaten by something slower. The shelf is at saturation. Everything born here is in excess of what can live, and the excess is the mechanism. Your intervention, even if it worked, would change a rate that has held for longer than your species has had cities. Sit with it.

## The mind

This is the part most preparation guides leave out, and it is the part that will decide whether you leave the shelf well.
