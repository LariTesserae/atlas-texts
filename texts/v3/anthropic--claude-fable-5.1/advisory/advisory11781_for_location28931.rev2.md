**Before you go: understand what kind of place this is**

Most dangerous places announce themselves. This one does not. Nothing here will chase you, nothing is poisonous that you're told of, no one is hostile. The bowl will kill you the way cold water kills — quietly, by degrees, while you're paying attention to something else. So the first preparation is a change of posture. You are not going somewhere to see something. You are going somewhere that will be working on you the whole time you're there, and your job is mostly to notice that and keep ahead of it.

Three things are doing the work: the altitude, the wet cold, and the light. Each is manageable alone. Together they compound, and the thin air means your judgment — the tool you'd use to manage them — is the first thing to go soft.

---

**Altitude**

The air is thin enough that heat doesn't stay in it. That puts you somewhere high — high enough that an unacclimatized body will start to fail within hours. Headache, nausea, a strange stupidity, sleep that won't come or won't stop. Worse if you push.

If your means of travel allows any choice in arrival, do not arrive in the bowl. Arrive below it, as far below as you can stand, and come up over days. If you can't — if you'll simply be there — then prepare as a climber would for a rapid ascent: spend the preceding weeks sleeping at altitude if you can get to it, or use a hypoxic tent, and carry acetazolamide, started a day or two before. Carry dexamethasone for emergencies and know the signs of the emergency. Know that the only cure for serious altitude illness is descent, and that from this place descent means the lip, and beneath the lip is cloud. You may not be able to descend. Plan as though you cannot.

Move slowly from the first minute. Not slowly like a tourist — slowly like the people there, who have a rhythm you'll recognize once you watch: lift, pause, set, pause. That rhythm is not ceremony. It's what the air allows.

---

**The wet cold**

This is the one I'd worry about most, because it's the one that feels least dramatic.

Everything in the bowl is wet, and everything wet is a degree from ice. There is no wind, which is a mercy, but it also means no drying. Nothing you bring will ever be dry again once it touches the place. Your gloves. The seat of your trousers when you crouch. Your sleeves, when you put your hands in a gap to steady yourself. There is no fire — nothing to burn — so there is no recovery. The heat you arrive with is the heat you have, plus whatever your food and your body can make.

So: no cotton, anywhere, at all. Wool or synthetic against the skin. A fully waterproof shell over everything, with a hood, and taped seams, and sleeves long enough to cover the wrists when you reach. Waterproof trousers you can crouch in. Boots that are waterproof and *stay* waterproof under long contact with running water — which is to say, rubber or fully lined, not merely treated leather — and a stiff sole that will hold on slick rock. Two or three pairs of thick wool socks in a dry bag, and vapor-barrier liners if you're staying more than a few hours; wet socks against skin at that temperature will take toes.

Gloves are a genuine problem. Waterproof gloves are clumsy; the people work bare-handed, and the stones are shaped for bare hands. If you plan to help — and you may find you want to, or that it's expected in some silent way — you'll take your gloves off, and then you'll have perhaps ten or fifteen minutes before your hands stop being useful. Have a routine: gloves off, work, gloves back on, hands under your armpits until they hurt and then stop hurting. Carry a second pair of gloves sealed dry. Neoprene is worth considering for the working pair; it stays warm wet.

Bring a closed-cell foam pad, small, and never sit or kneel on bare stone without it. Bring a bivvy sack even for a day trip, because a day trip can become a night if you get hurt.

Eat constantly. Fat and sugar. You are burning fuel to stay warm and to breathe thin air and to squint, and your appetite at altitude will lie to you and say you're not hungry. Set a timer.

And drink — but not the water off the walls, or not straight. It's probably clean, coming out of rock like that, but it's a degree above freezing, and a liter of it inside you is a liter of heat gone. Carry a wide-mouth bottle, fill it, and keep it inside your jacket against your body for a while before you drink. Better yet, bring a small stove and fuel, and warm it. You will be the only warm thing in the bowl besides one pool. Do not be shy about that.

**The light**

Take this as seriously as the cold. Omnidirectional light off snowfields at altitude is exactly the condition that causes snow blindness, and snow blindness comes on after the damage is done — you feel fine for hours, then your eyes fill with sand and you can't open them. In this place, blind, you are in a maze of shoulder-wide passages beside a drop.

Glacier glasses: very dark, wraparound, with side shields and a nose guard. Not fashion sunglasses. Bring two pairs, and keep the second on a cord around your neck inside your jacket, because if you lose the first you have perhaps two hours before you're in trouble. Goggles are also acceptable and may be better, since the light comes from below as well as above.

Cover your skin. The light comes up off wet rock, so it'll burn the underside of your nose and chin and the roof of your mouth if you breathe with your mouth open. A face covering — a buff pulled up, a balaclava — is worth more than sunscreen. If you use sunscreen, use a mineral one, sparingly, and then keep those hands out of the water and away from the animals. Amphibian skin is a membrane; what's on your fingers goes into them.

Expect headaches from squinting even behind the glass. Expect that you will not be able to tell what time it is, because there is no shadow and no sun. Bring a watch, and look at it, and believe it over your sense of the day.

---

**Footing and the ground**

Black wet rock with a skin of moving water and ice that forms and melts within the hour. There is no reliable step. Stiff soles help; anything with a soft, rounded edge will skate. Microspikes would help you and score the stone, and every stone here has been handled for centuries — I'd leave them off and go slower instead.

The ground moves. Learn what the locals know without being told: **watch the frogs.** A room with frogs in its walls is a room that will stand a while. A room the frogs have left is about to shift. **Watch the birds.** Where they crowd thickest, the wall is soundest. Where they won't land, don't stand, and certainly don't stand under. Never rest, and never sleep, beneath a slab roof — they slide, and a slab that slides a hand's breadth onto your leg has ended your visit.

Never rest in a passage. They drop; the walls fold in. And in a place where nothing is more than an arm's length from anything, a stopped body in a passage is a stopped town. If you need to stop, go toward the open side, where the stacking thins — but not too far, because then there is the lip.

The lip: a few strides of bare wet rock, then air, then light coming up through cloud. Do not go to the edge to see. There is nothing to see. Treat the last body-length before the drop as though it were already the drop.

---

**How to be there**

You are going to be the loudest thing in the bowl. Understand that before you speak.

The people do not talk while they work and they are always working. Your voice, at normal volume, will be an event. Your zipper will be an event. If you must speak, speak the way they do — a syllable, low, to the person next to you. Better to say nothing. Better still to learn, quickly, that you can be present in a place without narrating it.

Do not touch stones casually. Every stone here is set, and every set stone is a decision someone made today. If you lift one, you have joined the work, and you had better know where it goes. You won't, at first. So don't lift. Watch. Watch for a long time — longer than feels reasonable — until you see the shape of what they do: the checking of gaps for animals before setting, the hands out at either end of a dropping passage, the way a room is let go when water comes up and its stones go straight into the walls beside it. When you think you understand, you probably don't yet. Watch more.

If, eventually, a wall leans toward you and there is no one else near, put your hands out. That is the one role the place offers a stranger without instruction: be something for the stones to lean into. Hold them until someone comes. Then step back and let them set.

Do not build. Do not stack a stone for the pleasure of it. Do not build on the warm pool; the frogs need it and the people don't touch it. Do not touch the grey poles — wood comes from very far below and there is almost none. Do not stand on the one slab the birds avoid. You don't know why. Neither, perhaps, do they. That's enough.

Don't move the animals. Salamanders packed in a crevice, snails on a wet wall, frogs at your feet — step around, reach around, choose another gap. You'll see the locals do exactly this. It isn't tenderness. The animals are the instruments the town reads itself with.

Leave nothing behind. Not a wrapper, not a wrapped meal, not a coin. There are no new stones here; whatever you drop becomes part of a system that has run on the same material for longer than anyone remembers, and it will be picked up and turned over and set into a wall or carried to the lip. Pack everything out, including your own waste — everything that goes into the ground here comes back up as a spring through someone's floor. Watch where the people go for that, if you can, and go there. It will likely be the lip.

On gifts: I'd be careful. Wood would be treasured beyond anything you can imagine, and that is exactly the reason to hesitate. This is a town whose entire principle is that nothing is fixed, and a thing that can't be taken apart and put back into a wall is a thing that doesn't fit. If you bring something, bring something that eats or something that warms and is consumed, and leaves no object. Or bring nothing, and give your hands for an afternoon, and go.

**What it will do to you**

The bowl is small. You can cross it in the time it takes to eat. And you will feel, within an hour, both that it is far too small and that you cannot get your bearings in it — because there is no horizon, no shadow, no direction, no sound to steer by, and the walls you walked past this morning are not quite where they were. The disorientation is not a failure of yours. It's the place. Move slowly and carry a compass, though the compass will only tell you which way the open side is, and you'll know that anyway.

The silence will get into you. All that motion and the only sound is drip and the click of a stone. The pressure of water under gravel, felt more than heard. Several hundred wings folding, once, when a slab goes — and that will make you jump the first time and then you'll find yourself waiting for it. People who spend long enough in places like this report hearing their own pulse, their own blood, and some find that unbearable. If you know you don't do well with silence, plan a short visit.

And there is a particular grief in the place, which I'd warn you of the way I'd warn you of the cold: it comes on slowly and you don't notice it until it's already in. You'll watch a room be let go — a room someone slept in — taken apart in an hour and stacked into the rooms beside it, and a spring run clear over the floor, and pale salamanders lie out in it with their gills open. And the next morning someone will start a wall there. Not the same wall. And you'll realize that this is not a disaster to them, and not a repair, and not really even an event — it's just what the afternoon was. The instinct you brought with you, to *fix*, to make something *last*, to *finish*, has no purchase here, and the place will show you that not by argument but by making it seem, after a while, faintly ridiculous. Some people find that a relief. Some people find it very hard. Know which you're likely to be.

---

**Duration**

Go for a day first. Arrive, if you can, at the open side where the stacking gives out, so you don't appear in a passage in someone's way. Stay four or five hours. Eat twice. Watch. Leave before your judgment goes, which means leave earlier than you want to.

If you go back — and I think you might — then think about a night, and think about it carefully. Nowhere is dry to sleep. Nothing overhead is safe. You'd sleep in a bivvy sack on a foam pad against a wall on the open side, where the passages are wide and the stacking is low, close enough to the lip that you'd sleep with your feet toward it and your head toward the town, so that if you rolled you'd roll into stone. You'd sleep, if you slept, under that light — it doesn't go away; it comes off the snowfields even at night — and you'd wake to drip, and click, and the small dark shift of a few hundred birds settling somewhere else. In the morning the wall you slept against would have a lean it didn't have when you lay down, and someone would already be there, hands on it, not looking at you.

Pack light. Move slow. Cover your eyes. Watch the frogs. Say nothing. Hold what leans on you until someone comes.
