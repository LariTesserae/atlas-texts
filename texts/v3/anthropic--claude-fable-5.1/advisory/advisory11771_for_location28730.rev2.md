**Preparing for the high finished country**

Begin with your lungs, because they will decide how long you can stay.

The description says the air is breathable "thinly, for a while," and you should take both qualifiers seriously. Whatever warms the plateau from below is compensating for temperature, not for pressure. The sky goes to plum at noon because there is very little air above you, and that means there is very little air *in* you, either. Expect the equivalent of a high mountain summit with no summit—no descent available, no lower camp, nowhere the air thickens. Every step of the plateau is the top.

So acclimatize before you arrive, not after. Spend weeks at altitude somewhere in your own world first, as high as you can live. Learn what your own hypoxia feels like: the headache, the nausea, the way your thoughts get slippery and confident at the same time. You need to recognize those signs in yourself with no one to point them out, because on the plateau there will be no one. If you have access to bottled oxygen or a concentrator, bring it, and treat it as your clock. When it is half gone, you turn around. Do not negotiate with this.

Understand also that thin air is dry air. You will lose water through your breath faster than you have ever lost it, and there is no water here—the frost is a rumor that burns off before it finishes rising. Every drop you drink, you carry in. Budget for more than you think and then add a day's worth. Dehydration at altitude compounds everything else: the headaches worsen, the judgment slides, the cold gets into you faster.

**On the sun.**

Little air means little filter. The light that reaches the ground has not been softened by much of anything. The pale flour reflects it back up at you. You will burn in places you did not know could burn—under the chin, inside the nostrils, the roof of the mouth if you breathe with it open, which you will, because you will be gasping.

Cover everything. Long sleeves, gloves, a wide brim, a cloth over the lower face that also serves against the dust. Glacier goggles with side shields, not ordinary sunglasses; the reflected glare from a pale plain under a dark sky is a snowblindness setup, and snowblindness eight hours from your exit point is a serious problem. Bring a spare pair. Bring the thickest sunscreen you can tolerate and reapply it obsessively. You will not feel the burn happening. You will feel it that night.

---

**On the flour.**

This is what will get into everything, and it is the thing you should think about most.

It is a mineral dust finer than flour, with a faint metallic smell, and it moves on its own. Set aside the strangeness for a moment and treat it first as what it plainly is: an ultrafine particulate that you will be breathing for as long as you are here. Ground feldspar and "something finer" is a description of a lung hazard. Wear a proper respirator—not a bandana, not a surgical mask, a fitted one with particulate filters—whenever the surface is smoking, and assume the surface is always smoking a little. Change filters more often than the manufacturer says. Between the dust and the thin air, breathing is going to be laborious in a way that wears on you, and you should accept that and slow down rather than pull the mask off for relief.

Keep it off your skin where you can. It is very cold to the touch, colder than the air, which means it will pull heat out of bare hands quickly, and it leaves a residue that smells like a coin. Whether that residue is doing anything to you is unknown. Gloves. Wash with a damp cloth, not water you can't spare.

Keep it out of your equipment. Seal everything in bags within bags. Zippers will grind; lubricate them before you go and bring spare closures. Anything with a lens should be cleaned only with a blower and a soft brush, never wiped—the dust is sharp at a scale you can't see. If you bring electronics, expect the dust to find its way in and behave unpredictably once there; a material that sorts itself by unknown properties might well have opinions about a circuit board. Bring analog backups for anything that matters: a mechanical watch, a paper record, a compass you have tested on arrival to see whether it points anywhere at all.

Now the strangeness. The flour gathers and builds and unbuilds. Over an hour a courtyard-sized patch of flat ground will raise itself into walls or a spiral, hand-high and sharp-edged, and then subside. Your first instinct will be to stand still and watch. This is fine, briefly. But understand three practical consequences.

First, anything you set down on the flour may not be where you left it. Loose objects circulate here; the same lens was found eighty kilometers apart a decade later. A dropped glove, a marker, a cached bottle—the ground may take it into a structure and carry it off when the structure fails. Do not set anything on the open surface that you cannot afford to lose. Do not cache supplies on the flour under any circumstances.

Second, your tracks will not last. Whatever you leave behind you as you walk will be absorbed and reworked within hours. You cannot retrace your steps. Every route must be navigated forward, deliberately, with your own fixes.

Third, the ground is not a stable place to sleep, and I'll come back to that.

**On finding your way.**

This may be the hardest practical problem the plateau poses. There are no peaks, no scarps, no gullies. The horizon is a level line in every direction. The middle distance rearranges itself while you watch it. Distance makes no sound. The eye, given a pale featureless plain under a dark sky, will start inventing things—this is a known effect in whiteouts and on salt flats, and here the plain actually *does* move, so you will not be able to tell your inventions from the ground's.

Do not travel by landmark. There are none you can trust except the fixed objects, and you can walk for three days without seeing one.

Test your compass immediately on arrival. If it holds a consistent bearing, treat it as a gift and still verify it. Learn the stars before you go, if you can learn anything about this world's sky in advance; if not, spend your first night charting them, and note the moon's behavior. Carry a pedometer or count paces. Keep a written log of every bearing and distance, on paper, in pencil, and keep it on your body. This is dead reckoning, and it is unforgiving, and it is what you have.

Then there are the grooves. A hand wide, a finger deep, ruler-straight, cut into the bedrock beneath the flour. They run for hundreds of kilometers without bending and meet at right angles. If you find one exposed in a bare patch, you have found the only reliable line in this country. Note its bearing precisely. If it runs in a direction you can use, walk it—but the flour covers it most of the time, so you will need to probe. A long thin rod, pushed down through the flour every few meters, will find the groove by feel. It is slow. It is also the closest thing to a road that exists here. If you can plan your route so that your exit point sits on a groove, and you can identify which groove, you have given yourself a real chance of getting home. Any grid cut by something that could cut a continent is probably regular; if you find two lines meeting, the spacing may tell you where the next one is.

Mark the fixed objects when you find them. Each is a survey point that will still be there in ten thousand years. Take its bearing from your last fixed point, log the distance, and sketch it—the plinths look alike but the wear patterns differ. Over time, if you or others return, this is how a map gets made.

Above all: do not go farther from your exit than you could walk back in half the time your air and water will last, at half the pace you managed on the way out. The way back is always slower. You will be more tired, more dehydrated, more hypoxic, and the plain will have changed.

**On the fixed objects, and where to sleep.**

The plinths, columns, and dishes are the only things here that will still be where they were when you wake up. The flour avoids them, leaving a ring of bare rock about half a meter wide. This ring is where you camp. Nowhere else.

The reason is not only that the flour moves. It is that something moves *over* the flour at night—a flat, heavy, eyeless thing the color of the ground, which passes over structures as they collapse and is slightly larger afterward. It is rare. Most who have walked here for months have never seen it. But it leaves a smear a meter wide through the assembling forms, and you are, in the dark, in a sleeping bag, a form assembled on the flour. The bare rock around a plinth is the one place the ground itself has agreed to leave alone, and the plinth is a wall at your back. Use it.

Two cautions about camping in the ring.

The crust. Around the bases of the standing objects, on the shadow side, there is a black-green lichen-thing thinner than paper that grows a centimeter per century. It is the only living green on this world. Some of those rings have been growing since before the plateau finished. Look before you sit. Look before you put down a pack. Sleep on the sunward side of the object, where the crust is not, and step around it as you would step around something holy, because for practical purposes it is. Nothing you can do here matters much to the plateau, except this.

The frost. In the last hour before dawn, the shadow side will fur over with grey frost that sublimates at first light. It never melts and yields no water; do not try to harvest it, and do not lie in it. Where it has been, the flour draws back for the rest of the day and leaves the underlying rock exposed—dark, glassy, smooth as the floor of a place where something heavy sat for a very long time. Which is to say: slippery. Cracked skulls do not heal well at altitude. Move carefully on the bare rock in the mornings.

The ground is very cold, colder than the air. This is the reverse of what your body expects. You will lose heat downward all night through whatever you are lying on, while the air stays merely cool. Bring the thickest insulated pad you can carry, and then a second one. Bring more sleeping bag than the air temperature suggests. Hypothermia through the ground is quiet and you can wake up well into it.

**On sound.**

You will hear your own blood. You will hear your straps creak and your breath rasp behind the respirator. You will not hear anything more than a few meters away, and nothing will hear you.

If you travel with a companion—and you should, if it is at all possible—stay within arm's reach or agree on visual signals. Shouting is useless. Whistles are useless. Radios may work, depending on what the flour does to them, but test them before you split up even a hundred meters. Bring a mirror for signaling and a bright cloth to wave. Agree in advance that if you lose sight of each other, both of you stop where you are and neither moves until the other is found. On a plain where the ground rearranges itself, two people searching for each other can circle forever.

If you travel alone, know that no one will come. Injury here is not an emergency; it is an ending, unless you can walk out on it. Tape your ankles. Take no risks with the glassy rock. Treat every small problem as serious.

---

**On the sky and the other thing.**

Very high, something long and thin sometimes crosses the sun. It has never landed. It moves against the wind. It is almost certainly not a threat to you, if only because it has never come lower than the last of the air. Note it if you see it. Do not spend your attention on it.

---

**On your mind.**

This is the part that people underprepare for, and it is the part that will most shape your time here.

The plateau is empty in a way that almost nowhere is. You can stand at the center of a hundred-kilometer circle and be the only pulse in it. There are no birds, no insects, no wind sound, no water sound. The sky is dark at noon. The ground breathes. The overwhelming sense the place gives off is of being *finished*—of having done what it was for and stopped—and being a warm, unfinished, breathing thing in the middle of that is a particular kind of loneliness. Some people find it clarifying. Some find it unbearable in a way they did not anticipate and cannot explain. You will not know which you are until you are there.

Some specific things to expect:

The flour is hypnotic. Watching a structure rise and fail is like watching a fire, and you can lose an hour without noticing. At altitude, with your judgment already softened, this is dangerous—not because the flour will hurt you, but because the day will be gone and you will not have moved and your water will be lower. Set a timer. When it goes off, look away and check your log.

You will see things at the edge of sight that are not there, and you will see things that *are* there and disbelieve them. The one person who saw the ground-animal was not sure afterward. You should hold your own perceptions loosely and your log tightly. Write down what you see with times and bearings. Trust the writing over the memory.

The late light is beautiful. For a few minutes before sunset the structures throw shadows and the whole plain looks like a settlement, a field of instruments, a plan half-executed. It is the one moment the plateau shows you what it might have been. Stop and see it. Then get to your plinth before the shadows go, because the structures will be collapsing in the dark and the ground-thing, if it is anywhere, will be moving.

Nights are long in feel if not in hours. There is nothing to do and nothing to hear. Bring something for your hands—a small task, a book you can read by a dim light with the dust kept off it. Do not stare at the plain in moonlight for too long. It will be smooth to the horizon and you will start to feel that the day was a dream and the plateau has always been this.

And the warmth from below. You will not stop noticing it. Your body knows what air this thin should feel like, and the wrongness of its mildness sits under everything. Whatever is keeping this world a few degrees warmer than physics wants it is older than the grooves in the rock and does not appear to be for anyone. The thought that you are breathing because a machine that has forgotten its purpose has not yet forgotten this one function is a strange thing to sleep on. Let it be strange. Do not try to resolve it. You are not going to find the mechanism, and you should not dig for it; the grooves go nowhere near it and neither should you.

**On taking things.**

The flour gives up small objects: a hinge with no oxidation, a lens that shows the same view a few seconds displaced. You will want one.

Consider that these things circulate. The same lens has been found twice, eighty kilometers apart. Whether the flour carried it or the lens has some tendency of its own, no one can say. Consider also that the flour avoids the fixed objects as if they are "still saying something the material can hear," and that an object which moves across a continent under its own preferences may not stop preferring things once it is in your pack, or in your house, or in your world.

If you take something, take one small thing, seal it, and watch it. If you find you cannot leave it behind, that is worth noting too.

Take nothing from the crust. Take no flour home; it does not stop being what it is in a jar. Photograph, sketch, log. The plateau has no shortage of time and will still be here.

---

**On leaving.**

Set your departure time before you arrive, and keep it. Not when the oxygen runs out; not when the water runs out; before either. Set it by the half-mark of whichever you have less of, and then move it earlier by a day.

Your route home is dead reckoning back to a fixed object you have logged, then to your exit. Do this in daylight. Start early, because the morning bare-rock patches around frost are treacherous and the assembling structures are lowest after dawn, when the night's forms have finished pouring themselves back into the plain. Walk slowly. Check your bearing every few hundred paces. If the ground has raised a wall across your line, go over it—they are only a hand high—rather than around; going around is how you drift.

When you are back through, wash everything. Clean the flour out of your gear with a brush and a vacuum, outdoors, and dispose of what you collect in a sealed container rather than pouring it onto your own ground. Watch your lungs for a few weeks. Drink more than feels necessary. Sleep low.

And expect the quiet to follow you for a while. Lowland places are loud with life in a way you stop noticing until you have been somewhere that has none. For some days the sound of a bird or running water will land on you like a small shock. That passes. The picture of the plain does not—the pale surface breathing under a dark sky, the plinths in their rings, the shadows at evening drawing a plan that no one is left to read. You do not need to do anything with it. It is finished. You were only passing through the one part of it that still moves.
