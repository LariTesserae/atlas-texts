## Preparing for the Yard

Start by accepting what kind of place this is. It is not a ruin and it is not a wilderness. It is a worksite between shifts, and the shift may resume in an hour or never, and you will not be able to tell which from inside it. Everything you do there should be done as though the crew will be back before you leave. That is the whole ethic of the visit, and most of the practical advice follows from it.

---

### The body: altitude and heat together

The two hazards here pull in opposite directions and that is what makes them dangerous. You are high — high enough that the air is thin and cloud stands level with the parapet — but the floor is warm and the air is saturated. Your body will be told by its skin that it is in a greenhouse and by its lungs that it is on a mountain. It will not reconcile these well.

Expect a headache in the first hour. Expect to sweat and for the sweat to do nothing, because the air is already full of water. Expect to be thirsty and not feel it, because your face is wet. Drink on a schedule, not on cue — a good mouthful every fifteen or twenty minutes, from your own supply. Bring more water than seems reasonable for a place where water runs in the channels and drips from every leaf, because none of that water is yours to drink. The channel water is milky and tastes, you can bet, of lime: slag leachate, alkaline, warm, probably harmless to touch at the wrist and probably not something you want in your stomach. The cloud-water pooling in the grating has passed over hot metal and slag dust on its way to you. Treat it as industrial runoff that happens to be pretty.

Move slowly. Not because anything will chase you but because at altitude, in heat, pushing through wet vegetation on a metal floor, you will tire faster than your judgement will report. If you find yourself breathing through your mouth, sit down on a cross-brace and wait until you aren't.

Bring salt. Bring something sugary. If you are prone to altitude sickness, bring whatever you take for it, and think twice about going at all.

### Clothing: the problem of wet warmth

You will be soaked within ten minutes of leaving the door, and nothing you wear will dry while you are there. Plan for that rather than against it.

Do not wear waterproofs. A shell jacket in that humidity is a personal sauna; you will cook inside it. Wear thin, fast-wicking synthetics that you don't mind being wet, in layers you can strip, and accept being damp to the skin. The warmth from the floor means you will not be cold while the machine is running. Pack, however, one dry insulating layer sealed in a bag, because if you have to leave through the door in the wall and the other side of that door is a bare mountain at the same altitude, wet clothes and moving cloud will take the heat out of you very fast. The yard is warm. The mountain it sits on almost certainly is not.

A hat with a brim helps with the drip. Gloves — thin, grippy, wet-capable — matter more than you'd think, because everything you hold onto is a steel upright slick with algae-free water and you will hold onto a great deal.

### Feet: the two floors

The grating is the surface you walk on. It is a metre above a floor of slag slabs that are still warm enough to flash dripping water into steam. Understand what that means. The floor plates carry heat up into your soles constantly; the grating itself may be hot enough in places to be uncomfortable through thin footwear, and a metre below your boots is a surface that would blister skin.

Wear boots with thick, stiff soles, closed toes, and good tread — a lugged sole that grips wet steel. No trainers, nothing with mesh. Check the grating before you commit weight to it near the western wall and near the sump; new construction has no wear to warn you, but it also has no history to reassure you, and a panel that has never been walked on has never been tested either. Watch for gaps where panels meet, and where the slag-crumb has spilled and grown over: the moss will bridge a gap you cannot see.

If you drop something through the grating, it is gone. Do not go down after it. There is no described access to the lower level, the seams between slabs are where something paler has welled up and set — which is to say, where the material was hottest and may still be — and the air down there will be hotter, wetter, and carrying whatever the slag gives off when water hits it.

Do not wear anything on your feet that you want to wear again. Slag dust is glass. It gets into seams and stays.

### Breathing: what the air is

Thick, green, hot metal, wet lime. The flint smell from the western doorway. These are not incidental colour. Hot slag and water produce, depending on the rock, hydrogen sulphide, sulphur dioxide, and an alkaline dust that irritates lungs. In the open yard, with cloud constantly spilling over the parapet and the racks acting as a filter, the air is probably tolerable. Near the moulds, near the sump, and anywhere within a few metres of the doorway, it may not be.

Carry a simple half-face respirator with acid-gas cartridges. You may not need it. If your eyes start to sting or your throat tightens, put it on and move back toward the racks, where the green is thickest — the plants are, in effect, the yard's own scrubber, and where they grow most densely the air will be cleanest. If you can, carry a small gas detector for sulphides. A canary in reverse: the moths do nothing at all except shiver when the valve sighs, so they will tell you nothing about the air.

Do not go through the western doorway. Not partway, not to look. The sound you hear is a furnace, the air coming out is warmer than the yard, the smell is flint, and the dark is total. Whatever is down there is either running unattended or running attended by something you have not met. Both cases mean the same thing for you.

### Seeing: no shadows, no view

The light is flat and shadowless and does not change with time. Every leaf looks the same distance away. This is a serious navigational hazard in a place full of identical steel racks in rows, and it gets worse in the afternoon when the cloud sits in the yard to shoulder height and you are walking through a white pool with only the rack-tops visible.

Bring a watch. Set an alarm for when you intend to leave and another for half an hour before that. The light will not tell you the time and your body will not either.

Mark the door you came through before you do anything else. Tie something bright to the frame — a length of flagging tape, a cloth — and note the wall it is in relative to the machine and the channel. The channel runs along the eastern wall; the machine rises above the sump at the low point; the moulds and the doorway are west. Those three fixtures are your compass, and the sound of the channel water — soft, continuous, throaty — is audible through the cloud when nothing else is. If you are lost, stop, listen for the water, walk to it, follow it downhill to the sump, and orient from the machine.

A headlamp is of limited use in cloud; it lights the water in the air and little else. A low, warm beam angled down at the grating is more useful than a bright one. Carry one anyway for the possibility that the light dims further than described.

### Hands: what not to touch

The machine is warm, humming, and turning a flywheel the size of a cartwheel. It is riveted plate, two storeys, with a fogged gauge and a valve that lifts to relieve pressure. It has run this long without you. Put your hand on the casing if you want to feel the hum — that is safe, and it is one of the things worth doing — but keep clear of the flywheel, do not touch the valve, do not wipe the gauge, and do not stand in the plume line when the valve sighs. It is low-pressure steam at intervals. It is still steam.

The ladle is tipped forward on its gantry as if about to pour. It has held liquid rock. Do not stand beneath it. Do not stand beneath the stalactite with the moss on its tip, however much you want to look at it closely; look from the side. Assume the gantry could move.

The moulds: the empty one pooled with rainwater is the only one you can safely approach the edge of. The half-full ones may have a crust over a warm interior. The full ones are cooler but their edges are glass. Do not climb on the rails.

The racks are the safest structures in the yard and you will be leaning on them constantly. They are still steel with vines on them. Check your grip.

### The made-ground rule, and what you carry

This is the part that matters most for the place rather than for you.

Plants grow only where the slag-crumb has been laid or spilled or tracked. Wherever a boot-print of it has landed, green follows. You are a boot. You will carry crumb from the troughs onto the grating and the concrete and, if you are careless, out through the door in the wall onto whatever is beyond it. In the yard that is a small thing — it is what the yard is for, eventually. Outside the yard, on a mountain where nothing like this crumb exists, it may be the beginning of something that was not supposed to start yet, or there.

So: brush your boots before you leave. Brush them on the bare grating, over the pooling water, where nothing grows. Check your trouser cuffs. Check your pack. Turn out your pockets. Do not take a handful of the crumb as a souvenir. If you must take something, take a photograph, and understand that in flat shadowless light with cloud in the air it will not look like anything.

The reverse also holds. Do not bring soil in. Do not bring seeds in — check your boot treads and your socks before you come, especially if you have been walking anywhere green in the previous week. The plants here have made a bargain with a particular substrate and they have not been thinned by anything that competes. Whatever you introduce will either die on the grating or find the crumb and never leave.

### Animals, and the thing in the racks

The bird, if you see it, will not mind you if you do not approach. Stand still and it will do what it does — one step, one stab, nothing — and then it will be gone in the interval when you look away. Do not try to see it leave. You will not.

The moths do nothing. Leave them on the undersides of the leaves. Do not lift a leaf to look. When the valve sighs and they all shiver at once, that is the only thing they will ever do for you, and it is enough.

Something moves in the racks ahead of you and stays ahead of you. This is the one thing in the yard I cannot tell you the nature of, and neither can the yard. Here is what I can say: it does not approach. It withdraws at exactly your pace. If you stop, it stops. If you push faster, you will arrive at moving leaves and empty trough and you will have driven it into a corner it did not choose, and that is not a position you want either of you in. Walk at the pace the place asks for, which is slow, and let the sound stay a row ahead. Treat it as a kind of escort. It is not interested in being seen. Respect that in the same way you would respect a door someone has closed.

### Conduct: the crew is coming back

Behave as if the shift resumes at the end of your visit. Do not move tools. Do not close the open mould or open the closed ones. Do not clear a path through the racks that was not there, however much the fronds lie across fronds. Do not pull the vine off the pipe. Do not try to read the gauge by wiping it. Leave the flagging tape on the door only until you leave, then take it with you.

If you find evidence of people — a bench, a cup, a written instruction, the word over the doorway you do not know — record it and leave it. Do not attempt the word. You do not know what it says and the doorway it is over is the one you are not going through.

### Leaving

Set your alarm early enough to find the door in cloud. Retrace by sound: channel, sump, machine, then along the wall to your marker. If the downward wind comes while you are looking, and the cloud lies flat for a minute and you see the whole of it — walls, moulds, ladle, the green on its bones, the letters over the doorway — stand still and look. It will not last, and it is the only view you will get. Then the cloud stands back up and you go.

Brush your boots on the bare grating. Turn out your pockets. Put on the dry layer before you open the door, because the other side of it is a mountain.

Put your hand once in a trough before you go, if you haven't. Warm, wet, full of roots, not yet earth. That is the thing you came to understand, and there is no other way to understand it than to feel that it is not finished.
