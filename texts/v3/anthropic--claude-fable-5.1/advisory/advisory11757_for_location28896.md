**Preparing for the Bowl**

Start with the noise, because everything else here is arranged around it.

You will hear the pumps before you see the place. If you come along the dike top — and you probably will, since the ring canal is the road — the roar arrives as a pressure in the air about half a mile out, a sound with no location, and then the ground falls away on one side and you are looking down into it. Six houses, six white plumes, a green floor with straight lines cut into it. Take a minute up there. It is the last quiet you will have, and it is also the only place you can see the whole thing at once and understand what it is: a hole that is being kept a hole.

Then walk down, and the sound closes over you like water.

**Your ears**

Bring earplugs, the soft foam kind, several pairs, and a set of the over-ear muffs that shooters and machinists use. You will feel foolish. Nobody who lives here wears them, and the children will look at you the way children look at a man who has arrived in a coat for a swim. Wear them anyway, at least for the first days, and especially in the first nights. The roar is loud enough that a week of it unprotected will leave you with a ringing that does not go away for a month, and the people here have all paid that price already without noticing they were paying it. You are not obliged to.

What you should understand before you arrive is that this is not the noise of a factory or a city. Those noises change. This one does not. It is a single enormous chord held forever, and the mind does two things with a sound like that. In the first hours it is unbearable — you cannot think, you cannot hear yourself, you feel your chest vibrating. Then, somewhere in the second day, the mind simply files it under *weather* and you stop hearing it as an event. That is a relief, but it is also a small loss, and you should notice the moment it happens, because from then on you will be a little more like the people who live here and a little less able to describe the place to anyone else.

Do not stand for long between two pump-houses. The description mentions the beat — two roars a few cycles apart interfering, a slow throb you feel in the teeth. It is real and it is worse than it sounds. Ten minutes of it and you are nauseous and cannot say why. Walk through those zones; do not stop in them. The people here know this without knowing they know it, and if you watch where they pause to talk you will see they do it near a house or near the rails, never at the midpoint.

Learn the voice. It is raised, flat, with the consonants pushed forward and the vowels shortened. It is not shouting — shouting doesn't carry any better here and it tires the throat. It is more like speaking to someone across a small room while facing them. You will find yourself unconsciously watching mouths, which is fine; everyone here lip-reads a little and nobody is offended by being watched. Don't whisper. Whispering is invisible here. If you want privacy you go indoors and shut a door, and even then you speak in the voice.

**Your body**

It is cold at the bottom and it is wet, and the wet is the kind that comes from below and from the air rather than from the sky. Dress for a cellar with a river running through it. Wool against the skin — cotton will take on the damp in the first hour and never give it back. A windproof outer layer, not because there is wind (the dike keeps it off) but because the mist from the outfalls is finer than rain and gets through anything that breathes. A hat. Two pairs of gloves so one can be drying.

Boots: rubber, to the knee, and a second pair of ordinary shoes for indoors, because the clay is the single most tenacious substance you will encounter. It is the color of a bruise and it behaves like one. It sticks, it gathers, it dries to a crust that comes off in plates and then turns back to mud the moment it meets a floor. Every doorway has a scraper and a brush and you will be judged, quietly, on how well you use them.

Bring dry bags — proper waterproof ones — for anything that must stay dry. Paper goes limp here in an afternoon. Books swell. A notebook left on a windowsill overnight will be damp through by morning. Matches are useless unless kept in a tin. Anything of iron or steel that you value will have a bloom of rust on it within a week: a pocketknife, the buckle on a bag, the rivets on your boots. The people here grease everything, constantly, and you will see the greaser going out on his trolley along the narrow rails every morning with his cans and his rags, and that is not maintenance, that is survival. Do the same with your own things or leave them at home.

The mornings are the wet time. You wake and the world is beaded — the rails, the wire, the undersides of leaves, the sill of your window. By noon it has burned off into a dry, even grey. By dusk it is coming back. Plan your day around this. Morning is for indoors and for talking. The middle of the day is for walking.

Expect a cough in the first week. It is the damp, not sickness, and it passes.

**Your feet, and where you put them**

The ground is dangerous in a way that does not look dangerous.

The drainage cuts are straight, deep — some of them six or eight feet — and their sides are still perfectly vertical, cut clay with the spade marks on it. In the open you can see them coming. But the grass between the houses has gone tall and coarse and full of meadowsweet and flag iris, and nobody cuts it, and the cuts run through the grass unseen. Walk in that grass in a straight line and you will, at some point, step onto nothing. People here know where the cuts are the way you know where the furniture is in your own house in the dark. You do not. Stay on the gravel and the rail-bed until someone has walked you round, and even then, probe with a stick in the long grass.

The surveyors' stakes are still out there in rows, most of them hidden by grass, and they are exactly ankle height.

The rails: they are narrow-gauge and the wagons on them are light, but the greasing trolley comes through quietly (everything is quiet here, under the roar) and it does not stop quickly. Walk beside the rails, not on them. If you hear a change in the pitch of the roar behind you, that is probably the trolley.

The trestles carrying the outfall pipes up the dike face are wet iron at a steep angle and the temptation to climb one and look down into the canal is enormous. Don't. The settling tanks are deep and unfenced and full of eels, which is a good thing if you have a bucket and a bad thing if you have fallen in.

And the dike itself. From the bowl it is a green slope with water on top of it. Go up it only on the steps or the roadways. If you are up there at night and step wrong, you go either into the ring canal — which has barge traffic and steep cut sides — or down forty feet of grass into the noise.

**Your eyes, and the geometry**

You will feel a small permanent wrongness in your inner ear here and it is worth naming so you can set it aside. Water is above you. Boats move along the sky. From the pumping floor you look up at a hull. This offends something old in the brain — the part that knows water goes down — and for the first day or two you will find yourself glancing up at the rim more than you mean to, checking. Everyone who visits does it. The residents have simply stopped.

The buildings lean. Not much — a degree or two — but each at its own angle, and once you have noticed it you cannot stop noticing. A door in the engineers' house swings shut on its own. Water spilled on a floor runs to the same corner every time. Your bed will be very slightly downhill toward the foot or one side, and you will sleep better if you turn it so your head is at the high end. The canteen step that used to be level: watch for it, because the whole town steps over it without looking and you will fall over it twice.

The light is flat and clear and casts almost no shadow, which sounds pleasant and is actually tiring. There is nothing for the eye to rest in. Everything is edges — the cut banks, the corners of the brick, the straight rails — and everything is the same distance from you in the same even grey. If you paint or photograph, this is a gift. If you don't, you will find yourself, around the third afternoon, going indoors to look at something dim.

**The people**

Forty houses, gaps between them full of willow. Nine children. A school with one room. A chapel with a crack that has been filled three times. This is a place with maybe eighty souls in it and everyone knows your name before you have said it, and knows which end you are sleeping at, which tells them who you are.

You will stay at the engineers' end. This is not unfriendly; it is simply how the town is laid out. The working families are at the other end. Between the two, along the central rail, is the canteen, and the canteen is where you should spend your evenings, because it is the one place where both ends sit down together and it is where you will learn everything worth knowing about the bowl.

Bring something. Not money; there is not much to spend it on. Bring things that are dry and stay dry — tea in tins, tobacco in tins, a bottle of something. Bring news, and be prepared to tell it slowly and more than once in the loud voice. Bring, if you can, something for the school: paper in a waterproof wrapping, pencils, a book with pictures. Nine children in a bowl a mile across with a roar over their heads have not seen much.

Do not ask what happens if the pumps stop. They know. Every one of them knows, and the knowing is in the flat voice and the way they walk past the houses without looking at the brass pins in the brickwork. There was a winter when Number Two stopped for two hours to change a bearing, and three children woke up crying in the silence and did not know why. That is the whole of the answer. If they want to tell you about it they will, in the canteen, late, and you should listen and not say anything clever.

Ask instead about the eels. Ask how the eels got up the outfall pipes against a forty-foot fall of water, and you will get six theories and an argument and probably an eel, fried, which is very good. Ask the greaser about the otters and he will not tell you where the holt is, but he will show you where he slows down, which is nearly the same thing.

**The other inhabitants**

Almost nothing here will hurt you. The rats are glossy and bold and will investigate anything edible left out, so keep your food in tins and your bag shut. The frogs on a warm evening will astonish you — they briefly out-sing the pumps, which should not be possible — and if you walk the channels at dusk you will hear them stop in a wave ahead of you and start again behind. The bats come out of the roof void at Number Four in a shape like smoke and pour over the outfall; go and stand under them once, it is worth the mist.

The hares will sit up in the meadowsweet ten feet from you and look at you with mild interest. Do not take this as an invitation. They are not tame; they simply cannot hear you, or have decided that the roar is silence and everything else is noise. Give them the courtesy of moving slowly.

The herons stand at the culvert mouths at the same intervals as the pump-houses and will not move for you. Go around.

There will be midges in the still evenings near the reed. Bring something for them.

**Leaving**

Give yourself a slow climb out. Go up the dike face by the steps in the late afternoon, and stop at the top, and turn round.

You will notice that you can no longer hear the pumps. You have not gone deaf; the sound is still there, still enormous; but you have been inside it for days and the brain has finished filing it away. From up here, with the canal at your feet and the barges sliding past at eye level and the bowl spread out below in its flat grey light — the six houses, the six white falls, the green table with its instruments set out at careful intervals — you will see the whole effort of the place at once, and hear none of it.

Then walk away along the dike top. The sound will fall behind you by degrees. Somewhere about a mile out it will drop below the level where the ear registers it as sound, and you will feel a pressure lift from your chest that you had not known was there. For the next few nights, in whatever quiet place you have gone to, you will lie awake listening to a hum that is not in the room, and you will understand the children, and you will think of the brass pins in the brickwork going down a finger's width a year, and the water going up.

Pack the earplugs in the outside pocket. You'll want them again.
