**Preparation notes for the lifted sea**

Start with the number, because everything else follows from it: thirty-two hundred meters. The water is warm and the light is kind and the place will spend every minute trying to convince you that it is a lagoon. It is not a lagoon. It is a high-altitude environment with a lagoon lying in it, and the two sets of rules do not cancel each other out. They stack, the way the highlands stack.

---

**Before you go**

Acclimatize. Spend two or three nights somewhere around two thousand meters before you go up the outer slopes, and then a night on the rim before you touch the water. Thirty-two hundred is not extreme, but it is enough to give an unacclimatized person headache, poor sleep, nausea, and a shortness of breath that will feel, once you are in the water, exactly like the beginning of drowning. You need to be able to tell the two apart. You cannot do that on the first day.

Understand what altitude does to being underwater. Surface pressure here is roughly two-thirds of what it is at sea level. If you intend to breathe compressed gas at depth, every table you know is wrong, and wrong in the direction that hurts you. If you are not already trained in altitude diving, the correct preparation is to not do it. Snorkel. Freedive shallow. This place rewards patience more than depth anyway — the drowned groves stand in the first fifteen meters, the reef is mostly shallower than that, and the meadow you can look at from the surface for a week and not exhaust.

Get your ears checked, and your sinuses. You will be in the water for hours because there is no cold to drive you out, and the water is not clean.

Learn to freedive with a partner, and learn the shallow-water blackout rules until they are boring. Warm water removes the cold shock that normally shortens breath-holds and keeps people cautious. Here you can float face-down in blood-warm water with the bottom lit twenty meters below and feel no urgency at all. The lack of urgency is the hazard.

---

**What to bring, and what to leave**

*A dry suit or a thick rash guard and hood — not for the water, for the air.* The water is body temperature. The air on the rim in the afternoon, when the cloud comes over, is not. You will climb out wet at three thousand meters into wind and fog, and that moment — five minutes on sharp rock while you find your footing — is where the actual hypothermia risk lives. Have a towel and a windproof layer waiting at your exit point, in a dry bag, weighted so the wind cannot take it.

*Full-coverage in the water anyway.* Thin, light, long sleeves and legs, gloves, a hood. Not for warmth. For contact. Everything on the reef is either sharp, colonized, or both, and you are going to touch things you did not intend to touch when the meadow closes around you.

*A nose clip. Wear it. This is not optional.* Warm, still, slightly saline water sitting over decaying organic material is exactly the environment in which certain amoebae thrive — the kind that enter through the nose and are very rarely survived. Do not let this water up your nose. Do not dive head-first from the rim. Do not clear your mask by exhaling through your nose without the clip in place. If you have a sinus infection, do not enter at all.

*Ear drops* — the drying, acidifying kind. Every evening.

*Antiseptic and a habit.* Any cut, any scrape, any coral graze, cleaned immediately and kept clean. Warm bacterial water plus fresh rock plus altitude-slowed healing is a bad combination.

*Water. All of it.* Do not drink from the basins. Faintly saline, warm, stagnant, and full of things without names. Rain collects in the seams above the waterline — that is your source, filtered and treated. You will need more than you think. Immersion in warm water and thin dry air both dehydrate you, and neither will make you feel thirsty.

*Food for the full stay plus margin.* There is one fish an hour and you are not going to eat it.

*Line.* A reel or a long floating tether, and a surface marker on it. Read the section on sound before you decide this is excessive.

*Lights,* waterproof, redundant, because the cloud comes at the same hour every afternoon and turns the water pearl-colored and directionless.

*Rope and anchors for the rim.* There are no beaches. There are no gentle slopes. The rock meets the water at the angle it broke at. You will need a fixed line at your entry point, and your entry point is your exit point, and you will mark it with something visible from the water and something you can find in fog.

*Sun protection* at a level that will feel excessive. Altitude UV reflected off an oval mirror the size of a town. Your lips, your ears, the backs of your hands on the surface.

*Nothing that lives.* Clean every piece of gear before you arrive — not for your sake, for the basin's. It is a sealed system a few thousand years old that is already being colonized from below by ferns and lichen and whatever the rain carries. It has no defenses. A seed in your boot tread or a spore in a wet bag is not a small thing here. Wherever you are coming from, and however you are coming, arrive clean.

---

**The rim**

The walls are young. There is no talus not because the rock is stable but because it has not had time to fall yet. Treat every ledge as untested. The seams — those dark, clean, unrounded cracks — are the rock telling you where it is still moving. Do not camp in front of one. Do not anchor a line in one if you can avoid it.

Wet lichen on fresh basalt is close to frictionless. The pale limestone is sharp enough to open skin through a glove. Move slowly on the rim and keep three points of contact, and remember that at this altitude you will be breathing hard doing it.

The hum you will hear underwater — and sometimes, at night, lying on the rock, through your chest — is the rock. Whatever lifted it has not finished. It is reasonable to expect tremors during your stay, probably small. If you feel one while you are on the wall, get down or get away from it, whichever is faster. If you feel one while you are in the water, surface calmly and get clear of anything overhead. The drowned mangroves are dead wood under hanging weight.

Cloud arrives in the afternoon, most afternoons, coming up the outer slopes and spilling over. On the rim this means whiteout: you can lose a marked exit point ten meters away. Plan to be out of the water and at your camp before it comes. Rain follows. The mornings are clear and are when you should do everything that requires seeing.

---

**In the water**

The clarity lies to you about depth. Twenty meters looks like six. You will look down at the meadow and think you could touch it, and on the first breath-hold you will discover that you cannot, and if you press on because it is *right there* you will discover the rest of the reasons this place is quiet. Set a depth limit before you enter, tie it to something you can see — the top of a particular mangrove tower, a specific coral head — and do not revise it in the water.

You are less buoyant here than in the sea. The water is only faintly saline and the air is thin. Adjust your weighting on the first shallow entry, close to the wall, with your partner watching.

The water does not cool as you go down. This is disorienting in a way that takes a while to name: your body uses temperature change as a depth cue without telling you, and here the cue is gone. You will find yourself deeper than you meant to be with no sensation of having descended. Watch your gauge or watch the light, not your skin.

The seagrass is a meter and a half long and dense enough to hide the bottom. If you go into it you will not be able to see your feet. It is not aggressive, but it is a net. Long blades wrap fins and wrists and the strap of a mask without any force at all, and if you are on a breath-hold at eight meters and something is wrapped around your ankle, the situation is what it is. Stay above the canopy of the grass. Look at it. Do not swim through it.

The drowned mangroves are snag hazards on every side. The black trunks are smooth, but the branch-work is everywhere, and the hanging weed conceals it. Do not swim under a grove if you are not certain you can surface where you entered. Do not swim through one.

The reef itself is welded shut — you read that correctly, a single porous mass you could not push a hand through — and it is not soft. It looks soft. Under the algae and the bread-dough sponges there is dead coral, and dead coral is a rasp. Assume every surface will cut you. Assume some of the things growing on it would prefer that you didn't touch them, and have chemistry to say so. Some sponges are toxic on contact. The glass-plate organism and the finger-width tubes have no names and therefore no safety data; give them the courtesy of distance.

Do not go into the eastern bowls. The description of them is one sentence and it ends with "nothing has been seen there." That is not an invitation. It is a depth reading, and the reading is: unknown, blue, and with something large occasionally moving at the edge of vision. The western basins, where the mangrove tips break the surface like candles, are shallow and legible and where you should stay.

---

**Sound and separation**

You cannot hear your partner. Understand this fully before you enter. The growth eats sound; ten meters is enough to erase a shout, a tank-bang, anything. A separated pair in this water cannot find each other by listening, and in the afternoon when the cloud lies on the surface, they cannot find each other by looking either.

So: line. You are connected to your partner or to the surface marker or both, always. Agree on pulls — one, two, three — and practice them at the wall before you go anywhere. Agree that separation means immediate surface, both of you, and wait at the surface, and do not go back down to look.

The one exception to the deafness is the tap. A stone from the rim, striking the surface, is a single soft note underwater and then a long tone, the whole basin rung. If you need to recall someone from the shore, that is how — one deliberate stone. Agree in advance what it means. Then do not drop any others, because a stone dropped yesterday is still lying on the grass today and will be there in a year with weed on it, and the place does not need your stones.

---

**The chemistry you cannot see**

This is a closed warm basin sitting on hot rock, with no tide, no exchange, no upwelling, and a great deal of dead coral and rotting seagrass on the floor. The fish died for a reason. The water is at or near the limit of what can breathe in it. Warm water holds little oxygen; still water holds less; water over decay holds least.

Near the bottom, and especially in low pockets of the reef and in the deep eastern bowls, expect the possibility of hydrogen sulfide and low dissolved oxygen. You will not smell it underwater. You may see it: a faint milkiness, a layer that looks like heat-shimmer, dark staining on rock, a patch where even the algae has gone. Do not descend into it. If you smell rotten eggs when you surface near the wall, move.

The rock beneath is warm enough to keep the whole body of water at blood temperature, which means gas is coming up through the floor somewhere. Carbon dioxide seeps are quiet, invisible, and dense; they collect in hollows in the rock above the waterline as readily as below it. Do not sleep in a hollow on the rim. Do not sleep in a low place at all. If your headache is worse in the morning than it was at night and you have already acclimatized, it may not be the altitude.

The seagrass leans one way and then, further off, another way, following currents with no obvious source. The source is the seepage — water leaving through fractures in the basin floor and walls. Where the meadow leans hardest, there is a crack drinking. Stay off the floor near the walls. A seam in fresh rock is exactly the shape and size of a person's arm, and the water is going into it.

---

**What it will do to you**

The quiet will get into you faster than you expect. No crackle, no fish, no cold, no current you can feel, light coming through the whole column like a good window. The drowned groves in a sourceless glow. The one silver fish, hanging vertical in the grass, not moving when you come near. Whatever tempo you brought with you, the place will spend the first day taking it away, and this is pleasant, and it is also how people lose track of breath-holds and hours and the afternoon cloud.

Set alarms. Count surface intervals out loud. Have your partner do the same for you. The place is not hostile; it simply does not care about your timescale, and it will not remind you of anything.

Expect to feel grief, which is strange to warn someone about, but it is coming. You will see the pale band on the wall where the water was. You will find the reef along the western sills standing a few centimeters into the dawn air, gone bone-white and crisp. You will understand that everything you are floating over is being abandoned at a rate it cannot perceive, and that you are seeing it because you happened to arrive in the last century or two of a few thousand years. The mangrove towers will stand in air and fall. Do not let the feeling make you careless. Do not let it make you want to take something home as a record. You are the record. Leave the rest.

---

**Leaving**

Do not ascend the outer slopes or travel onward to any lower pressure within a day of your last breath-hold dive, and considerably longer if you breathed compressed gas. The rules for flying after diving apply with more force here, not less.

Check your gear for passengers before you go — the reverse of the cleaning you did coming in. The basin has nothing to give you that you should carry out, and your bag is the most likely way for something from this place to arrive in a place where it should not be.

Take a last look from the rim in the morning, before the cloud. The chain of ovals going east until they blur. The candle-field in the west with the wind in it. The second landscape under the first, rippling, lit to the floor. It will not look like this for long, by its own measure. By yours, it will look like this for the rest of your life.
