**Preparing to visit the fen chamber**

Start with the fact that will organize everything else: you are going to spend some hours being the only warm, dry, dead-adjacent thing in a room whose whole activity is finding what used to be alive and making it alive again, on its own terms, as part of itself. You are made of formerly living material. So is most of what you own. The chamber is not hostile — nothing in the description suggests it can want anything in the way you want things — but it has a habit, and you will be inside the habit. Every piece of advice below comes down to the same principle: leave as little of yourself in that room as you can manage, and don't stay long enough for the room to start working on the rest.

---

**Before you descend: the surface**

You arrive above a frozen fen. That means somewhere subarctic or worse, and it means the ordinary problems of that kind of place come first and are not made less real by the strange thing underneath. Expect wind with nothing to break it, expect the light to be either endless or absent depending on season, expect the ground to be hard as pavement and the crack you're looking for to be the only feature. Dress for the surface as you would for any arctic day. You may spend more time on the surface — finding the crack, rigging, waiting for a companion — than you spend below.

Bring a companion. This is not optional in the way most travel advice is not optional. One person goes down; one stays on the rope with a watch and an agreed signal. The chamber gives you good reasons to lose track of time (see below), and the person on the surface is your only clock that the room cannot slow. Agree on a hard limit before anyone touches the rope, and agree that the surface person's call to come up is not a conversation.

The crack leaks smell and sound before it leaks anything else. If you're searching for it, listen: a granular roar, continuous, like a distant fire. You'll hear it before you smell the cold-green cellar smell, and you'll smell it before you see anything.

Frozen peat is hard as wood and can be worked like wood. If the crack needs widening, use a tool with a metal or fiberglass handle. Leave the wooden-handled axe at home, for reasons that will become obvious. Four meters is not far, but it's a crawl through a narrow passage into a chamber with a soft floor at the bottom, so treat it as a proper drop: harness, fixed line, rope long enough to reach the middle of the room with slack. Fix the line to something that will still be there — the peat, not the plant.

---

**What to wear, and what not to**

The rule is: nothing that used to be alive, to whatever degree you can manage it.

That means no wool, no cotton, no down, no leather, no silk. Synthetic base layers, synthetic insulation, a waterproof synthetic shell. Neoprene gloves, tall rubber-alternative or fully synthetic boots. This is a hedge, not a guarantee — the description's line about the plant taking back what was "once itself, or once alive, or once anything" is wide enough to swallow most materials if you follow it far enough, and petroleum was a forest once. But the observed evidence is that the plant reaches first and fastest for the obviously organic: dead sedge, dead springtails, whatever you shed into your own footprints. Give it as little of that as possible.

Think about what you carry, too. Paper notebooks were trees. Cork, cotton cord, wooden pencils, leather watch straps, a felt hat. Food. Go through your kit with the question "was this ever alive?" and set aside anything you answer yes to, or that you'd mind seeing turn grey-green.

Seal your cuffs. The plant moves water along the outside of its filaments, upward, and there is no reason to think water that gets onto your sleeve will behave any differently. Gaiters over boots, gloves under sleeves, hood over collar. You want a closed shell.

Cover every cut, scrape, or hangnail before you go. Don't shave that morning. The plant returns dead tissue to the living side of a line, and you are covered in a layer of dead tissue you don't think about, plus a small amount of blood you'd rather keep. Nobody knows what it does with an open wound. Don't find out.

And don't go down sick, exhausted, hypothermic, or injured. The chamber's business is at the boundary between dead and alive. Approach it from as far onto the living side as you can.

---

**The cold and the wet**

This is the mundane danger and it is the one most likely to actually hurt you.

The air is at freezing or a hair below. The walls sweat. The floor is a saturated mat that gives like a wet mattress, and every footstep wells up with water that aches on contact — real cold, not metaphorical. Your feet will get wet unless your boots are truly sealed to above the knee, and even then the cold conducts through. Feet go numb quickly in that. Numb feet on a floor that gives, under a ceiling that lowers to crawling height at the edges, in dim light, means falls. Falls mean hands on the floor, a knee in a pool, a whole flank pressed into the wall. Wear knee pads. Practice moving slowly on soft ground.

Cold-wet is worse than cold-dry for a body. Hypothermia in a room at exactly freezing, soaked, is a matter of a few hours for an active person and less for a still one. Set your time limit with this in mind before you set it for any other reason.

Bring, and leave at the surface with your companion: a complete dry change of clothes, a thermos of something hot, chemical hand and foot warmers, an insulated pad to stand on while you change. You will want heat immediately on coming up, and heat is the one thing the chamber does not have. Nothing down there is warm. That's worth remembering as a kind of orientation: you are the warmest thing in the room by about thirty-seven degrees, and the room is, among other things, an engine for moving heat around.

One more note on cold: the frozen zone at the edges — where the plant thins into frost and rime and the true peat begins — is colder than the ground it sits in, because the plant is putting its cold there. The crawl in and out passes through that zone. It will bite harder than the surface did. Don't linger in the transition; it is the coldest place you will be, and it's the place where you're on your knees with your gloves on the ground.

---

**Sound**

Bring hearing protection that you can put in and take out easily — foam plugs on a cord, or musician's earplugs that cut the level without deafening you. The animal chorus rises and falls on roughly an hour-long tide, and at its crest you'll have to shout to hear yourself. That's loud enough to damage hearing over an hour, and you'll be there for at least one crest.

But don't wear them the whole time. The sound is information. Under the chorus is the crackling of ice giving way to filament tips — the plant growing into frozen ground — and under that is a tone so low it sits in your chest rather than your ears. You'll want to hear those, especially the crackling, because a change in its rate or direction is one of your warning signs.

About the low tone: infrasound at that pitch is known, in ordinary places, to produce unease, pressure behind the eyes, nausea, a sense of being watched. Expect some of that and don't credit it too much. You may feel dread. Some of it is the room. Some of it is your sternum resonating. You won't be able to tell the difference and you don't need to.

Use the tide as a clock. Note where in the cycle you arrive — rising, cresting, falling. One full cycle is about an hour. Stay for no more than one cycle. When you hear the chorus climbing back toward the loudness you heard on arrival, you've had your hour; start moving toward the crack.

---

**Light**

The room is lit by its own condensation: every hanging drop holds a faint glow, the pools glow brighter, falling drops streak, your breath fogs and is briefly lit from inside. It's dim — grey-green, cave-dim — and it's beautiful, and it's also the only illumination that tells you where the plant is working hardest, because the light is brighter there.

You still need your own light. A headlamp with a red or very dim setting for moving around, and a brighter white setting held in reserve. Use the red for walking. Turn it off to look. The white light will flatten the room and drown the water-light, and you'll lose your ability to read the room's activity; save it for the crawl in and out and for an emergency.

Mark your exit. The chamber has no features that will stay put; the ceiling lowers all the way around; the crack you came in by will look like any other low place once you're twenty paces from it. Clip a small red LED beacon — not a chemical light stick, not anything that'll end up left behind — to the fixed rope where it enters the chamber. Keep the rope in your hand or your eye. If the room grows a finger's width a day generally, but a handspan toward *you* in the time it takes to look at something interesting, assume it can find the rope interesting too.

If you bring a camera, bring one that tolerates cold and condensation, acclimate it to the temperature before you go down so the lens doesn't fog, and forget about tripods on that floor. Long exposures handheld, or none. Honestly, expect the photographs to disappoint you and plan to remember instead.

---

**Breathing**

Your breath fogs constantly, and the fog lights briefly before dying grey. That means the water you exhale is being taken into whatever the light is. That's outbound; the question is inbound. Seed-sized flying things circle the drops under the ceiling. Something in the water glows. The air smells of green and cut stems. There's no evidence any of this is harmful to breathe, and no evidence it isn't.

A simple respirator or a good synthetic mask over nose and mouth is not a bad idea, and it has the side benefit of cutting the fog you produce, which reduces one more thing you're giving the room. If you find it's too much to manage with the cold and the noise and the crawl, at least keep your mouth closed and breathe through your nose. Don't taste anything. Don't touch your face with a glove that has touched a wall.

---

**Behaviour inside: the rules you should keep**

The room has its own rules, which the description lays out: water doesn't freeze where the plant wants water; nothing stays dead for long; nothing hurries. Here are yours.

*Keep moving, slowly.* Not pacing — the floor doesn't want that and you'll tire — but don't stand in one place for more than a few minutes. Your footprints fill with water, then with light, then with growth. Whatever you shed standing still — skin, fibre, warmth, breath — pools where you stand. Shift your weight, take a step, look from somewhere else. Think of it as never letting the room finish a sentence about you.

*Don't sit. Don't lie down. Don't lean.* The temptation is real: the floor is soft, you're cold and tired, the wall gives like a mattress and is a fraction warmer than the air. Contact time is the whole game. A springtail that stops moving is felted over within the hour. You are larger and it would take longer. Don't help it.

*Touch the wall once, if you must, and then look at your glove.* Everyone touches the wall. The description makes clear it's almost involuntary. Do it briefly, one gloved hand, flat, and then hold the glove up in the water-light and see whether anything is on it, whether the wet on it is moving, whether it's faintly lit. Wipe it on your shell, not on your face, not on your other glove. Don't grip a filament; water climbs the outside of them, and the outside of your hand is just another surface tilted the wrong way.

*Don't drop anything. Don't leave anything. Don't eat anything.* Standard advice, with teeth here. Food is dead organic matter; the plant's entire project is dead organic matter. A crumb is a gift. A glove is a gift. A hair is a gift, and you will leave some no matter what you do; the goal is to leave few.

*Don't take anything.* A filament in a jar does not obviously stop being the plant when it's in a jar. Springtails bud from the tips of hairs. Snails grow filament from their shells and root to the wall. Whatever a sample is, it's part of something that has one body and no seam, and you'd be carrying a piece of a habit out into places that haven't learned to expect it. Cold won't stop it — cold is what it uses. You'd need heat, and by then you've brought it somewhere with heat. Leave it.

*Be gentle with the animals and the wall.* Snails tear free of the wall with a wet sound when they leave; you can do that damage too, just by putting a foot wrong. There's no sign the plant retaliates. There's also no sign it distinguishes between a filament it grew and a filament you tore, and the larger shapes that move under the wall like a hand under a blanket are entirely unaccounted for. Don't give them a reason to come to the surface.

*Bring a canary.* This is the one thing you should deliberately leave. Before you go down, put a single dry leaf, or a small piece of dry bread, in a pocket where you can find it with gloves on. When you enter the chamber, set it down on a distinct spot near the rope, in the light, where you can see it from the middle of the room. Look at it every few minutes. It tells you the plant's current rate of interest in dead things that arrived today — which is to say, in you. If it's greening or softening or growing a down of filament before the chorus has crested once, your hour is shorter than you planned. If it's felted over, leave immediately. Whatever you do, don't pick it up again.

---

**What to look at, since you came to look**

The place rewards patience, which is exactly the problem, so decide beforehand what you want to see and go to it rather than drifting.

The growth face, at the far end where the ceiling comes down lowest, is the thing worth the trip. It's where the dead peat is being turned. If you crawl close — and you'll have to crawl, and it's cold there — and the water-light is right, you can see the band, thinner than a leaf, where a sedge stem that died ten thousand years ago takes on colour and give and becomes a filament turning toward the room. It doesn't look like eating. It looks like something being reminded.

The worms, if you can bear to watch one long enough, come off the wall the same way: half worm, half filament, the boundary sliding down the body until it drops into the wet. It's slow. It'll take longer than you want to give it. Give it a little and then move.

The ceiling lattice falls into near-hexagons in places and then breaks the pattern as if correcting itself. The hanging drops, thousands of them, beaded like strings. The way shadows fall upward from the pools. The ring of silence the animals make around your feet.

That last one. Look at it, and understand what it is. The animals are loud everywhere in the room and quiet around you. They've gathered and gone still. The plant has noticed you, in whatever way a body with no centre notices. That's your baseline. If the ring widens — if the quiet spreads outward — or if they start to climb, that's no longer baseline.

---

**Signs that mean leave now**

Not "soon." Now, moving toward the rope while you read the rest of this list.

- Filaments that were a handspan from you are touching you. The description says this happens "eventually." Eventually is your limit.
- Your footprints behind you are lit and something is growing in them. Same.
- The ring of quiet animals around your feet is wider than it was, or they're on your boots.
- The canary has changed.
- The frost at the edges has withdrawn from your boots or your rope, the way it withdraws from living filament. That would mean the plant has extended its liquid zone to include you, and that is not a category you want to be in.
- The crackling of ice has changed rate or seems to come from behind you, between you and the exit.
- Something under the wall is raising the felt toward you rather than passing by.
- Your breath fog stays lit longer than it did when you arrived.
- Your companion signals. No discussion.
- You find yourself negotiating with the time limit. This is the most reliable sign of all. The room does not hurry and it will teach you not to hurry, and that lesson is the mechanism by which people who meant to stay an hour stay four. When you catch yourself thinking *just until the worm drops*, that's the thought that means you go.

---

**Coming out**

The crawl back through the frozen zone will be the coldest part of the visit, on your knees, with numb feet, and by now some panic if you left for the right reasons. Go carefully anyway. Put your hands where you can see them.

On the surface, before anything else: strip. All of it, down to skin, in the wind, on the insulated pad your companion has ready. It's miserable. Do it. Everything you wore goes into a sealed synthetic bag, and — this is the part travelers resist — you look at every piece under a bright white light before it goes in. You're looking for wet that's still moving, for glow, for down. Especially boots and gloves and knees and anywhere you fell. Then the dry clothes, then the thermos, then the warmers, then and only then do you talk about what you saw.

Check your skin. Every inch you can reach and have your companion check the rest. Any patch that's wet when it should have dried, faintly luminous, faintly furred. Wipe with something dry and synthetic and burn or bag the wipe.

What's in the bag: the sensible thing is to burn it, as soon as you're somewhere fire is possible. Heat is the one condition the plant is not seen to use. If you can't bring yourself to do that, bag it doubly and don't open it anywhere cold and wet, and understand you're gambling. Whatever you do, don't put it in a freezer to be safe. That isn't safe. That's home.

Expect your hearing to ring for a day. Expect to be more tired than the effort accounts for; that's the cold and the sound and the infrasound and the attention. Expect to want to go back.

---

**A last thing**

The description ends by noting the plant has not made a way out, and won't stop you leaving. Both are true and they're the whole shape of the danger. Nothing there will chase you or hold you. It only does one thing, very slowly, to everything it finds that used to be alive, and it's very good at it, and it's had ten thousand years to be patient. The room has no threat you can react to. It has only a pace, and the pace is designed — no, not designed, *inclined* — to become your pace.

So go down warm, closed, synthetic, watched from above, with a leaf in your pocket and a limit you've promised someone else. Look at what you came to look at. Notice when the room begins to look back. Leave before the second crest. And don't bring anything or anyone you'd want returned in its original form — the plant does give things back, in its way, but what it gives back is wall.
