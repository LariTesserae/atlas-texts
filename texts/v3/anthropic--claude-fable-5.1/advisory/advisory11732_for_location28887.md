## Preparing for the Warm Shelf

Start by accepting that you are packing for two expeditions that happen to share a wall. The first is a high-altitude climb in thin, freezing air. The second is an extended stay in a wet tropical forest where nothing ever dries. Almost nothing you need for one is any use in the other, and the point where they meet — the lip of the shelf — is a few metres of rock where you will be in more danger than anywhere else on the trip, going up or coming down. Plan the whole thing around that lip.

---

### The approach and the wall

The lower part is honest mountaineering and should be treated as such. You will be walking up through five thousand metres before you even touch the basalt, and the wall itself is two kilometres of dark rock, iced at the base, wet above. Acclimatise properly on the way. Don't skip rest days because the forest is waiting; the forest has been waiting a long time and will not mind a few more nights. If you are not already a competent climber on mixed rock and ice, you should not be leading this wall. Go with people who are.

Understand what the wall is made of. Low down it is glazed with old ice, which is a known thing. Higher, the ice gives way to running water that is warm to the touch in air that is well below freezing, and the rock is furred with moss and hung with drip. Warm water on cold rock in freezing air is exactly the recipe for verglas wherever the water cools even slightly, so expect thin, unpredictable glaze in the shaded seams and on any surface the flow has just left. Wet moss on basalt is about the worst climbing medium there is. Protection in the seams will be placed into running water. Ropes will get soaked and then freeze.

The warm water is a trap. It feels pleasant on your hands and you will want to let it run over you. Don't. A wet body in sub-zero air at six thousand metres loses heat catastrophically once it stops climbing, and the warmth in the water is not enough to make up for evaporation. Keep your shell sealed, keep your gloves dry, and treat the seeps the way you'd treat any water on a winter climb: as something to stay out of.

Notice, as you get higher, that the mist off the falls above is moving *toward* the wall and *up* it. You are climbing into the intake of a system. The air near the top may be slightly warmer and noticeably damper than the air below, and the smell — leaf-rot and something like clove — will reach you well before the forest does. Take it as a sign you are close and not as permission to shed layers.

---

### The lip

The edge is not a summit and does not behave like one. On a normal mountain, the last move onto the top puts you in the same air you've been in for hours. Here, within a rope-length, you go from thin freezing air into air that is warm, thick, and saturated. Your body has spent days adjusting to hypoxia. It will not know what to do with this for a while. Expect to feel drunk, or suddenly very heavy, or to have your ears complain. Sit down. Give it an hour before you do anything that requires judgement.

Everything at the rim is soaked, permanently, by spray from the falls and by mist coming back up over the edge. The rock there is honeycombed with finger-width tunnels running with water and full of frogs. It is slippery in a way that does not look slippery. Do not walk the rim for the view. Get well back into the trees before you take your harness off.

Now the important part: you have to come back down this same edge, and when you do, you will be coming out of days or weeks of heat and rain, sweat-soaked, with clothing that has not been dry since you arrived, and you will be stepping into below-freezing air within a few metres of leaving the canopy. That transition will kill you if you are casual about it. So, on arrival, do this: strip out of your climbing layers before they can rot, dry them as best you can (they will not dry — you will have to settle for wrung out), and seal them in the best waterproof bags you own, with desiccant if you have it, and cache them somewhere back from the rim where the rain is lighter, off the ground and marked so you can find them again. Your cold kit is your ticket out. Treat it as more precious than your food.

---

### Living wet

Once you are in, the conditions are simple and relentless: it is warm, it is wet, and it stays that way. Fine rain most hours, heavy rain every evening, mist dropping through the canopy all night. The soil is a half-metre of warm sponge. The paths are streams. Every leaf sheds water onto you.

Nothing organic survives this well. Leather will go green. Cotton will rot on you. Paper will turn to felt. Bring synthetics and wool and nothing else, and bring more socks than you think is reasonable, sealed in individual dry bags. The single most likely thing to end your trip early is your feet: skin that stays wet and warm for days macerates, cracks, and infects. Dry your feet every night even though nothing else is dry — a towel kept in a sealed bag, used only for this. Powder them. Sleep with them bare and elevated. Check between the toes every morning. Do the same for your armpits and groin, which will develop fungal problems in this air if you let them. Antifungal cream is not optional.

Sleep off the ground. A hammock with a good tarp, and a bug net, because a forest with no winter and standing water in every bromeliad will have insects, and you should assume some of them bite, whether or not the description mentions them. Whether your repellent works on local biology is an open question; bring it anyway. Assume leeches on the paths. Check yourself at dusk.

Fire is going to be nearly impossible. The wood is saturated to the core and has been for centuries, and there is no dry tinder anywhere. Bring a stove and fuel for every meal, and bring more than you calculate, because you will burn extra trying to dry small things and warm yourself at night — not because the nights are cold, but because after enough days of being wet, warmth from a stove is a psychological need.

Water is everywhere and looks clean and smells strange. Filter it and treat it. It is warm, it is full of frog eggs and tannin and whatever has been rotting in the canopy above, and there is no reason to trust it just because it is beautiful. The clove smell is part of the place; if it changes, or goes away, pay attention.

Eat what you brought. Do not eat the frogs; anything breeding that densely in bromeliads in a forest with no predators has had a very long time to become poisonous, and the calm of the animals is not the calm of things that are safe to eat, it is the calm of things nothing eats. Do not eat the snails, which the hoofed animals depend on. Do not eat the fruit unless you can afford to lose a day to your gut. Nothing here is scarce for the things that live here. Everything is scarce for you.

The heat is real and total, and if the air on the shelf is as thick as it seems — closer to lowland pressure than to seven thousand metres — the main risk is heat exhaustion and simply not drinking enough. If instead the air is warm but still thin, you are looking at the worst combination there is: hypoxia plus heat plus humidity. You will know which within the first hour. If it is the second, do not stay long.

Bring sun protection. The light is soft and shadowless and feels gentle, and at that elevation you should assume it is not.

---

### Finding your way

This is the problem the description underplays. There is no horizon. The canopy closes over you, the mist comes down through it, and the light casts no hard shadows, so you cannot read direction from the sun. The sound of the falls is everywhere and nearly identical in every direction. A compass may or may not work over basalt in a place where gravity has been locally renegotiated — check it against a known bearing at the rim before you trust it, and don't be surprised if it wanders. There is no satellite over your head. There is nothing over your head but a lens of cold.

So navigate like it's the nineteenth century. Cord, tags, and a notebook you have already accepted will be ruined by the end. Mark your route from the cache at the rim. Use the hoof-paths, which go somewhere and have gone there for a very long time, and use the groove-worn circuits around the great trees as fixed points — each one is unique and you can learn them. The roar of the falls is at least a rough edge-detector: louder means you are approaching the rim, and you do not want to approach the rim in mist. The spindle on the eastern bench is the one landmark that stands above the trees; if you can see it, you know where you are.

Move slowly. The paths are stream-beds with stones in them, and the stones are slick, and a sprained ankle here means someone else carrying your cold kit down a two-kilometre wall.

---

### The animals

They will not fear you. They will part around you and close behind you and go back to what they were doing. This is pleasant for about a day and then it becomes something you have to manage.

Don't feed them. Don't touch them. The hyrax-things will steal small objects if they can get to them, so keep your gear zipped and hung. The tapir-things are small and unafraid and still have hooves and weight; don't hang your hammock across a path they use, and don't sleep in the groove around a big tree — that groove is theirs and they walk it at night. Watch where you step, for the snails, which are slow and white and easy to see and which the whole calcium economy of the shelf depends on.

Wear earplugs at dusk if you value your hearing. The frog chorus at the evening rain is a physical thing, a wall of sound, and it goes on for a long time. Take them out afterward, though, because the night is when the place tells you the most.

The seven-note call in the upper canopy: nobody has seen what makes it. You will want to. Don't spend your trip climbing trees in the rain looking for it. If it wanted to be seen it would have been seen by now.

---

### The made things

You will find them if you look, and you may find them if you don't. Straight seams in the rock. The spindle. The circular pool that is always exactly full. The rings in the moss on the northern saddle, visible only from above. Discs and rods and a sheet of something that will not tear, turned up by the animals and left lying.

Leave all of it exactly as it is. This is not a rule about respect, though it is also that. It is a rule about not being the variable that a very old, very stable system finally has to respond to. Whatever made the shelf turned the water around and pinned the air and warmed the rock and then either left or stopped being findable. It has not needed to adjust for the forest, or the frogs, or the hyraxes, and it does not need to adjust for you, as long as you behave the way they do: pass through, take nothing, put nothing in.

That means biosecurity in both directions. Clean your boots and gear before you go up the wall — no seeds in your laces, no soil in your tread. The forest has absorbed a great deal over a long time without visible change, but it has never had to absorb anything that came with a person. And clean everything again before you go down. You do not want to carry spores off the shelf into a valley that has winter.

Don't camp near the spindle. Don't drink from the pool. Don't dig in the moss rings. If you pick something up to look at it, put it back where it was, the same way up. You will feel foolish being that careful about what looks like a stone. Be foolish.

---

### Time, and the tone, and leaving

The real hazard of the shelf is not the wet or the wall. It is that the place is content, and after a while you will be too, and contentment in a system with no gradients has no natural end.

Every day is the same day. Fine rain, heavy rain at dusk, mist by night, frogs, drip. The dusk lasts too long and the ceiling glows before it darkens and there is a tone under everything that you can hear if you stand still and want to. Nothing is scarce. Nothing is coming. The animals have been living inside this for longer than there have been people anywhere, and they are perfectly calm, and their calm is catching.

So decide, before you go up, how many nights you are staying. Write it down. Mark each night on something that will survive the wet — a notch in a stick, a knot in a cord. Do not renegotiate the number once you are on the shelf; the shelf is very persuasive, and it does not argue, it just goes on being pleasant. When the notches run out, go to the cache, and put on your cold kit, and go over the edge.

You will not want to. That is the point of writing the number down.

---

### The descent

Go to the cache the night before, not the morning of. Check that the cold kit is intact and as dry as it was going to get. Sleep near it, back from the rim.

In the morning, before you approach the edge, strip completely and dry yourself with whatever is least wet, and get into the cold layers while you are still under the trees and still warm. Do not walk to the rim in your forest clothes and change there; the rim is in the spray, and you will be cold and wet in freezing air before you have your harness buckled. Your hands need to work for the rope. Get them dry and gloved while they still can.

The first few metres over the edge are the crux of the whole trip. Warm thick air behind you, cold thin air below, mist blowing up the face into you, running water in every hold, and a body that has spent a week or more at what felt like two thousand metres and is about to be back at seven. Move deliberately. Expect to feel the altitude hit you hard and fast — you have effectively de-acclimatised. Do not rush the wall to get back into thicker air lower down; rushing on wet moss is how people leave the wall the fast way, and unlike the water, you will not be drawn back.

Below the rim, the wall drinks. That is where you are going. Get down it carefully, and clean your boots at the bottom, and take nothing with you but the notches in the stick.
