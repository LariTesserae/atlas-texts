**Preparing for the Dark Plateau**

Start with the honest inventory of what you are about to lose, because most of the mistakes travelers make here come from not believing it until they arrive.

You will not see. Not "it will be very dark." Not "your eyes will adjust." The suppression isn't a shortage of photons — it reaches past the retina. You will get no phosphenes when you press your eyes, which means the visual cortex itself is quiet, and that means every trick you own for cheating darkness is worthless. Night-vision optics amplify nothing. Thermal cameras will presumably form an image, but you will never look at the image. A glowing watch face, a phone screen, a headlamp — you may as well leave them home and save the weight, and you should, because weight matters at altitude and moisture kills them anyway. Whatever you bring must be *readable by hand or ear.* If a piece of equipment communicates with you only through your eyes, it does not exist in this place.

The corollary is that you are going to a world with two senses and a half. Hearing, touch, and smell. Taste if you're reckless. That's the kit you have. So the first and longest phase of preparation has nothing to do with gear.

---

**Train before you go, and train longer than feels reasonable**

Live blindfolded. Not an afternoon. Weeks, in stretches, at home, at first, then outdoors. The plateau will not care whether you can pour water without spilling, tie a knot by feel, find the zipper on a pack, eat without dropping half of it into the wet. You need those things to be automatic before you leave, because there is no light to check your work by and dropped things do not come back. The ground is a warm rotting mat with standing water in every hollow and small quick bodies moving through it. Anything that leaves your hand and isn't tethered is gone. Practice until your hands are more careful than your habits.

Learn to walk without sight in real terrain — woods, slopes, stream banks. You'll discover your feet are much smarter than you thought and your balance is much worse. You will learn to shorten your stride, to test before committing weight, to keep a hand out. Learn to use a staff, not as a cane for the blind is used but as a probe: sweeping ahead and to the sides, tapping to hear what gives sound back. Metal rings. Trunks thud. Water splashes. The clean edge of the tableland gives back nothing at all, and that nothing is the most important sound you'll learn to hear.

Practice listening. Sit in a noisy place — a mill, a waterfall, a highway underpass — and try to pick apart the layers. The plateau's roar has strata: a deep throb you feel in your teeth, a high hiss, a middle-register clatter, and under everything a pressure that isn't quite sound. Everything you need to know about where you are is encoded in how those layers shift. Where the throb grows, an engine is near. Where the hiss thins and the drip becomes loud, the forest is thinning toward marsh. Where everything ahead falls away into soft absence with warm wind moving up through it, stop walking. Stop walking *now.*

If you can, spend time at altitude — three thousand meters or more — for a couple of weeks before departure. The air there is thin under the warmth; your breathing runs fast. It's a strange combination, hot and wet and thin, and the body doesn't have a script for it. Acclimatize to the thin part where you can. Bring what you'd bring for altitude sickness. Know the signs in yourself, because there's no one to see your lips going blue.

**Your body is the most vulnerable thing you're bringing**

You will be wet every moment you are there. Water condenses on your skin faster than it evaporates. Take this seriously, because the warmth makes it seem harmless and it is not. Skin that stays saturated for days macerates — it softens, splits, peels, and every crack becomes an entry point for whatever grows in warm standing water. Feet go first. Then the groin, the armpits, under the pack straps, behind the knees. Trench foot is not a cold-weather condition; it's a wet-feet condition, and this is the wettest place you will ever stand.

So: skin care is your medical priority. Dry your feet whenever you stop, even knowing they'll be wet again in minutes. Change socks — bring more socks than seems sane, in sealed bags, and understand that "dry" is relative. Use barrier ointment on everything that rubs. Bring antifungal treatment, a lot of it. Bring wound care that assumes every cut will stay wet; nothing will scab properly, so think in terms of waterproof sealing, not gauze. Cover any cut immediately. Warm saturated air full of unfamiliar microbes is not where you want an open wound.

The noise is a slower harm. You'll be near engines often — they're where the hard ground is, where the warmth concentrates, where you'll orient — and at the wall the roar isn't a background but the whole acoustic world, plus a stroke you feel through your spine. Days of that damage hearing, and hearing is now half your perception. This is a real dilemma. Ear protection deafens you to the clicks and the silences that tell you what's moving. The compromise most experienced travelers settle on is to sleep protected, walk unprotected, and spend as little time at the wall itself as your purposes allow. The low frequency — the pressure-that-isn't-sound — has its own effects: nausea, unease, a dull dread that has no object. If you feel it building, walk away from the engine until it eases. It's the place, not you.

You will also not sleep well, and your body clock will drift, because nothing marks time. More on that below, but budget for exhaustion. Everything takes longer without sight. A camp that takes ten minutes to make at home takes forty. Plan half the daily distance you think you can do, then halve it again.

**Gear: what survives, and what doesn't**

Rule one, already stated: nothing that talks to you through a screen or a light.

Rule two: everything rots. Iron there has gone to lace. Paper will be pulp in a day. Cotton will mildew and fall apart. Leather will slime. Cheap steel will pit. Plan for materials that don't mind being wet forever: bronze and brass fittings, stainless or titanium blades and buckles, synthetic webbing, silicone, waxed and sealed synthetics. Wool holds its shape wet and doesn't rot fast; merino next to the skin is not a bad choice even though warmth isn't the concern. Synthetic quick-dry layers for the rest, and accept that "quick-dry" is a fiction here.

Rule three: everything is tethered. Every tool, every bottle, every bag, on a lanyard to you or to the pack. Practice clipping and unclipping blind. Build the habit of returning things to the exact same place on your body every time — knife on the left hip, bottle on the right, staff always in the right hand — so your body knows the inventory without your eyes.

Rule four: keep a dry core. A few double-sealed bags with your most important things: medications, spare socks, food, whatever you need for the return. Open them rarely and briefly. Nothing stays dry that is opened often.

Specific items worth the weight:

*A staff*, long enough to probe ahead a full stride, with a hard tip that rings on metal and stone. Not collapsible; you can't afford it failing.

*A tactile timepiece.* A mechanical watch with a raised or open dial you can feel, or a minute-repeater that chimes the time when you press it. Mechanical, because batteries and moisture are enemies and because you cannot read a display. Time is the only anchor you'll have.

*Chain, not rope.* Rope swells, rots, and frays where things nibble it. A few dozen meters of light stainless chain or a synthetic line with metal core, for tethering to a companion and for marking. Yes, it's heavy. It's also the only thing you'll bring that will still be where you left it.

*A fixed-length measure.* Knotted cord is traditional, but knotted cord rots. Know your pace count and bring a measuring line you can trust, because you'll be navigating by counting.

*Gloves*, thin enough to feel through, tough enough to touch pipe. The pipes are hot enough to hurt if you hold on. The vents throw plumes you'll feel a hundred paces off; walk into one blind and you'll be scalded. Every engine you approach, approach slowly, hand out, reading the temperature gradient.

*Chemical water treatment* in generous quantity. Boiling is unreliable: a flame doesn't just go invisible, it seems to *struggle*, and the air is so saturated you can't count on sustaining fire. Pump filters clog fast in water that thick with life. Tablets or drops are the answer. Bring twice what you'd calculate. And even treated, be cautious of the moat water around engines — it's warm and swarming. Rain catch off the canopy is the safest source, and the canopy delivers it constantly in slow drips. A wide-mouthed collector left under the trees fills itself.

*Food* sealed in single-use portions, because you cannot see mold and you cannot reseal anything properly in that air. There is nothing there you can eat with any confidence; the metal-moss smells of pennies, which is your body telling you no. Bring every calorie you'll burn and then more, because the altitude and the constant wet cold-that-isn't-cold both raise your burn.

*A whistle or bell*, with reservations. A whistle can find a lost companion in the roar — a high tone cuts through better than a voice. But every sound you make is heard. See below.

Leave behind: anything with a battery you plan to depend on; anything paper; matches (they're only useful as a way of confirming the rule, and you'll believe it soon enough); cameras, obviously; hope of a record other than the one in your hands and ears.

**Navigation, which is to say, not getting lost forever**

This is the part that kills people who are otherwise competent. The plateau has no visible landmarks, no sun, no horizon, and its own weather that erases scent trails and drowns sound. Rain fronts come through for what feels like hours, and when they pass, the place you were standing sounds different. If you walk off in a direction with no plan for coming back, you will not come back.

Fortunately the place has a structure, and the structure is readable.

*The engines are fixed.* They have not moved in longer than anything alive there remembers. They are the only permanent, findable, unmistakable features: a hill of warm pitted iron in a clearing of hard ground, a moat of warm water around it, a stroke you feel through the ground long before you reach the wall. You can always find an engine by walking toward the throb. Make an engine your anchor. Not the exit point itself — your point of arrival is just a spot in the wet, indistinguishable from any other — but the *nearest engine to your exit point.*

The procedure is this. When you arrive, don't move for a while. Listen. Find the direction of the nearest throb. Then walk to it, counting every pace, keeping as straight a line as trunks allow and noting every deviation. When you reach the wall, follow it — always the same direction, say clockwise with your right hand on it — until you find a feature you can recognize by touch and will recognize again: a stub of ladder, a particular pipe junction, a ridge of shell colonies with a distinctive shape. Fix your chain there if you can. Then walk your count back out along the reverse bearing and confirm you can find your exit. Do it twice before you go anywhere else. Every excursion after that starts and ends at that fixed point on that wall.

*The pipes are roads.* Body-thick, sweating, running at reachable height from engine to engine, a day or two's walk apart. The forest has grown into them but not broken them. You can follow a pipe with a gloved hand and never be lost, because at the end is an engine and along the way is your route back. Don't hold on — they burn — but trail your fingers along the growth on top. Where a pipe branches, mark the branch with something you can feel: a wrapped bit of chain, a notch cut in the growth, something.

*The trunks lean toward heat.* In any given stretch, every trunk leans the same way, toward the nearest engine. Put your hands on three trunks and you have a compass needle. When the lean changes, you've crossed the midline between two engines, which is useful to know and useful to dread, because the midlines are where the warmth runs thin and the forest gives way to low rubbery mats and standing water and you will be moving through marsh with nothing to hold.

*The edge announces itself, once.* The roar ahead of you goes soft and vast, the ground under your staff finds nothing, and warm wet wind comes up out of the absence. There's no slope, no scree, no warning gradient. If you feel that change, stop, back up, and go another way. Don't approach it out of curiosity. Whatever is below is exhaling across the whole tableland, and the edge is the lip of its mouth, and things that live near it have presumably learned what you have not.

Count everything. Paces, turns, engines passed, pipe junctions. Speak the counts aloud to your companion. Write nothing, because nothing you write survives. Your memory, disciplined, is your map.

And bring a companion. Two people, tethered, are exponentially safer than one. One can hold the pipe while the other probes. One can stay at the anchor while the other ranges. And one can talk, which matters more than you'd think.

**The animals, and the fact that you are one**

There are more of them than you expect, and none of them have eyes, and you will be handled by them.

Things in the water bump your ankles. Something dog-sized lives in the spiral ridges of the trunks and clicks constantly, and the clicks come back to it off you, and it knows exactly where you are and how big and how you're moving. Something long and low moves through the mats near the pipes and lifts its head toward your warmth and stays lifted, tasting. Piping in the canopy you feel in your sinuses more than hear. None of them fear you, because fear here is learned by being touched, not seen, and you haven't touched them yet.

Understand what you are to them. You are warm — much warmer than the ambient in most places, comparable to a pipe. You are loud: footsteps, breathing, the rustle of gear, a heartbeat. You move in the water and push pressure waves ahead of you. In a world that navigates by heat and sound and current, you are a beacon. Everything that hunts by heat will come to see what you are. Most of it will conclude you are not food and go about its business. Not all of it.

The predators announce themselves as silence: a hole in the clicking, the shape of something that has learned to be quiet, moving through. When the clicking stops around you, stand still, back to a trunk or a wall, and wait. The commotion, when it comes, will probably be somewhere else. But you should assume that the thing that makes silence has a heat sense, and that you are the warmest thing for a hundred paces, and behave accordingly: keep your head low, keep your hands in, keep your blade where your hand already knows it is.

Don't handle what you don't have to, even though you can, even though they let you. Smooth blank heads and soft mouths are irresistible, and you'll want to, and a traveler is allowed a little of that — it's part of why anyone comes. But some of what lives here will sting or bite or secrete, and you will not see the swelling start. If you do touch, use gloves, and wash your hands with treated water after.

Keep your wounds covered. Keep food sealed. Don't sleep on the mat if you can help it; string a hammock between trunks, high enough that the low animals can't taste your heat, and accept that the clicking things will investigate you all night. They aren't hostile. They're just checking.

The moats around the engines are the richest places for life and the most dangerous for you: warm, thick, swarming, with larger slower things surfacing like buckets lifted from wells. Cross them where the tremor has worn the bottom hard and the water is shallowest. Don't wade to the wall in the deep places.

**Time and sleep**

There is no hour, so you have to make one. Set a schedule by your watch and keep it rigidly: walk this many hours, rest this many, sleep this many. Your body will try to drift toward a longer day, as bodies do in the dark, and you'll find yourself awake for thirty hours and then exhausted for twenty, and exhausted travelers walk off edges. Chime your watch every time you stop. Say the time aloud. Anchor the day to the count of engines passed, the meals eaten, the sock changes made.

Sleeping near an engine has advantages — hard dry ground, warmth, an unmistakable landmark — and the price is the roar and the tremor and the fact that everything alive concentrates there. Sleeping in the forest between engines is quieter and softer and wetter and lonelier. Most travelers find they can't sleep at the wall at all and settle for camping a few hundred counted paces off, where the throb is a presence but not a fist. Wherever you sleep, tether yourselves to each other and to a trunk. People roll. People wake disoriented and walk.

**The mind, which is the real terrain**

Everything above is manageable with training. This is the part that isn't.

Complete darkness does things to people, and this darkness is more complete than any you've been in — no glow at the edge of a door, no stars, no hand in front of your face, not even the shifting phosphene noise your own visual system normally produces. It's an absence so total that the mind at first refuses it and keeps straining, and the straining exhausts you. You will catch yourself with your eyes wide open, searching. Learn to close them. There's nothing to be gained by keeping them open and it's restful to stop trying. Many travelers describe the moment they stop trying to see as the moment the place opens to them: the layers of the roar separate, the drips become individual, the trunk under the hand gets a texture like a face.

Ordinary sensory deprivation produces hallucinations within hours. Here, visual hallucinations seem not to occur — the suppression takes those too — but auditory ones do, and they're hard to distinguish from the real, because the real is a constant layered roar full of half-patterns. You will hear voices in the hiss. You will hear your name in the clicking. You will become convinced the far engines are answering each other in something like speech. Have a companion, and a rule: what only one of you hears, neither of you acts on.

The loneliness has a specific flavor. You are in a place that has never been seen, by anyone, ever, and that does not need to be. Some people find that unbearable. Others find it the only real reason to go. Know which you are before you leave, or be ready to find out.

And there's the fact of the engines. They are running, and there is nobody in them, and they have weathered past the point of having been built for anything. You'll want to know why. You'll put your hands on the ghost of a rivet and try to reconstruct a purpose. You will get nowhere, and the getting nowhere can become an obsession that eats your time and your judgment. The engines don't say. The place isn't a puzzle; it's a condition. Accept that early. Touch the iron, feel the stroke come up through your spine, and let it be what it is.

Don't try to get into an engine. There are no doors you'll find and the searching takes you into hot vents and deep moat. Don't try to stop one, or adjust one, or take pieces. The loop closed a long time ago and it's fine without you, and you have no way of knowing what a stopped engine would do to the tableland downwind.

Leave the ruins alone too. The low cut-stone wall, the ground that rings hollow, the iron post with the ring at the top standing alone in a marsh: these are evidence that someone was here and did not stay, or did not stay in a form that leaves marks. Hollow ground can be a chamber roof, and chamber roofs in a place where iron goes to lace are not to be walked on. A post with a ring in a marsh is where something was tied. You don't know what. Don't tie anything there yourself.

---

**Leaving**

Plan your exit from before you enter. Know your window, know your count back to the anchor engine, know the bearing from the wall to your arrival point. Give yourself twice the time you think you'll need for the return, because you'll be tired and the rain will have changed the sounds.

Prepare for light. Your pupils have been fully dilated for days and your visual system has been silent. The return will hurt — travelers report it as a blow — and it will disorient you badly, because your body has recalibrated to a world where the eyes don't matter and now they suddenly do, hugely, all at once. Have dark glasses waiting at the exit point. Have someone there if you can. Expect nausea. Expect to stumble more in the light, at first, than you did in the dark.

Expect your gear to be ruined and quarantine it anyway. Everything you carried is furred with something, and you don't know what it does in a world with light.

And expect, quietly, to have been changed. People come back from the plateau with their hands more awake than they were. They listen differently. Some of them find that sight, returned, feels loud and shallow, and it takes a while to trust it again. Some of them miss the stroke of the piston coming up through the spine, the drip arriving from a roof they never saw, the low animal lifting its head toward their heat with something that wasn't affection and wasn't hunger and wasn't anything with a name.

It doesn't need you. It has never been seen. It will go on exactly as it did before you came, and if you prepare well, so will you.
