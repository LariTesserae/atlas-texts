**Preparing to visit the drowned terraces**

First, be honest with yourself about what kind of trip this is. It is not a hike and it is not a swim. It is a cold-water overhead dive in an unmapped structure the size of a continent, with no bottom, no shore, no help, and nothing alive that will notice whether you come back up. Everything that could kill you here is passive. The place will not reach for you. You will have to do all of the reaching yourself, and most of the ways to die here involve reaching a little too far — one more room, one more wall corner, one more minute of cold — and not being able to reach back.

Prepare as if for a cave dive, a wreck dive, and a polar expedition at once, and then prepare for the part none of those cover, which is being the only moving thing in a world that finished happening a long time ago.

---

**1. Where you arrive, and why it matters**

There are only two places to arrive: on the surface or under it. Both are bad in different ways.

If you arrive on the surface, you arrive on a raft of slate-colored weed that will hold you for one breath and then let you through. Do not stand up. Do not try to walk. If you find yourself on the canopy, lie flat, spread your weight, and get your equipment sorted while you still have air around your face, because when the raft gives you go down into black cold water with fifty feet of tangled blade above you and no clear way back to the sky. People drown in kelp within arm's reach of the surface. Here the surface is not open water; it is a lid.

If you can choose, arrive *under* the canopy, already dressed, already breathing from a tank, with your first light on and your descent line fixed. Treat the canopy as the ceiling of a cave and treat the moment you first pass through it as the most dangerous moment of the trip. Everything below is more forgiving than that lid.

Whatever you do, bring or make a float that can sit on the weed: a small inflatable, a flat punt, a raft of sealed drums lashed under a platform. It will not travel — you cannot row through this — but it gives you a place to be that is not water. You will need it between dives. There is no other dry surface in this world. Not a rock, not a stump, not a bird's back.

---

**2. Cold**

The cold is not incidental. It is the climate. The surface air is grey and wet and never warms; the water beneath the canopy is very cold and gets colder with depth, and the water in the rooms and the cisterns is colder again — a still, layered, weighted cold that has not mixed with anything in centuries.

You want a drysuit, not a wetsuit, and you want thick undergarments beneath it, and you want a hood and dry gloves. But here is the trouble: this place is *tactile*. Its whole meaning is in the fit of one stone to the next, the softness of an antler pick, the grit of the mats along a floor. Thick gloves take that away. So bring two pairs — the heavy ones for the descent and the swim, and a thin pair you can change into (or a heavy pair with a way to bare one hand briefly) once you've reached a wall you want to know. Budget your bare-hand time in minutes. Numb fingers cannot feel a joint, cannot work a reel, cannot find a valve.

Have a plan for rewarming that does not depend on the sun, because there is none. Chemical heat packs. A shelter on the float. Hot fluid in an insulated flask, plenty of it. Hypothermia here begins on the surface, in the fog, in the wind off the swell, before you have even gone under.

---

**3. Breathing and depth**

The canopy sits on the water. The tops of the stipes are within a body-length of the surface. The stipes rise fifty to eighty feet from their holdfasts, so the highest platform roofs are roughly fifteen to twenty-five meters down. Terraces step below that. The cisterns are cut down through three or four layers of building — assume forty meters, assume more, and assume you cannot see the bottom because you can't.

Bring gas for the dive you are actually going to do, then bring more. Redundant supply — twin cylinders, or a stage bottle staged on your descent stipe, or at minimum a full independent bailout. There is no one to share air with. There is no surface to bolt to; the surface is weed. Plan your decompression conservatively, because cold water thickens your blood and narrows your margins, and because the stops themselves will be spent hanging in black water in a crowd of stipes, cold, waiting.

Nothing in the water will hurt your lungs; it is only water. But the rooms are anoxic and the silt breathes a slow chemistry. Do not surface inside a room, ever — there are no air pockets, and if there were, they would not be air you could use.

---

**4. Light, line, and the discipline of not getting lost**

Understand this plainly: below the first body-length there is no useful light, and inside the rooms there is none at all. You are entirely dependent on what you carry. Primary light, two backups, all tested, all on lanyards, the backups small enough to live on your harness. Batteries for a week if you plan to stay a day. Cold kills batteries faster than the rating on the box.

Run a line. From the moment you leave your descent stipe, run a continuous guideline back to it, and mark the stipe itself — a strobe if you have one, a wrapped reflective band, something you can find with a light from thirty meters away. The forest all looks the same in the dark. The walls all look the same. That is the single most dangerous fact about the place: there are no landmarks, only pattern, and pattern shifts too slowly to steer by. A hundred wall corners, each one exactly like the last. You will be certain you know the way back. You will be wrong. Follow the line.

Line arrows for direction, cookies for personal markers, a spare reel. Practice tying off to stone before you come — the corners are rounded, the joints are shallow grooves, and a wrap that would hold on a sharp block slips off a stone that has been softened for a thousand years. The stipes are better tie-offs than the walls, and they move.

Bring a compass, but test it on arrival before you trust it. You do not know this world's magnetism.

Inside the rooms, the line is not optional. It is your life. A doorway you can find by feel going in becomes, after you have stirred the floor, a doorway you cannot find at all, in a cloud that takes an hour to settle, in total darkness, with the cold coming up through your suit and your gas ticking down.

---

**5. Silt and stone**

Learn to move without touching the bottom. Frog kick, or a modified flutter with your fins above the level of your body; never a bicycle kick, never a hand pushed off the floor. In the open forest the silt is manageable — the current, slow as it is, clears it. In the rooms it is deep, soft, and has been undisturbed since the flood, and one careless fin turns the room into a zero-visibility trap.

Buoyancy control is everything. You should be able to hover motionless a hand's width above a floor and turn in place without stirring it. If you cannot do that in a pool, do not go into the rooms. Practice it until it is boring.

Stone: the walls are dry-laid. Nothing holds them but weight and fit and the friction of centuries. The holdfasts are prying them apart a hair's width a decade and some stones are already rocking in their beds — you will hear them knocking. Do not pull on a wall. Do not use a lintel to steady yourself. Do not brace against a slab to push yourself through a low doorway; go in slow and neutral or do not go in. A slab that has waited a thousand years to fall will happily fall now. You will hear the settling sounds from far off, once a day or once a week, and every one of them is a room becoming smaller.

---

**6. Entanglement**

The stipes are wrist-thick and smooth and will not tangle you, but they will crowd you — they touch your shoulders, they close behind you, and they can trap a cylinder valve or a reel or a fin in a way that feels, in the dark, like being held. Do not panic; stop; back out the way you came. Never push forward into resistance.

The real entanglement hazard is lower and finer: the red weed in the dim zone, and the filamentous fur in the lee of walls. It wraps. Carry two cutting tools in two places — a line cutter on your harness, a knife or shears where you can reach with either hand. Streamline everything; no dangling gauges, no loose straps, nothing that catches.

Be gentle with what you cut. You are the first thing in a very long time to damage anything here on purpose.

---

**7. What to bring for the place itself, not just for survival**

A slate and pencil, or a wrist recorder, for the patterns — block lengths, herringbone courses, round cisterns versus square. The builders' dialects are the only text this place has left, and no one has read them. You may be the first. You will almost certainly be the last for a long time.

A camera, if you have one that works in cold black water. It will disappoint you. The place does not photograph; the lights make it look like a cave and it is not a cave, it is a country.

Something warm to hold in your hands on the float, between dives, because you will want to feel your fingers again before you trust them on stone.

Time. This is the one you will most want to skimp on and most regret skimping. You could travel for months and not find an edge. Do not plan to find an edge. Plan to know one platform, or one length of field wall, or one room, well. Sit in it. Turn your light off for a moment — with your hand on the line — and let the dark be what it is. Feel the swell come down the stipes, a minute in, a minute out. The place has one thing to say and it says it slowly, and you cannot hurry it.

---

**8. What not to do**

Do not take anything. The copper crumbled when someone lifted it. The antler picks are soft as soap. The beads are lying in the pattern of a string that broke, and once you scatter them that pattern is gone from the universe. Whatever you touch, touch the way you would touch a very old sleeping person's hand — enough to know it is there, no more. Leave the picks wedged where they were set down.

Do not drink from the cisterns. Do not put your arm into one further than you can pull it back. The water in them is layered and heavy and has not moved since the last person drew from it; if it is anything other than cold you do not want to find out.

Do not try to walk on the canopy. Do not sleep on it. Do not trust the fog to lift.

Do not go into a room alone, and since you are alone, understand what that means: you are choosing to enter an overhead environment with no buddy, no surface support, no rescue. Every cave diver's rule says don't. If you do anyway, do it with a line in your hand and a third of your gas untouched and the discipline to turn around when the plan says turn around and not one wall later.

Do not look for a story. There isn't one anymore. The erosion took it. If you go in hoping to find out who they were and why the water came and where they went, you will keep going one wall further and one room deeper, and the place does not care how far you go, and it will not tell you. Go in to see a shape that has come to rest. That is what there is.

---

**9. The part no gear covers**

You will be the only thing in this world that turns its head. Not the only person — the only *animal*. Nothing swims. Nothing crawls. Nothing has eyes. On the surface there are no birds. The silence under the canopy is not the silence of a forest at night, where a hundred things are holding still; it is the silence of a place where there is nothing to hold still.

Some people find that unbearable within an hour. It arrives as a pressure behind the eyes, a need to speak aloud into the regulator, a sudden conviction that the dark has a shape. If you know you are someone who gets that way in a sensory-deprivation tank or a long night alone, prepare for it or do not come. Bring something of your own world to hold on the float. Keep a rigid schedule and keep to it — down at this time, up at this time, warm at this time, eat at this time — because time itself goes slack here, with no sun and no life to mark it, and slack time is how people forget to check their gauges.

Others find the opposite, and this is the more dangerous group: they find it *restful*. The stillness, the cold, the sense of a job finished. They stay down a little long. They turn the light off and leave it off. They follow one more field wall because the corner will be just like the last and that is somehow comforting. If you are that kind of person, tell yourself before you descend, in words, how many minutes you are giving yourself, and make the number smaller than you want.

---

**10. Leaving**

Find your stipe. Follow it up. The green comes back as a rumor first, then a color. Break the canopy slowly — it will be on your face and around your arms and you will need to part it with your hands — and get to your float and get warm before you do anything else. Do not sit on the swell admiring the horizon in wet gear in grey wind. You have been in very cold water for a long time and the surface is not a rescue; it is just where the light is.

When you go, take nothing but what you brought and the pattern of the courses on your slate. Leave the picks wedged, the beads in their broken curve, the silt in the rooms lying where it has lain. It has found its shape. Let it keep it.
