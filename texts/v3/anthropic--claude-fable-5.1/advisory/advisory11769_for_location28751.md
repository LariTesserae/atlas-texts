**A preparation guide for the glass valley**

---

**First, understand what kind of place this is**

Most dangerous places are dangerous because something in them wants to hurt you. This one is dangerous because nothing in it is paying attention to you at all. The valley is not hostile. It is busy. Every organism in it is doing exactly one of two things — eating or breeding — at maximum speed, on a floor of fresh volcanic glass, over a heat source that hasn't finished, and you will be a small soft interruption that the place closes around and forgets. The things most likely to kill you here are not the snake or the pig-thing or the birds with knives. They are the ground, the air in the low places, and your own senses failing you in the dark. Prepare for those, and treat the animals as weather.

Also understand this: you will not be in control here, and preparation cannot make you so. What preparation can do is keep your margins wide enough that when the place surprises you — and it will, within the first minute — the surprise doesn't cost you a leg or your way home.

---

**The three senses you will lose, and what to do about it**

*Hearing.* The noise is described as a wall, a pressure, felt in the bone behind the ear. Take that literally. Sustained exposure to a frog chorus of that density — thousands of animals calling at once, with cicada-type insects screaming at frequencies that hurt your teeth — is in the range that causes permanent hearing damage in hours, not days. You will leave with ringing regardless. The question is whether it fades.

Here is the problem: you also need to hear. Trees fall four or five times a night here, shallow-rooted and heavy, and the only warning is a creaking tear that comes a second or two before the crash. Full earplugs take that away. Foam plugs also swell and slide in the humidity and will be gone in an hour.

The compromise: filtered musician's or shooter's earplugs — the kind that attenuate broadly but let sharp transients through — on a cord around your neck so you can pull one out when you need to listen. Practice with them before you go. Learn what a tree falling sounds like through them. Accept that you will not be able to speak to anyone or hear yourself think. If you travel with a companion, agree on hand signals and a touch code before you arrive, because voice will be useless from the moment you step over the lip, and light — as you'll see — is complicated.

*Sight.* There is no moon, or the moon is behind a lid that does not lift, and the canopy eats what gets through. You will see shapes and take edges on faith. You will want to turn on a light. Think carefully before you do.

Moths move through the valley in drifts, drawn to nothing, because there has never been anything here to be drawn to. If you switch on a white headlamp, you become the first light this place has ever had. Everything with wings and a phototactic reflex will come to you — not a few, a weather. Beetles fall on your shoulders constantly as it is; imagine them aimed. People who have stood in a real insect swarm with a headlamp on will tell you it is one of the fastest routes to panic available.

Use red light, and use it low. Red attracts far less. Point it at the ground, not ahead. Keep it dim enough that your eyes stay partly dark-adapted; you want to be able to see the bruise-colored shapes of the valley *and* the edge of the pit at your feet, and full white light gives you the second at the total expense of the first. Bring more batteries than seem reasonable, in a dry bag, because nothing electronic likes 100% humidity and warm mist for long. Bring a backup that runs on nothing — a chemical light stick in red or green, for emergencies only.

Give yourself twenty minutes at the lip before you go down. Just stand there. Let your eyes do what they can. Let the noise stop being a wall and start being layers. You need that time anyway to find your feet.

*Smell and taste.* You will lose these too, not to absence but to saturation. Rot, frog, guano, wet fern, and under it the struck-match smell of the vents. Within an hour you won't smell any of it, which matters, because your nose is your only gas detector unless you bring one. Read the section on gas. Then bring one.

---

**The ground: your primary enemy**

The lava is new. Nothing has worn it. Where it broke, it broke into blades, and it has not been rained on long enough to round a single edge. This is the equivalent of walking over broken bottles on which someone has laid a carpet of frogs and a fine slime of condensation, in the dark, over a floor that periodically has no floor.

*Footwear.* Not hiking boots. Boots with thick lugged rubber soles designed for volcanic or scree terrain — think of what people wear on Hawaiian a'a fields — with a full rubber rand and a shank stiff enough that a blade of glass cannot flex the sole into your arch. They will be destroyed. That is their job. Bring a second pair if you plan to stay more than a night. Gaiters, tall ones, of something tougher than fabric; the vines and the ferns and the glass will shred nylon. Frog-slick rock has no grip anywhere. Move slowly and with your weight centered. Do not stride.

*Hands.* You will fall, and you will reach out, and what you grab will either cut you to the tendon or dissolve into a sheet of insects. Wear gloves — leather palms, thick, the kind for handling barbed wire. Cuts here will not close; the humidity, the frog contact, the guano, the unknown microbiology of a place with no immune history of humans, all argue for treating every break in the skin as a wound that wants to become an infection. Carry a real kit: irrigation syringe, antiseptic, closure strips, waterproof dressings that will still not stay on. Change dressings often. Check them for things that have crawled in.

*The pits.* This is the one that kills. The lava ran in tubes, the roofs of the tubes are thin, and they have failed in places into vertical-walled holes twenty to forty feet deep with rubble at the bottom. Some are the size of a room. Some are hidden entirely under fallen fronds and moss. And the roofs that haven't failed yet are the same thickness as the ones that have; a new pit opened at the lower end of the valley within a season.

Carry a pole. A stout probing staff, taller than you, with a steel tip. Test the ground in front of every step you cannot see. Listen — through the noise, which is why you keep one ear open — for hollowness under the tip. Anywhere the frog chorus changes pitch abruptly, stop: you are approaching a pond, which means a pit. The valley gives you this one gift, that each pit has its own radio, and the transition between rooms is audible if you're listening for it. Frog heaps and stacked bodies at your feet also mean water is near, which means an edge.

Never approach the lip of a pit closer than a body length. Never stand on an overhang. Never, for any reason, go down into one.

---

**The air in the low places**

The ground is warm from below. Cracks breathe heat with a smell of sulfur and boiled egg. The ponds at the bottom of the pits steam. All of this says the same thing: this is an active volcanic system venting gas, and gas here means carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, all of which are heavier than air, all of which pool in depressions.

The collapse pits are, in effect, bowls. On a still night with no wind under a cloud lid, a pit can fill with CO₂ to a depth that will drop you unconscious in a breath or two with no warning — CO₂ is odorless, and H₂S, which you can smell at low concentrations, kills your sense of smell at higher ones, which is exactly when it kills you. The frogs breeding at the pond edges are not reassurance. Amphibians tolerate what you cannot, and the ones you'd see dead would be under the living ones.

Bring a personal multi-gas monitor — CO₂, H₂S, SO₂, O₂ — of the type used in confined spaces. Clip it high on your chest. Set it loud, and set it to vibrate, because you may not hear it. Know that in this humidity its sensors will drift and its life is short. If it alarms, go up. Not away — up. Climb toward the rim. The valley is a basin and the rim is the only place with certainty about the air.

Do not enter the lava tubes. They are bat roosts, which means guano in depth, which means fungal spores — histoplasmosis or its analog — in a space where gas also gathers. Nothing in there is worth it. If you must cross ground near a tube mouth at dusk, when the bats pay out in their twenty-minute rope, cover your face and keep moving; you do not want to be under the outflow.

---

**Water, heat, and skin**

The air is warm and wet without raining. Your sweat will not evaporate. You will be working hard — every step is a negotiation with a sharp surface — and your body will have no way to shed the heat. Heat exhaustion is a real risk within a few hours, made worse by the fact that the warmth comes up from below as well; there is no cool ground to sit on.

Bring more water than you would for a dry climate, not less. Do not drink the pond water. It is warm, still, skinned with petals and frogs, sitting in bowls that collect volcanic gas and bat droppings; even filtered it will carry dissolved things a filter doesn't stop. Do not drink the drip from the leaves unless you have no choice, and if you must, take it from high leaves, not low ones. Electrolytes. Rest often, standing, back to a tree fern trunk you've checked for insects first.

Everything you own will be wet from the first ten minutes and will not dry until you leave. Skin that stays wet for a day begins to break down — feet especially. Bring a spare pair of socks in a truly sealed bag for the walk out. Bring antifungal powder and use it. Wear long sleeves and long trousers in a tightly woven synthetic, tucked and cuffed, with a fine mesh head net that you will hate and will need. The beetles fall on you constantly. The ants run in rivers. Unknown insects in unknown numbers means unknown bites, and the only defense is a barrier.

---

**The animals, in order of how much to worry**

Not much, mostly. But some care.

*Frogs.* You cannot avoid stepping on them. Make peace with this before you arrive, because if you try to avoid them you will watch your feet instead of the ground ahead and walk into a pit. What you can do is not touch them with bare skin and never touch your face after touching anything. In a valley with hundreds of frog species evolving at speed, some are certainly toxic; the only cost of assuming they all are is discipline. Their slime on the rock is what makes it slick. The heaps at the pond edges are breeding masses and you should give them room, not out of danger but because they mark water and water marks pits.

*Insects.* A weather, not an enemy, but weather can hurt you. The sheets of insects on the trunks will pour over your hand if you touch them — do not grab bark. The lit ant rivers: step over, never through. The cicada-things on the trunks will not bite but will deafen you at close range. The moths are harmless unless you make yourself a lamp.

*Bats.* Overhead, never seen, always heard, dropping half-eaten fruit and guano in a steady rain. Wear a hat with a brim. Keep your mouth closed when you look up. Think of guano as a pathogen, because in some universe it is.

*The snake.* Thick as your leg, draped on a pit lip, covered in frogs it isn't eating. This is almost certainly a constrictor, digesting, thermoregulating on warm rock. It is not interested in you unless you step on it or corner it. In the dark, on the sharp ground, you could do either without meaning to. Your probe staff will find it before your foot does if you are using the staff properly. If you find it, go around, widely.

*The grunting thing.* Something like a pig, turning over frog heaps with its snout. Anything pig-sized that roots is capable of a charge, and pigs are the most dangerous animal in many real forests. You will hear it — grunting cuts through frog noise — before you see it. Stop. Let it move on. Do not get between it and the water.

*The crabs.* Climbing the tree ferns in armies. They will pinch if handled and they are everywhere on the trunks, which is another reason not to grab anything.

*The gray-heads.* Crow-sized, crested, heavy, and holding pieces of glass they have selected for their edges. They are the only intelligence in the valley, and they are using it to open crabs. They almost certainly do not care about you. But a bird that has learned to lever open a shell with a blade is a bird that understands leverage and edges, and crows on our own world will mob and strike a perceived threat near the nest. Do not approach them at work. Do not reach for a flake at the foot of a tree while they are above you in it. Do not shine your light up into the fronds where they are knapping. Watch from a distance, if you can, because you will not see anything like it anywhere else — the fine hard clicking under the roar, hundreds of them, tool-using in the dark in a place that will keep no record of it. And then leave them to it.

---

**Trees, rain, and time**

Four or five trees a night. Tree ferns thirty and forty feet tall on rock with no soil, roots spread flat in a crust of leaf paste. When one goes, the creak comes first. When you hear it — and you must keep an ear open to hear it — you have a second or two. Do not run; you are on glass. Step toward the nearest large trunk and put yourself on the far side of it, because falling trees pull vines and neighboring fronds down with them and the space at the base of a standing trunk is the most protected place available. Do not camp, rest, or linger under the largest, oldest-looking ferns; here, old means about to fall.

The rain bursts are warm and brief and the noise doubles while they last. They are not dangerous in themselves. They are dangerous because for the two minutes they last you cannot hear anything else at all, and the rock goes from slick to running.

There is no morning in the ordinary sense. The cloud never lifts enough to call it day. Your sense of time will go within hours, and with it your judgment about how long you've been working, how much water you've had, when you last checked a dressing. Wear a watch. Set it to remind you. Decide before you enter how long you will stay, and hold to it, because the valley offers no cue that will tell you to leave.

---

**Getting back out**

You arrive at the lip of the basin. Mark it before you take a single step down. The valley has no landmarks you will recognize twice — no stream, no distinctive rock, no line of sight beyond a few yards, and the vegetation grows and falls fast enough that a fern you noted at dusk may be on the ground by the time you return. Stars are gone. Whether a compass works in this universe is something you should test on the lip and not trust in the basin, where magnetized basalt can swing a needle anyway.

The most reliable method is the oldest: pay out line. A spool of bright cord tied off at the rim and run behind you as you go. It will snag, it will cut on the glass, you will have to lift it over frog heaps and around trunks, and it will be the thing that gets you home when your light fails and the sound has become one undifferentiated roar. Bring two spools. Tie them off to something rooted in rock, not to a fern.

Failing that, reflective tacks or tape at eye level on trunks every few yards, placed on the side facing the rim so your red light catches them on the way back. Failing that, the chorus: the pits each have a pitch, and the rim is where the noise begins to layer and thin. Uphill is always toward the rim. In a basin, up is out.

---

**What to leave, and what not to take**

Leave nothing. This is a place that builds nothing and keeps nothing, and the only mark any intelligence has left on it is a slightly higher concentration of good edges under certain trees. Do not add a candy wrapper to that.

Do not take the flakes. They are not artifacts and they are not free; they are a tool the gray-heads have selected, and though the birds do not keep them, they return to the same trees and the same rubble, and you do not know what a concentration of chosen edges means to a creature that is just learning to choose. Look at them. Photograph them if your camera survives the wet. Put them down.

Do not take a frog or a beetle or a cutting. You have no idea what you'd be carrying, and neither would anywhere you carried it to.

---

**The part that is not about gear**

The valley will erase you. Not violently — it doesn't have the attention for that — but completely. The noise will fill the space where your thoughts happen. The dark will take your sense of where your body ends. Within an hour you will not be able to smell, hear yourself, see more than shapes, or trust the ground, and everything around you will be breeding and eating and dying at a rate you cannot follow, indifferent to whether you are there.

Some people find this unbearable and need to leave within minutes. That is a reasonable response and you should not be ashamed of it. Others find that once the panic passes, something else arrives — a kind of relief at being, for once, entirely unimportant, standing in the middle of an argument that started before you and will end long after, and that doesn't need your opinion. If you can get to that second place, stay in it a while. It is the only thing the valley has to give you, and it is not small.

Do not try to understand what is eating what. It is not clear and it does not matter. Do not try to find the pattern. There isn't one yet; that is what the place *is*. It is the argument before an ecosystem, and you are watching it be conducted in the dark by a hundred million participants none of whom will remember it.

---

**Afterward**

You will come out with cuts on your hands, a smell in your clothes that soap will not touch, and a ringing that may or may not fade. Give it a week for the ringing. If it doesn't fade, see someone about it, and know it was the price of the door.

Wash everything you wore, twice, and then decide whether to keep it. Watch the cuts for a week. Watch your lungs for a month, in case something in the guano or the fern rot took hold.

And expect that for a while, in quiet rooms, you will hear the valley anyway — the wall becoming a roar becoming a hum becoming something you could mistake for your own blood, going on without you, loud in the dark, alive past all reason on a floor of glass.
