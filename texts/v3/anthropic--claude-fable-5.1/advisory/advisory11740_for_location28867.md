**Preparing for The Table**

Begin with what this place is not. It is not hostile. Nothing on the table will chase you, bite you, trap you, or want anything from you. The weather will not turn. The ground will not give. You will not fall. You can go up entirely unprepared and come down unharmed, and most people who go up do.

The ones who do not come down are also unharmed.

That is the shape of the hazard, and every part of your preparation should be built around it. The table is a place where things stop, and it stops them gently, and it does not notice whether you are one of the things. Your job is to notice. Everything below is in service of that.

---

**The decision you make at the bottom**

Before you begin the climb — before, not on the ledge, not on the table — decide how long you will stay, and decide it in a unit the table cannot touch.

Do not decide in hours of daylight. The sun does not move up there; you will get no dusk to tell you it is time. Do not decide in terms of what you want to see — "until I've reached the horn-cairn," "until I've found the figure" — because the distances up there do not agree with one another and a goal set in space is a goal you can walk toward forever. Decide in something you carry: a number of ticks on a mechanical watch, a number of steps counted on a tally cord, a number of your own breaths — though be careful with that last one, for reasons that will become clear.

Then tell someone at the bottom what the number is. Say it aloud. If you are going with a companion, agree on it together and agree that either of you can invoke it and the other will turn without argument. The table does not argue. You will find that you don't want to either, and that is exactly when you must.

Write the number on the back of your hand. Grey on skin. Look at it when you feel restful.

---

**The body**

The approach is high, dry, and cold in a particular way. Prepare for altitude as you would for any serious mountain: acclimatize somewhere else beforehand, because there is nowhere on this mountain to do it — the slopes are as thin-aired as the top, and the climb "takes longer than it should," which means you will be at altitude, working, for longer than your plan says. Assume half again. Assume double and be pleasantly surprised.

There is no water on the table. There is no snow, there has never been snow, and the white on the ledges is salt. Nothing up there will wet your mouth. Carry every drop you will drink, for the climb, the stay, and the descent, and then carry more, because the dry cold pulls moisture out of you without your noticing and because the descent, like the climb, will take longer than it should.

The cold is a cellar cold — no wind to speak of, no wet, no edge. This is easier to dress for than a true winter and easier to underestimate. Layers, a good hat, gloves you can work in. You will not be sweating much and you will not be moving fast. You will, if you are not careful, be standing still for long periods, and standing still in a cellar for hours will go into your bones by a route you don't feel until it's there.

Food: bring it, and bring it in a form you can eat without stopping, without sitting, without setting anything down. This matters more than it sounds like it should. Come back to it.

Boots: the scree does not shift. Every stone has settled where it will always be. This makes the footing paradoxically safe and paradoxically tiring — there is no give, no roll, nothing to absorb the step. Your knees will know. The table itself is matte grey stone worn smooth; it is not slick, but it is not kind either.

Do not bring anything that requires you to be still to use it. No tripod. No sketchbook you'd want to sit down with. No book. If you bring a camera, keep it in your hand or around your neck; do not set it on a stone to frame a shot. You will want to. The light is even and unmoving and every composition is available forever. Resist. If you want to know what a photograph of the table shows, ask someone who has taken one — reports differ, and the differing is the most interesting thing about them.

---

**The pulse**

Your heartbeat is the only thing on the table that is not in equilibrium. The account you have read calls it rude. It is. It is also the thing that makes you a visitor rather than a resident, and it is the thing you must protect.

Everything up there invites you to slow. The breath-sum lies over the whole surface like weather and your lungs will want to find its rhythm, except it has no rhythm — it has a level — and the only way to match a level is to slow toward it indefinitely. The animals breathe once in a hundred counts. The figure breathes once in a hundred counts. There is no reason to think the process that brought them to that rate was anything other than comfortable.

So: keep your pulse rude.

Move. Not frantically — you'll exhaust yourself in the thin air — but continuously. Do not sit. Do not lean against anything. Do not lie down, ever, for any reason; the stone circles are the sleeping places of people who did not get up, and even if you avoid the circles the whole table is a floor.

Talk. Aloud, to yourself if alone, to your companion if not. Say what you see. Say the number on your hand. Sing, if you can stand your own voice; the acoustics are strange — sound seems to arrive at its destination without traveling — and hearing yourself will be uncomfortable, and uncomfortable is what you want.

Eat on a schedule set by your watch, not by hunger. You may not get hungry. Time does not seem to run there in the way that makes bodies hungry, and the absence of appetite is one of the first things people report and one of the first signs. Eat anyway, walking, from your hand.

Check your breath. Count between one inhale and the next. If you reach ten, walk faster. If you reach twenty, turn around and start back, whatever your number says. If you are with a companion, count each other's. Take turns. Do not let a silence go longer than a minute without one of you saying something into it.

None of this is dramatic. That is the point. The slowing is not dramatic either.

---

**The climb**

Ordinary, as the account says, and then less so. Scree that does not move. Rock that has stopped being any particular kind of rock. Nothing growing, nothing fallen, nothing recent. You may find this eerie on the way up; you will find it merely accurate on the way down.

The salt crusts on the upper ledges will look like frost. Do not touch them. Not because they are dangerous but because of what happens when you do — they crackle under your hand and then are found not to have crackled, and this is your first small lesson in the table's way of being, and it is better to take the lesson from a distance. It's also a habit worth forming early: on this mountain, do not touch what you do not need to touch. Most things up there are finished. Contact suggests otherwise.

Pace yourself for a climb half again as long as it looks. You will not be able to judge your progress by the summit's approach; the grey line stays where it is until suddenly it is underfoot.

---

**Arriving**

The last ledge, and then the level.

Take a moment here, on the edge, before you step in. Look back at the way you came. Fix it. Not by landmarks on the table — there will be none that stay put — but by the ledge itself: its shape, the pattern of salt on it, the specific stones. This is the one place on the mountain that you know is real in the ordinary way, and you will want to be able to recognize it. Some travelers leave a marker here. There is a question, discussed further on, about whether anything set down on the table can be picked up again — but the ledge is not the table, quite, and a bright cloth tied to your own ice axe driven into a crack seems to remain yours.

Then step in.

You will see boulders first. Your mind will offer you boulders because that is what belongs on a high flat waste, and for a few seconds you will have the last ordinary view you'll have up there. Then one of them will be a deer, and then all of them will be what they are.

The first hour is the hard hour. The account is honest about this. The eyes are already on you when you turn, and you will turn a great deal in the first hour, and the eye will be there each time, wet and black and utterly without expectation. People describe the feeling in various ways — being looked through, being looked at by a wall, being the only thing in a room full of portraits. It passes. What replaces it is worse, because what replaces it is rest.

When you feel the attention become restful, look at your hand.

---

**Moving among them**

You will have to pass through. There is no path and they stand flank to flank.

Do not try to squeeze. Do not turn sideways or lift your pack or look for the wider gap. Simply walk toward the space between two animals as though it were wide enough, and you will be on the far side of it, and nothing will have happened. There is no sensation. Some people find this the most upsetting thing on the table and some find it the easiest; there is no predicting which you'll be. What is certain is that hesitating in front of the gap does not help — the gap does not widen for the patient — and that trying to work out what has occurred does not help either. Three explanations are available and none is preferred. Choose one to tell yourself and stop thinking about it.

Do not touch the animals. You may not be able to; you may pass through the hand you reach out with. But if you can, don't. The lichen is grown through their hair and their hair through the lichen, and the salt is on their spines, and they are woven into the place in a way that has taken longer than anything you can imagine and is done. You are not woven. Keep it that way.

Do not try to feed them, speak to them, or wake them. They are awake.

You will begin to recognize individuals. A stag with an antler broken at the second tine. A lynx with a hooked scar. You will see the stag again, in another place, in other company, and it will be the same stag, and you will want to understand this. You will be tempted to walk from one stag to the next and count them, or to try to find the edge of the stag's distribution, or to stand between two of him and look from one to the other for a long time.

Do not do this. It is the table's version of a maze, and it is not malicious, and it has no exit, because there is nothing to find. Whether one stag stands in many places or many places stand around one stag is a question the table does not answer and the stag does not care about, and a traveler who cares about it has begun to stop. Note the stag. Say aloud, "There is the stag again." Keep walking.

---

**The marks**

You will find the things the people left. Stone circles, knee-high. A knapping floor with the flakes still sharp. A core set down beside an awl and a scraper, in order, mid-task. A cairn of horns. A hide staked out to cure in a wind that does not blow.

These are the most important things on the table for your purposes, because they show you exactly what stopping looks like from the outside.

It looks like tidiness. It looks like someone who was in the middle of something and set the tools down carefully, edge to edge, in the order they would need them next, and stood up, and did not sit back down. Not violence, not collapse, not a struggle. A pause that became a position. The tools are still there because the person who set them down is still there — perhaps a mile off, perhaps at a distance you cannot close, wrapped in skins, with salt on the shoulders and a rosette of lichen on the back of one hand.

So the rule about the marks is simple and total: do not add to them.

Do not set anything down. Not to rest your arms, not to take a photograph, not for a moment. Whatever you carry up, carry the whole time. There is a real possibility — reported often enough to take seriously — that anything set carefully on the table stays set, that it has been placed rather than dropped, that it is now one of the marks. Some travelers have tested this with a stone and found the stone would not come up. Some have found it came up fine. Enough have found it wouldn't that you should not need to know which kind of day you are having.

If something falls — a glove, a lens cap — it fell. That is different. Falling is not placing. Pick it up quickly and without ceremony. But be honest with yourself about the difference. You will know.

Do not take anything either. The flakes on the knapping floor are sharp and beautiful and struck yesterday and there for eternity, and they belong to the person who struck them, and that person is not gone. Leave the tools in their order.

Do not step inside a stone circle. Do not sit on one. You may pass through them as you pass through the animals, and probably will, without meaning to; that is fine. But do not enter one deliberately and do not linger within. They are sleeping places, and the one with the small deer standing at its exact centre should tell you everything about what sleeping here means.

The horn-cairn is the one thing on the table that has a direction, and it will offer itself as a landmark. It is not one. It faces the way the shadows fall from every side, which means it faces you, always, which means it tells you nothing about where you are. Look at it, since you will want to. Then look at your hand.

---

**The figure**

You may see it. Not everyone does. It stands among the animals as they stand, at a distance that may be an hour or several days, and every approach delivers you to the same distance.

Do not approach.

Not because it is dangerous. Because it cannot be reached, and every step toward it is a step you will have to take back, and because the walking-toward is itself a kind of stopping — a purpose that the table has arranged, out of what the account calls courtesy, to be permanent. You will walk and walk and the figure will remain exactly there, slightly closer than the horizon, breathing once in a hundred counts, and you will lose the count, and begin again, and there is your number on your hand going unread.

Look at it. Let it look at you — it already is. Understand that it is a traveler, that it came up, that it set its tools down in order. Understand that it is not suffering and is not dead and is not, in any sense the table would recognize, a warning. It is simply what a person is when a person has been here long enough.

Then say something aloud, and walk in another direction.

---

**Leaving**

The number arrives. It arrives on your watch or your cord or your hand, and it arrives in a place where nothing arrives, and you will feel it as an intrusion — a rudeness, a pulse — and that is correct. Honor it.

You will not want to go. Not desperately; the table does not produce desperation. You will simply find that the reasons to leave have gone quiet, and that the reasons to stay have not so much multiplied as *settled*, the way water finds level. The valley below will seem like a place where things happen, which is to say a place of noise and change and unfinished tasks, and up here nothing is unfinished, and the animals are so patient, and the light is so even. You will think: a little longer. You will think it in a calm voice.

The calm voice is the table's. Yours is the one that says the number.

Turn around.

Finding the way back is not a matter of navigation, because navigation does not work here — the shadows fall the same way relative to you wherever you go, the cairn faces you from every side, the same stag stands at every distance. Do not try to reverse your route; you do not have one. What seems to work, for the people who have come down, is intention held steadily. You walk toward the ledge you fixed in your mind when you arrived, the specific stones and the specific salt, and you keep walking, and you keep talking, and you do not stop to look at anything, and at some point the table's edge is in front of you. The distances do not agree with one another, which cuts both ways: the ledge may be a day off and also just there.

If you have a companion, hold hands for this part. Not for comfort, though it is comforting. So that if one of you slows, the other feels it.

You may find your own way back is longer than the way in. You may find it is shorter. You may find the ledge exactly where you left it with the bright cloth still tied to the axe, and this will be the most reassuring thing you have ever seen.

Step off the table onto the ledge before you stop moving. Then you can stop, for a moment, and look back at the grey rank of them receding to the horizon, and hear the breath-sum for the last time. Then start down.

The descent takes longer than it should. You knew that. You brought the water.

---

**After**

The table will not notice you were there. You will.

For some time afterward — days, sometimes weeks — the world below will seem loud and provisional. Things will move that do not need to move. People will change position for no reason. You will find yourself standing very still in rooms, and you will find that you can hold a single breath for longer than you used to, and you will find this pleasant. Be careful with the pleasant.

You will also, likely, want to go back. Not urgently. The way one wants to return to a room where one slept well. The table is restful in a way nothing else is, because everything else is still becoming something and the table has already become it. There is a real gift in having stood in a finished place. Most travelers who go once and come down are glad they went, and they mean it.

But note what the account says at the end, because it is the whole of the danger stated plainly: *one can stand among them for as long as one likes.* That is not a permission. It is a description of what "as long as one likes" turns into when liking has no end. The people who built the circles liked it. The figure with the spear liked it. The deer at the centre of the ring, for all anyone can tell, liked it.

If you go back, go with a smaller number. If you go back a third time, do not.

---

**In short**

Decide your limit at the bottom and write it on your hand. Acclimatize elsewhere; carry all your water; dress for a cellar. Bring nothing you would want to set down, and set nothing down. Pass through the gaps without trying. Do not touch the salt, the animals, the lichen, the tools, the horns. Do not enter the circles. Do not chase the stag or approach the figure. Move continuously, speak aloud, eat by the watch, count your breaths and your companion's, and when the count between breaths reaches twenty, leave regardless of the number. When the number comes, leave regardless of everything else. Walk toward the ledge you fixed on arrival and keep walking until it is under you.

Nothing there will stop you from leaving. Nothing there will stop you from staying. The table does not hold anyone. It only waits, without waiting, and lets the level find you.

Be rude. Keep your pulse. Come down.
