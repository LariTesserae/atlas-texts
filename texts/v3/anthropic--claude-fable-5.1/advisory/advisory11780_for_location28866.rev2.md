# Preparing for the Oven at Tsen Hollow

Start by understanding what this place actually asks of a body, because the description makes it sound contemplative and it is — but it is also a high, dry, hot hole in the ground with no water for a very long way in any direction, and it is those three plain facts that will decide whether you come out of it with a story or don't come out.

## The shape of the danger

Most dangerous places announce themselves. The Oven doesn't. Nothing in it is hostile to you as far as anyone knows. The grazers lift off your hand without complaint. The hunters have never been seen to take an interest in anything larger than a grazer. The room's only recorded violence is the small, quiet kind that happens in every ecosystem, and it happens to things the size of your palm.

What will hurt you is subtraction. The plateau subtracts oxygen. The room subtracts water. Between them they subtract your judgment, slowly, in a place with no light and no clock and a floor that makes it feel entirely reasonable to sit for another hour. The traveler who fails here doesn't get eaten. They get thirsty and vague and stand up too fast in the dark, or they walk back out onto a cold plateau soaked in sweat with the wrong bearing.

Prepare for the subtraction. Everything else is texture.

## Altitude

The air is "thin enough to notice." That phrasing is polite. Bare welded rhyolite with no plants and a sky that hard means you are probably somewhere between 3,500 and 4,500 meters — high enough that an unacclimatized sea-level body will feel it within hours and may feel it badly by the next morning.

If you can choose your arrival point, arrive lower and walk up over two or three days. If you can't — and multiverse travel often doesn't offer the choice — then arrive, go no farther than the entrance the first day, and sleep. Learn the headache, the nausea, the way your breath doesn't quite land. Those are the ordinary complaints of altitude and they pass. What doesn't pass — a wet cough, confusion, stumbling, a headache that painkillers don't touch — means you go down, or you leave. There is no going down on the plateau. There is only leaving.

Acetazolamide if you have access to it and know how to use it. Ibuprofen for the headache. Nothing heroic.

Note also that the room itself is a closed chamber at the low end of a sloping passage, warmed from below by volcanic breath. Its air is not the plateau's air. It is thick, mineral, faintly sulphurous, and it is entirely possible that carbon dioxide pools there, or that the sulphur compounds run higher than your nose will report once it has adjusted. The creatures live in it, but they are built for it. You are not. A small electronic gas monitor with an audible alarm is the single most useful piece of equipment you could carry into the Oven, and if you can't get one, then treat every headache that begins *inside* the room as an instruction to stand up and walk toward the cooler passage until it eases.

## Water

There is none. Not on the shelf, not in the seam, not in the room. Whatever you drink, you carried up.

The room is dry heat — sauna heat without the steam — and dry heat is the kind that lies to you. Your sweat evaporates the instant it forms. You never feel wet. You feel, if anything, fine, and then you feel a bit light, and then you stand up and the room tilts. Assume you will lose something close to a liter an hour in there, more during the hot pulses. Assume you will lose a great deal more than usual on the plateau too, because the wind is cold and dry and at altitude you breathe hard and every breath leaves wet.

Carry more than you think you need, then add half again. Carry it in sealed containers with good lids, because a spill in the Oven doesn't just cost you the water — it puts water into a system that has never had any, and you don't know what that does. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst. Thirst is a late signal at altitude and a nearly useless one in dry heat. Bring electrolytes; sweat is not just water, and a body that's had three liters of plain water and no salt can be in worse trouble than one that's had two.

Lip balm. A small saline spray for your nose, because the membranes will crack and bleed in that air and a nosebleed in total darkness is an unpleasant surprise. Eye drops if you have them, and use them sparingly — your eyes will ache from the dryness long before they ache from the dark.

## Heat and the transition

Bread-oven heat, kiln heat: people have sat in the room for hours and left grooves in its ceiling, so it is survivable, but it is well above anything your body considers comfortable — think of the hottest sauna you've been in, minus the humidity that makes a sauna feel so hot, and then remove the door.

Treat it the way you'd treat any extreme heat. Limit your first exposure to half an hour. Come back out into the seam, where the temperature drops with every bend, and see how you feel. Go back in for longer only if you feel entirely well. Know the signs of heat exhaustion — dizziness, nausea, a headache, a heart that's racing while you're sitting still — and know that in the dark you will have to feel them rather than see them in a companion's face.

The floor is hotter than the air. It is also alive, but the heat is the first thing. Wear boots with thick soles, and don't sit directly on the floor; the flat cool stone by the entrance — the seat, the one the previous visitors set — is there for a reason, and it's the right place for you too.

Then the exit. This is where people get hurt in places like this. You come up the seam warm and damp and loose-limbed and step out into a wind that has never carried rain, at altitude, at night, with the temperature perhaps sixty or seventy degrees lower than the room you just left. Your body will have opened every vessel it has to shed heat and it will now dump that heat into the wind. Have a heavy layer waiting at the entrance — a down jacket, a wool blanket, something you can put on before you've walked ten steps. Have a dry base layer to change into if you were sweating. Have a windproof shell. The cold on that shelf will feel clean and wonderful for about two minutes, and then it will simply be cold.

## Darkness

The room is dark in a way most modern humans have never experienced. Not dim. Dark. The kind of dark where your eyes eventually invent things, and then, after an hour, begin to find the things that are actually there.

Bring a light. Bring a *dim red* light, the sort used on ships and in observatories, and use it for the passage in — for the two bends, for the place where the floor becomes floor-that-moves, for finding the seat. Then turn it off. Nothing in the room can see, so your light does no harm to them. But it does harm to you: a bright white beam sweeping across that ceiling will show you the whole of it at once, every layered body, every shell, and you will not be able to unsee it, and you will never have the slow arrival of the pinprick lights and the redness in the far passage that is the reason to come. Decide beforehand which visit you want. There isn't a wrong answer. But there is a choice, and it should be a choice rather than a reflex.

Practice sitting in darkness before you go. A closet, a windowless room, an hour with your eyes open. Notice what your mind does. The Oven is that, plus the whisper, plus the knowledge of what the whisper is. Some people find it the most peaceful hour of their lives. Some find that the moment they understand the ceiling is *populated*, their chest closes and they need to leave, and that's a fine and sane response and you should honor it if it comes. The seam is thirty paces. You can be under sky in a minute.

## Time

There is no day in the room. There is the hot phase and the cool phase, an hour or two each, and there is a floor that seems to want you to stay.

Carry a watch you can read without light — one that speaks, or vibrates, or has raised hands you can feel. Set alarms: one for water, one for the halfway point of your allotted time, one for leaving. Obey the last one absolutely. You will not want to. The room dilates time the way all dark places do, and the whisper is soothing in a way you won't expect, and you will think "just until the next hot phase." Leave when the alarm says.

## The passages

There are two. The one you came in by, which bends twice and rises toward sky. And the far one, which is narrower, leads down, carries a redness from somewhere below and, in the cool phase, a single dry click now and then.

Keep track of which is which. In total darkness in a round room, after two hours, this is less obvious than it sounds. The simplest method: keep your seat by the entrance, and never move so far from it that you couldn't find it by walking back along the wall. The entrance passage is the cooler one. The far passage is the warmer, and the redness lives in it. If you feel warmth ahead of you and cool behind, you're pointed the wrong way.

Don't go down the far passage. The account says no one has gone and returned to describe it, and there is nothing in that sentence that improves on inspection. It is not a mystery you are equipped to solve. It is a hole that gets hotter.

## The living surface

You will step on them. There's no way not to, though they make way for you as they make way for everything, and the floor will move under your boots with a kind of soft accommodation that is more unsettling than solid ground would be. Walk slowly. Place each foot. Not for their sake — they seem sturdy — but for yours, because a floor that shifts is a floor you can fall on, and a fall in the dark on hot rock among a million small armored things is a bad way to spend your visit.

Gloves are a genuine decision. The film that coats the rock comes off on skin as a dark velvet that will not wash — there is nothing to wash with — and that carries, or seems to carry, a warmth of its own for a day or two after. No one knows what it is. It may be perfectly inert. It may not. A thin leather glove lets you feel the tide of grazers under your palm and keeps the film off your skin. A bare hand gives you the whole experience and gives the film a ride home. If you go bare, bring wipes and use them thoroughly before you cross out of this world, and understand that thoroughly is not a guarantee.

Don't collect. Don't take a grazer, a hunter, a scraping of film. Not because they are rare — they are not — but because the room has a single rule, evidenced by everything the previous visitors did and didn't leave, and the rule is that nothing comes out that didn't go in. Honor that in both directions: bring nothing in you don't intend to carry out. No food. Not a crumb. Organic matter in the Oven is eaten, and you don't know what happens when the decomposers meet something from another world.

## The larger thing

Once or twice, you may feel the floor near your feet shift as if something the size of a dog were moving through the living layer without disturbing it. You will want to move. Don't. It makes way for everything and everything makes way for it; be everything. Sit still, breathe, let it pass. It has never been described as doing anything but passing.

## Company

Go with someone if you can. The seam is two abreast. One of you can sit in the cooler part of the passage while the other is in the room, and swap. A voice from the passage — a single word, every so often — does something for the mind that no amount of preparation does alone, and it means someone is awake and cool and thinking when the person in the room is neither.

If you go alone, tell someone where you've gone and when you intend to come back, and mean the second part.

## The plateau

Coming out is not the end. The shelf runs featureless in every direction, and it is easy — in the starlight, cold, tired, still half in the room — to walk off in a wrong direction and not realize it for an hour. Before you go in, build a cairn at your exit point, or two, in a line pointing the way home. The previous visitors built a wall from stacked stone; the material is there. Take a compass bearing, and take it twice, because volcanic rock can pull a needle. A featureless plateau at altitude at night is its own small wilderness, and it kills people in a way that is entirely ordinary.

## What you owe the place

It has been tended. Somebody piled those stones. Somebody sat where you will sit and cut marks into a slab and grooves into a dome, and then took everything they'd brought back out with them. You don't know who and you don't know why, but the shape of their behavior is a set of instructions, and the instructions are: come quietly, sit, watch, leave nothing, go.

If you want to leave a mark, scratch one more line onto the slab outside, at the height of a seated person's shoulder, with a sharp stone, in the light. That is the only mark the room has ever allowed to stay, and it is because it is not inside.

Then walk your cairn line home, drink, sleep low if you can, and in a day or two watch the velvet wear off your palm and try to decide whether the warmth you felt coming off it was real.
