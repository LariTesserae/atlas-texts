**Preparing for the Breathing Shaft**

Start with this: the place is not hostile, and it will hurt you anyway. Nothing there wants anything from you. The birds don't care, the lizards don't care, the valve doesn't care. The damage it does is the incidental damage of a thing that is very large and very old and not arranged for human bodies, and almost all of it is avoidable if you understand three things before you start up the stair — that it is loud enough to injure you, that it is high enough to injure you, and that it is pleasant enough that you will not want to leave when you should.

---

**The sound**

Take the description at its word. If you cannot hear your own boots on iron a hundred steps below the lip, and if a lizard can clatter across a grating without registering, you are walking into a sound field that in any industrial setting would come with mandatory protection and a time limit. Low, edgeless, continuous. That kind of tone is deceptive: it doesn't feel like noise the way a scream or a machine does. It feels like weather, and the text says as much. But your cochlea does not know the difference between a beautiful roar and an ugly one, and a tone you experience as "pressure in the chest" is still moving the hair cells in your inner ear far past what they were built to survive for hours at a stretch.

So: hearing protection, and double it. Foam plugs seated properly, then muffs over them. Understand what this will and won't do. Muffs and plugs together take off perhaps thirty-odd decibels, which is a great deal and not nearly enough — very low frequencies leak around and through protection, and beyond a certain intensity the sound simply enters through your skull and your ribcage and your teeth, protection or no. You will still hear it. You will still feel it. The plugs are not there to give you silence; they're there so that the part of you that has to hear for the rest of your life comes back down the stair with you.

Some people will tell you the protection spoils the place. Ignore them. The place is in your sternum, not your ears. You'll lose nothing.

Expect, whatever you do, a period afterward of muffled hearing and probably ringing. That's a temporary threshold shift and it usually resolves. If it doesn't resolve in a day or two, it wasn't temporary, and you'll wish you'd doubled up. Limit your time inside — an hour or two is generous — and understand that the limit is not a suggestion, because the place will make you want to break it. More on that below.

Expect also the effects that have nothing to do with hearing. Very low continuous sound at high intensity can do odd things to the body: a fluttering in the chest where the tone finds the resonance of your lungs, a faint nausea, a swimming in the vision when your eyes themselves are vibrating a little in their sockets. Blurred sight, mild dizziness, a sense that the floor is not entirely still. On a grating over a drop, at altitude, these matter. If you feel them, get your back against the wall, sit down, and don't stand up quickly. They usually settle as your body accepts the vibration, or they don't, in which case you go.

---

**The height**

The sky is the dark blue of true altitude; frost never leaves the outer casing; the stair is the last ascent of something that was already high. Read that as: you are somewhere in the range where unacclimatised people get sick and where the sick get stupid. Whatever your world's mountains have taught you, apply it here. Come up over days, not hours. Know the signs of altitude illness — the headache that doesn't answer to water and rest, the nausea, the odd clumsiness, the breathlessness at rest — and take them seriously, because up there the usual escape (descend, now) means going down a long iron stair in a diminished state, which is its own danger.

The warm air fools people. It's warm, it's wet, it feels like a valley, and the body relaxes into it as though it had come down instead of up. It hasn't. The chamber is open to the sky through the louvres; the pressure inside is the pressure outside. There is no more oxygen in the shaft's breath than in the thin air around the mountain. If anything, be a little wary of what *is* in it. The breath is "faintly mineral," which is a polite word for gases from deep underground. Birds, lizards and insects thrive in it, so it is not poison, but birds and lizards are not you. If you have a small gas meter, carry it and glance at it. If you don't, pay attention to headache and drowsiness, which have a dozen possible causes up there and all of them mean *leave*.

---

**The wet, and the cold that follows it**

You will be rained on the entire time. Not hard — a fine, constant drip off every edge — but constant, and warm, and you will stop noticing it within minutes, which is the problem. You'll leave the chamber soaked. And the stair down is on the outside of the casing, in frost, in thin air, in wind.

Wet clothing at altitude in the cold is how people die of exposure a few hundred metres from safety. Prepare for it as a certainty, not a possibility. Carry a complete dry layer — base, insulation, shell — sealed in a waterproof bag that you do not open inside. Stop at the lip on the way out, out of the drip but before you're on the open stair, and change. Wring out the wet things and pack them away; don't wear them down. Hat and gloves in the dry bag too. Your hands will be on that bright-worn handrail for a long time.

Inside, wear something that doesn't mind being soaked and won't chill you while you're in the warm — you can be comfortable in there in very little. Footwear is the exception. Wet iron grating, wet moss, wet rust: everything underfoot is slick, and the grating has been corroding for as long as the moss has been growing. Boots with real grip. Test each step of the catwalk and ladder before you trust it. Assume the rung with the broad-leaved plant on it is weaker than the one without.

Cameras and instruments fog instantly going from the frost outside to the warm wet inside. If you care about them, bag them, let them warm up sealed, and open the bag inside. Or accept that the place doesn't photograph well anyway and leave them.

---

**The floor, and what's under it**

The grating is a floor over a hole that goes down further than anyone has followed. The rivets are letting go one at a time, rarely, over years. The plates are held by a casing so old that its rust has become soil. None of this means the grating will fail beneath you today. All of it means you should behave as though it might.

Stay off the middle. Keep to the walls, where the plating is thickest and the structure is doubled by the catwalk supports. If you carry a short length of line and a harness, there are rivet heads the size of fists on every course — not a rated anchor, but better than nothing, and clipping in to something costs you little. Watch the roof. When a rivet goes it comes down with iron and fern and a clang you'll feel rather than hear. A hard hat is not absurd.

And — this is the part that matters — notice the lean. The text is very clear about it: everything living leans in toward the grating, toward the dark, toward the sound, and after a while you do too. Don't romanticise that. On a slick floor over an unmeasured drop, a body that has quietly adopted a bias toward the centre is a body drifting toward the edge. Every so often, deliberately, take a step *back*. Touch the wall. Correct.

---

**Communication and time**

Nothing you say will be heard. Not by your companion, not by yourself. Prepare for that as you'd prepare for a dive: every signal agreed before you go in, practised, simple. *Up. Out. Look. I'm all right. I'm not all right. Time.* Hands on shoulders. A waterproof slate and grease pencil for anything that won't fit in a gesture. Don't rely on radios; even if they work, you can't hear them.

Then the harder part. Every account of this place says the same thing in the same words: *you stay longer than you mean to*. The roar becomes weather, then quiet. Time stops behaving. The birds turn, the rain falls at its unchanging rate, and there is nothing in the room to mark an hour from the next one. You will not make a good decision about when to leave while you are inside. So make it outside, before you climb, and bind yourself to it.

Set a timer — one that vibrates against the skin or flashes, since anything that beeps is useless — and treat it as an order, not a prompt. If you go with someone, agree that when either one signals *Time*, both go, without the discussion you couldn't have anyway. Leave with light enough to get down the stair. The lamp in its cage may or may not be lit and the louvres only give you the sky; if you come out at dusk you are on an exposed iron stair at altitude in the dark with damaged hearing and a body that's forgotten it's cold. Carry a headlamp regardless.

---

**Conduct**

Leave the wheel where you found it. It does nothing at a quarter turn; that is not an invitation to try a full one. The bell, the valve, the lamp — someone made them for a reason that has gone, and the place has settled into a shape without that reason. You are a visitor in a settlement of moss and rust and lizards that has taken a very long time to reach its terms. Step on iron, not green. Don't pick the ferns; the filmy ones tear at a touch and can live nowhere else on the mountain. Don't handle the lizards, warm and unafraid as they are; their fearlessness is a fact about the roar, not about you, and it's not yours to teach them otherwise. Don't wave at the birds.

Bring your own water. It rains in there, and the rain is condensed shaft-breath off rusted iron — probably harmless, possibly not, certainly not worth finding out at altitude.

Go with one other person if you can. Not for the chamber, where company reduces to a hand on a shoulder and then to nothing. For the stair, both ways, and for the hour afterward.

---

**Afterward**

The descent is where people get hurt. You'll be cold in a way you didn't feel arrive, tired in a way the warmth disguised, hearing badly, possibly still faintly dizzy, on a long exposed stair whose handrail has been polished by every hand before yours. Go slowly. Both hands. Don't talk yet; your voice will sound wrong and it will distract you.

At the bottom, sit. Eat something. Let the ringing settle. Expect the ordinary sounds of the world — wind, a bird that you can actually hear, your own footsteps — to seem thin and slightly indecent for a while, as if they were interrupting something. That passes. Expect also something like a low mood, or a hollowness, the way one feels leaving a shore. That passes too, though not as quickly, and there's no gear for it. It is simply what it costs to stand for an hour in the one warm room on the mountain and then go back down into the ordinary cold. Most people think it was worth it. Prepare well enough that you're able to think so too.
