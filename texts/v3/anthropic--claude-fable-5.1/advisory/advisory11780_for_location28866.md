# Preparing for the Oven at Tsen Hollow

## First, understand what kind of place this is

Most dangerous places announce themselves. This one does not. There is nothing in the Oven that will attack you, nothing that is trying to hurt you, no cliff to fall from, no current to pull you under. The room has made way for everything that has entered it for longer than anyone has been counting, and it will make way for you.

The danger is that the room is *indifferent* in a very particular direction. Everything in it has been shaped by the absence of water and the presence of heat. You are a large, soft, leaking bag of water walking into a kiln. Nothing there wants you. But nothing there will stop the kiln from working on you either, and by the time you notice, you will be several hours from anything cold and several days from anything wet.

So this is not a preparation against a hostile place. It is a preparation for entering a place where the only thing that can go wrong is you, quietly, over hours.

---

## The approach is half the visit

Before you ever feel the warm breath on your face, you have to cross the shelf. Do not underestimate the shelf.

**Altitude.** The air is "thin enough to notice." That means at least three thousand meters, possibly a good deal more. If you are coming from sea level in any world, give yourself time. Two or three days of ascent if you can arrange it. If you cannot — if the way you arrive drops you straight onto the plateau — then move slowly, drink more than you think you need, and watch yourself for the headache, the nausea, the odd breathlessness at rest. Altitude sickness in the open would be bad. Altitude sickness inside the Oven, where the air is already thin and sulphurous and the heat is pulling fluid out of you, could kill you without ever raising its voice.

**Sun.** Bare polished rock, no shade, hard blue sky, thin air. The ultraviolet up there will be brutal, and the glassy ground will throw it back up at you from below. Cover everything. Wide brim, long sleeves, gloves for the crossing, dark glasses. Your face will burn from underneath. Your lips will crack before you ever reach the hollow, and they need to last.

**Cold.** The same thin dry air that cooks you by day lets the heat out by night faster than you would believe. The plateau at night will be well below freezing. You are going to come out of the Oven, where you have spent hours at bread-oven temperature with every blood vessel in your skin wide open, directly into that. Plan for it. More on this later.

**Wind.** It has "never once carried rain." It has also never once stopped. It is a drying wind, a scouring wind, and on open glass with nothing to break it, it will take moisture off you steadily the whole way across. Cover your mouth and nose with something.

**Navigation.** The seam is "only a slightly darker seam in a dark cliff." The plateau has nothing on it. No landmarks, no vegetation, no water to follow. If you arrive at some distance from the hollow, you will need to find the cliff edge and then walk it. Bring what you need to find your way back to wherever you came in — cairns if you are willing to disturb the emptiness that much, or a compass and count, or a knotted cord. Stars are excellent up there; learn a few before you go if you can. Do not rely on anything electronic that needs light to read.

**Footing.** Welded ash and rhyolite glass. It is smooth in most places and edged like a broken bottle in others. Thick soles, good ankles, and don't hurry.

## Water: the entire question

Everything about this visit reduces to one number, and that number is how much water you can carry against how long you want to stay.

Assume you will lose water at every stage: crossing the shelf under sun and wind, inside the Oven under heat, crossing back at night under cold and thin air. In dry heat you will sweat and it will evaporate the instant it reaches the air. You will not feel wet. You will not feel that you are losing anything. This is the trap of every desert, and the Oven is a desert folded up and set over a fire.

For a single day's visit — walk in, several hours inside, walk out — carry no less than five liters per person, and if you can carry eight, carry eight. Add electrolytes. Plain water in that much heat, drunk that fast, will wash the salt out of you and leave you cramping and stupid.

Drink before you go in. Drink a great deal. Then, inside, sip. A bladder with a bite valve is the right tool, worn under your shirt so the tube stays sealed and nothing spills.

**Spill nothing.** I want to be plain about this because I do not know what happens if you do. Every creature in that room has been shaped by ten thousand generations of never encountering free water. Their whole chemistry is built around drawing moisture from the film and from each other, and holding it with "miserliness." What a spilled cupful does to that room — whether it is ignored, whether it draws them, whether it kills the film where it lands — nobody has recorded. There is a bowl carved into the floor beside the seat, glazed by something once put in and taken out. It may have held water. It may have been an offering, or a test, or a bribe. Do not repeat the experiment casually. Drink from a sealed line and keep your bottles closed.

And do not stay past your water. Set a turnaround volume, not a turnaround time. When the bladder is half empty, you begin walking out, whatever the room is doing.

## The heat, and what it does to a body

The description calls it kiln heat, bread-oven heat. Read that as somewhere well above body temperature — hot enough that you cannot lose heat to the air by radiation, so that sweating is your *only* cooling mechanism, and sweating is exactly what the room punishes.

The heat also pulses. There is an ebb of hours and then a flow of an hour or two during which the floor becomes "perceptibly hotter." You will want to be there for a hot phase, because that is when the room moves and the lights gather. You also need to know that the hot phase is when you are most likely to get into trouble, and it comes on gradually enough that you may not mark the moment it crossed from uncomfortable to dangerous.

Learn the signs in advance and say them aloud to yourself before you enter: headache, nausea, confusion, stopping sweating, feeling suddenly cold in a hot room, a racing heart at rest. Any one of these and you go up the seam immediately. Do not wait to see if it passes. The seam is thirty paces with two bends and then sky, and the heat "falls away like a coat." Twenty minutes of sitting in the cold outside is the treatment. There is no other treatment available within a great many miles.

Clothing for inside: light, loose, breathable, natural fiber, long-sleeved, long-legged. You want a layer between your skin and the surfaces, and you want it to hold and spread what sweat you produce rather than let it vanish instantly. Cover your head with cloth, not a hat. Wear boots with thick soles — the floor is "not quite solid and never quite still" and it is warm enough to hurt through thin leather over hours.

Do not lie down. Do not sit anywhere except on the seat. The floor is hotter than the air, and it is where the weight of the population lives.

---

## Air

Mineral, sulphurous, thick, with scorched hair. That is a volcanic room, and volcanic rooms have gases in them.

The saving grace is that the Oven breathes *out* — you feel the warm breath on your face before you ever enter. That means the pressure inside is pushing air up the seam, and stale or heavy gas is not pooling at the entrance. But the room is a dome with a floor that slopes down toward a far passage, and the far passage goes deeper. Heavy gas would settle low and toward the back. Stay near the entrance side. Stay standing. If you have a way to detect carbon dioxide or hydrogen sulphide, bring it; if you do not, then treat any headache or dizziness as gas until proven otherwise, which means: leave.

If your lungs are already compromised — asthma, anything chronic — think hard about whether to go in at all. Thin air, sulphur, and a fine dust of dead things at kiln temperature is not an environment that forgives a bad chest.

---

## Light, and the argument for going without

Bring a light. Keep it off.

Nothing in the room can see, so a light will not disturb the creatures directly, but it will destroy the visit. The redness down the far passage and the drifting pinprick stars on the walls are at the absolute threshold of human vision. One glance at a headlamp and they will be gone for half an hour, longer. And the whole experience of the room — knowing it by standing in it — is built on the fact that you cannot see. Turn on a light and you will see a cave full of bugs. Leave it off and you will stand inside something that has no name.

If you must have light for safety — finding the seat, checking the exit — make it red and make it dim and use it once, at the beginning, then put it away where you will not fumble for it in a moment of nerves.

Do not bring any kind of flame. There is no reason to; the room is already an oven. And an open flame in a chamber full of sulphurous gas and a floor of dry chitin is not a risk you need to take to prove anything.

---

## Finding your way out in total darkness

The seam has two bends. The room has two passages: the one you came in by and the narrower one that goes down. In complete darkness, after hours of standing, having lost every external reference, it is possible — not likely, but possible — to walk to the wrong opening.

The wrong opening is the one nobody has come back from.

So: tie a cord. Anchor it at the stacked stones outside, run it along the floor of the seam, and bring the end with you to the seat. The grazers will not mind. When it is time to go, put your hand on the cord and follow it. This is not paranoia. This is the same precaution divers use, for the same reason — that the sense of direction which serves you so well in daylight is not merely diminished in the dark but actively wrong, and confident.

Alternatively, if you are traveling with another person, one of you can stay at the seat by the entrance while the other stands at the room's center. Voices carry in there. You can call each other home.

---

## Time

There is no day. There is a pulse of one to two hours hot, then a long ebb. If you want to feel a full cycle — and you should, it is the room's heartbeat and the thing that makes sense of everything else — you will need three to five hours inside.

That is at the outer edge of what your water and your body will comfortably allow. Bring a way to know the time that does not need light: a watch with raised markers you can read by touch, or one that vibrates on the hour. Without it, you will not be able to tell whether you have stood there for one hour or six, and the room is very good at making you stop caring.

## What you will actually do in there

You will walk in. The light will go at the second bend. You will feel the walls close and the heat climb and the floor go smooth under your boots. You will come into the room and feel the space open around you without seeing it.

Find the seat. It is just inside the entrance, under the living surface, a flat stone cooler and smoother than the rest. Brush the grazers off — they come away easily and return when you stop — and sit. This is where people have sat. You are not the first and it is good to sit where the first ones sat.

Then wait. Let your eyes stay open. Let the whisper of the room settle into the background until it becomes the silence. After a long while you will begin to see the lights, and after a longer while you will begin to see them move, and after a longer while still you will feel the hot phase come on and the whisper quicken, and you will understand — not in words — what the grooves in the dome are a picture of.

Rest your hand on the wall. Feel the tide pass under your palm. This is permitted. It is the room's way of speaking, and you should let it.

Do not eat inside. The room reads the air by taste, and you do not want to introduce a new flavor into a place that has had the same flavor for ten thousand years. Eat before, outside, well away from the entrance, and clean your hands.

Do not go down the far passage. When the click comes — and in the cool phase it may — you will feel a pull toward it that is almost physical. Every human who has ever stood in the dark and heard a single dry clear sound from further down has felt it. Sit with it. The people who cut the grooves in the dome felt it too, and they went home.

## The dog-sized thing, and whether you should be afraid

Once or twice you may feel something large move through the mass near your feet — a slow displacement, a shift in the warm floor, something the size of a dog passing without disturbing anything because it is *of* the room in a way you are not.

I do not believe it will hurt you. It has passed through this room for longer than there have been people to describe it, and the people who described it went home to do so. The room's settlement — "everything makes way for everything else" — appears to extend to visitors.

But you are a novelty in one specific respect: you are wet. You are the wettest thing that has ever been in that room, by a great margin. And the hunters, and the larger thing, and whatever eats the dead, all draw moisture "from each other." I would not expect any of them to test you. I also would not give them the opportunity. Keep your skin covered below the wrists. Keep your boots laced to the top and your trousers tucked into them. Keep moving your feet occasionally so that nothing settles. And when you come out, before you put on your cold-weather layers, shake out your clothing thoroughly and check the seams and folds by touch.

## Coming out

This is the moment people do not plan for, and it is the second most dangerous moment of the visit.

You have been for hours at kiln heat. Your skin is flushed, your blood vessels are open, you are dehydrated and tired and your judgment is a little softened. You walk up the seam and the heat falls off you and at the second bend the sky comes back, and then you step out onto the shelf into a wind that has never carried rain, at an altitude where the night is well below freezing.

The temperature difference could easily be forty degrees or more. Your body, wide open to shed heat, will dump it into that wind faster than you can believe. You will go from overheated to hypothermic in the time it takes to walk fifty paces.

So: before you go in, leave your warm layers just inside the entrance, past the first bend where the wind does not reach — a heavy coat, a hat, gloves, something for the neck. When you come up the seam, stop at the first bend. Sit down on the warm floor. Put everything on. Drink. Eat something. Wait until you feel yourself steady. *Then* step out into the stars.

If you are walking back across the shelf at night, and you may well be — the description assumes it — make sure you know your route before you go in, because you will be finding it by starlight in a cold wind with a tired brain. There is a case for camping on the shelf near the entrance and walking out at dawn. The warm breath from the seam will keep a bivouac tolerable if you set it just to one side of the opening, out of the wind but in the exhalation.

## What to carry

Not a rule. A list to argue with.

- Water: five to eight liters per person, in a sealed bladder for inside and bottles for outside. Electrolyte salts.
- Sun: wide brim, long sleeves, dark glasses, something for the face and lips. Lip balm — a lot of it.
- Cold: a serious coat, hat, gloves, neck cover, left at the first bend.
- Inside clothing: light, loose, long, natural, covering wrists and ankles. Thick-soled boots.
- A red light, dim, kept off.
- A cord long enough to run from the stacked stones to the seat.
- A watch you can read by touch.
- Eye lubricant. The dry air will make your eyes ache, and you will want to keep them open.
- A dry cloth for your hands.
- Something to eat outside, before and after. Nothing that crumbles.
- If you have one: a detector for heavy gas.
- Nothing you intend to leave.

---

## What to leave, and what to take

Nothing and nothing.

The people who came before left "no tool, no bead, no bone." They left scratches on a slab outside and grooves on the dome inside, both of them made with nothing but stone and time and attention. Whether you add a scratch to the tally by the door is your own business. I would not blame you; it is the habit of a hand with a long wait, and you will have waited. But do not carry anything in that you do not carry out, and do not carry out anything the room did not give you.

The room will give you one thing whether you want it or not. On the palm you rested against the wall there will be a faint dark velvet that will not wash off, because there is nothing to wash it with. It is the film — the thing at the bottom of the whole ecology, the life that lives on hot rock and breathes sulphur. It will wear away in a day or two. It is very probably harmless. Still: do not put that hand to your mouth or your eyes until it is gone, and do not scrub at it, and if you hold it near your face in the dark and feel the smallest warmth coming off it, do not be too quick to decide whether you imagined it.

---

## Who should not go

Anyone with a heart condition. The combination of altitude and kiln heat is exactly the combination that stops hearts.

Anyone with a chronic respiratory condition, for the reasons above.

Anyone who does not do well in the dark, in close spaces, or with the sensation of many small things moving nearby. This is not a place to test yourself. Panic in the Oven — a rush for the exit in total darkness, a stumble onto the living floor, a wrong turn toward the far passage — is how the visit becomes something other than a visit.

Anyone who intends to go alone without telling someone where they have gone and when to expect them back. The shelf is empty. No one will pass.

---

## Last

The Oven at Tsen Hollow is one of the quietest places you will ever stand, and one of the most crowded. It is life without any of the things that we were taught life requires, packed so densely that it has stopped being a collection of creatures and become a single warm surface that breathes with the mountain. It has been doing this for longer than the rock outside has been dry, which is to say, for longer than there has been rock outside.

You are going to enter it as a stranger, stand in it for a few hours as a guest, and leave it as exactly what you were, minus a few liters of water and plus a mark on your palm that fades. It will not remember you. It did not remember the ones who cut the grooves in the dome. That is not the room's failing.
