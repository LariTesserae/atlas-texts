**Preparing for the Pressing Ground**

Start by understanding what kind of danger this place is. It is not a predator. Nothing in the basin will notice you, chase you, or want you. It is also not a dead place, where the only hazards are your own clumsiness. It is a third thing: a very slow, very patient process that takes up whatever is left inside it, and the whole discipline of visiting is to be *in* it without being *left* in it. Almost everything below follows from that.

---

**Getting down, and getting back up**

The rim is the first problem and the last one. A lip of braided grey root, wide enough to walk on, slick — that is a surface with no friction and a drop of about a house-height on the inside. The mat below would probably catch you, since it gives before it holds, but a house-height onto a surface that yields and then stops is exactly how ankles go, and a turned ankle here means you sit on warm wet tissue for a long time.

Bring a rope you trust and something to anchor it to that is not the plant. Do not tie to the root-rim. Root that has been braided and wet for centuries is strong, but it is also alive, and anything living here is slowly moving; a knot set at noon may be a knot in a slightly different place by evening. Anchor to the ordinary ground outside the rim — stake, deadman, a tree if there is one — and run the rope over the lip. Leave it fixed for the whole visit. You will be tired and wet coming back up, and the root will be exactly as slick as it was.

Wear something on your hands with grip. On the rim, walk as if on wet ice, because that is what it is.

Mark your exit. The light is featureless and the fog erases the floor; from the middle of the basin one stretch of grey rim looks precisely like another. Use a marker that is *yours* and *high*: a bright cloth on a pole driven into the outside ground, visible above the lip. Don't hang it on the root. Don't set anything you want back down on the mat.

---

**Feet and legs**

You will be wet from the knee down for every minute you spend on the floor, and the water will be the temperature of your own body. That sounds pleasant. It is not, over hours. Warm wet skin softens, wrinkles, and splits; the warmth means your legs can't shed heat, so on a still day you will feel heavier and stupider than you expect, and your feet will be cooking gently in your boots.

Do not try to keep the water out. A waterproof boot fills through the top with the first deep step and then holds a warm bath against your foot all day. Wear something that drains — neoprene wading boots, or a mesh boot with a firm sole — and accept the wet. Two pairs of socks is a mistake; wool that drains is the right one. Bring far more dry socks than you think, and a towel, and an antifungal powder or ointment, and when you come up to the rim in the evening take everything off below the waist, dry your legs completely, and let the skin see air before you sleep. Check between your toes. This is the least romantic paragraph in the guide and the one most likely to matter.

The mat gives a finger's width and holds. Trust it, but walk flat-footed. Don't use a pointed trekking pole; the tip punches into tissue and the tissue closes around it, and pulling it back out is unpleasant in a way that is hard to describe. A wide-basketed pole, or none.

Cover any cut on your legs before you go down, and cover it well. The text of the place is clear that seeds take root in rust, and that the mat absorbs what falls on it. Nobody has established what it does with an open wound, and you should not be the one who finds out.

---

**Ears and eyes**

The basin is loud, and the loudness is mostly harmless — the pump and the hiss and the ticking are a wall of sound you will stop hearing as sound after the first hour. The danger is the *crack*. Once an hour or so, unpredictably, a pod the size of a boot bursts somewhere in the mat with a report that rings your ears, and it flings its contents ten paces.

Carry earplugs on a cord around your neck. You will not want them in all the time, because the pump is worth hearing and because the plugs make the fog feel worse. But put them in when you work close to anything, and put them in at night.

Wear eye protection whenever you are on the floor. Wraparound, not glasses. Ten paces is a wide circle and you cannot see the pods in the fog until you are next to one. If you do see a pod — and you'll learn the look, a swelling in the mat with a pale seam — do not touch the seam, and do not linger downwind. Move on.

---

**Hands**

You will want to touch things. The flywheel is warm and has a pulse. The bark over the iron feels like tree and gives like something else. Touch, if you like; the place does not mind. But:

Never put a hand, a finger, a strap, or a sleeve between the coils of tendril on the flywheel's axle and the shaft. The tightening is slow — slower than a minute hand — but it is a living cable thick as a wrist that does nothing but tighten, and it will not let go for you. The same goes for anywhere a vine is wrapped around a fixed thing: the transformer insulators, the pipe cradles. Look at the older coils, brown and slack, falling into husks. That is the release mechanism. It runs on death and takes years.

The iron under the bark is rust and cast edges. Wear gloves. Have your tetanus protection current, in whatever form your home world offers it.

Don't put your hands in the amber troughs. The first is water, warm. By the fourth it is thick and sweet-smelling and it does not want to leave your skin. By the sixth it is setting, and you will have to cut it off your glove.

Don't drink the water. Don't taste the amber. The place tells you what the water is. Bring your own — more than you think, because you will sweat in that warmth without noticing.

---

**The beetle**

Leave it alone. It is the only animal you will see, and it has not learned fear, and it will let you get very close. You could pick it up. Don't. If there is one thing in the basin that could be called a resident rather than a component, it's that beetle, and you are the guest.

---

**The blocks**

Here is the real question, the one you should answer before you go down, not while standing at the shelf with an amber loaf in your hands.

Nobody will stop you taking one. The place doesn't keep them; the mat eventually grows over the lowest and they are gone. In that sense a block is refuse, and lifting one from the stack is no more theft than picking up a fallen leaf.

But look at what's inside. A moth. Copper wire. A seed — or a rivet, or something that is both. The plant is bounded. It does not cross the rim. It has *no interest* in the ordinary land beyond. And you are proposing to pick up a sealed, slowly-clouding package containing what may be a viable seed, and walk it out over the rim and into another world.

Perhaps the seed is dead. Perhaps it only roots in rust, and there is no rust where you're going. Perhaps the plant's boundedness is a choice and not a limit, and it would not grow anywhere else if you begged it. You don't know. The place will not tell you. And "nothing here has an edge that stays an edge" is a warning as much as a description.

My own counsel: photograph them, sketch them, sit with them, and leave them on the shelf. If you must take one, take one of the newer, clearest blocks with something inarguably inert inside — the wire, not the seed — and keep it sealed in something hard, and be honest with yourself that you are doing something whose consequences you cannot see the end of. The basin certainly can't; it has never once thought about what happens past its rim.

---

**Time**

Bring a watch, and look at it. The light here is a flat overcast that could be any hour of a mild day, the wheel moves slower than a minute hand, and the pump's eight-or-nine-second cycle is exactly the sort of rhythm a human mind falls into and stops counting. People lose afternoons here, and not because anything is done to them.

Decide before you go how long you are staying, and hold yourself to it. An afternoon shows you every machine. One night on the rim lets you hear the place with the light gone — the blocks holding a faint glow, the fog rising to your waist, the single crack in the dark — and that is worth the discomfort. A second day, maybe, if you want to watch the beetle come back and confirm the sixth trough has thickened since yesterday.

Do not stay a season. Not because harm will come; because the place's whole argument is that nothing needs to hurry and nothing needs you, and it is a very persuasive argument, and the Pressing Ground would take your tent stakes and your rope and your boots and eventually your unattended pack exactly as gently as it took the flywheel, and grow bark over them, and put them to use. You would not be hurt. You would simply, after a while, find that leaving had become a project.

---

**Sleeping**

On the rim, not the floor. The fog comes up to the waist at night and the mat stays body-warm and wet, and lying on it for eight hours is a bad idea for every reason given above about skin, plus one more: you don't know what the mat does with something warm and still that stays in one place all night. It absorbs seeds. It closes over iron. Don't be the experiment.

The rim is slick and narrow and has a drop on one side. Sleep tethered to your outside anchor. Or — better — sleep on the ordinary ground just beyond the lip, where the plant has no interest, and go back over in the morning. The sound will carry. You'll still hear the pump.

---

**Gear, in summary**

A fixed rope and an outside anchor. Grip gloves. Draining boots and many wool socks, towel, antifungal. Wraparound eye protection. Earplugs on a cord. A wide-basketed pole or none. Your own water, more than you'd carry elsewhere. Wound covering, applied before descent. A watch. A high, bright marker for your exit. A hard sealed container if you have decided, against advice, to carry a block.

Nothing iron that you want back. Nothing you will set down and walk away from.

---

**What it will be like**

You will spend the first hour disbelieving the ground and the second hour forgetting you ever did. You will find the flywheel and put your palm on it and feel it move, and something in you will be very quiet for a while. You will find the pump and understand it and then understand that understanding it doesn't help. You will stand at the shelf looking at the blocks and have the argument with yourself that this guide has already had for you.

And then you will sit on the lip at evening with your feet in the warm fog, the double stroke coming up from below, and you will feel the specific loneliness of a place that is entirely, loudly, patiently occupied and has no room in its occupation for anyone. It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is *busy*, at a pace you cannot see, with a job that is itself.

That's the thing to prepare for, more than the wet or the noise. You are going to visit something that is complete. Bring what you need to stay dry and unhurt and to get back over the rim, and bring, if you can, the kind of attention that doesn't need to be returned. Then climb your rope, dry your feet, and leave it going *hunh — hunh* behind you in the dark, exactly as it was before you came, exactly as it will be after.
