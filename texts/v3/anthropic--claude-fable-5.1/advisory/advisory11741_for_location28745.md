# Preparing for the Tableland

## First, about arrival

You cannot climb it. Read that again, because every instinct you have about high places says otherwise. There are no sides. Whatever translocation you use has to put you *on the surface*, not at the foot of it, and you should have your operator confirm the target altitude against a lowland baseline before you go. If you arrive below, you will be standing in wet foothills looking up at a wall of cloud that goes on forever, and that is the whole of your trip.

This matters for a second reason. Ordinary mountain travel gives you days of ascent to acclimatize. Here you go from sea level to the altitude of a serious summit in the time it takes to step through. If you have never spent time above four thousand meters, do so first — somewhere else, somewhere with sides — and go to the tableland within a week or two of coming down while your blood still remembers. If you can't, accept that your first two or three days will be headache, nausea, broken sleep, and a strange indifference to everything, and plan to do nothing during them but sit inside a dwelling wall and breathe.

Arrive in the morning. The cloud is usually down in the morning, and you want to learn the place in its ordinary state before you see it clear.

## What it is actually like to stand there

Grey. A hundred paces of it in every direction, a room with no walls, the floor soft and wet and giving under your boots. The cold is not dramatic. You will think, at first, that you have dressed too warmly. Give it an hour. It is the cold of a stone cellar — a degree or two from freezing, saturated, patient — and it goes into cloth, into leather, into the joints of your fingers, and it stays. Nothing dries. Understand that phrase as a physical law and not a complaint. The sock you take off wet tonight will be wet tomorrow and the day after.

Everything drips. You will notice this for perhaps a day and then you will stop noticing it, and that is the first small warning about the place: your senses settle in very quickly, because there is so little for them to do.

And there is the silence. It has a texture you have never encountered because you have never, anywhere, been in a landscape with no animals in it. No insect hum. No bird. Nothing in the moss. The wind is thin and the vegetation is too low to hiss. What you hear is your own breath, your own blood in your ears, the sound of your boots coming out of the sward, and — if you are very still — nothing. People who go to the deep desert or the polar ice talk about silence, but those places have wind that speaks and ice that groans. This is quieter. Some travelers find it holy. Some find it unbearable within hours and need to talk aloud to themselves. Either is fine. Just know it is coming.

Then, toward evening, the cloud lifts, and you will understand why anyone lives here. The floor goes on to the edge of the world. Every leaf carries a lit drop. The pale stone glows. Far off, alone, a thread of smoke. It is one of the great sights available anywhere, and it lasts about an hour, and then the cold slams down and the ice starts to ring in the channels and you had better be inside.

## The one rule, and what it does to your luggage

Here is the thing you must actually take seriously, above the cold and the altitude and the fog: *on the tableland, things that are sufficiently alike become one thing.*

This is not folklore and it is not gradual enough to ignore. Two similar stones set down within sight of one another are one stone by the next morning. Two identical objects are one object in hours. The more alike, the faster. The people who live here have built an entire civilization around never repeating anything, and you are about to walk in carrying a kit that was designed by a culture that believes in spares.

Go through your pack with this in mind and you will find it is almost entirely redundancy. Two of everything. Six pairs of socks. A strip of thirty tablets. A box of matches. Fifty rounds. Twelve energy bars from the same manufacturer. A stack of coins. Every one of these is, on the plateau, a single item that you have paid for many times over — and worse, the merger does not always take one position or the other. Sometimes the survivor sits at a point *between* where the two were. Sometimes that point is inside something else.

The packing principle is simple to state and difficult to execute: **bring one of everything, and let no thing be the same as any other thing you carry.**

In practice:

- **Socks, gloves, layers.** Buy each one different — different color, different weight, different maker. If you must bring two of a type, cut a distinct notch in each, sew a different button on, dye one. Ugly is fine. Ugly is safe.
- **Boots.** The paired-limb problem. Your two hands do not merge, and the locals walk on two feet, so the rule seems to respect wholes — a left and a right are parts of a pair, not two like things. But don't push it. Buy boots that are visibly, structurally different from each other, or take a knife to the one you like less. The inhabitants' footwear, when you see it, is never symmetrical, and they have had a very long time to learn why.
- **Medication.** This is the genuinely dangerous one. A bottle of identical pills will be one pill by morning. Bring drugs in the smallest quantity you can survive on, in as many different forms as you can — tablets from one maker, capsules from another, a liquid, a patch. Or accept that you are bringing a single dose and plan your trip around that. Do not gamble on an opaque container protecting the contents; the rule speaks of "within sight," but whose sight is meant is not clear, and no one has tested it on something that mattered.
- **Food.** No animals means no meat, no eggs, no honey, no fish, no leather to chew in extremity. The plants do not flower, so no fruit, no seed, no nectar. The locals eat the moss and the willow's inner bark and some part of the heath, prepared in ways you will be shown and will not enjoy. Bring your own, and bring *variety* — not a case of one thing but one each of forty things. Every ration a different meal. It is heavier and it is the only way.
- **Money.** Useless. Coins merge, and no one here wants them anyway. See below on what they do want.
- **Ammunition, matches, batteries, spare parts, backup anything.** Understand that on the tableland *there is no such thing as a backup*. A backup is by definition a likeness. Every system you carry is single-point-of-failure by law of nature. Choose one good knife, one fire-starting method that will work in thin wet air, one light. If it breaks, it is broken.
- **Photographs, notes, recordings.** Two photographs of the same shoulder in the same fog are one photograph. Two journal entries reading "Fog. Cold. Walked." are one entry, and — this is the part to attend to — so, very possibly, are the two days they describe.

## Moving

Visibility in the cloud is a hundred paces. The land has no features taller than a person and thousands of near-identical low swells the locals call shoulders. There is nothing to sight on. There is no river to follow because there are no rivers. A compass should work; test it against the sun when the sky clears, because you will be depending on it absolutely. Any satellite-based positioning you carry will be talking to a sky that does not have satellites in it.

Dead reckoning fails here in a way it fails nowhere else, because *distances change*. Two shoulders that were nearly the same height are, over months, drawn together, and one morning the mile between them is gone. A local who walked that mile all her life finds the walk is shorter and cannot remember what was in the missing part. You will not have her advantage of noticing. You will simply be somewhere other than where your pacing says you are.

The workable answer is walls. The fog-fences run for a mile or more, straight, chest-high, and each one ends at a cistern, and near a cistern is a dwelling. If you are lost in cloud, walk in one direction until you hit a wall, then follow it. It will bring you to water and, eventually, to a person. Do not cross a wall by climbing it and do not knock a stone from it. Every block was shaped and marked by hand to be unlike every other block on the continent, and the ledgers that record those marks are the closest thing this culture has to scripture.

Walk on the moss, not the bare stone. The stone is polished, wet, and in the morning glazed with ice; the moss is a wet mattress and it will exhaust you but it will not drop you. Do not push through the willow thickets unless you must. Stems in there are four thousand years old and grow a fingernail's width a century; the branch you snap for convenience was alive when your world's earliest cities were founded.

Do not take a route twice. This is not superstition. A walk repeated in the same fog at the same pace is two like moments, and like moments close up.

## The people

You will not meet many. One or two to a dwelling, one dwelling to a horizon. They keep apart from each other by discipline, not preference — two cisterns of the same volume must be a day apart, families visit across walls, lovers live at the length of a mile — and they will keep apart from you for the same reason, and you should let them set the distance.

They will be very interested in you. Understand why: you are the most *specific* thing to arrive on the plateau in living memory. Everything about you is unprecedented — your clothes, your speech, the shape of your tools, the way you hold a cup. Specificity is the highest value here, higher than beauty (beauty, they will tell you, is a kind of likeness), and you are walking specificity. This is a great advantage and you should not abuse it.

What they want from you is difference. Not gifts in the usual sense — they have no use for wealth — but a *thing with no precedent*. A spoon of a shape they have never seen. A fold of cloth in a weave they don't know. A word. A song, if it has never been sung here, though be careful: the great artistic form of the plateau is the single unrepeatable object, and a song you can sing twice is less valued than one you sing once and never again. Bring three or four small things from home that are odd, singular, and that you will not miss. Give one to each household that shelters you. You will be remembered in the ledgers.

Do not offer to help build anything. Do not stack two stones. Do not set your cup beside their cup. Do not, above all, copy a mark you have seen on a wall — not in your notebook, not in the mud with a stick. The whole edifice of the culture rests on no mark ever being made twice, and a visitor who scratches a stolen glyph into a stone is not committing vandalism, they are committing a kind of small murder against the wall.

If you are invited into a dwelling, you will be given willow-wood heat, water that tastes of nothing at all, and a sleeping-shelf. Take them. Take the shelf they offer, not the one you'd prefer. There will be tools on pegs and each hangs at its own angle; do not straighten them. If you see a dwelling out on a far shoulder with a cold hearth and tools still hanging and no one in it, do not go in and do not sleep there. It is marked and left. Someone was recently there and is also, now, someone else, and you do not want to lie down in their shape.

## Time, and yourself

This is the part most travelers underestimate, because the object-merging is vivid and the person-merging is quiet.

The plateau wears at *you* the same way it wears at stones: by finding what is alike and closing it up. Days that resemble each other become one day. A week of fog-walking, camp, fog-walking, camp can be experienced as a single long morning, and when you finally look at the sky and count, you will have been here longer than you remember and will not be able to say what happened in the gap. This is not confusion. The time is *gone*. It has healed shut.

Longer exposure does something the locals have a word for, best rendered as *becoming general*. A person who lives too many like days begins to lose edge. Others find it hard to say which of them they are speaking to. Their dwelling drifts toward their neighbor's. Their face begins to look, to them, a little borrowed. The end state is one of those cold hearths out on the far shoulders.

You are protected for a while by sheer foreignness — you are unlike everything — but foreignness fades into routine faster than you think, and the plateau is very good at routine. Countermeasures, all of them practiced by the inhabitants:

- **Vary everything.** Wake at a different hour. Eat a different food. Walk a different direction. Camp in a different orientation. Do a thing each day you have never done. This is why the packing list demands variety: the forty different rations are not just to keep them from merging with each other, they are to keep your *days* from merging.
- **Write, but write specifically.** A journal makes each day a particular thing that happened. But the entry must be particular. Not "fog, cold, walked." What color was the light when it thinned. Which shoulder. What the water tasted of (nothing, but nothing in what way). Bad writing is dangerous here in a way it is nowhere else.
- **Make something.** Anything, so long as it has not been made before. The locals do this as a discipline, a kind of hygiene. Whittle a shape. Bend wire. It does not have to be good. Beauty is likeness. It has to be *itself*.
- **Do not travel with someone like you.** Two travelers from the same world with the same training and the same gear are, to the plateau, very nearly one traveler, and the plateau will act on that. Absolutely never twins or siblings. If you go with a companion, choose someone as unlike you as you can stand, and sleep at opposite ends of the dwelling, and do not fall into the same habits. The locals keep their own families at the length of a wall for exactly this reason. It is not coldness. It is how they keep each other alive.
- **Watch for the signs.** Difficulty naming the day. A host who hesitates before addressing you. Waking in a place slightly different from where you lay down. The feeling that your own hands are someone's you knew once. Any of these: leave, now, that day.

## Leaving

Go once. A second visit is a likeness of the first, and there is a real possibility the two trips fold together — that you return home from the second and find you have only ever been once, and cannot say which time it was. If you must go again, go in a different season, by a different route, alone if you were accompanied and accompanied if you were alone, and do everything wrong on purpose.

Time your departure so that you have seen the cloud lift at least once. Not for the beauty, though it is that. Because the lifting is the one moment on the tableland that is different every time — the fog tears in a new shape, the light strikes new drops, the far city shows itself in a new scatter of walls and smoke — and it is the one memory of the place you can be sure will stay a single, particular thing, and not wear down against all the others into something general and grey.

Before you step through, count your belongings. Do not be surprised by what is missing, or by what is now one thing where you brought two. Do not be surprised if the trip was shorter, or longer, than you remember.

And when you get home, sit for a while somewhere with birds in it. You will find them unbearably loud. That is normal. It passes. Mostly.
