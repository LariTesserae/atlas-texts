**Preparing for the fissure**

Read the description again before you do anything else, and this time read it as a coroner would. Almost everything you need to know about this place is stated plainly, and the single most important fact is buried in a sentence about leaves.

*No insects. Nothing in the garden has ever been eaten. No bird has found the slot. No bat.*

A warm, wet, sheltered chamber with an open slot to a living forest, standing plants, and a decades-old lunchbox — and nothing has ever crawled into it. Not a spider. Not a springtail. Not a single moth blundering down toward the only light for thirty meters in any direction. Insects find warm lamps in caves through cracks you cannot fit a pencil into. They have not found this one. The narrator attributes this, gently, to "whatever the rock breathed out when it first cracked open," and then moves on. You should not move on. That sentence is the whole visit.

So: this is first and foremost a gas hazard, and you should prepare for it as one. Everything else — the heat, the dark, the sharp rock, the live wire, the exit problem — is real and will be dealt with below, but it is all secondary to the air.

---

**The air**

A young fissure over a geothermal source, closed on every side but a slot far overhead, with no wind and no circulation. Carbon dioxide seeps from young volcanic rock and is heavier than air; in a chamber shaped like an inverted keel it will pool at the floor, exactly where you will be standing and exactly where the garden grows. Plants are fine with this. Plants like it. Hydrogen sulfide is also heavier than air, also geothermal, and has the unpleasant property of destroying your ability to smell it at precisely the concentrations that matter. Radon is a given. Oxygen may simply be low.

The plants are alive because plants breathe backwards from you. The animals are absent because they breathe the way you do.

You need a calibrated four-gas meter — oxygen, CO₂, H₂S, and either CO or a broad lower-explosive-limit sensor — turned on and reading *before* you commit to the floor. Not a smartphone app. Not your nose. If you have any means of arriving above the floor rather than on it, arrive above and lower the meter first on a cord. If you arrive on the floor, the meter is the first thing in your hand, before the light, before anything.

Set your own thresholds in advance and write them on the back of your hand, because you will not think clearly in heat and dark:
- Oxygen below 19.5%: you leave. Not "you consider leaving."
- CO₂ above 1%: you leave. Above 0.5%, you shorten the visit and note any headache.
- H₂S above 10 ppm: you leave immediately, holding your breath if you can.
- Any reading that changes quickly: you leave.

Carry an escape respirator — a ten- or fifteen-minute compressed-air hood, the kind refinery workers keep on their belts. Not a filter mask; filters do nothing for low oxygen or CO₂. If you have access to full self-contained breathing apparatus and the strength to use it in 38-degree heat, that is the correct equipment for a first visit, and you should feel no embarrassment about looking like an industrial confined-space entrant, because that is exactly what you are.

Do not go alone if you can help it. Confined-space deaths happen in pairs and threes precisely because the second person goes in after the first. If you must go alone, tell someone your entry time and your hard exit time and make it a short one.

And notice the early symptoms: a headache that seems like the heat's fault, breathlessness that seems like the heat's fault, a strange calm or a strange irritation. In this chamber the heat will explain away everything your body is trying to tell you. Don't let it.

---

**The heat**

The description is careful here and you should take it literally. Rock "warm the way a body is warm" — mid-thirties Celsius. Air "warmer still," past the point where skin stops registering a boundary — that is skin temperature, 35 to 37 degrees, and likely higher. Humidity near saturation: there is a sweating pipe, a dripping garden, hanging roots transpiring, and no air movement whatsoever.

That combination is dangerous in a way that dry heat is not. Your body cools by evaporating sweat, and evaporation needs two things: air that is not already full of water, and air that moves. This chamber has neither. You will sweat freely and it will do almost nothing. The narrator stands "long enough for the sweat to run." That is not atmosphere. That is the beginning of heat exhaustion, and the transition from exhaustion to stroke can happen inside an hour in conditions like these, faster if you are exerting yourself on a rope.

Prepare as follows:
- Pre-hydrate for a day beforehand. Bring more water than seems reasonable — at least two liters for a one-hour visit, with electrolytes. You will drink it all.
- Wear the minimum that protects you: light, loose, long-sleeved synthetic against the rock edges, not cotton, which will simply become a warm wet bandage. Consider a cooling vest with frozen inserts; it will buy you thirty or forty minutes.
- Set a hard time limit and honor it. Forty-five minutes on a first visit. The place will make you want to stand and stand; the description is a hymn to standing still and letting the eyes adjust. Standing still in a sauna is still a sauna.
- Know the signs: cramps, nausea, dizziness, confusion, the moment you stop sweating. Any one of them is your exit.
- Have a plan for cooling on the way out and at the top. The surface forest will feel like a refrigerator. That is fine. Let it.

---

**The dark**

Your eyes will need twenty to thirty minutes to reach full dark adaptation, and the description is honest that this is disorienting — "long enough to become uncertain whether the darkness is total." Prepare for that uncertainty rather than fighting it.

Bring a headlamp with a red mode and a white mode. Enter on red, dim, so your adaptation is not ruined, and so that you can find the work lamp the way the narrator does — as a discovery rather than a fact. Switch to white only when you need to inspect footing, or on the way out. Bring a second headlamp and a small handheld. Batteries die faster in heat than you think.

Carry glowsticks or small LED markers and drop one at your arrival point and one at the base of your exit line. The chamber is fifteen to twenty meters across with a single light source at chest height; everything will orient toward the lamp, including you, and you can lose your bearings more thoroughly than seems possible in a room that size.

Do not depend on the work lamp. It is dimming. It has been dimming for years. It is on a circuit "no one thought to close," and it may close while you are there.

---

**The floor, the walls, and what cuts**

The rock parted cleanly along planes "still sharp enough to cut," with a glassy skin. This is unweathered fracture surface — think of the edges of freshly broken volcanic glass. Young rock has not had rain or roots or feet to round it.

- Boots with thick, stiff soles and good ankle support. Not trail runners. The floor is warm enough to be felt through soles; thin soles will feel it more and protect you less.
- Gloves — leather palm, or a cut-resistant liner under a work glove. You will want to touch the walls for balance in the dark. The walls will cut your hands.
- A helmet. Things fall through the slot: seeds, mud, branches, presumably the occasional stone. Roots have been hanging for decades and some are as thick as forearms; nothing hangs forever.
- Kneepads if you intend to crouch near the garden, which you will.

The wet circle beneath the sweating pipe — the darkened rock that "has become permanent" — will be slick. Glassy rock plus a thin film of warm mineral water is as slippery as ice. Move around the garden, not through it.

---

**The pipes and the lamp**

Three iron pipes, plate-diameter, running the width of the chamber two meters up, carrying heat from somewhere and humming with the engine that drives it. Treat all three as live pressure lines carrying hot fluid, because that is what they are.

Do not touch them. "Not scorching. Deliberate." — that is the narrator holding a hand *near* one. Iron at 60 degrees will burn skin in seconds of contact; iron carrying hot water or steam may be hotter than the surface reading suggests, and decades of humidity and root acids will have thinned it. The sweating pipe is the most suspect: a pipe that beads along its underside in a warm chamber is more likely leaking through corrosion than condensing, and a corroded pressure line under a mat of roots is a failure waiting for a reason.

Do not touch the red valve wheel. Do not turn anything. Whatever is running the engine far above has been running it for decades without you; the moment you change the state of the system is the moment you become responsible for what it does.

The lamp: caged, corroded, chest height, in an atmosphere at or near saturation, on a cable that vanishes into the dark. This is a live electrical fixture in a wet room with corroded insulation. Do not touch the cage. Do not touch the cable. Do not lean on it, brush it, or try to read the bulb. If you feel a tingle anywhere near it, back away. The plants pressed against the cage are fine because they are not grounded through boots into wet rock and they do not have hearts.

Also — and this is the softer point — do not knock it. It is the only argument for color in the place, and a few dozen pale stems have spent years bending toward it. Break it and you have ended the garden.

---

**Water**

Do not drink the drips. Whatever they are — condensate, leak, root-water — they have run over corroded iron and through decades of mineral bloom. You are carrying your own. Drink that.

Consider bringing a small vial if you want a sample; that is a reasonable thing to want. Consider also whether taking anything at all from a place that has so little in it is something you want to do.

---

**What you bring in on your boots**

The garden exists because "seeds, probably, carried in on the mud of boots" arrived here. You are, whether you like it or not, the next boot. Clean your soles before you enter — scrape, brush, wipe down. The chamber has one ecosystem and it is fragile in the specific way of things that have never been challenged. A single robust weed seed from your last world could outcompete the whole pale crowd in a season.

Do not touch the lunchbox. The "pale bloom" inside could be gypsum, sulfur, or a fungus that has had thirty years alone with a sandwich. None of those is something you want to disturb into the air you are breathing, and if the atmosphere is what the missing insects suggest, you already have enough in your lungs.

Do not touch the garden. Do not test whether the etiolated stems are as soft as they look. They are. They will break.

---

**Getting out**

This is the part that people underestimate, and it is the part that kills them in places like this.

The only exit is a slot in the ceiling, twenty to thirty meters up, through a curtain of hanging roots. There is a ladder bolted to the north wall that once reached something and now "meets only root" — meaning it is missing its top, or the thing it met is gone, or both. Its bolts have spent decades in hot saturated air in young rock. Do not use it. Do not use the roots either; a root that is tan and thickened and braided looks climbable and is not rated for anything. Do not use the pipes.

If you are arriving by conventional means, you are descending on a rope you have anchored above, in the forest, to something living and large, and you are ascending the same rope, and you have practiced ascending in heat with a full pack before you ever came here, because ascending twenty-five meters of rope in 38-degree saturated air is a serious physical event and you may have to do it while mildly hypoxic. A partner topside on the rope with a haul system is not paranoia.

If you arrive and leave by other means — as travelers between worlds sometimes can — then you still plan the rope, because the one certainty about a means of leaving is that it may not be there when you want it. Carry an emergency ascender and enough rope to reach the slot, and be honest with yourself about whether you can throw a line through a root curtain thirty meters overhead in the dark. You probably cannot. Which is why you arrive from above.

Set your exit time before you enter. When it comes, go. The place will still be there. It is very good at that.

---

**What it will be like**

You will arrive and the heat will be on you before you can see anything, like walking into a mouth. Your shirt will be wet within a minute. You will hear your own breath and nothing else, and then, after a while, a drip, and then, much later, another. The dark will feel total and then it will not; the lamp will assemble itself out of nothing, a dull orange coin at chest height, and you will find you have already drifted toward it without deciding to.

Around it you will see the garden, and you will feel something for it that is hard to name — the same feeling one has for a plant on a windowsill in a house that has been empty a long time. The stems are the color of old paper. They lean. Everything in the chamber leans, including you.

Above, the roots will not move. You will look up into them and your eyes will not find the slot, only a place where the dark is slightly less certain. Below, the rock will be warm through your boots, warm the way a living thing is warm, and you will be aware of your own weight on it in a way you are not usually aware.

Lay a gloved palm on a root and you will feel the hum. The narrator is right about that. It comes through the wood from the iron from the engine from somewhere you will never see, and it is the only thing in the chamber that is doing anything, and it has been doing it for decades without anyone.

You will want to stay. That is the specific danger of this place — not that it is hostile, but that it is not, that it is patient and quiet and still, and stillness is contagious, and the heat makes stillness feel like wisdom. Check your meter. Check your watch. Drink. When the time you set arrives, put your hand on your rope, and climb toward the forest, and leave the lamp to its small pale crowd.
