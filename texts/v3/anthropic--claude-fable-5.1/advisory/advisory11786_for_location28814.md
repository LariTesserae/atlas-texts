**Before anything else: understand what kind of place this is**

It is not hostile. That is the first thing to get straight, because your instincts will tell you otherwise within the first minute and you will spend the whole visit fighting them if you don't settle this now. The plateau does not want you dead. It does not want anything. It is a warm stone with wind over it, and the wind has no more intention than water going downhill. Nearly everything that will hurt you here will hurt you because you were standing in the wrong place when something indifferent happened — a gust came round, a shaft was under the green, the cloud closed. There is no malice to outwit. There is only attention, and the attention has to be constant, and constant attention is exhausting in a way that no single danger is.

So the preparation is less about equipment than about a posture. You are going to a place where nothing stays still except the ground, and the ground has holes in it. Prepare to be small, slow, low, and wet, for as long as you are there, and to be pushed around the entire time by something you cannot see or predict, and to hear nothing but it. If you can hold that in your mind as the baseline — not an emergency, just the weather — you will do all right.

**Arrival and the air**

If you have any control over where you come through, come through low. Come through on the slope, days below the top, and walk up. The tableland rises so gradually that the walk is not hard, and it will do for your blood what nothing else can: give it time. Whoever wrote that visitors' blood "goes wrong for a while" was being gentle. Arriving directly at the summit from sea level is the same as being dropped on a very high mountain with no acclimatisation, and you will spend the first day with a hammering headache, nausea, no appetite, a pulse you can feel in your teeth, and a fog over your thinking that makes you stupid exactly when you need not to be. Some people get worse than that — fluid in the lungs, fluid in the brain — and the only cure is descent, which on the plateau means days of walking through the wind while sick. Do not put yourself there. Two or three nights on the slope, each higher than the last, and you will arrive at the top merely breathless instead of ill.

If you cannot control your arrival point, bring the medication that helps with altitude and start it before you leave. Bring painkillers. Plan to do nothing on the first day but lie down, drink, and breathe. Lie down anyway; you will want to. Standing is hard here for other reasons.

Whichever way you arrive, the air is thin and will stay thin. You will move slowly. Everything takes twice as long. Sleep is poor and shallow at altitude even in perfect conditions, and these are not perfect conditions.

**The three things you are actually fighting**

It helps to name them separately, because they compound and you need to address each one.

*The wind.* Not the strength of it — though it is strong — but the fact that it has no direction and no rhythm. You cannot brace against it because it will come round behind you. You cannot shelter from it because there is no lee. You cannot time it because it changes mid-gust. What this means practically is that you can never let anything go. Not for a second. A hat, a glove, a map, a cup — if it leaves your hand or your body it is gone, lifted a hundred feet and dropped somewhere you will never find it. Everything you own must be attached to you or to something heavy, always. Tie cord to everything. Sew loops onto everything. Practise, before you go, the habit of never setting anything down.

The wind will also knock you over. Adults get knocked over here; children and the elderly live near the ground. When you walk, you walk bent, with a wide stance, and you stop and drop into a crouch when you feel a downdraft coming — you'll learn to see them as a ring of flattened green rushing toward you. Do not try to stand through a downdraft. Kneel and put your hands on the stone.

*The wet.* Everything is damp, always, and it is not the clean cold damp of a mountain stream but a warm, heavy, clinging damp that gets into wool and skin and stays. There is no dry place on the surface of the plateau. There is no fire to dry anything by. Your clothes will be wet from the first hour and remain wet until you leave. This is survivable — people live here — but it changes what you bring and how you care for your body. Skin softens and splits. Feet rot inside boots. The creases of the body chafe raw. Small cuts go bad. Ears, plugged all day against the noise, get infected. You must undress in shelter every night, dry yourself as best you can with a cloth you have kept in a sealed bag against your chest, examine your feet by touch, and let your skin breathe against the warm stone for as long as you can bear before dressing again. This is not optional. Travellers who skip it are limping by the third day.

*The noise.* This is the one that people underestimate most, because they imagine loud and think of a concert or a storm. It is not that. It is a pressure that lives in your chest and never releases, layered with thousands of shifting tones that never resolve, and it goes on without a single pause for the whole of your stay except for a few seconds once a day. It will damage your hearing permanently — the people who live here go deaf young — and no protection you bring will fully stop it, because a great deal of it is below the range of hearing and comes up through the soles of your feet. But you must protect what you can. Wear plugs and muffs together, always, even sleeping. And be ready for what the low sound does to the mind. Infrasound of this kind causes a formless dread, a sense that something is wrong or something is coming, a queasiness that is not quite nausea. This is a physical effect and not a perception. Know that before you go so that when you feel it — and you will feel it, in the first hour — you can name it and set it aside rather than believing it.

**Walking**

Walking on the plateau is the single most dangerous thing you will do, and you will do a great deal of it.

The mat is thick, springy, and continuous, and beneath it the stone is drilled with shafts of every width from a wrist to a house, worn smooth as glass, going down to depths nobody knows. The mat grows over the narrow ones completely. It grows partway across the wider ones and hangs there, a green lip over nothing. In flat cloud light the open shafts are simply dark spots, and the covered ones are nothing at all.

So: you never walk without a probe. A stiff pole as long as you are tall, driven into the mat ahead of each step, hard, before you commit your weight. Not tapped — driven. The mat is dense enough to give a false resistance to a light touch. You are testing whether there is stone beneath it, and the answer has to come back from the stone. This is slow. It is meant to be slow. A day's walk here covers what would be a morning's walk anywhere else, and that is correct.

You never walk in cloud. When the visibility drops to an arm's length — and it does, often, for hours — you stop where you are, crouch, and wait. This will feel unbearable, because the wind is pushing you and the sound is in your chest and every instinct says to move. You do not move. The people who live here do not move in cloud, and they know the ground.

You never walk alone. Two people minimum, and the second carries rope, and both of you have practised, before you came, what you will do if one goes into a shaft. Understand the problem clearly: the walls are smooth. There is nothing to grip, nothing to brace against, and the person who fell — if they are alive and not wedged — cannot climb out. The one on top cannot simply pull; a body is heavy and the rim is slick and wet. You need a haul system that gives you mechanical advantage, anchored to stone, and you need to be able to build it in a wind that is trying to take the rope from your hands, wearing gloves, without being able to hear your partner. Learn it. Rehearse it until it is stupid and automatic. And then decide, honestly, before you go, how far down you would go to retrieve someone, and tell each other.

Whether to walk roped together is a real question. A short rope means that if one of you drops through a covered shaft, the other might arrest the fall — or might be pulled in after. The people here do not rope. They walk single file, the leader probing, the follower stepping exactly in the leader's steps. I would do the same, and carry the rope for afterwards.

The narrow shafts are their own hazard. A wrist-wide hole under the mat will take your leg to the knee and break the ankle when the rest of you keeps moving. Probe. Always probe.

Look for the stone rings. A ring of stacked stones around a shaft mouth means people, present or past, and means that the shaft itself is a known thing and the ground around it has been walked. These are the only landmarks the plateau has. Learn where they are relative to each other. Nobody here has a map; they have the knowledge of which ring is a day's walk from which in which direction under which kind of wind, and if you can attach yourself to someone who has that knowledge, do.

Bring a compass and do not trust it until you have tested it. You do not know whether this world has a magnetic field or where it points. Bring a way of marking your trail — the mat tears and rolls away, so nothing left on the surface stays, but you can stack three stones on bare rock, and stones stay. The plateau is very poor in loose stones. Carry a few.

The horizon curves. You can see an enormous distance when the cloud lifts, and there is nothing in it. People find this either transcendent or unbearable and rarely know which they will be until they are standing in it.

**Shelter**

Nothing you bring for shelter will work on the surface. A tent will be gone before you have it staked. A tarp will be gone faster. A bivouac sack will fill with wind and drag you across the mat. Even a scrape in a basin gives you nothing, because the wind does not come from one side.

You have two options. The first is to do what the people do: go down. An abandoned shaft — a ring of stones half-swallowed by green, no rag on a pole, no sign of use — is a shelter already made. Ten feet down the wind is a roar overhead and nothing more. The walls are warm. Water drips. This is where you should sleep, and where you should spend every hour you are not moving.

But test the air first. The shafts breathe. Sometimes they breathe outward, warm and sulphurous, and what comes up from below is not always air you can live on. Heavy gases pool in the bottoms of holes. Carry a small candle or lamp — one of the few uses for a flame here, since nothing burns on the surface — and lower it on a cord before you go down. If it dims or goes out, the shaft is not yours. If it burns, go down slowly, and if your head aches or your breathing quickens beyond what altitude explains or the smell is strong, come up. Keep the candle burning beside you while you sleep; if it goes out in the night, you get out. The people here have lived with these shafts for generations and know which ones to trust. If you can be invited into an occupied one, that is safer than any abandoned one. But do not go into an occupied one uninvited. The ring of stones is a claim, and it is the only claim anyone here can make.

The second option, if you cannot go down, is to lie flat on bare stone in the shallowest basin you can find, with your body as low as it will go and every item you own lashed to you, and simply endure the night. The stone is warm and will keep you from dying of cold. The wind will not let you sleep. You will be there in the morning. This is a last resort.

A word about the warm stone, because it is the plateau's one gift and you should take it. It is warm enough to sleep against directly. It is warm enough to slowly heat food set on it in a covered pot, over hours. It is warm enough that if you strip your wet clothes and lay them flat on bare rock in a shaft where the wind cannot reach, they will be less wet by morning — never dry, but less. The people here have no fire and do not need it. Neither do you. Bring a stove if you want hot food quickly, and fuel, because there is nothing here that burns, but do not think of fire as survival. The stone is survival.

**Water, food, and the body**

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There is no stream, no pool, no place to dip a bottle. There is the drip. Set a wide vessel at the bottom of your shelter shaft when you arrive and it will be full by morning. Wring the mat over a cup and it will give you a mouthful of slightly green water that tastes of felt. Hold a cloth up in cloud and squeeze it. This is condensation, and it is clean, or as clean as the stone it ran over. I would not bother filtering it. I would bother collecting far more than I thought I needed, because the altitude and the wind dry you out faster than you notice, and because the vessel you set out to collect it must be weighted or wedged or it will be gone.

Food: bring all of it. The sour fruits in the mat are edible and filling and the people live on them, but you will not know which are ripe or how to prepare them and the taste is a punishment until you're used to it. There is nothing to hunt that you will find. Bring dense food that does not care about being wet — fats, dried meat, hard bread, nuts, honey. Salt; you will sweat more than you think under the wind. Bring more than you need, because everything here takes longer than you planned.

Bring, for the body: antifungal powder and cream, in quantity. A barrier ointment for chafing. A great deal of foot care — you will have to fight for your feet every single night. Ear drops. A saline wash. Blister supplies. Splinting material for the ankle you or your companion will turn in a covered shaft. Something for motion sickness — the constant push of wind on a springing surface, with the cloud tearing overhead, produces a seasick feeling in many people, and the low sound makes it worse. Something to help you sleep, because you will not sleep well.

Bring goggles. The wind carries grit, water, and torn stalks of runner at speed, and it lifts them and drops them from a hundred feet. An eye injury here, days from anywhere, in cloud, is a disaster.

**What to wear**

Everything close-fitting. Nothing that flaps, nothing that can be gripped by wind. A hood that seals around the face. Layers that keep some warmth when soaked — wool, or synthetics made for wet cold; nothing that collapses when wet, nothing filled with down. Waterproof outer layers are worth less here than you would think, because the wet comes from the air itself and from the ground and from your own sweat trapped inside, and after the first day you will be wet under the shell regardless. Wear the shell for the wind, not the water.

Footwear: soft, grippy soles, because you are walking on wet vegetation over smooth stone and hard soles will slide. Ankle support, for the covered shafts. Gaiters sealed tight to keep the mat's wet out of the boot as long as possible. Accept that the boots will be wet from the first morning and never dry. Bring a second pair of insoles and swap them nightly, laying the wet pair on warm stone.

Gloves. The mat is tough and the stone is rough and you will be pulling yourself along and probing constantly. Gloves with a good grip when wet, and a spare pair, and both pairs on cords.

Hearing protection, as said, worn always. Bring spares of the plugs; they get lost, they get foul.

And tie everything on. I keep returning to this because it is the thing travellers fail at. Sew a loop onto everything. Run a cord from every object to your belt or harness. Practise, in your own world, for a week beforehand, never setting anything down, and see how hard it is.

**The people**

There are a few hundred of them across a vastness that takes days to cross, in groups of a dozen or two, in the warm dark under the stone rings. They are deaf, most of them. They speak with their hands and with a shouting that is felt in the throat and chest rather than heard. They have stone and bone and fibre and nothing else. They have never seen metal.

You will not learn their hand language before you arrive; there is nowhere to learn it. What you can bring is patience, and the willingness to be slow and clear and to repeat yourself, and the understanding that pointing and showing will get you most of what you need. They are used to communicating without sound. You will be the one at a disadvantage, not them.

Approach a stone ring slowly and from a distance where you can be seen, and then stop, and wait. Sit down. Sitting is the natural posture here — everyone sits, out of the wind — and it is not threatening. If someone comes up, let them look at you. If no one comes, do not go down. Move on.

If you are taken in, you will be given a place on the platform in the dark, and the hum of the shaft will come up through your spine all night, and it will be the safest you are on the whole plateau. Give something back. Think carefully about what. A steel knife would change a person's life here — it would change a group's life — and you should think about whether that is a thing you want to do, in a place where nothing has been mined and the tools have been stone for as long as anyone remembers. I would not bring metal tools as gifts. I would bring things that are useful and do not remake the world: strong cordage, good cloth, a mirror, needles of bone or shell, salt, fat, dried fruit that is not sour.
