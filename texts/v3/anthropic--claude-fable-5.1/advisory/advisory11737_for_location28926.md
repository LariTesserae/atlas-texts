**Before anything else: understand what kind of danger this is**

This is not a hostile place. Nothing here hunts, poisons, collapses, or floods. The danger is subtler and, for an ordinary body, quite real: it is a place with no relief in it. No shade, no evening, no cool ground, no wind off water, no night to sleep through, no way of telling how long you have been there except the state of your own body. Everything that would normally rescue a tired traveler — the sun going down, a breeze, a dark corner — has been removed, not by malice but by completion. You will be the only thing in the basin that gets worse. Prepare for that specifically.

---

**The descent and, more importantly, the ascent**

The lip is rounded, smooth, warm, and uncracked. Read that as a climber would: there is nothing to anchor to. No crack for a cam, no flake for a sling, no rough patch for friction. The thumb-scoops are shallow — pressed, not cut — and they spiral, so you will be moving diagonally across a face with no other holds and no fall protection the rock itself can offer.

The solution is behind you, not in front. The plain above is ordinary, and ordinary ground takes anchors. Before you go over the edge, bury a deadman or drive stakes well back from the lip in the plain's dust, and fix a line. Bring far more rope than seems reasonable; you cannot see the far wall clearly, which means the near wall is tall, and you will not know its height until you are hanging from it. The stone is glass-smooth, so the rope will not abrade — but it will slide, so knot it for hand-holds or bring a descender and ascender.

Downclimb facing the rock, feet in the scoops, one hand on the fixed line. Thin gloves: the stone is warm, not hot, but sweaty palms on smooth warm rock are the real problem. Shoes with a soft, grippy sole rather than deep lugs — you want rubber in contact with a shallow rounded depression, not an edge that isn't there.

Then remember: you will climb back up tired, thirsty, and half-dazzled, at the end of a day that had no end to mark it. Whatever strength the descent takes from you, budget twice that for the return. Set your turnaround by the state of your water and your legs, not by the light — the light will tell you nothing.

Before you leave the lip, look down and fix where the scoops meet the mat. Note the nearest structure, the nearest swell, the shape of the wall around it. From below, that spiral will be the only mark on a pale wall in a basin whose size you do not know, with no shadows to give the rock relief. Losing the exit is the most plausible way to die here.

---

**Heat that does not build and does not break**

The heat comes from above and from below, and it holds. There is no cooler hour to travel in, no cold ground to lie against, no reason for your core temperature to ever come down while you are inside. Sitting is not rest; the stems warm you through your clothes. Treat the entire visit as the hottest part of a desert afternoon that has decided not to move.

Water: carry in more than you think you need — plan on five or six liters per person per waking day, with electrolytes, and assume you cannot cool it. The basin's water is almost certainly clean; it has had nothing happen to it, and that includes contamination. But it is blood-warm, and blood-warm water is hard to drink in quantity and does nothing to cool you. Filter or test it before trusting it, use it to extend your own supply rather than replace it, and take it from the wider channels rather than the small seams, where a single bead may be that seam's entire portion. Whether the seams refill is not something you want to be the one to discover.

Do not enter the pools. They are exactly your temperature, which sounds pleasant and is precisely the problem — the water will not draw heat from you. Their floor is stem, their depth unknown, and the one place in the whole account where anything moved was a stem-tip curling underwater. Walk around, as the first visitor did.

Know the early signs of heat exhaustion in yourself — headache, irritability, the moment you stop sweating — and act on them by leaving, because there is nothing in the basin that will bring you back down.

---

**Light from everywhere**

The walls are nearly white and the sky is clear and nothing casts a shadow. This is a snowfield with the sun on it, and it will do what snowfields do: burn the underside of your chin, your nostrils, the inside of your ears, the backs of your knees, and, if you let it, your corneas.

Glacier-grade sunglasses with side shields, and a spare pair, because if you lose the first you will be functionally blind within hours. A brimmed hat helps less than usual, since half the light comes up from the mat and off the walls; wear it anyway, and add a buff or scarf over the lower face. Long sleeves, long trousers, loose and light-colored, closed at the wrists and ankles. Sunscreen on skin that never normally needs it. If you sleep, you will need an eye mask, because the light will not dim for you.

Expect your eyes to stop fighting the glare after a while and simply accept it. That acceptance is not adaptation. The damage continues whether or not you are squinting.

---

**Walking on the plant**

The mat gives and then holds, and your foot never finds a gap — but your arm pushed down goes to the shoulder, which means a foot driven hard enough could follow it. Do not run. Do not jump from the swells. Place your feet, keep your weight over them, and let the stems settle before you commit. The upper surfaces are described as waxy, and every seam holds water: this is a field of wrist-thick, lightly greased tubes. Ankle support is worth its weight. A trekking pole with a broad basket, rather than a point, gives you a third contact that will not slide between the stems.

A leg that does go down between stems will find it warmer the deeper it goes and the stems closing gently around it. Carry a partner if you can. If you cannot, carry the discipline to move slowly.

The swells are the only high ground. Use them to see, to reorient toward the wall, to find your spiral again. Note that they run roughly parallel; travelling along one is easy and crossing between them means dropping into the channels.

---

**Time**

The sun does not move. There is no evening. The bead of water you looked at an hour ago is the same bead. Your body will lose the thread of the day within a few hours, and once it does you will underestimate how long you have been in there by a wide margin, because nothing around you has aged.

Bring a watch you trust and set alarms — to drink, to eat, to turn around. Decide your maximum stay before you go over the lip and write it down. Do not revise it from inside. The place is quiet, warm, bright, and asks nothing of you, and it will feel like rest in a way that has nothing to do with your body actually resting. That is the specific trap: it is a held breath, and you are not. You will still need to exhale.

If you must sleep, do it on a groundsheet, not directly on the stems, with an eye mask, near your exit, with an alarm. You will not sleep well. Plan the visit as a long day rather than an overnight, if you can.

---

**The structures, and what you owe them**

The stones are unmortared and simply placed. You could probably move one. Do not. Not out of superstition — the place wants nothing and will likely not punish you — but because in a basin where nothing has yet happened, moving a stone would be the first thing to happen, and you have no idea what the second thing would be. The hut is full of stem to its sill; there is nothing inside to enter. The line of uprights leads nowhere; walking it will not take you anywhere either.

Treat the whole basin as you would treat a wet painting. Touch, look, sit if you must, take nothing, leave nothing. This is harder than it sounds, because you are made of things that fall off. Sweat drips into seams. Hair, skin, crumbs, the dust from the ordinary plain in the treads of your boots — you will be the first litter this place has ever received. Clean your boots at the lip before you descend. Pack out everything, including your own waste, in sealed bags; there is no soil to bury anything in and no decay to take it. Eat over a cloth. Carry nothing alive, not even a seed in a pocket, and check yourself for stowaways on the way out — that one slow beetle did not need company.

Do not make fire. Do not cut a stem to see what is inside. Do not test whether the rock can be scratched. Every one of those questions has an answer, and the answer is a change, and the change is yours to carry.

---

**Your mind**

The quiet is total enough that you will hear your own blood, and under it a low tone you feel more than hear. There are no rustles, no insects, no wind at the bottom. Some people find this restful for an hour and unbearable after three. Bring something ordinary and human — a task, a companion, a small routine — to hold onto. Speak aloud occasionally; your voice will not echo and that, too, is strange.

Expect a specific loneliness that is not fear. You will be the only gradient: the only thing thirstier, hotter, further along. The place is not indifferent to you exactly; it is simply finished, and you are not, and you will feel that difference as a kind of mild, persistent exclusion. It is not a judgment. Do not take it as one, and do not try to earn your way in by staying longer.

---

**Leaving**

Go while you still have a full liter, working eyes, and legs that can trust a shallow scoop in smooth rock. Climb facing in, hand on your fixed line, and do not look back down until you are on the ordinary plain.

Then look. It will be exactly as it was. The wind on the plain will seem loud. The dust will seem like a miracle. Drink, sit in a real shadow, and give your eyes an hour in the dimness they have forgotten.

You have changed by the length of a day. That is the whole point of having gone; do not resent it.
