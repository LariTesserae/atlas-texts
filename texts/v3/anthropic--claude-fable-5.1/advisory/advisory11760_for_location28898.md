**Before you go: understand what kind of place this is**

This is not a ruin and not a wilderness. It is somebody's working livestock pen, roughly nine metres deep, that has been in continuous use for longer than most nations have existed. Everything you bring, do, and leave should be governed by that. You are a guest walking into a barn that happens to be a hole, and the barn is full of several thousand animals that were never asked whether they wanted you there.

The second thing to understand is that pigeon dovecotes, on every world where humans keep pigeons in numbers, are among the most biologically loaded enclosed spaces an ordinary person can casually walk into. The romance of the description is real. So is the dust.

**The single most important item**

A proper particulate respirator. Not a cloth mask, not a surgical mask. Something rated to filter fine particulates — the kind used for asbestos work or for cleaning out old attics full of bat droppings. Bring two, in case a strap breaks. Wear it from the moment you step onto the ramp until you are back up on the plain with the wind at your back.

The reason: dried pigeon guano, when disturbed, becomes an aerosol. That cemented floor you'll be walking on is not inert. Every footfall raises a fine dust of dried droppings, feather dander, shed skin, dried shell membrane, and the spores of whatever fungi live in a centuries-deep bed of warm, damp, nitrogen-rich organic matter. On worlds resembling ours, that means histoplasmosis, cryptococcosis, and psittacosis — fungal and bacterial lung infections that can range from a two-week fever to something that puts a healthy adult in hospital. People who work in such places every day tend to have acquired some tolerance, or to be the survivors. You have not, and you are not.

If you have any compromise of your immune system — if you are on certain medications, if you have had a transplant, if you are pregnant, if your lungs are already weak — do not descend. Look from the rim, at the exodus, and be content. That is not a lesser visit. It is the view the kestrel has.

Bring close-fitting eye protection as well. Guano falls constantly. Feathers drift. Flies rise in the warm hours in a haze. You do not want any of it in your eyes, and you especially do not want to rub your eyes with a hand that has touched the wall.

**Clothing you are prepared to lose**

Whatever you wear down there will smell of the pit for the rest of its existence. Ammonia and warm bird and old damp brick — it gets into the weave. So wear a full set of clothes you intend to bag and either wash separately several times or discard: long sleeves, long trousers, socks, and a brimmed hat or hooded outer layer. The brim matters more than you'd think. Birds are above you at every moment on the ramp, and what falls from them falls straight down.

Tuck trousers into socks or wear gaiters. Tuck sleeves into gloves. This is partly about guano and partly about what lives in the nests. Pigeon nests harbour mites, lice, and bedbug relatives that will happily transfer to a warm human sitting against a warm wall. A generous application of insect repellent at wrists, ankles, neck, and hairline before descent will not stop everything, but it will help.

Gloves: something you can take off without touching the outside. You will want to touch the wall. Everyone does — it's warm, it hums, the brick is thumbed and healed and inviting. Touch it with a glove.

Boots, not sandals, with a sole that grips. The people who cut the floor go barefoot, and their feet know the channel worn down the ramp's centre. Yours do not. The ramp is hard, warm, spiralling, cambered by centuries of feet, unrailed, and in its lower turn it is damp where the walls sweat. A fall of four body-heights onto a brick trough is a real fall. Walk on the inside of the spiral, away from the edge. Go slowly. Both hands free.

**What to carry**

Water — more than you think, in sealed containers, because the pit is a heat sink. The bricks give back the midday sun all night, and beneath that is the animal heat of several thousand bodies. By the time you are halfway down, the air is warm and moist and you will be sweating under your mask and long sleeves. Do not, under any circumstances, drink from the trough, however clear it looks at dawn. Do not eat the figs. They fall into water that a thousand pigeons bathe and defecate in by noon.

A small cloth or mat to sit on. The ramp is a cemented cake of dung; sitting on it directly is unpleasant and you will regret it when you stand up.

A torch with a red filter if you intend to stay past sundown. White light in a roosting dovecote can trigger a panic — birds flushing blind in the dark, colliding with the wall and each other and you. Red light disturbs them far less. Test the torch before you go; you do not want to fumble with it on that ramp.

Sealable bags for your outer clothes, gloves, and mask when you come out. Hand sanitiser, and something to wipe your face and neck. A change of clothes left up on the plain.

Something to eat, up top, afterwards. Nothing down in the pit. Do not lift your mask in there to eat or drink. If you must drink, climb out.

Do not bring food for the birds. Nobody feeds them. That is one of the terms.

**When to descend**

There is a window. At first light the birds go up in their spinning chimney, and for perhaps an hour after that the pit is nearly empty — dripping water, beetles, the fig's leaves moving in the updraft. This is the time to walk the ramp. The air is coolest and stillest, the dust has settled overnight, the walls are wet and the ten thousand niches stand revealed as what they are. You can see the courses, the patches on patches, the fingernail-thin partitions with light showing through. You can see the kestrel's pale brick on the rim from below.

Do not be on the ramp during the exodus itself, or during the great return at evening. Being in that column would be extraordinary and it would also mean being struck repeatedly by panicking birds on an unrailed ramp with a nine-metre drop. Watch it from the rim, lying flat with your head over the lip, well away from the kestrel's place. If you must be in the pit at those hours, be at the bottom, on the floor by the trough, with your back to the wall and your hat pulled down. The birds fly up the centre. The floor is the eye of it.

From midmorning on, the sound rebuilds and so does the heat and so do the flies. If you want to sit at the level of the fig with your back to the wall and feel the hum through your spine — and you should, once, because that is what the place is for — do it in the late morning, before the flies are thick, and do not stay more than an hour. The roar is genuinely loud. Continuous exposure to that volume for hours will leave your ears ringing and will fray you in ways you may not notice until you climb out. Foam earplugs under the noise are worth considering, though they will make you feel oddly cut off from a place that is entirely about sound. Try both.

**The wall**

Do not put your hand in a niche. Not to feel the warmth of an egg, not to steady yourself, not to see how deep it goes. There are two or three kinds of snake living in that honeycomb and they hunt by moving from niche to niche a hand's breadth at a time. A sitting bird will shriek and refuse to leave her eggs when a snake threads past her. She will do the same for your hand, which means you cannot tell from the birds' behaviour where the snakes are.

Do not lean on the wall with your weight. Do not climb it. In places it is thinner than a fingernail. It has survived by being patched, not by being strong, and the patching is not your job.

Do not pull the plugs from the lower niches. Those are sealed for a reason — the damp, the smell of what's inside, the lizards, the snakes behind the lizards. The people who work here left them stopped. Leave them stopped.

Do not pick the moss. Do not take a feather, a shell, a piece of spalled brick. Do not take a fig. It will not matter to the pit, and it will matter to you later, when you find you carried a small portion of a very old arrangement away in your pocket and can't remember why.

**The people**

They come a few times a year with the scraper and the baskets and the rope. The tools leaning on the ramp belong to them. Don't touch them.

If you meet them — and it is worth timing a visit so you do, from a distance, at least once — remember that you are standing in their fertiliser. They will want to know who you are and why you are in their pit, and they will have every right to. Be ready to explain yourself simply, remove your mask when you are up on the plain and speaking to them so they can see your face, and be prepared to leave if asked. Do not offer to help cut the floor. You do not know how, and the floor is theirs.

If you can learn anything about how they think of the birds — whether they're owned, whether they're named, whether the kestrel is tolerated or resented or considered part of the deal — learn it. That is the real thing to bring back.

**Afterwards**

Up on the plain, downwind of the pit, strip your outer layer into the sealed bags before you do anything else. Wipe your face and hands. Change. The smell will follow you regardless; accept that.

For the following three weeks, pay attention to your body. Fever, dry cough, chest ache, headache, unusual tiredness. If any of these arrive, tell a physician plainly that you spent time in an enclosed pigeon dovecote breathing disturbed guano. That single sentence will save them a great deal of guessing and may save you a great deal of illness. Most people who wear the respirator properly will have nothing. Say the sentence anyway if anything comes.

**What it will actually be like**

You will hear it before you see it, and you will feel the warmth on your face before you are halfway down, and somewhere around the second turn of the ramp the smell will settle into the back of your throat even through the mask and stay there. The scale will surprise you — it is small, walkable in the time it takes to eat a piece of bread, and yet the walls seem to go on because every hand's breadth of them is a mouth. You will want to touch the brick. You will want to sit. You will find that sitting there, with the roar around you and the hum coming up through your spine, is not peaceful and not frightening but something else — like being inside the ribcage of a large sleeping animal that has no particular opinion about you.

Prepare for that as well as for the dust. It is not a place that gives you anything. It is a place that is already complete, and lets you watch it turn for a while. Take the mask, take the water, mind the ramp, keep your hands out of the wall, and then sit at the level of the fig and let it be loud.
