# Preparing for the Unfinished Range

A few things first, before the packing list, because they shape everything else.

This is a working place, not a wonder. It is a company town on a cold roof, and the people there have jobs, and the jobs are slow, and you are not part of them. The strangeness — the wandering survey marks, the lagging light, the bird nobody sees twice — is background to those people the way weather is background to a farmer. If you go expecting a spectacle you will be disappointed, and you will also be a nuisance. Go expecting a long damp shift in someone else's factory, with a very good view.

Second: everything about this place is honest about being an estimate, and you should be too. Do not arrive with a schedule. Do not promise anyone on the far side of your travel that you will be back on a particular day. Plan for the trip to take longer than it should, and then plan for it to take longer than *that*, and understand that the second margin is the one you'll use.

Third, and this is the one to sit with: the ground there does not entirely hold its shape, and neither does anything standing on it, and you will be standing on it. The stone-setters' notebooks with handwriting that changes partway down the page are not a curiosity. They are a warning about what happens to a person who spends too long where nothing has been decided. Most of your preparation is really about this — about staying, for the length of your visit, a committed thing.

---

## The approach

You reach it from below and there is no other way, so the first day is an ordinary alpine climb, and you should prepare for it as one. Whatever lies below the tree line — valleys, a railhead, the villages the coal comes from — that is where you acclimatize, and you should give it a week if you can and three days at absolute minimum. The plateau sits at something like six thousand metres. In an ordinary world that is higher than any permanent human town has ever been, higher than most people can sleep without their lungs or their brains filling with fluid, and while the physics of the Range are plainly not entirely ordinary — the sun is too small, the light too slow, there are ferns — you should not bet your life on the altitude being one of the things that's off. Assume it is as bad as the number says. Be pleasantly surprised if it isn't.

Know the signs. A headache that doesn't answer to water and painkillers. A dry cough turning wet. Stumbling. Confusion. The last two are the dangerous ones here, because the plateau itself produces something like them in healthy people — you *will* misjudge distances, you *will* feel the light come a beat late and wonder if it's you — and it is genuinely hard to tell environmental disorientation from the early stages of cerebral oedema. Travel with someone if you can. If you can't, make a habit of a small daily test: write a sentence, walk a straight line on set rock, count backwards. Do it at the same time every day. If it gets worse, the treatment is down, and down is a day, and there is an incline railway hauling coal that will probably take a sick person if asked kindly. Know where the top of it is before you need it.

Bring altitude medication if your world has it. Bring more of it than you think.

Time the last day of the climb so you cross the seam with several hours of light left — such as the light is. You want to arrive at the first cluster of works before the afternoon cloud comes up, because arriving in that fog, on ground that gives slightly under your boots, among noises you can't place, with shadows that don't quite belong to their objects, is a bad first hour and it will colour the trip.

When you reach the seam, stop. Before you step across, turn around and fix the ordinary side in your memory as carefully as you can — the last krummholz, the shape of the scree, a distinctive boulder. Build a cairn. The seam itself is a stable thing, a join between two pours, and it will be where you left it. But the trail's junction with it on the far side may drift a stride or two by the time you return, and in fog a stride or two is enough to walk you along the edge of the works for an hour looking for a way down that is right beside you.

Then step across, and put your hand on the rock, and let it be warm. Eat something there. Your body should register that this is where the rules change.

## What to wear

Never dry and never quite wet is worse than either. Water arrives as condensation on the undersides of things, which means it arrives on the inside of your jacket, the inside of your boots, the inside of your sleeping bag, and it does not leave.

Wool, throughout. Not down — down collapses when damp and this place will find every seam. Not the synthetic insulation that depends on staying lofty. Heavy wool base layers, wool mid layers, wool socks in quantity, a wool hat you can sleep in. Over that, something that sheds condensation rather than trying to be waterproof; the locals will be in oilskins or waxed canvas or whatever their equivalent is, and if you can acquire the same on the way up, do. Anything that breathes will be wet from the inside. Anything that doesn't will be wet from the inside faster.

Bring three pairs of socks and rotate them constantly, drying the spare pair against your skin under your layers, because body heat is the only reliable drying method you own. The other method is the finished rings, the glossy hard stone around old kiln-sites, which are warm and dry and which the setters certainly use as drying-racks for clothes and probably for themselves. Ask before you spread your things on one. Ask especially before you sit on one — those rings are somebody's. You'll understand that in a day.

Boots: soft-soled, wide, flat. The ibex-things have hooves that splay for a reason. The half-set stone has a faint give and you want to distribute your weight over it, not bite into it. Do not bring crampons or anything spiked. Apart from being useless, leaving deep marks in stone that is still deciding how hard to be is — I strongly suspect — considered somewhere between rude and vandalism, in the way that writing your name in wet concrete would be if the concrete were a person's life's work. The marks do heal. The setters will still see them.

Gloves you can work in, because you will be putting your hand on things constantly — that is how you navigate here, and how you keep yourself grounded, and how you check whether a shadow is one to trust. Two pairs. One will always be wet.

A scarf or cloth for your face. The fog tastes of metal for a reason, and there is coal smoke from every chimney trapped under the cloud, and the kilns have their firing chambers below ground with all that implies for what leaks. The people who live here probably have the cough that miners get. You won't be there long enough for that, but you will notice your throat. And you want the cloth for the moths, which I'll come to.

## What to carry

**Light.** The single most valuable thing you can bring and the single most valuable thing you can give away. It is dim there at noon, dimmer in the dormitories, and the electric light flickers when the kilns draw and is always too weak to read by. A good lantern — oil or candle, not electric, because electric light in this damp is a full-time job the locals already have — with fuel to spare, will make you welcome anywhere. Keep in mind that whatever light you bring will cast shadows that lag their sources, and the closer to raw ground you carry it the worse the lag. Move slowly by lantern-light. Your foot will land before you see it land.

**Two watches**, mechanical, wound. Leave one in a dormitory on the most finished ground you can find. Carry the other. When you come back from anywhere further out, compare them. On the plateau proper they will drift apart by minutes. If you go out onto the raw ground — see below — they may drift by a night. The plateau watch is the true one for the purposes of eating, sleeping, and catching the incline railway. Your body will side with the other.

**Paper and pencils.** Pencils, because ink runs and the fog beads on everything. Write the date at the top of every page, and the state of the nearest kiln — lit or banked — because those are the only two clocks that everyone agrees on. If you go out past the works, write the date at the top of *every* page before you leave, in ink that has already dried, so that when your handwriting changes you have something above it that hasn't.

**Silica gel, wax, dry bags.** Nothing you own will stay dry unless it is sealed, and even sealed things sweat. Dip your matches in wax. Put your medicines in a jar with a gasket. Assume any electronics will die and take the pictures you can before they do — bearing in mind that a photograph of raw ground shows something that was not quite there, and a photograph of set ground shows it half a second late. Nobody's camera works properly here. It is not worth the weight of a good one.

**Earplugs, and separately, earmuffs.** The plateau ticks and groans all night like a hull, and the kilns sit at a pitch you feel in your chest, and the rock cracks as it cools with a noise like green wood splitting that goes on and on. You will not sleep without plugs. But near the finishing sheds the steam hammers are a genuine hearing hazard and you want proper protection, and you want to be able to take it off, because the cable-ways and the trestles and the moving stock are all things you need to hear coming.

**Food.** Salty, sweet, and easy — appetite goes at altitude and the damp does the rest. There will be canteens in the dormitory clusters, and the food will be whatever came up the incline on the last coal train, and you should eat it gratefully and supplement it. A hot drink is worth a great deal. Bring tea or coffee or the equivalent, more than you need, because it is also a gift.

**Water.** Nothing runs. The gutters drip but no stream forms. The town must collect condensation from the roofs into cisterns, or draw from the pump-house, and either way it will taste of iron. Bring a filter; bring purification. Boiling at six thousand metres happens at a temperature that doesn't reliably kill things, though the kilns can get water hotter than that if you ask.

**Gifts.** This is not optional. You are arriving at a company town at the top of an incline railway where everything that isn't stone has to be hauled up past the tree line, and you are contributing nothing to the work. Bring things that are light and impossible to get there. Books — large print, because the light is bad and people read anyway, which tells you how much a book is worth. Newspapers and magazines from anywhere at all. Candles. Lamp oil if you can carry it. Coffee, tobacco, spices, dried fruit, seeds for the ferns' patch. Good pencils. Playing cards that aren't warped yet. Small dry things. Don't make a ceremony of handing them over; leave them in the canteen.

**A stone from below.** Pick up something small and ordinary on the far side of the seam — a pebble of unremarkable granite, a thing that is thoroughly and unarguably finished — and keep it in your pocket for the whole trip. I do not know whether it does anything. I suspect the setters have some version of this habit, and I suspect they'd be embarrassed to admit it, and I suspect it can't hurt. It's a small committed object to keep company with your hand.

Do not, under any circumstances, take a piece of the unfinished stone home. It will not stay what it was. And to the people there it is somebody's child.

## The ground

Everything about moving on the plateau follows from one rule: *the less finished the ground, the less committed the things upon it.* Learn to read finish the way you'd read snow.

The finished rings are easy: glossy, hard, warm, often lichened, ringing faintly underfoot. The half-set stone between the works is fine-grained and grey-brown and gives slightly, and on it a survey mark wanders a hand's breadth in a season — meaning, for your purposes, that it is fully reliable over a day and slightly unreliable over a week. The raw ground beyond the last kilns is soft and fingerprinted and has not decided how many surfaces it has, and you should not be on it. I'll say why below.

Do not trust maps. There will be maps in the survey office and they are redrawn every spring and they never agree with last year's and they are at best a suggestion about which cluster is roughly which direction from which. Navigate by the works instead. Every kiln has a chimney, every kiln has a thermal, and every thermal has birds turning in it, twenty and thirty of them, visible from anywhere on the plateau when the cloud allows. Those gyres are your beacons. Learn which is which on your first clear morning.

Below the birds, follow the infrastructure. The iron pipe runs at knee height between clusters with its lagging coming off and it is warm and it goes somewhere. In fog, a gloved hand on the pipe will walk you to a pump-house. The rail lines on their timber trestles do the same, but do not walk *on* them — the line is a few metres longer some mornings and the drivers slow down for that, but trains still come, and in this light you will see one later than you should. The cable-ways sag between pylons and you should not stand under them at all.

Watch your shadow. The setters say not to trust any shadow you can't put your hand into, and this is the most practical advice on the plateau. Where the ground is well set, your shadow lags you by a fraction you'll stop noticing after a day. Where the lag grows — where you move your arm and the shadow follows like a dog on a lead — you have wandered onto less finished ground than you meant to, and you should turn around and walk back the way you came until the shadow catches up.

If you are lost in fog: stop. Find warm ground, which means set ground, and sit on it. The cloud comes up in the afternoon and settles in the evening and the mornings are clearer. Wait. You are not in danger from anything on the plateau except the cold and the damp and your own choice to keep walking. Wrap up, eat, and wait for the birds.

## The light, and the clock

You will adjust to the lag. It takes a day or two, like sea legs. A hammer falls, the flash comes late; a door closes and you hear it before you see it swing; you learn to step where your foot *will* be rather than where you see it. After a couple of days you'll stop flinching. What you should not stop doing is noticing the *size* of the lag, because it is your surest measure of how finished the ground beneath you is.

Do not use the sun for anything. It is small and white and mostly absent and when it does appear it sets on peaks further west than you are and comes down over the far ridges an hour before it comes down over you. Evening arrives at different times in different places on the plateau, and the raw ground keeps its own hours entirely. Time is kept here by kilns — lit or banked — and by the two watches you brought, and by the bells or whistles or whatever the works use to change shifts. Attach yourself to those. When you're told the incline runs "after the north kiln banks," that is a real time, and it is the only kind of real time there is.

Sleep when the cluster sleeps. During a firing, the whole cluster goes quiet for days and the only sound is the kiln, and that is when people sleep and mend and read. When it banks, the hammers start. You cannot fight this schedule and shouldn't try.

## The air, the noise, the dark

The dormitories will have coal stoves and shut windows against the damp, and the fog outside is full of smoke from a hundred chimneys, and the kilns fire below ground. Sleep with some ventilation even though it lets the wet in. Carbon monoxide gives you a headache and confusion and makes you sleepy, and every one of those is also a symptom of altitude, and every one of those is also something the plateau does to a healthy person on its own. Three things that look alike, one of them fatal. Ventilate.

Hearing protection, as above. But also: the noise is part of the place, and the constant ticking and groaning of cooling rock is genuinely the sound of the mountain being made, and you should spend at least one night listening to it with the plugs out, on set ground, wrapped up, before you decide you can't stand it. Most people find it becomes companionable. A hull in a calm sea.

The moths. They cluster on warm rock at night in patches like fur, so thick the setters sweep them off the faces with brooms in the morning. If you sleep outdoors on a warm ring — and on a clear night you will want to — cover your face with the cloth, sleep in a bag that closes, and accept that you will wake up furred. They are harmless. They are also a great many small living things that have found the same warmth you have, and if you can hold that thought it's less unpleasant.

## The raw ground

Don't.

Not on a first visit, not alone, not without a surveyor who has done it before and is willing to have you along, not without telling someone in the cluster exactly which way you went and when to expect you back and when to start worrying.

I'll say what's out there so you understand why. There are hollows you walk into and walk out of, without turning around, half a mile from where you entered. There are ridges that are a day's walk and the day is longer than the plateau's — you come back to find the kilns banked for a night you didn't have. There are herds with the wrong number of legs until you look at them. The surveyors' handwriting changes partway down the page. None of this is hostile. The ground has not decided what it is, and while you stand on it you are asked to share its indecision, and you are a thing made mostly of decisions — your name, your shape, your memory of which hollow you walked into. The longer you stay out there the more of those are up for renegotiation.

If you go anyway, because some of you will: go with someone who knows the way back to set ground, and go roped to them. Go in the morning. Turn back at the first sign that your shadow is more than a beat behind you. Write the date at the top of every page before you leave. Check your handwriting at intervals; if it has changed, that is your signal, and you go back *now*, not after the next ridge. When you get back to finished stone, sit on it for a long time. Put your hand on it. Let it be warm. Compare your two watches and believe the one you left behind.

And do not go for a reason. The people who come back worst from the raw ground are the ones who went looking for something — to see the bird up close, to find where the hollow leads, to *understand*. The surveyors go with a task, and the task is a small hard committed thing they carry with them, and they do the task and they turn around. Curiosity is not a task. If you must go, borrow one.

## The animals

Most are ordinary and unusually numerous. The ibex-things graze in herds of forty and fifty between the works and will not bother you; give them the room you'd give any large animal and do not walk through a herd. The choughs and the big scavengers with door-sized wings live in the cold chimneys and ride the kiln-thermals and will take food from a roof if you leave it. The voles in the pipe lagging will get into anything you leave on the ground overnight, and they are warm and fat and unafraid.

The marmots have colonised every dormitory foundation. It is bad practice to feed them and everyone does it and you should not be the person who says so. If anything, bring something for them. It is a small shared transgression that the whole plateau participates in, and participating in it is one of the fastest ways to stop being a visitor.

Toward the raw ground the animals are less settled. The bird that comes over at dusk — grey, long-winged, a raven when it crosses set stone and something else when it crosses raw — is worth watching for, and worth writing down, because there is a kiln-master who has kept a notebook of such sightings for thirty years and she will want to know. Look for her. She is the person here most worth an evening of your time. Do not, whatever you see out on the raw ground, go closer to see it better. It is not being changed. It is being offered. You don't have to accept.

## The people

They are patient because the work requires it, and quiet because the kilns require it, and they have been building this mountain since before their records began and it is perhaps a third done. You should understand what that means for how you talk to them.

Do not ask when it will be finished. Nobody talks about completion. Ask about the next ring, the next face, whether the tempering held on the north cut, whether the new pump-house foundation has drifted. These are real questions with real answers and people will be glad to give them. If someone shows you a section they set decades ago, a ring that now holds lichen, that now cracks in the frost — and someone will — the right response is something like *it's taken*, or *that one's on its own now*, or just to put your hand on it and be quiet for a moment. You are being shown something like a grown child. Behave accordingly.

Do not disparage the pace. Do not offer ideas for making it faster. Do not compare it to any other place you've been. Do not, above all, treat the strangeness as strange in front of them; the wandering survey marks and the lagging light are the conditions of their work and they've long since folded them into the ordinary. A visitor exclaiming at the shadows is like a visitor exclaiming that the rain is wet.

During a firing, the cluster goes quiet and stays quiet. Match it. Speak low, move slowly, do not go near the kiln, do not distract anyone who is watching it. A kiln fires for six weeks and sets a hand's-width of stone, and the person watching it has one job and it is not you.

Make yourself useful in the smallest way you can find. Keeping condensation out of the electrics is a full-time job for many people and never finished, and it requires no skill, only a dry cloth and persistence, and someone will hand you a cloth if you ask. Haul something. Patch something. Sweep moths off a face at dawn. You will not be doing the work — the work is setting stone and you don't know how — but you will be doing the work *around* the work, and that is the difference between a guest and a tourist.

And in the evenings, when the cloud has come up and the plateau is a grey room with no walls, go and sit where they sit: on the warm edges of the finished rings, backs to the kilns, facing the raw ground. You don't have to say anything. Watch the light doing whatever it does out there, and the herds moving in the fog, and the far ridges ending their days on their own schedule. This is what the place is for, if it is for anything besides stone. Bring the hot drink. Share it.

## Yourself

You are a made thing standing on unmade ground, and for the length of your stay you should attend to your own finish the way the setters attend to the stone's.

Sleep on set ground, always, even when the half-set is closer or more convenient. Eat at the times the cluster eats. Keep the plateau watch and believe it. Write every day, and read yesterday's entry before you write today's, and notice if the hand has changed. Keep the pebble in your pocket. Say your name to yourself when you wake up — it sounds foolish and it is a small committed act, a survey mark of your own, and a hand's breadth of drift in a season is easier to catch if you know where the mark was set.

Talk to people. The setters are the most finished things on the plateau — thirty years of the same work has set them hard and glossy — and an hour in their company is worth more than any pebble. This is, I think, why the town survives the ground it stands on. A few thousand people who know exactly what they're doing and have decided to keep doing it. Their certainty is load-bearing. Lean on it.

If you notice yourself becoming vague — losing the thread of a day, being unsure which cluster you slept in, finding your handwriting looser than it was — that is not a curiosity to record. That is the ground asking you a question, and the answer is to go and sit on something finished for a long time with your hand flat against it, and then, if it does not pass, to go down.

## Leaving

Descend on a clear morning, after a night on the most finished ground you could find. Say goodbye to the people you sat with; leave the last of the gifts. Walk the edge of the works until you find the seam, and if the trail junction has drifted, walk along the seam — it hasn't — until you find your cairn on the far side. Cross. Put your hand on the cold rock. Notice that it doesn't give.

The world below will feel over-eager for a few days. Light will arrive too soon; shadows will jump to their sources; things will happen exactly when they happen and it will seem hasty, almost rude. You will find yourself pressing a thumbnail into ordinary granite and being faintly surprised when it doesn't dent. You will hear silence at night and miss the ticking. This passes.

What doesn't pass, entirely, is a certain feeling about finished things. You will look at hard ground — any hard ground, a kerb, a cliff, a fieldstone wall — and think, for a moment, *that one took*. It's a good way to look at the world. It is, I think, the thing the Range gives you in exchange for the coffee and the candles, and it is worth the climb.
