## Preparing for the Fen Chamber

### First, understand what kind of danger this is

Most places a traveler prepares for are dangerous because something in them wants to hurt you, or because the environment is indifferent to you and will kill you by accident. This is neither. Nothing in the chamber is hostile. Nothing in it is even indifferent, exactly. The plant is *attentive*, in the way a tide is attentive to a shoreline, and its attention takes one form: anything that used to be alive gets brought back across the line into the living side of things, and then it is the plant.

You are made almost entirely of things that used to be alive or are still alive. So are your clothes, probably. So is your rope, if you chose it badly.

The plant does not do this quickly. That is the whole shape of the risk. Nothing here hurries, and the room rewards patience so richly — the worm finishing its becoming, the frost withdrawing from a new filament, the light climbing a drop — that the danger is not that you'll be attacked but that you'll stand still to watch, and stand still a little longer, and the room will do what it does with things that are standing still. The springtail that stops moving is wall within the hour. The snail that sits too long has to tear itself free. You are bigger and warmer and it will take longer with you, but it has already begun by the time you notice the filaments on your shoulder, and it will not have meant anything by it.

So prepare for it the way you'd prepare for a slow current, not a predator: know exactly how long you're staying before you go in, and go in with something that will pull you out when that time is up.

### Getting in and getting out

You enter through a crack in frozen peat, four meters down. The peat is hard as wood — frozen fen, ten thousand years deep — and the region around the crack is where the plant is dumping its cold, so it may be far colder than the surface, and the rime in the outer zone is described as harder than the ground it sits in. Assume the mouth of the crack is the coldest, most brittle, most awkward part of the whole trip.

- **Anchor at the surface, well back from the crack**, in ground that isn't part of the thinned, rimed zone. Ice screws or deadman anchors in frozen peat should hold, but test them. Do not anchor to anything inside.
- **Synthetic rope only.** Nylon or dyneema. No natural fiber anywhere in the system — no hemp, no cotton sheath, no leather on the harness. The rope will hang inside the chamber while you're down there, beaded with lit condensation, and filaments will grow toward it as they grow toward everything. Check it before you climb. Feel along it for felt.
- **Surface partner, non-negotiable.** Someone stays up top with the other end, a watch, and a hard limit. They pull on the rope at the agreed time whether or not you answer. You may not hear them call: the room reaches deafening at the crest of the animal chorus, and you will have earplugs in.
- **Ice tool.** The frost outside the living zone is being actively fed cold. The crack you climbed down may have narrowed with rime by the time you climb up, and this rime is not soft. Bring something to clear it with.
- **A dry change at the surface**, inside a shelter or tent, *before* you go down. You will come up soaked to the knee at minimum, and probably damp everywhere, into what is presumably a hard arctic surface. The transition from wet-cold below to dry-cold above is where people lose toes.

Note also that the entrance is not the plant's idea. It hasn't made a way out and it won't. The crack exists because of some accident of ice, and the chamber grows a finger's width a day. Nothing you see on the way in should be assumed to be there on the way out in the same shape. Look at it carefully. Clip a light to it.

### What to wear: the dead-matter principle

This is the core of preparation and it runs against almost every cold-weather instinct you have.

The plant converts dead organic matter. That is what the growth face is doing to ten-thousand-year-old sedge. Wool is dead organic matter. Cotton is dead organic matter. Leather, down, silk, natural rubber, wood, paper, cork, waxed canvas — all of it is exactly the kind of thing the plant has a very old habit of taking back. You do not want to be wearing the plant's food into the plant.

**Everything you wear and carry should be synthetic**, to the extent that's achievable:

- Polyester or polypropylene base layers. Fleece midlayers. Synthetic-fill insulation, not down.
- Fully synthetic waterproof boots — rubber-molded or plastic-shelled, no leather uppers, no wool felt liners. Neoprene socks over synthetic liners. The floor is a soaked mattress at freezing; your feet will ache within seconds and water will well up around every step. Plan for immersion, not dampness.
- Gaiters or waders sealed at the knee.
- Thin waterproof gloves. Yes, this means you mostly won't touch the wall barehanded. I'll come back to that.
- A hood or balaclava that covers your hair completely. Hair is dead. So are your fingernails and the outermost layer of your skin, and you shed it constantly. You cannot stop being a source of dead organic matter, but you can stop *offering* it.
- No wooden tool handles. No paper notebook — use a plastic slate or a waterproof electronic device. No food.

There is an honest uncertainty here worth naming: petroleum-derived synthetics were, a very long time ago, plankton. The description says the plant returns to life "everything that was once itself, or once alive, or once anything." If its reach extended that far, the room would be a different room — it would have no need to work slowly through recognizable peat. Its method at the growth face is to find old stems and re-enter them, structure to structure. Polyester has no biological structure left to remember. I'd wager on synthetics being ignored or merely felted-over the way any surface is. I would not wager on wool.

### Light

Do not bring a bright white headlamp as your primary light. You will need one — for the descent, for the crack, for emergencies — but the moment you turn it on inside, the chamber becomes a wet moss cave and stops being what it is. The light is *in the water*. Every hanging drop is a faint star; the pools are brighter than the air; your breath glows briefly and dies grey; shadows fall upward. It takes a few minutes for your eyes to find it, and a headlamp on full will reset that every time.

Bring a dim red light or a very low white setting for moving safely, and let your eyes adapt for the rest. Give it five minutes at the bottom of the rope before you take a step.

Then use the light as an instrument, because the description gives you a tool: **the water glows brightest where the plant is working hardest.** The ceiling more than the floor. The growth face more than the ceiling. Which means that if the filaments and the floor around *you* begin to brighten, the plant is working on you. That is your earliest warning, before anything touches your shoulder. Watch your own footprints. When they fill with light and something begins to grow in them, you are already later than you should be.

### Sound

Three layers, and you need a different relationship with each.

The low tone that lives in the sternum you cannot block and should not try to; it's the note of the whole mass, and it will be there when you close your eyes that night. The granular roar — thousands of ice-cracks per minute as filament tips press into frozen ground — is loud but steady. The animal chorus is the problem: a shrill stridulation from every surface, rising on a tide about an hour long, deafening at the crest.

- **Attenuating earplugs on a lanyard**, not full-block. You want to cut the peak, not lose the room. You need to hear the tide turning, because the tide is your clock.
- **Plan your visit inside one cycle.** Descend in a trough. Do what you came to do while it rises. Be on the rope before the second crest, or at the very latest as it comes down. Two crests means you've been there something like two hours, and that is too long to have been standing in the same body of water.
- Agree with your surface partner on rope signals — tugs, not voice. At the crest you cannot hear yourself.

One more thing about sound. The animals gather around your feet and go *silent* there — a ring of quiet in the roar. Learn to notice that ring. It's the room's attention made audible by absence.

### Time and stillness: the actual rules

Write these on your slate before you go down. They sound simple. They will not feel simple once you're watching a worm's boundary move.

1. **Never sit. Never lie down. Never sleep.** Not even for a moment, not even against the wall to rest, not even on the drier-looking mat near the center. A springtail is wall within the hour. Your surface area is larger and your temperature is higher; you'd have longer. You do not know how much longer.
2. **Do not stand in one place longer than a few minutes.** Shift your weight. Take a step. Move in a slow circuit of the standing zone rather than planting yourself. Every footprint fills with water and light and, eventually, growth; keep making new ones.
3. **Do not crawl.** The edges of the chamber lower to crawling height and the growth face at the far end is low. Crawling puts your whole torso against the tissue. View the growth face from a deep crouch, never prone, and don't hold the crouch.
4. **Check your shoulders, your hood, your rope, your pack every few minutes**, by hand, since you may not see it in the dim. Filaments a handspan away become filaments touching you, and they are "faintly warm," which is exactly the thing that will make you not mind.
5. **Do not touch where the felt is raised.** There are larger shapes moving under the wall like a hand under a blanket. Nothing is known about them.
6. **Brush yourself before you climb.** Anything that has begun to felt onto you, take it off *inside*, at the base of the rope, and leave it. Do not bring the plant up.

### The temptation, and how to handle it

The description keeps inviting you to put your hand against the wall, to trace a drop with a fingertip, to wait long enough to see a worm finish becoming a worm. Every one of those is the right instinct for understanding the place and the wrong instinct for leaving it.

If you must touch the wall barehanded — and I understand the pull; a wall that is wet and gives and is a fraction warmer than freezing, with water running slowly *upward* on it, is worth one touch in a lifetime — then do it once, briefly, with a hand you have just wiped, and put the glove back on. Do not rest your palm there while you think about it. Your skin is shedding dead cells into the felt the whole time and the felt notices.

The worm you will not see finish. The drop you can trace for a fingernail's width. Accept in advance that the room is built on a timescale you can visit but not inhabit, and that the correct way to see the slow things is to come back in a year, when the room is a little larger and the low note is a little lower, and see what has changed — not to stay and wait.

### What not to bring down, and what not to bring up

Down: no food, no drink beyond what's sealed in plastic, nothing you'd have to set down. No crumbs, no wrappers, no spit. Not because it would hurt the plant but because everything you leave becomes the plant, and there is something you may not want on your conscience about being the person who introduced a granola bar into a body that has spent ten thousand years remembering sedge.

Up: nothing living, nothing that was ever living. Not a springtail in a vial, not a pinch of felt, not a snail. *Nothing here is dead for long,* and there is no rot in the room. What that means for a fragment removed to your pack, among your fleece and your food, is unknown, and you do not want to find out in a tent on a frozen fen at night. Water is the one exception I'd consider: a sealed plastic vial of pool-water, to see whether the light persists away from the plant. My guess is that it goes grey within minutes, the way your breath does. Guesses are the reason to check.

### Bodily preparation

- Eat well and warm before descending. You'll burn a lot keeping wet feet alive.
- No open cuts, scrapes, blisters, or hangnails. Tape anything doubtful. Blood and the raw edge of a wound are precisely the kind of boundary-material the plant is good at.
- Trim nails. Tie back or cover hair completely.
- Empty your bladder at the surface. Do not, under any circumstances, relieve yourself inside.
- If you take a fall and go into a pool — likely, the floor is a mattress and the ceiling is uneven — get up *immediately*. Do not lie there recovering. Recover on your feet.

### What it will actually be like

The smell reaches you before anything else, through the crack: green, wet, cellar-cold, cut stems under it. It's not unpleasant. It's the smell of a greenhouse in a place there should not be one, and something in you will relax at it, which is the first thing to be careful of.

The roar you'll hear as fire and it will take a minute at the bottom of the rope to stop expecting warmth. It's cold — not bitter, just steady, the air right at freezing, everything wet, your breath fogging constantly and briefly lit from inside. Your feet will hurt immediately and then go to a dull ache and you should keep track of that ache as a second clock.

Then the room resolves. Grey-green, dim, the felt of the walls, the roots overhead, and it looks like a moss cave until you've stood there long enough to lose the seams and realize you are inside one body that has made a space to stand in. That realization is quiet and rather large. You will probably want to stand very still while you have it. Take a step instead.

The animals are the thing most travelers underrate beforehand. The floor shivers. The chorus builds until it hurts. Flies the size of seeds land on drops and the drops move faster. Worms hang from the wall half-made. It is not disgusting; it's too orderly for that, and the animals are clearly *of* the room, not infesting it. But if you're squeamish about a floor that moves, know that now.

And then, if you've stayed as long as you should, you'll notice — because you were told to notice — that the roots near your shoulder are closer than they were, and warm, and the springtails have gone quiet around your boots, and your footprints behind you are glowing. That's not a threat. It's the room extending the same courtesy it extends to everything. Say goodbye to it if you like. Then brush your shoulders, walk to the rope, signal, and climb.

### Summary checklist

- Synthetic everything: clothing, insulation, boots, socks, gloves, rope, harness, tool handles, notebook
- Full hair and skin coverage; nails trimmed; wounds taped
- Immersion-rated waterproof boots and knee-high gaiters or waders
- Synthetic rope, surface anchors well back from the crack, ice tool for rime
- Surface partner with a hard time limit and rope signals
- Dry change and shelter at the surface before descent
- Dim red light for inside; bright lamp for the crack and emergencies
- Attenuating earplugs on a lanyard
- Plastic slate with the rules written on it
- Sealed water only; no food; nothing to set down
- Visit planned within one chorus cycle; out before the second crest
- Never sit, lie, sleep, crawl, or stand still for long
