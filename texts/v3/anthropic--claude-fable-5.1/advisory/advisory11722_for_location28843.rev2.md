**Preparing for the plateau**

Start by discarding the word "visit." You do not visit this place the way you visit a city, even though it will look like one. You go up onto it the way you go out onto a glacier or into a very large cave: as something small and warm that leaks, entering something enormous and cold that does not. Everything about your preparation follows from that. The plateau is not hostile. It has no idea you are there. But it is high, it is wet, it is cold, it is featureless in the way that only intricate things can be featureless, and it will quietly begin to turn everything you bring into stone.

---

**I. The three things that will actually hurt you**

Before the wonder, the physiology. The account is right that you will remember the birds. But what will decide whether you come back down in a week or not at all is altitude, cold, and wet, and they work together.

*Altitude.* The description gives the tell-tale signs: a sky harder blue than it should be, a noon sun that puts no heat into the stone, trees giving up at the boundary. Wherever you place the altitude in your own body's terms, treat it as high — the kind of high where an unacclimatized person gets a headache the first night, loses appetite, sleeps badly, and may get seriously ill if they push. Do not ascend from the forest straight onto the flats in a day. Spend at least one night, preferably two, at the treeline before going up. Once up, keep the first two days short and low-exertion. Drink far more than you think you need — the air is thin and, despite the fog, thin air pulls water out of you fast. Learn the difference between "I have a headache and I'm tired" (normal, rest) and "I am confused, I am stumbling, I have a cough that produces froth" (go down immediately, in fog if you have to, following your own route markers). Bring whatever your world has for altitude sickness, and understand that the only real cure is descent, and that descent here means the escarpment, which you must know how to find.

*Cold.* Not dramatic cold. Not the cold of blizzards. Something more patient: air that never warms, stone that never warms, water that sits at exactly the air's temperature so that you cannot even use it to tell hot from cold by touch. The account says the walls are "always the same temperature as the air." That is the temperature you will live at. Expect a few degrees above freezing at midday in the sun, near or below it at night, with almost no radiant warmth ever reaching you. This is the temperature range in which people die of hypothermia most often, because it doesn't feel like an emergency. It feels like being uncomfortable for a long time.

*Wet.* And you will be uncomfortable for a long time, because nothing dries. Understand this fully before you pack a single item. The fog rolls in every day by noon. The rock weeps. Every surface you touch is wet. Every place you might sit is under a millimeter of water. The air is saturated. Sweat does not evaporate; it just joins the general dampness. A garment that gets wet on the first morning will still be wet when you leave. You will be operating in a permanent cold drizzle even on days it does not rain.

Cold plus wet plus thin air plus exertion is how strong people stop making good decisions. Treat these three as one hazard and build your kit and your schedule around it.

---

**II. Clothing: assume you will never be dry again**

Cotton will kill you here. Say that to yourself a few times.

You want layers that hold warmth while soaked — wool or the synthetic equivalent of wool, whatever your world calls it. A wicking base layer, a thick insulating layer that does not collapse when wet (down is useless here; it will be a sodden clump by the second afternoon), and a shell over everything that is genuinely waterproof, not merely water-resistant. The shell matters less for rain — the rain is soft — than for the fog, which is a rain that surrounds you from every direction including below, and for the wind that tears the fog off in the afternoon and will strip heat out of anything damp.

Bring two full sets of insulating layers and keep one sealed in a waterproof bag that you do not open except inside your shelter. This is your sleeping set. It goes on dry at night and comes off in the morning and goes back in the bag, and you put the wet set back on. This is miserable. It is also the single most important discipline of the trip. If both sets get wet, you have lost your margin.

Gloves: waterproof over-mitts with wool liners, and spare liners. You will be touching wet stone constantly — navigating by hand in the fog, steadying yourself on terrace walls — and your hands will be the first thing to go numb and the thing you most need working. The stone in the tunnels is sharp-edged where it's never been rained on; expect small cuts on your fingertips that will not heal while you're up there.

A hat that covers your ears. A hood over that. A scarf or buff over your mouth — partly for warmth, partly because the fog "tastes faintly of chalk" and after a few days of breathing it your throat will be raw.

Sunglasses. This will feel absurd to pack for a place that is fog from noon onward, and it is not. In the clear mornings and the lit late afternoons you will be standing on a white plain of pale stone and mirror-flat pools, at high altitude, under a hard sky. The light comes at you from above and from below. This is the exact recipe for snow blindness, and it can happen in a single unprotected morning. Bring dark glasses with side coverage, and a spare pair. Sunscreen too, for the same reason, on the parts of your face the scarf doesn't cover.

---

**III. Feet**

Your feet deserve their own section because they will be in cold water more or less continuously.

The terraces are covered in a skin of standing water. The channels — the "streets" — are polished smooth and wet. The tunnels are ankle-deep. There is no dry ground. You will not have dry feet for the duration.

Waterproof boots are a trap here; the water comes in over the top in the tunnels and then stays inside. Better: boots that drain and dry (they won't dry, but at least they won't hold a bath), worn over wool socks, with a rigid enough sole to protect you from the scalloped texture and the broken straw-lattice underfoot. Some travelers do better with neoprene socks under boots, accepting warm-wet over cold-wet. Whichever you choose, bring more socks than seems reasonable — a dry pair, from the sealed bag, for every night — and check your feet every evening. Trench foot is a real risk after a few days of this, and it starts as numbness and pale, wrinkled skin. If you see it, you dry your feet by whatever means necessary and keep them dry and elevated for a night, even if it costs you a day.

Traction: travertine is treacherous. The dry crests are rough and grippy. The wet flats are like walking on soap. The algal mats around the pool rims are worse than ice. The polished channel floors are worse than that. Your soles should be soft rubber with deep tread, and even so, move slowly, keep your weight low, and test every step on anything sloped. A trekking pole — two — is not optional. They save your knees on the descent through the terraces and they let you probe the ground ahead in fog.

---

**IV. What the water does to your things**

Now the strange part of packing, the part you would not think of anywhere else.

The water is saturated with lime and it deposits lime on everything it touches. A hair's thickness in a season, the account says. That sounds like nothing, and over a week it is nearly nothing on the stone. But you are not stone. You are a moving bundle of fabrics and straps and buckles and seams, constantly wetted and constantly exposed to cold air, which is exactly the condition under which the water lets its load go. Spider silk is a fossil by the following year. Your gear will not fossilize in a week, but it will start.

Expect: a white crust on the eyelets and laces of your boots by day three. Zippers that stiffen and then stop. Pack straps that go rigid and chalky where they've been wet longest. A grey-white bloom on any leather. Metal buckles and pole tips that scale over. Your hair, if it's long and gets wet daily, will go stiff and pale at the ends. Anything you set down on the stone overnight will have a faint ring bonded to it by morning that you can chip off with a fingernail — for now.

So: minimize metal moving parts. Prefer buckles and toggles to zippers where you can. Bring a small stiff brush and a bottle of something mildly acidic — vinegar, or whatever the local equivalent is — to clean scale off critical hardware each evening: pole locks, stove valves, the closures on your dry bag. Do not leave anything you value in a pool. Do not leave anything you value on wet stone for longer than a night. Do not, if you use ropes for any reason, leave a rope fixed for more than a day and then trust it; the lime works into the fibers and stiffens them, and stiffened fiber fails.

Your cookware will scale badly. Every time you boil this water, you'll leave a chalk deposit inside the pot. Scrape it out daily or the pot loses efficiency and the water starts to taste even more like the inside of a kettle.

---

**V. Water and food**

Yes, you can drink it. It is very hard water — chalky, faintly mineral, slightly warm-feeling on the tongue even though it's cold, a taste that some find unpleasant and everyone finds odd. In most people it produces nothing worse than a mild laxative effect in the first days, which is worth knowing when you're planning your day's route through a city with no privacy. It is not poisonous. It is not, as far as anyone has found, contaminated with anything worse than the algae and the fly larvae, but filter or boil anyway; the algal mats are exactly where the flies breed, and you don't want to find out what else lives in them.

Better water: the rain, and the pools furthest from the springs and channels. Better still, the drips from the straws in the tunnels, if you're patient enough to hold a cup under them — that water has traveled slowly and set down most of its load already. Best of all, catch rainwater off your shelter fly. Bring more collapsible capacity than you'd expect; you'll want to catch it when it comes.

Do not count on finding anything to eat. Nothing grows here that a human can live on. The moss is calcified. The cushion plants are rare and tiny. The snails are the size of rice grains and would take you a day to gather a mouthful of. The birds are too few to hunt and it would be a sin to try. Bring every calorie you'll consume, and bring more than you plan for, because cold and altitude both increase what your body burns and because people come back down "a week later or a month later" and do not seem to have planned for the month.

Food that survives wet and cold and is dense: fats, nuts, dried meat, hard cheese, oats, anything you can eat without cooking if your stove fails. Plan on hot food at least once a day for morale and core temperature, which brings us to fuel.

There is no fuel here. Not a stick. "Nothing with a woody stem." You cannot make a fire on the plateau and you should not imagine you can. Every meal you heat, every liter you boil, comes from fuel you carried up. Bring a stove that runs reliably in cold, damp, thin air — liquid fuel or a cold-rated gas — and calculate your fuel need for the trip and then multiply it. Fuel is heavy. This is the hardest trade-off you will make in packing, and it's the one that sets the length of your trip. When the fuel runs low, you leave. That is your clock.

---

**VI. Shelter and sleeping**

There is nowhere to pitch a tent that is not wet stone under a skin of water, and there is nothing to drive a peg into. Bring a shelter that can be pitched freestanding or guyed to rocks — and there are plenty of loose chunks of fallen terrace to weigh guy lines with. Put a groundsheet down first, then a second one, because the first will be under a millimeter of water by the time you've finished pitching. A raised sleeping pad with real insulation value is essential; the stone will pull heat out of you all night if you let it.

Choose your site with two things in mind. First: don't camp under a terrace lip or a leaning tower or on a dome. Lips break off "waterlogged past their strength" and drop into the pool below; you don't want to be in the pool. Domes are "low and cracked" roofs over dead pools, and cracked means cracked. Towers lean and sometimes fall. Camp on a flat, on the old, low, grey-green stone, away from anything overhanging. Second: camp where you can find your way back to the escarpment in fog. Which brings us to the largest problem.

Nights are quiet in a way that some travelers find unbearable. The only sounds are the drips and the occasional distant collapse. Some people bring something to play or read to themselves just to hear a human voice. There's no shame in it.

**VII. Navigation: the actual danger**

The plateau is where nobody has found the end. Travelers go up for a week and come back talking about it from different sides and their accounts don't join. Read that not as mysticism but as a warning about a specific, mundane, lethal problem: this is an enormous, intricate, self-similar landscape, pale and unmarked, in which you cannot see more than an arm's length for half of every day.

Every terrace looks like every other terrace. Every lane between cream walls looks like the last one. The towers that might serve as landmarks are the first thing the fog swallows. The sun is available in the morning and for two or three hours in the late afternoon, and otherwise you're in a white room with wet walls. Sound doesn't carry, so you can't call to a companion or hear a landmark. And the channels — the streets — curve, and lead downhill toward the sinks, and the sinks are not where you want to end up.

So:

*Carry a compass* and check that it works at the treeline before you go up. There's no reason to think it won't, but the plateau has been described as having its own weather and its own internal directions, and you should verify rather than assume.

*Mark your route.* Not by scratching the stone, which will grow over, but with something bright, biodegradable, and removable that you take back down with you. Colored cord tied around loose chunks and set on crests. Small flags. Whatever you use, set them close enough together that you can see the next from the last in fog — and fog visibility here can be under two meters — which means you'll set a great many of them, and that's fine. This is how you get down. Treat it as seriously as you'd treat a rope on a cliff.

*Keep a written log.* Bearings, paces, terrace counts, distinctive features: "third terrace down from the leaning tower, channel bears left, arch at the end." Write it in pencil on waterproof paper, in a case, and back it up with a second copy. Your memory will not distinguish the fourth rippled lane from the ninth. Your log will.

*Move in the clear windows.* Cover ground in the early morning and late afternoon when you can see. Spend the fog hours in camp or making short, marked, out-and-back explorations from it. Don't be traveling through unfamiliar terrain when the fog comes in — and it comes in fast, "by an hour after sunrise the pools have begun to breathe."

*Know your way to the edge.* The escarpment is the way down. Before you go deep, fix its bearing from your first camp and keep updating it. If everything else fails — fuel gone, food gone, sick, lost — you go to the edge and follow it, because somewhere along it is the way you came up.

*Set a turnaround.* A date. Write it down before you leave the trees. Tell someone. When it comes, you turn around, regardless of what you've found or haven't. The people who come back a month late almost certainly didn't mean to.

If you are going with companions — and you should — agree on a fog protocol before you go up. Voices don't carry; the lime "deadens everything." A whistle works better than a shout but not much better. Stay within touching distance in fog, or rope up loosely, and if anyone gets separated, the rule is: stay where you are and wait for the wind. The fog lifts every afternoon. Whoever's lost stays put; the others find them once they can see. Never split up and wander looking for each other in the white.

**VIII. Moving through the city**

A few specific things about footing and structure.

*Terrace rims* are the most tempting place to walk — they're raised, they're rounded, they connect one flat to the next like paths — and they're the most likely thing to break under you. Old lips are waterlogged and soft inside. Walk on the flats behind the rim, not on it. When you have to step down from one terrace to the next, step down onto the flat below, not onto the lip of the pool.

*Channels* are the natural routes, and they're smooth and fast, and they run downhill, and downhill is toward the sinks and away from the edge. Use them but keep your bearings. The polished floors are treacherous. Where a channel goes under an arch, look at the arch before you go under; the undercutting that made it is still happening.

*Domes* are roofs over dead pools. Don't walk on them. If one is in your way, go around, even if around is a long way.

*Towers* are the only high ground, and the temptation to climb one for a view in the clear morning is strong, and some of them can be climbed. Look for the falcon first; if it's on the tower, the tower is its, leave it. Look at the base; if the tower is leaning, or if the terracing at its base looks fresh, the ground has been moving and the tower may be about to. Assume every hold is wet and every scallop is fragile. Never climb in fog, because you won't be able to get down.

*The new terraces*, the ones that are "only a rim of soft white sludge," are exactly that — sludge. Step into one and you go through the crust into cold lime mud up to the knee, and it's hard to get out of, and everything you get it on will set. Learn what fresh deposit looks like — bright white, slightly rounded, wet-looking even in the wind — and stay off it.

*Collapse.* You'll hear it. The wet-cloth sound. It means a lip broke somewhere within ten meters. It's not usually dangerous, but if you're near a tower or under a ledge when you hear it, move away from the overhang.

---

**IX. The tunnels**

The deeper districts are the most beautiful part of the plateau and the part where you should be most careful.

The tunnels are roofed channels, lit from above through gaps, floored with ankle-deep still water so clear you can't see it. You'll be walking blind into cold water on every step, unable to judge depth by eye. Use your poles. The floor is lattice in places — broken straws re-cemented — and will hold you, but there are also gaps where the lattice hasn't closed, and a foot going through into a deeper pocket is how ankles break. Probe first.

The roof grows straws, a meter long, thin as wheat stalks, fragile beyond anything else here. Every one you snap off has been growing since before your grandparents were born. Move with your head down and your pack low, and if the tunnel narrows to where you'd have to break them to pass, don't pass.

The tunnels are also the only places on the plateau where fog doesn't fully reach and where wind doesn't reach at all, which makes them tempting shelter. Resist it. They're channels, which means they're where water goes; a shift in the springs upstream — which happens on the plateau's schedule, not yours — could raise that ankle-deep water while you sleep. They're roofed by terraces that grew together, and the roofs have gaps, which means they're not entirely sound. And a tunnel is the one place on the plateau where you truly cannot navigate by sun. Go in, marvel, come out before the afternoon wind. Don't spend a night in one.

The white moss and the rice-grain snails live along the seams where wall meets floor. Don't step there. They're the closest thing this place has to inhabitants, and there are very few of them.

---

**X. The living things**

You are a large warm animal in a place where the total living tissue per square kilometer is less than the stone made in an hour. You are, briefly, the biggest thing in the ecosystem. Behave accordingly.

The flies are harmless and slow; they'll settle on you and leave when you move. Don't bother swatting. The spiders are harmless. The algal mats are the base of the entire food web and are exactly where you'll want to step to get around a pool — don't; go across the flats.

The dipper-like bird is the one you'll remember, and the one whose nest cavities in the towers you'll be tempted to look into. If you find one and put your head in, you'll smell feathers and lime and see the calcifying droppings. Then take your head out and don't come back to that tower. There aren't many of these birds and each one needs a very large stretch of city to feed itself, and one abandoned nest is a real fraction of the population.

The falcon you'll hear once. If you see it on a tower, that tower is off-limits. It has the largest territory of anything here and it is, functionally, alone. It will not bother you. Don't bother it.

Leave nothing behind, and this is more than the usual admonition, because here what you leave doesn't rot or blow away. It gets built into the walls. A dropped wrapper becomes a fossil in a decade. Carry everything out.

---

**XI. What it does to your head**

Everything above is mechanical. This part isn't, and travelers who prepared perfectly for the cold and the wet have still come down badly shaken by things they didn't plan for.

The quiet is the first thing. Not silence — the dripping is constant — but a deadened quiet in which your own footsteps go soft into the stone and your voice stops a few meters from your mouth. Many people find the first day of it soothing and the third day of it very hard. The description says you can hear your own pulse. You can also hear your own breathing, and your own swallowing, and after a while your own thoughts, and for some people that's the point and for others it's the thing that sends them back down early. Know which you are, or prepare to find out.

The fog is the second. Half of every day inside a white room with wet walls, unable to see the hand in front of you, navigating by touch, hearing collapses you can't locate. This is disorienting in a way that goes beyond the practical. People report a loosening of time in the fog — an hour that felt like ten minutes, a morning that felt like a day — and the "week or a month" of the travelers' accounts is probably partly this. Keep a watch and trust it over your sense of time. Mark the days in your log.

The sameness is the third. The terraces repeat. The lanes repeat. The scallops on the walls repeat at every scale from the finger's width to the tower's height. After a few days your eye stops finding differences and the whole place flattens into a single texture, and this is when people get lost, because they stop paying attention to the small differences that were their landmarks. Force yourself to note specifics every day. Draw things. Describe them in words. Keep your attention sharp against the erosion.

And the fourth is the hardest to describe. The plateau is "already in its own state." It isn't waiting. It has no ruins, because nothing here was ever finished; it has no future, because it's already what it's going to be. You'll walk through it for days and it will not respond to your presence in any way, and it was doing this for a very long time before you came and will do it for a very long time after, and the wall forming at your feet, one grain at a time, will be head-high to someone who is not born yet and whose grandchildren will not be born yet. Some people find this the most consoling thing they've ever encountered. Others find it crushing. Almost everyone finds it, for at least one afternoon in the sideways light with every scallop throwing its small shadow, unbearably beautiful, and then the fog comes back and they're cold and wet and it's just stone again. Expect the swing. It's part of the place.

---

**XII. Leaving**

You'll know it's time when one of your clocks runs out — fuel, food, the date you wrote down, or your body telling you the altitude has stopped being tolerable. Any one of them is enough. Don't wait for two.

Follow your markers back. Collect them as you go. Check your log against what you see. If the fog is in, wait for the wind; it always comes. When you reach the edge, you'll see the last pool spilling over the lip into the fog-filled valley and the tops of the trees below, and you'll follow the edge to the place you came up, and go down.

Below the treeline, the first thing you'll notice is the sound coming back — your footsteps, birds that aren't the one bird, the wind in leaves. The second is warmth in the stone. The third, when you unpack, is the white crust on everything: the boot laces gone stiff, the pole locks scaled, a faint chalk bloom on the pack where it sat wet all week. Some of it will brush off. Some won't. Most travelers keep something with it still on.

Your dry set of clothes, if you kept the discipline, will be dry. Put it on. Sit in the sun. Let it heat you. It will be the first time in a week the sun has put any heat into anything you touched, and you'll understand then what the plateau had been quietly withholding the whole time, and you will probably, once you're warm, want to go back.
