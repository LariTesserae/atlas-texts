**Preparing for the bowl**

Start by accepting that this is not a place you visit so much as a condition you enter, and that your body will begin objecting to three things at once within the first minute: the light, the wet cold, and the air. Everything else — the moving ground, the silence, the people, the animals in the walls — you can learn while you are there, if you have handled those three well enough to keep your eyes and your fingers and your judgment. If you have not, you will learn nothing, because you will be blind, or shivering too hard to set a stone, or sitting on the floor with a headache that makes you want to lie down in a room whose frogs have just left.

**Eyes first, before anything**

You will arrive squinting and you will never stop. The description is exact about this: the brightness is not weather, it is the medium. Snowfields above the rim, a white sky, and every square inch of the bowl varnished with water, all of it throwing light back at you from every angle, including up from the floor and off the wet undersides of ledges. There is no shade to step into. There is no direction to turn your face away from.

At altitude, on snow, unprotected eyes burn in a few hours — photokeratitis, snow blindness, which feels like sand under the eyelids and takes a day or two of darkness to heal. Here you will not have a day or two of darkness. So: put your eye protection on before you open your eyes on arrival, and do not take it off. Dark lenses that wrap the whole eye, with side shields, are the minimum. But think about the mist. The bowl breathes water; glass lenses will fog in the cold and you will spend the day wiping them with wet fingers. The better answer is the old one: slit goggles, carved bone or dense wood or even thick leather, with a horizontal cut that lets in a line of light and nothing else. They do not fog. They halve your peripheral vision, which matters in a place where walls lean, but a fogged lens does worse. Bring both. Bring a spare of each.

Protect the skin the light comes up under, too — the underside of the chin, the lips, the rims of the nostrils, the eyelids, the backs of the hands when you hold them out for a stone. Reflected light burns the places sun never reaches. Grease them. And understand one thing the locals almost certainly know without being told: you look at your hands. You look at the stone in front of you. You look at the floor a stride ahead. You do not look up at the walls to admire them, because the walls are white with light and there is nothing to see in them anyway except more light. The people crouched in the passages are not being humble. They are keeping their eyes.

One more thing about the light. There is no shadow that lasts, and shadow is how you have judged depth your whole life. Steps will be nearer or farther than they look. Ledges will not read as ledges. Move with your hands out. Touch the edge of a thing before you commit your weight to it. You will feel foolish for the first hour and then you will notice that everyone else does the same.

**Wet cold, and the fact that nothing dries**

The water on every surface is a degree from freezing and the air is too thin to hold warmth. There is no fire — no fuel, a few grey poles treated as heirlooms, and nothing to burn them in. What this means for you, practically, is that you will be damp within minutes of arrival and you will stay damp until you leave, and you must dress for that and not for dry cold.

Wool, not down. Down is the warmest thing there is until it gets wet, and then it is a bag of cold paste against your back. Wool insulates when it is soaked. Wear it in layers, with a shell over it that will keep the running water and the drifting mist mostly on the outside, and accept that "mostly" is the best you will get. Consider a vapor barrier next to your skin so your own sweat does not add to the problem. You will sweat; lifting stone in thin air is work.

Your hands are the crisis. You will want to touch things, you will need to touch things — to steady yourself, to receive a stone that is leaning into you, to feel for the edge of a passage in the flat light — and every one of those things is wet and cold enough to numb your fingers in minutes. Numb fingers drop stones. A dropped stone in a passage a shoulder wide, in a wall that is already leaning, is how a wall folds on someone. Gloves that grip wet rock and keep some warmth; a second pair sealed dry inside your clothing; a third pair if you can manage it. Change them when your fingers stop reporting. Do not wait until they hurt, because the hurt stops, and that is worse.

Feet: soft rubber soles that grip on slick stone, not stiff mountain lugs that skate. Wool socks and a dry pair sealed away. If you stay more than a day, you are on a trench-foot clock, and the only cure is dry, which does not exist here.

Look at what the people wear. It will be hide, gone stiff and pale like the roofs, and it will tell you what works.

**Air**

You cannot walk up to this place gradually, which is the ordinary way a body learns to live at height. You will simply be there, and your blood will not know what to do. Within an hour: shortness of breath on any exertion, a dull headache behind the eyes, possibly nausea. Within a day, for some people, worse. If you have access to medication that helps the body adjust to altitude, take it in advance. If you do not, the medicine is pace.

Watch how the people move. Slowly, and with long pauses between movements. You will read this as care — and it is care, the stones demand it — but it is also the air. They are not resting. They are breathing. Match them. Lift one stone, set it, breathe until your breath is even, lift the next. If you try to work at the rate your body remembers from lower down, you will be sitting on a wet floor within the hour with your head between your knees while a wall you were meant to be holding leans away from you.

Drink. The air is dry even though everything else is wet, and altitude pulls water out of you fast. There is water everywhere. Filter it — it has been running under a settlement for a very long time, and the settlement has salamanders and snails and people in it — but drink constantly.

**Arriving**

You will not choose where you appear, but you can choose what you do first. Eyes closed. Goggles on. Sit down where you are, if you are not already down, and do not put a hand on anything to steady yourself — not a wall, not a slab, nothing. Breathe until your breath is even. Then open your eyes through the slit and find the lip: it is the one direction in which the light comes up rather than down, a bar of white where the bowl ends and the cloud begins. Turn your back to it. Now find the nearest person. There will be one; they are everywhere once you know how to look, crouched, a stone in both hands. Stay where they can see you. Do not move until you have watched them move.

If you have arrived inside a room or a passage, you are in the way. Every passage is a shoulder wide and every room is somebody's work in progress. Get to the open low ground toward the lip — not the lip itself, but the loose stacking before it — as directly and slowly as you can, and reorient from there.

**How to move**

The ground moves. Not while you watch, but between one look and the next. Every rule for moving in the bowl follows from that.

Do not lean on a wall. Ever. Not to rest, not to steady yourself, not for a moment. A wall that holds your weight is a wall you have just made a decision about, and you do not know how to make that decision. Walk in the center of passages with your hands raised and slightly out, so that if a wall leans, it leans into your palms and not your shoulder.

Do not step on a slab roof. Do not step under one unless a person from here has gone under it first and is still there. Slab roofs slide a hand's breadth without warning, and a hand's breadth is enough to open a room to the sky or close a passage on a leg.

Do not enter a room that is empty of frogs. This is the single most important thing in this section and you will not believe it until you have seen it. The frogs leave before the floor humps. A room with no frogs is a room the ground is about to take. If you are in a room and you notice the frogs going, go.

Watch your ankles. Floors hump and drop half a step between morning and noon, and in the flat light you will not see it until your foot finds it. Feel for the floor. Step onto the center of a stone, never its edge; the edges are where stones rock.

And when you feel the floor shift under you — you will, a settling, a tilt no larger than a breath — stop. Do not lunge for a wall. Stand, feel which way the ground wants to go, and step the other way onto something that is not moving. The people do this without looking up. You will do it badly the first few times and then you will do it.

**Reading the walls**

The animals are not decoration and they are not wildlife. They are the settlement's instruments, and the people read them the way you read a gauge.

The salamanders, pale and red-gilled, packed into every wet crevice, do not move. They are not a signal of anything except that a crevice is wet, which every crevice is. Do not touch them. Amphibian skin is delicate and yours is greasy; and beyond that, when a woman lifts a stone and finds a salamander in the place she meant to set the next one, she chooses another stone. That is the rule. You will follow it too: never set a stone where something is living.

The frogs are the ground's early warning. Learn what a room full of frogs looks like — dark, beaded, still, eyes that do not glitter — so that you know instantly what a room without them looks like.

The birds are the structural survey. They crowd thickest on the walls that will stand longest. A wall-top empty of birds is a wall you should not be near. And they rise all together when a slab slides, in a soft blur that is the loudest sound of the day, and when they rise everyone in the bowl looks toward where they rose from. If you cause them to rise — by stumbling into a wall, by reaching toward one, by any clumsiness — you will have made the loudest sound of the day and every eye in the settlement will be on you. Do not do this. Walk below them, not at them. Keep your hands low near the roof-lines.

The snails are the only living things here that will not tell you anything. Do not take a shell. Their shells are worn thin as fingernail and belong to the bowl as much as the stones do.

**The people, and what a stranger's hands are for**

They will not greet you. They will not explain. They speak, if at all, in a syllable, low, to the person beside them, and the sound goes flat on the stone and is gone. Do not take this for hostility. Speech does not carry here — the air is too thin, the wet rock eats it — and they have spent their whole lives learning that the mouth is not how you tell someone a wall is going. You tell them by being at the other end of it with your hands out.

So: do not talk. Do not ask. If you must communicate, gesture, and keep the gesture small. Watch what they do with their hands and their eyes when a passage begins to drop, because that is the language.

You will want to help. The whole settlement is being taken down and put back up around you, all the time, and the wish to lift a stone will be almost physical. Resist it, at first. Every stone here has been handled for centuries and has learned several ways to fit. You do not know any of them. A stone you set will be a stone someone has to lift again and reset behind you, quietly, without looking at you, and you will have cost them work and breath.

What a stranger's hands are for is receiving. When a passage begins to lean, stand at one end of it — where no one is already standing — and hold your hands out, and let the stones come to you, and pass them to the person beside you, who knows where they go. Carry a slab a few steps if someone loads it onto your shoulder. Be a pair of hands that does not decide anything. That is useful, and it is the only useful thing you can be on the first day, and it is what the children here do until they have learned the stones.

Do not pick up a stone from the ground unless someone is looking at you and at the stone. Do not put a stone down anywhere except into another pair of hands.

**Being unwatched**

Understand what the silence means for your safety. If a slab slides onto your leg in a room at the edge of the settlement and you shout, your shout will travel perhaps a few strides and die. No one will come because no one will hear. The people are not in danger from this because they are always in each other's sight; someone is at each end of every passage that is about to drop before it drops. You are not in anyone's sight unless you put yourself there.

Stay within view of at least one person at all times. If you must move to a place where no one is, tell someone with your hands first — point at yourself, point where you are going — and come back within the time it takes to eat something. Carry a piece of cloth in a color that does not exist here: red, or a hard yellow, anything that is not black stone and white light. If you need help, hold it up. The birds are black and the people are dark and the walls are dark and everything else is white; a red cloth raised above a roof-line will be seen from across the bowl, where a voice would not be heard from across a room.

**Food, water, sleep**

Bring all your food, and bring the kind that needs no heat and no dry hands: dense, fatty, ready to eat from the wrapper. There is no fire to cook on and your fingers will not manage anything fiddly. Eat more than you think you need; the cold and the altitude are burning through you.

What the people eat you will probably not see, and you should not ask. It may be the snails; it may be the frogs; it may be something scraped off the stone that you cannot see. Eat your own food discreetly, sitting low, in the loose ground toward the lip, not in a passage where you are in the way and not in a room where the roof is a slab.

Water is everywhere and always cold. Filter it and drink it.
