**Preparing for the Roaring Shelf**

The first thing to understand is that you are not preparing for a place. You are preparing for a sound, and the place is the small amount of ground around the sound where a person can stand.

Everything else follows from that.

---

**Your body, before you leave**

See a dentist. This is not a joke. Every account of the shelf mentions the fillings, and the reason is that a metal filling in a tooth is a small hard object bonded to bone, and bone conducts, and the whole shelf is bone-conduction at a scale you have never felt. Old amalgam work rattles. Cracked teeth crack further. If you have anything loose, unfinished, or aching, fix it now, at sea level, where a dentist can hear you describe the pain.

See a doctor about your ears. Not for the noise — nothing a doctor gives you will help with the noise — but to know the baseline. Know what you are giving up. If you have ever had a burst eardrum, a badly infected ear, or vertigo you couldn't explain, understand that the inner ear is the organ the shelf attacks first and hardest, and that you are walking in with a weakness.

Then think about altitude. The lip is ten thousand feet up and the sky above it goes violet, which means it is effectively higher than that; the air is thin enough to make the light hard. If you have never spent time above eight thousand feet, spend some, somewhere else, first. Learn what your own altitude sickness feels like — the headache, the nausea, the bad sleep — so that when it arrives on the face you can tell it apart from what the sound is doing to you. Because the sound also causes headache, nausea, and bad sleep, and if you cannot tell them apart you will not know whether to go up or go down.

Be fit enough to climb for days carrying everything, because there is nothing at the top. No wood, no water you can trust, no food you did not bring or catch. The face is not technical in the mountaineering sense — people climb it with rope and hands — but it is long, and it cuts, and the air runs out as you go.

---

**Your mind, before you leave**

Here is the thing nobody says plainly: you will not be able to talk to anyone from the last switchback onward. Not shout. Not lean in close. Nothing. The workers use hands and chalk, and they have had time to learn each other.

So learn a sign language before you go. Any one. Learn it properly, with a partner, so that your hands are quick and you do not have to think. If you are travelling with someone, learn the same one. If you are travelling alone, learn one anyway, because the people on the shelf have built up a set of signs over the years and a person who already thinks in hands will pick theirs up in a week, and a person who does not may never manage more than pointing.

Carry chalk. Carry a lot of chalk, wrapped so the shaking does not grind it to dust in your pack. Carry a slate or a board. Practice writing short.

And sit with the idea of a sound that does not stop. Not a loud room you can leave. Not a machine you can switch off. Most people have never been inside anything without pause or variation for more than a few hours, and there is a part of the mind that waits, always, for the change — the end of the song, the engine cutting, the storm passing. On the shelf that part waits and waits and is not answered, and after a few days it does something. For some people it goes quiet, and they describe a strange peace. For others it does not go quiet, and they come down the face early and shaking, and cannot explain what was wrong except that nothing ever stopped.

You cannot know which you are in advance. But you can know that it is coming.

---

**The kit**

Start with your hands and feet, because the climb comes first.

The basalt is glass. It has never been rained on for long enough to dull, never grown lichen, never been walked. Every edge is a broken bottle. Ordinary climbing gloves will be cut through in a day. Wear heavy leather over a liner, and bring a second pair, and bring leather scraps and heavy thread to patch them. Kneepads. Boots with soles thick enough that a fin of rock will not come through, and stiff enough to stand on an edge. Eye protection on the climb — the rock does not just cut, it flakes, and a flake off glassy basalt is a razor moving fast. Rope, and a way to anchor to a rock that does not want to be anchored to; expect to leave hardware behind.

Then the cold. Two degrees above freezing, always, day and night, summer and winter. Dry cold, which is kinder than wet cold, but the wind off a cold sea at altitude is not nothing even when the shelf itself has almost no wind. Layers. Wool, because wool does not care about damp and will not melt on you if you manage to get a stove going. A good hood.

Now the sound.

Understand what hearing protection can and cannot do. Plugs and muffs together will protect the eardrum from the airborne part of the roar, and this matters — it is the difference between a year before deafness and a month. Wear both, always, from the last switchback. Buy the best muffs you can find and bring a spare set, because the foam degrades and the seals crack in cold. Some of the workers wear padded hoods over the muffs, thick quilted things that cover the whole skull; make one or buy one.

But none of it stops the pressure. The roar comes up through the rock into your feet and through your feet into your spine and it rings your ribcage and your skull from the inside, and no earplug reaches that. Your teeth will buzz. Your vision may blur slightly at moments — eyeballs have a resonant frequency, and the note is low enough to be near it. Your gut will be unsettled for the first days; low frequencies do that. Expect to feel seasick standing on solid rock. Expect to feel your own heartbeat as a thing out of step with something larger.

So: ginger, or whatever you use for nausea. Things you can eat when you do not want to eat. Padding for sleeping that lifts you off the rock — the rock is the bell; a thick mat and a foam layer between you and it makes a real difference, and the workers sleep in huts with floors laid on boulders for exactly that reason. If you can find or make a hammock, better.

Bring nothing made of glass. Bring nothing fragile. Bring no instruments you care about — clocks, compasses, anything with a fine mechanism. The shaking is small and constant and it works on everything. Rivets tighten or loosen. Threads back out. Check your buckles every morning.

Fuel: there is nothing to burn on the shelf. Bring a stove and a lot of fuel, and know that stoves burn badly at altitude and worse in cold. The old hands render fish oil and burn that in lamps; it is possible, and it stinks, and it is what you will fall back on if you stay.

Water: the sea is called a sea, and nobody says whether it is salt. Assume it is until you have tested it. Bring what you can carry for the climb and a means of desalinating or of collecting — though collecting what, on a shelf with no rain except the storm that never comes closer, is a real question. Ask the workers by chalk on your first day. It is the first thing to ask.

Food: the fish are there, and they do not know to fear a hand. But you must catch them from the shallows at the rim, from the flat iron boats or from the edge, and you cannot go in the water, and you should not eat anything that fluoresces orange until someone who has lived there tells you it is safe. Bring enough for the climb and the first week, and expect to live on pale eyeless fish after that.

---

**The climb**

You will hear it for days before you reach the lip. Use that time. It comes on slowly, a rumour in the ground and then a presence in the air, and by the second day of it you will be able to tell whether your body is going to accept it or fight it. Pay attention. If you find yourself clenching, if your sleep is going wrong in a way that is not the altitude, be honest about whether you should turn around at the switchback before the last one.

Climb slowly. The altitude is real and the rock punishes haste. Put on the hearing protection well before you think you need it; the roar rises faster than you expect once you are near the top, and there is a moment — everyone describes it — where it stops being a thing you hear and becomes a thing you are standing inside, and you want your ears already sealed when that moment arrives.

Then the lip. Stop there. Sit down, if you can find a place to sit that does not cut you. Look. Grey water level as a spirit gauge, shivering, no far shore, iron towers standing up out of it miles apart, and somewhere out on it a wall of rain standing still. Let your ribs learn the note. Do not go to the edge of the water yet.

---

**On the shelf**

Stay away from the water's edge until you have watched it for a while. The shore is a knife, the water is two degrees above freezing, and the water near the edge trembles in a lattice that never moves. If you fall in you have cold shock first and the vibration after, and the vibration in even the shallows is enough to hurt; further out it bursts eardrums in seconds and takes the inner ear apart after. Nobody swims. Do not be the person who tests it.

Do not go toward the towers. Not by boat, not by any route. The gyres around them are slow and permanent and the water there is darker and the note is loudest and the plating is worn to mirror by shaking alone. The boats go out only along the rim, and the people in them wear hoods and still come back with their heads ringing.

Find the huts. Go to the nearest one and stand outside it where you can be seen and hold up your chalk. The people who work the shelf are few and quiet and not unfriendly, but they have made their peace with something and they do not have a lot to spare for someone who has not. Ask short questions. Offer to help with the fish. Learn their hands.

Face your shelter away from the water. Every hut on the shelf has its windows turned to the rock, and it is not for the view.

Expect the first few nights to be terrible. You will not sleep, or you will sleep in fragments, and you will wake with your jaw clenched and your heart going. This passes for most people by the fourth or fifth night. If it has not passed by the tenth, go down.

Expect the animals to arrive without warning. The water can be empty for an hour and then thick. They are deaf and blind and unafraid and they will come to your hand. The fish are fine to eat and the ribbon things are not for eating. If you see a back, or a fin, or a long slow displacement off the northern towers, do not do anything. It is not interested in you. Note that the rock under your feet shifts, very slightly, when it surfaces, and then note that the note itself did not change, and think about what that means about the size of the bell.

Expect the light to be ordinary, and expect that to unsettle you more than gloom would. A hard blue sky, sharp shadows off the towers, every rivet visible a mile away. It looks like a place. It sounds like the end of the world. The gap between those two things is a large part of what the shelf does to a mind, and the best defence is simply to have been told.

---

**What it does over time**

Deafness comes within a year even with the hoods. The workers know this and most leave within a season, and you should plan to. Set a date before you climb and chalk it somewhere you will see it every day, because the shelf is very good at making dates seem unimportant. Nothing changes there — no tides, no seasons, no weather except the one that stands still — and a place where nothing changes does not remind you that time is passing.

There is a pull. It is worth naming. After the body stops panicking, after the sound becomes pressure and the pressure becomes the shape of the world, some people find that the shelf is the calmest place they have ever been. Everything is settled. The engines pump, the storm stands, the gyres turn, the note holds. Nothing wants anything from you. The animals come to your hand. And the thought arrives, quietly, that going down means going back to a world where things end and start and change and demand, and that here they do not, and that you could just stay a little longer.

That is when you go down. Not because the thought is wrong — it may be right — but because you should make that decision at sea level with your hearing intact, and not on a shelf where the sound has been deciding things for you for weeks.

The one in the southern hut has been up there eleven years and communicates by flag. The board outside says STILL RINGING. Go and look at it if you want. Then look at your own board, at the date you chalked, and start down before the light goes.

---

**Coming down**

Keep the hearing protection on until the third switchback. The roar drops slower on the descent than it rose; you will think it is safe before it is.

Your ears will ring for weeks after. Your sense of balance will be off. Your sleep will be strange, because the silence is wrong now — you have spent a season inside something that filled every space, and ordinary quiet will feel like a held breath. People report waking in the night listening for it. Some report that they never fully stop.

Have someone to talk to when you get down. Not about the shelf necessarily. Just talk. Use your voice. Hear a voice answer. It takes a few days for that to feel like a thing you are allowed to do again.

And check your teeth.
