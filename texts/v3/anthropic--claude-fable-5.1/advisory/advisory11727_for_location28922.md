**Notes for a visitor to the hollow city**

The first thing to understand is that this place will not hurt you. Nothing in it wants anything from you; nothing in it is hungry in your direction. The badger is not going to come out. The stone is sound. You could, in principle, walk down through all eight tiers and back up again with nothing worse than wet knees. Most of the preparation is not against danger in the ordinary sense. It is against the place's one real property, which is that it *keeps* things — and you are a thing, and you will be tired, and there will be a great many ledges at exactly the right height to sit on.

So: prepare as for a cold wet climb, and prepare as for a place that would be perfectly content if you never left it.

---

**Getting there, and the lip**

The fen above is the only part of this that behaves like ordinary country, and it is not very kind country. Wind from the west, unbroken; ground that is firm turf in one stride and ankle-deep water in the next; nothing to steer by but a few thorn trees that all lean the same way. You will need waterproof boots to the shin, and you will need a compass or something that does the job of one, because the hollow has no rise, no marker, no ring of stones, no change in the grass. People have described walking within twenty paces of it and not knowing.

Which means the other thing about arriving: you may arrive at the edge without warning. The ground simply opens. If you are walking with your head down against the wind you can put a foot over. Walk slowly once you think you're close, and walk *into* the wind rather than with it, because with the wind at your back you lean forward and the lip is a step you do not know is there. Better still, probe ahead with a stick. It is undignified and it works.

When you do find the edge, stop. Look down before you go down. From the pasture you see straight into the top storey — terraces, ladders, the heron on the south side — and this is the only place you will have a view of the whole. Fix the layout in your head. Once you are inside, the tiers hide one another and the light gives you no help.

**Cold, and what to wear against it**

The cold down there is not the cold of the fen. Up top it is a moving cold; you can walk it off. Down in the hollow the air does not move at all, and it is saturated, and the stone is colder than the air. It is a cold that comes through fabric by conduction — every time you lean on a wall, kneel on a tread, put a hand on a ladder rung — and it does not stop when you stand up. Think of it as the cold of a cellar in which someone has left a hose running for a thousand years.

Wool, not down. Down collapses when it is damp and everything here is damp. Two or three wool layers over a synthetic base that wicks, and a shell over that which is windproof mostly for the walk in and out — down in the hole you may find yourself taking the shell off because it holds the wet against you. A wool hat, and a spare in a sealed bag, because the first one will be soaked from lintel-drips within the hour. Gloves that are thin enough to feel the stone through and grippy enough to hold wet ash. Bring two pairs.

Your knees and the top of your head will take the punishment. The doorways are for someone bent double, and the lintels are wet and hard and exactly at the height of a bowed forehead. Soft kneepads under the trousers, and either a beanie thick enough to take a knock or a low-profile climbing helmet if you can bear to. Choose the helmet. Nobody is watching except the wrens.

Footwear is the one place where the place's comfort and your own agree. Hard-soled boots will skate on liverwort and tear the moss off the treads. Wear something with a soft, flexible rubber sole — approach shoes, or the kind of boot a drystone waller wears — and you will hold better and do less harm. Carry the fen boots in your pack and change at the lip. Yes, your feet will get wet either way. The point is grip.

**Moving through it**

Ladders first. They are split ash, silver, old beyond guessing, and nothing here rots, so they are probably sound. Test them anyway; hold the stiles, not the rungs, and keep three points on. They lean between the tiers at an angle that was right for someone shorter than you. The flights of stairs — nine and eleven, always — were cut to a rise a body finds without thinking, and that is true, and it is also true that they are narrow, wet, and turn at the corners over drops of a full storey. Face the wall on the descent.

Then the three-foot rule: nothing here is more than three feet from something else. That is the description's phrase and you should take it as a warning about your shoulders and your pack. Pack small. A day sack, nothing on the outside of it, no trekking poles strapped on to catch on doorframes. You will spend much of your time sideways or bent, and anything that sticks out will hook a lintel or knock a shell-lamp off a ledge — and if it does, see below, because the lamp will *stay* where it lands, and that is now what the place looks like.

Go slowly. Everyone who goes down says the same thing, that within a few minutes they were moving at the pace of the place without having decided to. Let that happen. It is the right speed for wet stone in poor depth perception. Just notice that it is happening.

**The light**

There are no shadows. You will have read that and not understood what it means for your feet. Edges disappear — the rim of a cistern, the lip of a step, the point where a terrace stops and a drop begins. The water in the cisterns is so still and so clear that it reads as a square of pale floor. People have stepped into them.

Go down with the expectation that your eyes are lying about depth, and check every edge with a foot before you trust it with your weight. A stick helps here too. So does a companion walking a pace behind who can tell you when your heel is at a rim — except that they can't tell you, because of the silence, which brings us to:

**Sound, and having company**

Speech stops a foot from your mouth. This is not an exaggeration you can shout your way out of. If you go in with a companion, agree a set of touch signals on the pasture beforehand: a hand on the shoulder for *stop*, two taps for *look*, a squeeze on the arm for *time to go*. Keep within arm's reach of each other, because you will not hear a slip, a fall, or a call, and the tiers hide one another after a very short distance.

There is a subtler thing about the silence. Most people go quiet within minutes without trying to. Then, after a while longer, they stop wanting to speak, and then they stop wanting to move much, and this is the beginning of the thing you are here to resist. So: decide in advance that you will deliberately break your own stillness at intervals — flex your hands, roll your shoulders, shift your weight. Not to be loud; the place won't let you. Just to remind your body that it is a visitor and not a fixture.

Do not go alone if you can help it. Not because of the animals. Because it is much easier to leave with someone tapping your arm.

**The rules that matter**

The place has one law and everything else follows from it: *what is put down, stays.*

Put nothing down. Not a glove while you retie a boot. Not your pack while you rest. Not a water bottle on a ledge for a moment. The moss will not eat it and the damp will not rot it; it will simply be there, and become part of the arrangement, and your glove will have a collar of moss growing up to it and stopping, next to the mallet, forever. Everything you bring in is attached to you — clipped, lanyarded, in a pocket — and everything you bring in leaves with you.

Take nothing. The mallet, the bone point, the cord, the three stacked stones on the second tier whose meaning is not available. You will want the mallet; it is beautiful and it fits the hand. Leave it. The balance this place has arrived at includes the mallet, and it is not yours to find out what happens when the count is wrong.

Don't drink from the cisterns. The water is almost certainly clean. It is also *full to within a finger of the rim and stays there*, and you are not going to test what the place does about a level that drops. Carry your own water — inside your jacket, or it will be near freezing — and drink it standing.

Bring no fire. The hearths have no soot; the shell-lamps have a black residue that may never have burned. Whatever the reason, fire is not part of what has been agreed here. A cold electric light is fine to carry but you will not need it — the chambers are lit from somewhere around the corners — and you should not go into the chambers far anyway. They go back into the earth, they are low, and there is no way to know their length. Look in. Do not go in past the point where you can see the doorway behind you.

Don't move the animals. The toads have the lower doorways and if there is a toad in the doorway you want, that is not your doorway. Step round the snails where you can; you won't manage always. Give the stoat the right of way. Leave the heron's terrace alone entirely. Whatever the badger is — tenant, magistrate, or just a badger — the chamber with the black smell is closed to you. This is not sentiment. Every creature here is at a number, and the number holds, and you are not part of it.

**Time**

You cannot rely on your own sense of how long you have been down. There is no sun to move — the light comes from everywhere — the frost does not melt, the flower does not close, nothing marks an hour. People who have been in for what they thought was twenty minutes have come out to find the day gone. Others have come out and found less time passed than they expected. Both are the place's doing.

Carry a timepiece that does not depend on anything in the hollow: a wound watch, a mechanical timer. Set it before you go over the lip and put it where you will feel it — against your wrist or your chest — because you won't hear it. Give yourself a hard limit. Two hours is generous. When it goes off, you leave, not from where you are but from wherever you have to walk to reach the nearest flight up. Do not finish looking at the thing you are looking at.

If you can, leave someone on the pasture above with a rope. Not to haul you out; to lower one end over the lip at the agreed time. You will see it. You will not hear anything they shout.

**Coming out**

You climb the last flight and the wind hits you and it is *enormous*. Loud in a way you had forgotten sound could be. You will stagger. Sit down on the grass — the grass is fine; the grass is ordinary — and let your ears come back, and drink something, and change your boots.

Then, before you walk away: mark the place. From the outside, on the pasture, not from within. A stake, a cairn of your own stones brought from elsewhere, a heap of the fen's turf. The ground closes over without a rise or marker and you will not find it again without help. Whether you *want* to find it again is a question to answer once you are somewhere warm.

**Who should not go**

The tired. The grieving. Anyone who has lately felt that they have finished something and would like, for a while, to be finished. The hollow is very kind to that feeling. It is a town at its full and proper population, at rest inside its holding, requiring nothing from anyone, and the ledges are at just the right height, and the cold is a cold that stops bothering you after a time, and the light is the light of a place that has nothing left to hide. It would take you in with a collar of moss and stop there. It would not mean any harm by it.

Go when you are restless. Go with someone who will tap your arm. Leave before you want to.
