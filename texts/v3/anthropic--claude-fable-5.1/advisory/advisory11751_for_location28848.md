# Preparing for the Upland Mat

## First, the two things that will kill you quietly

Everything dramatic about this place — the tongues, the spires, the cracking — is survivable with care. The two things most likely to end a visit are boring: the altitude, and the absence of water. Deal with these first and everything else becomes manageable.

**The altitude.** You are going somewhere above the cloud-floor of an entire world. The sky is bruised because there is not enough atmosphere above you to scatter blue. Your body will not have enough oxygen. The usual response to altitude sickness — descend, quickly, a thousand meters or more — is not available here. There is no "down." Down is a grey shelf of vapor at the plateau's edge, hours or days away, and below it is a planet you know nothing about. You cannot plan to bail out.

So arrive acclimatized. Spend time somewhere high but survivable before you cross. Carry acetazolamide and know how to use it; carry dexamethasone and know that if you need it, your visit is over and you should be leaving by whatever route brought you. Bring a pulse oximeter and check it more often than feels necessary, because hypoxia's first symptom is not noticing hypoxia. Bring supplemental oxygen if you can — a small bottle for sleeping, at least, because sleep is when altitude does its damage, and you will already be sleeping badly for other reasons.

Expect to be slow. The locals are slow; they are thin and swaddled and they move like people conserving something, and you should copy them. Do not walk fast on the mat. Do not carry heavy packs long distances. Sit down before you feel you need to.

**The water.** There is none. Read that again as a physical fact and not as a poetic one. The air is so dry your breath does not fog. Every exhalation takes water out of you and nothing puts it back. Your skin will crack in a day. Your eyes will hurt. The plant's solvent is an oil — pine resin and cold coins — and you cannot drink it, and you should be very cautious about the distilled fraction the townspeople drink, because they have had a thousand generations to adapt to it and you have had none.

Bring every drop you will drink, plus a margin, and let the water be your clock. When it is half gone, turn around. This is the single most important rule of the place. Water is heavy and you are at altitude and this is going to constrain how long you stay to a few days at most, and that is correct. This is not somewhere to linger on a first visit.

Also bring: lip balm, a heavy skin barrier cream, saline eye drops, saline nasal spray. Nosebleeds are near-certain. Breathe through a scarf or a mask to catch some of your own moisture. The locals are swaddled to the eyes; that is not only for cold.

## The cold and the sun

The sun gives light without warmth. Up here the radiation reaching you is stronger than the warmth would suggest — thin air filters little. Cover skin. Wear dark glasses even though the plain is dark, because the sky is bright in ways the eye doesn't register, and because the dry air will strip your corneas without help. Sunburn on the backs of hands and the tops of ears is easy to get and slow to heal at altitude with no moisture.

The cold is dry cold, which lies. It does not feel as cold as it is. Frostbite here will arrive without the wet ache that usually warns you. Check your fingers and toes on a schedule, not on a feeling. Layer in wool and natural fibers rather than synthetics — partly for warmth, but mostly because of the next section.

Dusk is the dangerous hour. When the shadow-line crosses, the temperature drops fast enough to hear. Be indoors, or at least be still and layered, before the cracking starts to roll toward you. The plain announces the cold a few minutes before it lands. Learn to use that.

## Fire, vapor, and static

Think about what this place is: a continent-sized sheet of living tissue saturated with an oil, venting volatile vapor from every seam, in air so dry that your clothing will spark. Add the violet gas discharge in the spire crowns. This is an atmosphere waiting for a reason.

**No open flame. Ever.** No lighters, no matches, no stove that burns. Bring chemical heat packs and a battery. The townspeople cook with the solvent, which means it is not casually flammable at ambient temperature — but the vapor is another matter, and it pools, and you cannot see where it has pooled at night.

**Static.** Synthetic fleece and nylon shells will crackle here. Wear wool and cotton against the skin and choose outer layers that don't build charge. Touch the mat with your bare hand before you handle anything metal; it seems to ground reasonably well, being wet with something conductive-smelling. A small metallic taste in the mouth, a prickle on the arm hair — treat these as warnings and move to higher ground, which on this plain means "onto a ridge."

**The vapor.** The pale mist that vents from the cracks is heavier than air and sags into low places. Do not breathe it. The locals are careful about this and so should you be. Carry a half-face respirator with organic-vapor cartridges and wear it whenever you are out at dusk, whenever you are in a hollow, whenever the cracking is fast. Carry a simple combustible-gas detector if you can source one — even a cheap one — and believe it when it complains. Sleep on ridges or in the town, never in a fold.

When a crack sounds directly beneath your feet, step sideways immediately and hold your breath. The jet is brief but it comes up hot and fast and you do not want a face full of it.

**Contamination.** The solvent gets into everything. It will soften rubber gaskets, swell some plastics, cloud some lenses, and leave a resinous film on optics and skin. Test your gear against a strong terpene solvent before you go and leave behind whatever fails. Bring nitrile gloves in quantity for anything involving touching the mat — the oil is probably not acutely toxic, since the locals bathe in it, but it will strip your skin, and you have no water to wash it off with. Bring wet wipes. Many. They will feel absurdly luxurious.

## Walking on it

The mat is not ground. It gives under your boot and pushes back, and it pulses, and after an hour of walking your legs will feel the way legs feel after a day on a trampoline. Your gait will adjust and your knees will complain. Plan short distances.

The scales are waxy. Dry, they grip; where oil has beaded along a seam or a scraped patch, they are slick as ice. Boots with soft, deeply lugged soles do better than hard ones. Bring a staff — the locals carry staves for signaling, and you will want one for the same reason and also for probing.

Because: **the pores.** They are the width of an arm, puckered, dark, and you will step over dozens without noticing. What comes out of them takes under a second. There is no fighting it and no outrunning it. This is the primary lethal hazard of the open plain, and the rule is simple: *do not walk alone on the open mat.* Go with someone from the town who knows the ground. Watch where they step and step there. If you must move on your own, walk on the healed ridges — the old seams — which are raised, denser, and appear to have fewer pores. Probe ahead with your staff. Watch the scraper herds: they graze where the pores are quiet, and they scatter outward in a ring when a tongue takes one, and that ring tells you where a mouth is.

Learn the stamping rhythm. The children at the perimeter do it every afternoon; ask to join them, or at least to watch, until you can produce the pattern with your staff. It is not a guarantee. The tongues near town have learned it over generations; the ones out on the open plain have not. But it is the one thing you can do that the local ecology recognizes as *this thing is not food.*

Do not put your hand into a pore. Do not put anything into a pore. Do not sit down next to one.

## The scrapers and the gliders

The scrapers are not dangerous in intent. They are dangerous in mass. A herd is dozens or hundreds of armored plates, some the size of doors, moving in a body, and they do not route around you. If a herd is coming, get onto a ridge, stand your staff upright, and let them flow past. Do not lie down. Do not try to walk through them. Their carapace edges are sharp enough to pry up scales, which means sharp enough to open your shin. The rasping is very loud up close and you will feel it through your boots.

Walking behind a herd is actually the safest way to cross open mat — the ground is scraped pale, the pores are visible, and the tongues have just fed. The locals do this. Watch for it.

The gliders are enormous and weigh almost nothing. They are not known to attack anything on the ground. They nest in the spire crowns, they cry two notes all night, and the only real hazard is being startled by a translucent wing the width of a house passing between you and the stars. Do not approach nests. Do not handle the oily seed-dust they carry if you can help it — it is the plant's, and it thickens the mat wherever it lands, and you do not want to find out what it does in a wound.

## The sound

You will not be able to think clearly for the first day. The drone lives in the sternum and it does something to the mind — a low-grade unease, a difficulty concentrating, a tendency to nausea — that infrasound reliably does to humans. It fades. Not because it stops but because you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as the shape of the room. Give yourself that day. Do nothing important during it.

Bring good earplugs and over-ear protection both, and wear the plugs to sleep. Bring more plugs than you think — they get oily and stop sealing. The cracking at dusk is loud enough to damage hearing over time and you will want to be able to hear the gliders, the herd, and your guide.

Communicating over the drone is hard. The townspeople speak in a pitched, half-sung register that carries. You will not manage this immediately. Bring a whistle. Agree on hand signals with your guide before you go out. Learn to feel messages in the mat — a stave struck at a distance arrives as a shudder underfoot before it arrives as a sound, and once you know to attend to it, you will.

The silence ring around each spire — about ten meters out — is disorienting to step into. The drone simply stops. Your ears ring. Some people find it intolerable and some find it the only relief on the whole plateau. Either way, do not use it as a place to rest without watching the ground, because you have just lost your primary way of hearing a pore open.

## The spires

Do not touch them. This is the only rule the locals will insist on, and you should adopt it without needing to know why.

Do not chip them. The material stays warm for days and then becomes something else, and nobody knows what is happening inside a chip during those days, and you do not want it in your pocket. "Not entirely present" is a phrase used by people who have watched things pass through the fluting that should not fit. A spire is a machine still running its original program on an unknown power source. Treat it as you would a live reactor of unfamiliar design: with curiosity, at a distance.

Do not stand in the vapor column at dusk. It is the plain's whole day of exhalation being drawn up into the crown, concentrated, and you have already agreed not to breathe that. Stand upwind and outside the silence ring. From there the violet glow and the slow turning spiral are as beautiful as anything you will see in any world, and you will be safe watching it.

If you are invited to help with the tending — clearing the band at the base where the cords enter — say yes, and do exactly what you are shown, and nothing else. This is the one thing the town does that it understands as *its job*, and being asked to help is the closest thing to being made welcome that this place offers. Wear gloves. The band is worn smooth by a thousand generations of hands and the scales there are thick and resinous. Do not touch the cords. Do not touch the shaft.

## The town

You will not see it until you know how to look. Humps, straight ridges, a thin column rising from a vent-chimney. Then a seam that is also a street, and a fold that is also a door, and the door is a muscle and it opens when someone inside decides it should.

Inside is the only warmth on the plateau. The walls are the plant, dark green-black, faintly pulsing, heated by the oil moving through them. Do not bring anything that might damage them: no blades near the walls, no flame, no hot metal. A room that has been cut will seal, but it will seal slowly and it is a gross discourtesy. The rooms are grown, over a year, around a form. They are not cheap.

The light is dim and violet and it will make everyone look ill, including you. Bring a headlamp with a red or amber filter for close work and do not shine white light into anyone's face; their eyes are adapted to something much softer.

Taps in the walls give warm solvent. It is for washing and cooking and it is *not for you to drink.* If you are offered the distilled fraction, the polite thing is to accept a small amount and hold it and taste it and thank them. Do not drink more than a mouthful. Nobody knows what a human gut does with it over time and you do not want to be the one who finds out three days from any help.

Food: the townspeople eat something, probably scraper and probably plant, and it is probably not digestible by you. Bring your own. Eat it privately if you can, because you cannot share it and they cannot share theirs, and it is easier for everyone not to make that a moment.

Sleep: they sleep through herds passing over the roof. You will not, the first night. Plugs, oxygen, and a wool cap over the ears. The room's pulse — one beat every eight or nine seconds, felt through the floor — is either soothing or maddening depending on the person. Try to decide it is soothing.

Manners: nobody here thinks of themselves as the builders' heirs or the plant's masters. They think of themselves as one more animal the plant tolerates because it is useful. The frame for your visit is the same. You are not a guest of the town; you are another creature the mat has not yet decided about. Be quiet where you can be, help where you are asked to, break nothing, and take nothing that is growing. If you want to be remembered kindly, carry something heavy for someone, or stamp the perimeter with the children, or clear scales at a spire base. Usefulness is the only currency, and it is not a metaphor.

## Do not leave things lying

The plant grows over what stays still. This is how the town makes rooms; it is also how the town will eventually make a mound where your pack used to be. A day is fine. A week and your gear will have a skin on it. Do not set down anything you want back, for longer than an afternoon, on open mat.

This applies to you. Do not sleep on the open mat. Not because of the tongues, though also because of the tongues. Because the mat is patient and you are warm and still and it has grown around better things than you.

## Navigation and getting out

The plain is flat and dark and featureless to every horizon except the spires, and the spires look alike. A compass may or may not work over a basalt shield; test it against the sun before you trust it. Count spires from the town and note their relative bearings, and note which ones have broken crowns, because that is the only individuality they have. At night they are violet points and you can steer by them, but every one looks like every other, and the town's humps vanish completely in the dark. Carry a bright beacon you can leave at your door, and a second you can carry. Walk out along a ridge and back along the same ridge.

Know how you are leaving before you arrive, and treat that route as sacred. There is no descent, no rescue, no second settlement you know of. If the altitude turns on you, or the water runs low, or you inhale something you shouldn't have, the only medicine is *elsewhere*, and elsewhere has to already be arranged.

## What it will actually be like

You will be uncomfortable the entire time — dry-mouthed, short of breath, cold at the extremities, ears ringing, mildly nauseated by a sound you can no longer hear. You will be frightened of the ground for the first day and cautious of it forever. You will not sleep well.

And at some point, probably at dusk, standing on a ridge outside the town with your respirator on and your staff planted, you will watch the shadow-line come across the plain and hear the cold arrive as a roll of gunfire, and see nine violet points wake up on nine horizons and start to drink the sky, and feel under your boots a heartbeat that is the same heartbeat a day's walk in any direction, and you will understand that you are standing on one thing that is a country and it is not dying and it does not need you.

That is what you came for. It is worth the discomfort. Prepare for the discomfort so you can stand there long enough to see it, and then go home while you still have water.
