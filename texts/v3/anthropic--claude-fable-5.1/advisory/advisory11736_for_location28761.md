**Preparing for the Sung Plain**

Start with the thing that will hurt you first, which is also the thing that makes the place what it is. You are going somewhere with a noise floor of a hundred and thirty decibels that does not stop, ever, and that does not get quieter as you move away from its sources. Understand what that means before you pack anything. An unprotected human ear begins to take permanent damage at that level within seconds. The best hearing protection you can buy — deep-seated foam plugs under heavy over-ear muffs, worn together, properly — takes perhaps thirty-five decibels off. That leaves you standing in something around ninety-five, which is a chainsaw held at arm's length, for as long as you stay. Hours of that will cost you something. Days will cost you more. You should go in having already decided that you will come out with less hearing than you had, and that the question is only how much less. If that trade is not acceptable, do not go. There is no clever equipment that changes it.

The sub-bass is a separate problem and no earplug touches it. The floor of the chord is below the range the ear reports as sound; it arrives through the sternum and the long bones and the sinuses, and at certain frequencies the eyeball itself resonates and your vision smears. The account speaks of feeling it in "the fluid of your eyes," and that is not a figure of speech. Expect blurred sight that comes and goes as the chord drifts. Expect nausea. Expect a bowel that behaves unpredictably — very low frequencies do this, and you should eat lightly and not be embarrassed. Expect a weight on the chest that feels, to the part of the brain that monitors such things, exactly like the onset of dread, and know in advance that the dread is the frequency and not a premonition. People with heart conditions, asthma, seizure disorders, inner-ear disorders, or who are pregnant should not go at all; the pressure field is not a place to find out how a compromised system responds. Motion-sickness medication helps a little. It is worth taking anyway.

Because you will not be able to speak, and no one will be able to hear you if you do, the single most important preparation is one you do at home, weeks ahead, with whoever is going with you: learn a language of hands. It does not need to be a full sign language, though that would be better. It needs to be a shared, drilled, unambiguous set of perhaps forty signs — stop, go, back, up, down, water, danger, I am sick, I cannot see, help me, camp here, how long, I want to leave. Drill them until they are reflexive, because you will be trying to use them while blurred and queasy and frightened, in tea-coloured dimness, with muffs clamped on your head. Carry waterproof slates and grease pencils as well, and a small strong light to shine on them. The tenders at the edge have been doing this for years and will have their own vocabulary; arrive early enough to learn it. Do not go in alone. Not because the place is malicious but because a person who falls on that stone, or loses their sight to a pressure node, or simply panics, cannot call for help. Stay in visual contact always. A tether is not a bad idea in the dimness under the nodes.

Now the ground. Everything you step on is a fracture edge that has never been weathered. The account says the lips are sharp enough to open a boot, and you should take that as a promise. Ordinary hiking boots will be cut. You want something with a cut-resistant upper and a thick, stiff, puncture-proof sole — the kind of boot made for glass-strewn demolition sites, not for trails — and Kevlar or leather gaiters over the shin, because you will stumble and the shelf edge will be at knee height. Wear cut-resistant gloves at all times; you will put your hands down constantly, and every surface you touch is a blade. Kneepads are not vanity. The shelves step down by a few meters at a time and you will be climbing down them, facing the rock, and the rock will be warm and the holds will be sharp. Carry a short length of rope and know how to use it for lowering packs and people.

The stone runs above body temperature from below. Think about what this does over a day. You cannot cool yourself by lying down. Your boot soles will soften if they are the wrong compound. Anything you set on the ground warms. You are walking on a stovetop set to a gentle simmer, in air that is humid near the nodes and dry between them, and your body will be shedding heat through sweat that, under the canopy, does not evaporate. Heat exhaustion is a real and likely outcome and it will feel, in the roar, like everything else already feels — heavy, sick, dizzy — so you will not recognise it. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst. Carry electrolytes. Carry more water than you think you can reasonably carry, because the pools are not to be drunk.

About the pools: they are black, still, fresh, and sitting in rock that carries a green tarnish. Green on dark grey stone in a wet place is very often copper, and water that has sat in new copper-bearing fractures is not water you want inside you, filter or no filter. Beyond the chemistry, something large lives in some of them and has not been clearly seen. Do not fill bottles at pool edges. Do not stand at pool edges at dusk. If you must take water from the plain, take it as the plain gives it back: in the mornings the warm stone sweats into the cooler air, and a tarp pitched low overnight will collect a surprising volume of clean condensation by first light. The drip from the node canopies is lifted pool water and carries whatever the pools carry, plus whatever the leaves add; treat it as suspect. Bring purification anyway for emergencies, and bring the knowledge that it may not be enough.

Bring all your food. Do not eat the fruit. The nodes have started producing it "without anyone having asked them to," which is a lovely sentence and a warning. The climbers take it and thrive; the climbers are not you.

The air carries stone dust, drawn up into the bands and rained back down at the columns as a fine grey fall. Near the nodes you are standing under a slow snow of pulverised rock, and you should have a proper particulate mask, not a bandana. The complication is that everything near the nodes is wet, and a wet mask clogs and stops working. Bring many. Change them. Between the nodes, in the dry country, the dust is thinner but the air is warm and you will be breathing hard; the mask is still worth wearing. Eye protection too — the dust, the drip, the occasional column of hot rain arriving with almost no warning, and the sharp light that comes off the fresh fracture faces.

Light is the next thing. The plain sits in a permanent late afternoon, filtered through the standing bands to the colour of weak tea, and your eyes will never fully adapt because it is neither dark nor light. Under the canopies the dimness thickens; the account says you could nearly cup it, and whatever is happening there, your headlamp will not behave the way it does at home. Test your lights at the edge before committing to them. Bring more than one, bring more batteries than seems sane, and bring at least one that throws a wide flood rather than a beam, because a tight beam in that thickened dark shows you a coin of moss and nothing else. Depth perception suffers in this light, and depth perception is what keeps your shin off the shelf edge. Move slowly. Move more slowly than you want to.

Then navigation, which is the problem that the tenders themselves have given up on. The grid is regular. The walk between two nodes is not. You will set out toward a dark vertical that is plainly three kilometres away and arrive in ninety minutes or in three hours, and the ground will not have looked different either way. This is not a thing you can fix with instruments. Whatever your usual position-finding relies on, it was built for a universe where the air does not hold the ground in place, and you should assume it will lie. Navigate by the nodes themselves — they are visible from anywhere, and you can always see the next three — and count them rather than counting steps or time. Budget supplies for the longest plausible version of every leg, then add half again. Keep the edge in sight or in memory as a direction, not a distance. Agree with your companions, before you go in, on a hard rule for turning back, and make it a rule about water and time remaining, not about how far you think you have come.

Where to rest is something the place decides for you. There are three kinds of country. At the node bases it is a rainforest floor a few hundred metres wide — wet, warm, thick with moss and root-curtain and drip, the loudest ground on the plain, and the place the animals congregate, including at dusk the things that follow the grazers. It is shade and it is water and it is not where you sleep. Between nodes it is dry warm sharp stone with nothing on it and no shelter from the vertical rain columns, and it is where you cross, fast, in daylight. And at the exact cancellation points between four nodes, there is a metre or two where the roar drops to something you could nearly speak through. These are the only places on the plain where a human body can rest without being pressed on. They are small. They are shared with pale things that have never lost their hearing and cannot leave, and you should treat them as a guest treats a household — pitch your tarp at the edge of the quiet, not the centre, do not handle the creatures, do not leave anything behind. Finding a cancellation point is a matter of walking a slow grid with your muffs on until the pressure lifts, and then marking the spot, because a metre to either side and it is gone. Learn to find them. They are where you will drink, write, sign to each other, and sleep.

Sleep is worth its own thought. The ground is too warm to lie on comfortably. The field lifts small light things — water in threads, dust, floaters the size of a hand — and while you are far too heavy to be taken, your hat is not, your map is not, a loose mask or a sock or a food wrapper is not. Weigh down or tie down everything. Sleep with your pack tethered to you. Do not pitch anything against the base of a node; the columns are growing at a rate you can nearly watch, and the arms and shelves extrude along lines of pressure that do not care what is beneath them. A camp that is clear at dusk may be leaned on by morning.

The animals need the same humility. Everything on the ground is deaf. You cannot warn a grazer you are coming; you cannot cough or shout or clap. The broad flat many-legged things cropping moss in the rain-shadow will not know you are there until they see you, and a startled armoured animal the size of a table has options you do not. Approach in their line of sight, slowly, from the front. Give the soft-billed herons their pools. The climbers in the root-curtains are quick and will steal — food, bright objects, anything small — and are the closest thing to ordinary wildlife you will meet; do not feed them, do not chase them, and do not follow them up. The ear-birds hunt at the band edges and are unlikely to trouble something your size, but they navigate by the shape you make in the field, and you make a large strange shape; if a flock alters course to circle you, stand still and let it read you and move on. At deep dusk, when the second darkness comes down on top of the first, the climbers go still and the low things move out from wherever they spend the day. Be in your quiet point by then, with your light off and your body small. The account does not say what follows the grazers. It does say the pools ripple with something large. Treat both as reasons to be elsewhere after dark.

There is a stranger preparation, and it is a preparation of attitude. The nodes listen. The chord drifts to follow what lives here — a hunting line, a population boom, the footfalls in the moss. You are a footfall. A single traveler passing through for a day is almost certainly beneath its notice. A party that camps in the same cancellation point three nights running, that crosses the same gap at the same hour, that establishes any pattern at all, may find on the third morning that the quiet has widened slightly to accommodate them, or that a shelf has grown a lip at the height of a resting hand. The tenders say this last and quietly. Take it as they mean it: you are inside something that is being made, and it is being made for whoever arrives, and if you arrive with intention the making will register you. Decide before you go in whether you want that. If you do not, vary your route, stay briefly, and leave nothing. If you do, understand that you are taking a small hand in the shaping of a city that was never for you, and that the animals who came first have a prior claim. Nothing there will stop you. That is precisely why you should stop yourself.

Practically, this means: short visits. Day walks from the acoustic shelters at the edge, returning before the deep dusk, are the sane pattern. One night in a cancellation point is an expedition. Longer than that and the hearing loss compounds, the heat compounds, the infrasound sickness compounds, and the place begins to notice you. The tenders live in shelters at the boundary for a reason.

Give the edge its due. The sound arrives a day's walk out, as pressure before it is noise, and the people who live at that distance have stopped noticing that they shout. Spend a day there before going in. Let your body learn the weight on the chest while you can still take the muffs off. Learn the tenders' signs. Ask them what has changed since the last person came through, because something will have. Ask them where the current cancellation points are; the points drift as the chord drifts. And when you come out, come out slowly, and stay at the edge again for a day. The silence beyond the field is not a relief at first. It is a hole where the pressure was, and the body reads the sudden absence as wrongness — a vertigo, a lightness, a sense that something has stopped that should not have. Your ears will ring for days. Your hearing will come back partially over a week or two and then stop coming back, and what does not return is gone. You will find that ordinary speech sounds thin and odd, and that your own voice, when you first hear it again, belongs to a stranger.

Pack, then, for a hot wet demolition site in permanent dusk where you cannot speak and cannot trust distance and where the ground is a knife. Pack double hearing protection and accept it is not enough. Pack cut-proof boots and gloves and gaiters, a wide-flood headlamp and spares, a low tarp and cord and weights, particulate masks in quantity, eye protection, electrolytes, all your water, all your food, waterproof slates, motion-sickness tablets, and a drilled vocabulary of forty signs shared with someone you trust. Pack a rope. Pack the hard rule for turning back, written down, agreed. Leave behind anything you would grieve to lose to a climber or to the field. Leave behind, if you can, the assumption that you are the audience. You are not. You are a footfall in a place that is learning what lives here, and the honest thing — the safe thing, which for once is the same — is to make that footfall light, and brief, and to be gone before the chord has finished turning to see who it was.
