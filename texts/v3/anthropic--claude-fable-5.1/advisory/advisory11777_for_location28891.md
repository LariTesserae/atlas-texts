**Before you go**

Understand what kind of place this is before you pack for it. It is not a wilderness. It is a room—three hundred paces by less than that, with a floor that is hot, a back wall that cannot be climbed, two sides that pinch shut, and one wall that is a drop into cloud. You will see all of it in the first hour. Everything after that is the harder part: staying there, in wet heat, on sharp rock, near three objects that do not explain themselves, without hurting yourself or touching what you should not.

Almost everything that could kill you here is ordinary. The heat. The edge. The fog. The rock. Prepare for those first and hardest. The strange things are, on the evidence, patient and indifferent, and will mostly leave you alone if you leave them alone.

---

**The heat is the real danger**

The rock gives back more than the sun puts in. That means the ground is a stove, the air is saturated, and your body's one cooling trick—sweating—does not work, because sweat cannot evaporate into air that is already full of water. You will be wet from the first minute and it will not cool you. This is the exact recipe for heat exhaustion, and heat exhaustion makes you clumsy, and clumsy on this shelf is how you end up in a pool, or a hole, or over the lip.

So:

- Do not exert. Walk slowly. Sit often. Treat every task as if you were at high altitude, because you are—the air *should* be thin here, and thick with water is not the same as thick with oxygen. Expect a headache the first day. Expect to be more breathless than the terrain justifies.
- Drink far more than feels necessary and replace salt. Bring electrolytes in quantity. Water you will have; what you'll lose is everything dissolved in it.
- Wear less than instinct says. A broad hat, loose light long sleeves (for the sun and the rock, not warmth), and nothing that holds water against your skin. A rain shell in the cloud will cook you. An umbrella is a better tool here than a jacket; it keeps the drip off without sealing you in.
- Sunscreen, and a lot of it, reapplied constantly because the wet will take it off. The sky is "hard" and close. You are high. You will burn through the haze, and burnt skin cannot regulate heat.
- Dry your feet whenever you sit. Skin that is warm and wet for days macerates, splits, grows things. Bring a second pair of socks and swap them into the dry when the cloud lifts; hang the wet pair off a fern where the sun can find them. Powder helps. Nothing here will dry completely, so aim for *less wet*.

If you find you cannot think clearly, stop where you are, sit against the mountain wall (not near the lip, not near the hole), get something with salt in you, and wait for the evening cloud. It is body-temperature, which is at least cooler than the ground.

---

**The rock**

It is young, and young stone is a blade. Vesicular, glassy, fractured clean, with no rounding anywhere. Thin-soled shoes will be cut through in a day and the ground beneath them is hot enough that the soles may soften. Bring the thickest, stiffest boots you own, and gloves—leather or something that can take a fall onto obsidian-edged basalt. You *will* put a hand down at some point. Make sure it is a gloved hand.

The moss is a trap of a gentle kind. It is thick, saturated, warm, and it gives like a mattress—and it hides the cracks beneath it, some of which hold water as hot as a bath and some of which may be hotter. Probe before you step. A trekking pole or a cut horsetail stem, pushed ahead of you into any moss you have not already crossed. The pools have a skin of mineral bloom that can look like solid ground in flat light.

Do not sit or lie directly on bare rock in the open. Sit on moss, or on the fern-trunk floor of the shelter, which its builder laid down for exactly this reason. Where the description says the ground is "too hot to lie on," believe it.

---

**The edge and the fog**

Twice a day—late morning and again toward evening—visibility drops to ten paces and everything becomes one continuous drip. The lip of the shelf, the hole, and the moat of ice-water around the cold block all vanish. The rock is wet and slick. This is the hour that kills visitors to places like this, and it happens on schedule, which means you can plan for it.

Rules:

- Do not move during the cloud unless you must. Be sitting somewhere safe before it arrives. You will feel it coming: the drip thickens, the ferns bow, the light goes flat.
- On your first clear morning, before you do anything else, lay guide lines. Bring cord—a lot of it, several hundred meters—and something to anchor it. Stakes will not go into this rock, but there are ferns and horsetail stands, and the seam's moss stripe runs straight and is a landmark in itself. Run a line from the shelter to wherever you sleep, if not the same place. Run a line from the shelter to your arrival point. Run a line that *stops* well short of the hole and tie something you can feel in the dark at its end.
- Put a physical ring around the hole. Not chalk, not paint—nothing sticks to running rock. Cairns of loose stone, knee-high, a full circle, at least three paces back from the rim. There is no rim, no spoil, no lip; in fog the hole is a slightly warmer patch of air with a faint orange tint and nothing else. Your feet will not warn you.
- Never approach the lip in cloud. Never approach it at all closer than you'd stand from the edge of a wet roof. You cannot see how far down the world resumes, and the water running toward the edge is running over rock that has never been worn smooth and will roll under a boot like marbles.
- A headlamp is close to useless in the cloud; it lights the water in the air and nothing else. Bring one for inside the shelter and do not rely on it outside.

Know where you arrived, exactly. If your way home depends on standing on a particular spot, mark it with a cairn and a line before you take one more step.

---

**Air**

Steam off young volcanic rock is rarely just water. Assume there may be carbon dioxide pooling in low fractures and in the hole, and possibly worse. You cannot smell carbon dioxide. Warning signs are a headache you attribute to altitude, then breathlessness you attribute to heat, then nothing.

Do not put your head down into any crack or pool. Do not lean over the hole—the air rising from it is warmer than the shelf and steady, which suggests it is pushing gas *up* and out rather than pooling, but "suggests" is not enough to bet a face on. If you have a gas meter, bring it. If you do not, a lighter held at ground level in the mornings is a crude check: if the flame struggles or dies low to the rock, get up and move to the high end of the shelf near the wall. Sleep with the shelter's opening toward the drop, where the air moves.

---

**Water**

You are surrounded by it and most of it you should not drink. The pools are hot mineral water with a bloom on them—white going blue—which on young volcanic ground means dissolved things you do not want. Not for drinking, not for washing cuts.

Condensation is your source. It gathers on every downward-facing surface. Rig a broad leaf or a sheet under a fern frond and you will collect a cup in an hour without effort. In the cloud you can collect it from your own umbrella. Still boil it if you can—everything here is warm and wet and biologically busy—but it is the cleanest water on the shelf.

The moat around the cold block is the other option. It is ice-water in a place where nothing else is cold, and there is a strong temptation to drink it and a stronger one to put your feet in it. Approach cautiously. Nothing grows near the block, and that may only be the cold, or may not be. Take a little the first day. Wait. If you are fine, the frost shed off its faces is the purest water you will find anywhere—it is the shelf's own breath, frozen. But do not touch the block itself with bare wet skin. Anything cold enough to grow frost in air this hot is cold enough to weld your palm to it.

---

**The three things that were made**

Here is the general principle: they are conditions, not machines. The seam is a rule water obeys. The block is a place that is simply cold. The hole is where rock has agreed to be absent. Nothing you have found has a switch, and things without switches cannot be turned off, which also means they cannot be turned on by accident. They have been here as long as the shelf and have not, on the evidence, harmed the ferns, the moss, the lizard, or the person who built the shelter. Treat them as fixed features of the landscape, with the respect you'd give a cliff or a hot spring.

*The seam.* You will have to decide whether to cross it. The plants avoid the downhill edge, which is a mild warning; the lizard crosses freely, which is a mild reassurance. If you must cross, do it in clear weather, step over it rather than on it, and do not put anything into it—no fingers, no pole, no dropped coin to see what happens. Do not try to force water across it. The straight stripe of moss on the wet side is the best landmark on the shelf and your guide lines can follow it; use it. Note where the shelter stands relative to the seam and camp on the same side. Whoever built it had longer to think about this than you will.

*The cold block.* It is the emptiest place here and the most restful to look at. Sit at the edge of its ring, not in it—the frost shedding makes footing in the moat treacherous and the fog that pools around its base is thick enough to hide the ice. Do not chip at it. Do not try to warm a patch of it with your hand to see if the frost comes back. It will, and your hand will have paid for the information.

*The hole.* Stay behind your cairns. Look at it in daylight, from a distance, once, so you know what the orange looks like and can recognize it in fog. Then leave it alone. Do not drop things in—the previous visitor, and you in your first hour, will have already established that nothing comes back. Do not shout down it. Do not rig a line and descend; the walls are poured glass and there is no way to know what a "far below" with a lamp in it is, or wants. If you find yourself fascinated, and you will, that is a reason to sit with your back to it for a while.

---

**The shelter and its occupant**

Someone was here, and stayed, and left things arranged. Do not rearrange them. The cup of powdered bloom, the bundled stems, the scratched stone with its line and circle—leave them exactly as found. If you use the shelter for sleep, and you should, it is the only floor here that was built to be lain on—sleep on the moss and keep your gear off the stone shelf.

Leave a note of your own. Scratch it into a flat rock, since paper will not survive a day. Say when you came, how long you stayed, and that you touched nothing. The circle on the stone could be the hole or the block; the line is the seam. If you learn which, do not add to their drawing. Make your own.

You may feel watched. In a place this small, with the cloud closing twice a day, that feeling is close to inevitable and mostly means nothing. But the roof is still alive, and the lashings still hold, and the possibilities the description raises—that they left, that they went down, that they are still here—are real possibilities. Behave as you would in someone's house when they have stepped out.

---

**The animal**

There is one lizard, or one at a time. It goes from cold to wet to warm on a circuit and does not fear you. Do not touch it, feed it, catch it, or follow it. Its throat has the seam's gray in it. Whatever it is, it belongs to the shelf in a way you do not, and it is the only thing here that visits all three made things without apparent harm. Watch it if you want to know where it is safe to stand. That is the most use it will be to you, and the most respect you can pay it.

If you ever see two, leave.

---

**How long to stay**

Not long. Two nights is enough to see both clouds twice, sleep in the shelter, watch the lizard make its round, and understand that nothing else is going to happen. The heat will wear you down after that regardless of how carefully you drink. The boredom will start to erode your caution—you will find yourself wondering what the seam feels like, or whether the block is cold all the way through, or whether the hole *really* has no bottom—and that erosion is the second most dangerous thing on the shelf after the edge.

Leave before you start experimenting. Leave in a clear window, with your lines coiled and your cairns standing for whoever is next. Take your rubbish and nothing else. Take no moss, no bloom, no horsetail stems, no frost in a bottle. The shelf is not finished and it is not yours to sample.

---

**What it will be like**

Loud in a soft way. The dripping never stops and never becomes rhythm, and after a day you will hear it in your sleep. Enclosed: the mountain at your back and the white in front are both walls, and the sky is a lid, and the fact that you can walk it in a quarter of an hour will start to matter. Beautiful in the way of very new things—the limes-green fronds, the black glass, the pools with their blue-white bloom, the cascade of visible air down the faces of the cold block. Unnerving in a quiet, low-frequency way that never spikes into fear: you will kneel at the seam and watch water stand up and refuse to fall, and something in you will keep waiting for it to fall, and it will not.

You will leave sunburnt, pruned, dehydrated, footsore, and having touched nothing. That is the correct outcome. The mountain is doing the rest, slowly, with ferns. It does not need help.
