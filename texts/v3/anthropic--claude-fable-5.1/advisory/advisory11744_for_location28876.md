**Preparing for the Roof Where the Wind Falls**

Read the description once more and notice what it does not say. It does not give a wind speed or a temperature or an altitude. It gives effects instead: rock polished past the point of having anything left to lose, sand that never stops moving, a sound that removes the sound of a stone landing at your feet. Work backward from the effects, because the effects are what you will be standing in.

The cloud floor is two thousand meters below you. The insects come up from three thousand meters below. The plateau is higher than anything for four hundred kilometers. That is very high — high enough that there is a fraction of the oxygen you are used to, high enough that the sky is the blue of deep water. The wind is steady enough to hover a bird in it and strong enough to sculpt quartzite into keels. The air is cool, wet, and never freezing. Put those three things together — thin air, hard wind, cold damp — and you have the three ordinary ways this place will hurt you. None of them is exotic. All of them are cumulative. None of them will announce itself.

---

**Before you go: your body**

The altitude is the thing you cannot buy your way past. If you can stage your journey through a world where you can spend several days at three or four thousand meters first, do it. If you cannot, plan for a short visit — hours, not days — and know the signs of altitude sickness well enough to recognize them in yourself when you are also deafened, cold, and leaning into a gale: headache, nausea, a strange clumsiness, a sense that your thinking has gone slow and thick. Every one of those symptoms can also be caused by the noise and the cold, which is the problem. You will not be able to tell which is which. Assume the worst one and act on it.

Talk to someone about acetazolamide. Bring it if you can. Bring a pulse oximeter and actually look at it.

Walking into a strong wind at that altitude is heavy exercise. You will be breathing hard within minutes. Do not plan on covering distance. Ten kilometers on this plateau is a serious day. Forty kilometers, the width of it, is not a walk. It is an expedition, and this is not a guide for that.

Get your hearing checked before and after. Not because it will reassure you, but because you should know what the place took.

---

**What to wear: the principle**

The principle is this: nothing that flaps, nothing that can be taken, nothing with a gap.

The wind is not gusty. That is worth understanding. Gusts are what make wind dangerous on ordinary mountains — the sudden shove, the lull that lets you overbalance, the lift under a jacket that turns you into a sail. Here there is one wind, one speed, one direction, forever. You will find, after a few minutes of terror, that you can lean into it at a fixed angle and stay there. Your body will learn it. But anything you wear that is not tight to you will be pulled downwind continuously and hard, and anything that comes loose is gone east before you can turn your head.

Base layers that wick. Insulating layers that work when damp — wool or synthetic, never down, because you will be damp within an hour. A shell over everything, waterproof, close-cut, with a hood that cinches around your face and not just around your head. Waterproof trousers or the sand will find your legs. Gaiters. Boots with soft, sticky rubber soles — the kind climbers use for approaches — because the rock has been polished by ten thousand years of grit and the lee sides are always running with water. Stiff-soled mountaineering boots will skate. You are not walking on scree; you are walking on wet glass with a texture.

A balaclava under the hood. Over the balaclava, sealed goggles — ski goggles with a foam seal, not sunglasses. There are two reasons. The first is sand: the troughs between the ridges carry a constant stream of it at roughly ankle to knee height, and every ridge you cross lifts some of it. The second is the insect traffic. Small pale moths and flies are being carried over this plateau at wind speed, and when you face west, they will hit your face like thrown rice. You will not want to open your mouth. Bring a buff or neck gaiter you can pull up over your nose, and plan to breathe through it.

The goggles need to be very dark and rated for full UV. You are above nearly all of the atmosphere's protection and the cloud floor is a mirror. Light comes from above and from below. Sunscreen everywhere skin might show, and put it under your nose and chin and inside your nostrils, because that is where the reflected light finds you. Lip balm with sunscreen. Reapply in the cavity.

Gloves with dexterity — thin liner gloves under waterproof shells — because you will need to operate your equipment and you cannot take your gloves off for long. Every item you carry that is smaller than your fist should be on a lanyard. Every item larger should be in a bag with a roll-top closure, because zippers full of quartzite sand stop working.

No hat that is not strapped. No scarf that trails. No map you have to unfold — if you bring one, it should be printed small and laminated and tied to you. Nothing on the outside of your pack that dangles.

---

**Hearing**

Take this seriously in a way that will feel excessive. The sound is described as something you feel in the sternum and the roots of the teeth. That is a sound pressure level that damages hearing not over years but over hours. Foam earplugs inserted properly, and over them, earmuffs of the kind used on flight lines. Both. Fit them before you arrive, not after.

Even with both, you will hear the wind. It will come through your skull. After an hour you will stop noticing it as sound and start noticing it as a kind of fatigue you cannot locate — a grinding irritability, difficulty holding a thought, a strange emotional flatness. This is what constant noise does to a nervous system. Expect it. Do not make decisions in it that you would not make tired.

There is no verbal communication on the open plateau. None. If you go with someone, agree on hand signals before you arrive and practice them until they are reflexive. At minimum: *stop*, *come here*, *I'm fine*, *I'm not fine*, *go to the cavity*, *look*, *down*. Point with your whole arm, not a finger. Bring a waterproof notebook and a pencil — not a pen — for anything more complicated, and understand you will need to be in the lee of something to use it. A whistle is useless. Any radio is useless unless it drives a bone-conduction earpiece under your muffs, and even then, plan as though it will fail.

Your sense of balance lives in your inner ear, and the description mentions the inner ear specifically. Some people will find that the sound, combined with the altitude and the constant lean, makes them unsteady in a way they cannot account for. Move slowly. Keep three points of contact when the ground is wet. Sit down if the world tilts.

---

**Arrival**

You are a traveler between worlds, so you will arrive by whatever means you arrive by, and this is the single most dangerous moment of the visit.

You will step out of a place with normal air into a place where the air is a wall. If you arrive standing upright, relaxed, facing the wrong way, you will be on your back or your face before you understand what has happened, and everything you were carrying loosely will be gone. Arrive braced. Arrive low — crouched, ideally on one knee — facing west, with your weight forward. Have your goggles on and your ears sealed before you go, not after. Have your hood cinched. It will feel absurd to do all this in whatever calm room you leave from. Do it anyway.

Choose your arrival point with care, as precisely as your means allow. You want the western or central plateau, well away from either lip, ideally in the lee of a standing rock or a large fluted ridge, where the air slows enough to let you get your bearings. You do not want the eastern edge. You do not want anywhere within a kilometer of the eastern edge. The band of balanced air where the swifts hang is two hundred meters wide, and behind it is a downdraft the size of a city. A human being who is blown into that does not come back.

Take a minute on arrival to do nothing but breathe and feel the angle. Then stand slowly.

---

**Moving**

The plateau is organized. Everything runs west to east — the ridges, the troughs, the lean of the standing rocks, the fog off the spine, the wind itself. This is your compass. You will never not know which way is west. What you may not know is how far you have gone or where you started, because every ridge is built on the same logic as every other ridge, and there is nothing below you but cloud. Mark your arrival point in your mind by its relationship to the largest formation you can see. Take a compass and a bearing anyway. A compass will probably work in a world with a magnetic field; anything that needs satellites will not.

Walking west, into the wind, is exhausting. Walking east, with it, is easy and dangerous — you will move faster than you mean to, and east is where the edge is. Walking north or south puts the wind broadside across you and is a constant fight for balance. Plan routes as a series of short legs between lees, and rest in each lee. The lee of a ridge is where the sand does not fly and the roar is fractionally less. It is also where the rock is wet. You will trade one hazard for another every time.

Two trekking poles help enormously, not as walking aids but as a third and fourth leg to plant upwind. Shorten them. Do not let go of them.

The basins are a hand deep and a few meters wide, black and flat, and because their surfaces never reflect anything they are hard to see until you are at them. Stepping into one is not dangerous in itself — cold, wet feet — but a fall on a wet quartzite rim is. Watch the ground.

Give the marmot burrows room. Do not walk over the sand-filled troughs where the burrows are; the ground there is the only soft ground on the plateau and you do not know how it is held together.

---

**Cold and wet**

The air is cool and near saturation, and it is moving fast, and you are at altitude working hard. This is the classic formula for hypothermia in people who never felt cold enough to worry. The wind strips heat from you faster than you can feel it going. The fog in the lee of the spine will soak the outside of your shell in minutes. Your breath in your buff will soak it from the inside.

Eat and drink more than you want to. Both are hard, because you cannot open your mouth in the open. Bring food you can eat quickly in a lee with your back to the wind: dense, high-calorie, not crumbly, in packaging you can open with gloves. Bring water in a bottle with a bite valve or a hose you can route under your buff. The basins are clear and probably very clean — condensed from ocean air onto bare rock — but birds and marmots use them, and you should filter or treat before drinking. Refilling from a basin means kneeling on wet rock with your back to the wind for a minute you will feel in your core. It is doable. It is not pleasant.

Watch each other for the early signs — fumbling, slowness, a sudden irrational cheerfulness or a sudden sullenness. In the noise, you will not be able to hear your companion's speech slur. You will have to see it.

---

**The eastern edge and the swifts**

You will want to see them. You should see them. Tens of thousands of birds lying on the wind in a wall above the edge, lit from below at sunset like coals, the ripple that passes through them when the falcon takes one — this is why anyone comes here.

See them from a distance. Find a standing rock or a ridge at least several hundred meters back from where the plateau ends, get in its lee, sit or lie down, and look east. The birds are large enough and numerous enough to be seen from there. Bring binoculars on a strap you have doubled. You do not need to go to the edge to see the layer. You cannot safely go to the edge. If you have rope and anchors and the skill to use them, you might belay to within sight of the drop, but ask yourself why. The wind does not stop at the lip. It goes over. It will take you with it.

The swifts will ignore you. The falcon will ignore you. The large white bird that patrols the basins may not — it is a scavenger, and it will look at you the way scavengers look at things. Do not feed it.

---

**The cavity**

At the foot of the long spine near the eastern edge, on the lee side, there is a fold in the rock four or five meters deep where the sound stops. This is the only place on the plateau where you can speak, hear, think clearly, operate anything with small parts, eat without a face full of grit, or make a decision. It is where you go if something has gone wrong. It is where you agree to meet if you are separated. Find it early. Note exactly how to get back to it.

It is also, be aware, near the eastern edge — that is where the spine is — and the fog streams past its mouth, so visibility in the immediate approach will be poor. Approach from the west, along the spine, keeping the rock on your left or right and never letting go of it.

Someone has sat there before you and knapped a hundred flakes of chert and left three points on a ledge. Leave them. Do not touch them, do not photograph them with a flash, do not move them to see them better. You are in someone's workshop. Whether they came last year or ten thousand years ago, they climbed higher than anything within four hundred kilometers to find a place to hear themselves, and they found this one, and it is theirs. It may mean there are people somewhere below the cloud. It may mean you should be prepared to meet one. Be gracious if you do; you have no idea what this place is to them.

Bring a headlamp for the cavity. It will be dim at the back even at midday.

---

**Night**

If you stay past sunset, understand that the wind will not slacken, the temperature will drop, and the fog will still stream, and there will be no ordinary place to shelter. A tent in the open is impossible. A bivy sack in the deep lee of the spine, or in the cavity itself, is survivable if you are dressed for cold and have a pad between you and the rock. The rock is wet. The stars, with nothing above you, will be extraordinary, and you will see them from inside a roar you can no longer hear. Some people will find this transcendent and some people will find it intolerable. You will not know which you are until you are there.

Most travelers should plan to leave before dark.

---

**Leaving**

Your means of leaving is your own. Whatever it is, make sure you can operate it in a gale, in the dark, with gloves on, with sand in it, and without hearing any sound it makes. If it has a small part, tie it down. If it needs calm to work, the cavity is your calm. Test it there on arrival. Have a second way out if a second way exists, because if your first fails, the only other route off this plateau is a sheer eastern face and a western lip pouring wind into three thousand meters of cloud, and you will not be walking either.

Afterward, expect the silence of wherever you go next to feel wrong — loud, somehow, or thin. Expect a ringing. Expect to be more tired than the distance you covered can account for. Expect to be sunburned in places you did not know could burn. Drink water. Sleep. Have your ears checked.

---

**What not to bring**

Do not bring a drone or anything that flies; it will be in the downdraft before you finish unpacking it. Do not bring anything you would be sad to lose, because you may lose it. Do not bring expectations of variety. Do not bring a plan that depends on covering ground.

Do not bring the idea that you will change anything. The plateau finished a long time ago. Whatever mark you think you might leave, the wind has already reached its position on the matter.

---

**What it will be like**

Overwhelming for the first hour. The noise fills every part of you that would normally be occupied by your own thoughts, and the light is a pressure, and the wind is a hand on your chest that never lifts. You will feel small and stupid and slow.

Then, if you have dressed right and moved carefully and gotten warm food into yourself in a lee, something shifts. The sound becomes a medium. The lean becomes a posture. You will find yourself standing on a bone-colored ridge in a hard blue light with a floor of cloud running to the ends of the world and a smear of birds hanging motionless over the edge of everything, and you will not be able to hear yourself breathe, and it will be — for a moment — perfectly clear why someone once carried a bag of stone up here to sit alone in the one quiet fold and work.

Then the cold will remind you, and you will go to the cavity, and you will hear the water running down the rock, and it will be the loudest thing you have ever heard.
