**Preparing for the Shelf**

Start by understanding what kind of place this is, because the ordinary categories will mislead you. It is not hostile. It is not haunted. It is not, in any way you can measure while you are standing on it, dangerous in the manner of a jungle or a glacier. It is a place that has reached its final state and is holding it, and you are the only thing on it that is still in motion toward some other state. Almost every preparation follows from that single fact.

---

**Your body**

The rock will cut you. This is the most mundane hazard and the one most likely to actually hurt you, so take it seriously before you take anything else seriously. Fresh-fractured stone with glassy planes and no weathering has edges like broken bottle glass, and it is everywhere, and it is wet. Wear cut-resistant gloves — the kind used for handling sheet metal — and never put a bare hand down to steady yourself. Wear trousers of a heavy, abrasion-resistant weave, and consider knee and shin protection, because you will slip, and when you slip on wet moss over dark stone you will not know where the edge of the moss is or what is under it. Boots should have soft, sticky rubber soles, not hard lugs; the rock is smooth and wet and there is nothing to bite into. Carry a real first-aid kit with sutures or closure strips, not just plasters. A deep clean cut on the shelf will not get infected, I think — nothing there rots faster than it should — but it will also bleed, and you are a long walk from anywhere.

Then there is the wet, which is the second thing. The air is saturated. Nothing you wear will ever dry while you are out on the stone, and your sweat will not evaporate. This means you can overheat climbing a flight of stairs and then chill through to shivering standing at the top, all in air that never changes temperature. Judging from the breath that shows, the air is perhaps eight or ten degrees Celsius, which is not cold until you have been damp in it for six hours. Dress in wool against the skin, never cotton, and a shell that sheds the drip. Carry a complete second set of clothes sealed in a dry bag, and do not open that bag until you are inside one of the buildings with the door behind you. Carry a third set of dry socks beyond what you think you need. Your feet will be the first thing to fail.

A wide-brimmed hat is worth more than it sounds. The condensation falls from every overhang, and the light, though flat and never harsh, is constant, and you will be under it for the whole of your stay.

---

**Time, which is the real problem**

There is no morning here and no evening. The sun stands a hand past noon and stays. Your body will not know when to eat or when to sleep, and left to itself it will do neither until it collapses. Travelers who go into constant-light environments on their own worlds — polar summer, deep cave systems with the lamps left on — report the same thing: without external cues, the body drifts, sleeps in short unsatisfying snatches, forgets meals, and the mind begins to fray at the edges within three or four days.

So bring your own time and impose it. A mechanical watch, wound; a second one as backup. Set a schedule before you arrive and keep it with a stubbornness that will feel absurd: eat at these hours, lie down at this hour, rise at this one. Write down each meal and each sleep in a waterproof notebook with a pencil. You will find this record oddly precious. It is the only evidence on the whole shelf that anything is passing.

Bring a sleeping mask, or plan to sleep inside the buildings, which are dark and dry and have narrow doors. Do not try to sleep in the open under that sun. You will lie there and listen to the water and your body will refuse.

You should also decide, before you go, how long you will stay, and treat that as a hard limit rather than a guideline. The place does not get more dangerous with time. That is precisely why you need the limit. Nothing will press you to leave. The temptation is not to flee; it is to stop.

---

**Navigation**

The shelf is enormous, repetitive, and almost without landmarks in the ordinary sense. One stepped cistern looks like another. One low dark building looks like the next. The walls run straight and enclose nothing, and you can follow one for twenty minutes and find it simply ends.

But you have one thing most travelers lack: the sun does not move. It is a fixed point in the sky, and that makes it a compass. Note its bearing relative to your point of arrival before you take a single step. Every direction on the shelf can be reckoned from it. Because it stands so high, shadows are short and not much use, but the sun itself will hold you oriented for as long as the sky is clear, which is always.

Mark your arrival point immediately and thoroughly. This is the one place on the shelf you must be able to find again. Do not use anything that depends on drying or setting — chalk, paint, tape will all fail on wet rock. Use objects. A bright cord tied around a stone, a cairn of three deliberately placed pieces, a piece of coloured cloth wedged in a fracture. Things set down on the shelf stay exactly as they were set down. The copper tools have been lying on their ledges for as long as anyone could say, unrusted, undisturbed, with the ore-green still on them. Your marker will be there when you return. It will be there, I suspect, long after you have stopped returning anywhere.

Which raises a question you should settle in your own conscience before you go: anything you leave here, you leave forever. Not until it decays. Forever, or as near as makes no difference. Carry out everything you can. What you must leave, leave with intention — a marker that a later traveler might be glad of, not a wrapper.

Do not expect to backtrack by your own trail. Your footprints in the moss will not hold. The animals' paths across the rock do not wear in, and yours will not either. Wherever you have walked will look, when you turn around, as though no one has ever walked there.

---

**Water and food**

Water is the one thing the shelf will give you freely. The seep is condensate from the cloud; it has run over fresh unweathered stone and through moss and nothing else. Drink from the channels rather than the cisterns — the moving sheet, not the standing pool — and filter it anyway, because caution costs nothing and the frogs sit on the channel edges with their throats still.

Food you must bring, all of it, for the whole stay, with a margin. This is not because the plants or the animals are poisonous; I have no reason to think so. It is because of the rule. Everything here is kept in exact number. The herd is always forty or fifty. The leaves fall at the rate they are made. The moss grows back at the rate it is grazed. If you take a fish from a cistern and eat it, either the shelf will replace it, in which case you have learned something you may not want to know, or it will not, in which case you have taken something from a place that has no way of making more. Either way, don't. Eat what you carried. Let the place stay in balance without you in the ledger.

Bring more food than the arithmetic says you need, because there is no day, and you will find yourself walking longer than you meant to, and hunger is one of the few clocks you will have.

---

**The buildings**

Use them. They are the only dry ground on the shelf, and that dryness is deliberate — someone laid those floors so the wet would not come in, and the work holds. The doorways are narrow; you will need to take off your pack and turn sideways. Inside, the air is still and cool and the sound of the water is muffled. This is where you change into dry clothes, eat, write, and sleep.

Do not move anything you find inside. If there is a tool set on a ledge, leave it on the ledge. If there is a stone on the floor, step around it. The interiors are as finished as everything else, and you should think of yourself as a guest in a room whose owner has stepped out and will not be back, but has not, either, entirely gone.

Do not light a fire in them. There is nothing to burn that you did not bring, and the smoke would be the only smoke the shelf has ever held, and you do not know what the place does with a thing it has never held before.

---

**The animals, and the figures**

The animals will not harm you and will not flee from you. The herd will lift its heads and look and go back to grazing. The birds will turn to watch you pass. This will feel, after the first hour, less like indifference and more like a kind of patience, and you should let that feeling pass through you without acting on it. Do not touch them. Do not feed them. Do not try to make one of the birds sing; you will see it open its beak and you will lean in and there will be nothing, and you will not like how that feels. Whether the cloud takes the sound or the birds simply never had it, the result is the same, and it is not yours to fix.

The figures are another matter. You will see them at the far edges of terraces — dark, upright, standing at a channel or walking a wall. They will be exactly far enough away that you cannot make out a face. If you walk toward one, you will arrive to find no one, or the same figure a terrace further off at the same distance, doing the same thing.

Do not chase them. This is a practical instruction before it is anything else: chasing them will pull you across the shelf, terrace after terrace, and by the time you give up you will be far from your marker with no trail behind you and a body that no longer knows how long it has been walking. The figures are, at the very least, a mechanism for exhausting you.

But I will say what my intuition tells me, which is that they are keeping to a number, as the goats are and the birds are, and that the number is not fixed forever, and that the shelf has a way of coming into balance with whatever is on it. If there are one or two more figures than there used to be, no one is counting. It is possible that some of them were travelers who set a hard limit on their stay and then, one quiet hour past noon, let it go. Keep your limit.

---

**What not to do**

Do not pick up the copper tools. Not because they are cursed — I do not think the shelf works by curse — but because they were set down, and the setting-down is part of the finished state. Also because you will be tempted, if you have any feel for stone, to take a chisel to one of the half-cut channels and finish the groove. The work is so nearly done. The line is so clear. Do not. Either the place will hold your cut forever, and you will have altered something that was meant to be exactly as it was, or the place will undo it at the rate you make it, and you will stand there working and watching the stone close behind your blade, and I do not know which of those would be worse for you.

Do not build. Do not stack stones except as a deliberate marker at your arrival point. Do not dam a channel to see what happens. Do not write your name in the moss. The rule of the place is that nothing accumulates, and you are the one thing there that accumulates by nature, and you should keep that to yourself.

Do not go near the edge. The stone ends in a clean fractured lip and the cloud begins, and the cloud is where the water goes to be gone. It does not rise and it does not sink. It is held at exactly the height where the rock stops. Nothing that goes into it comes back, and you have no way of knowing whether there is a drop of a hundred meters beneath it or no bottom at all. Treat the lip as a wall. Stay a body's length back from it, further if the rock is wet, which it is.

Do not descend into the cisterns, even the shallow-stepped ones. The dressed walls are so smooth the water lies on them like oil, and they will be so smooth under your boots.

Do not cross a slab bridge with a pack on if you can take it off and slide it across first. They have no rails, they are wet, and the fractures under them are deep.

---

**Your mind**

Understand that the shelf will affect you not through fear but through peace. It is quiet. It is cool. Nothing threatens. The light is kind. The water sounds go on and on and they are beautiful. You will find yourself sitting on a wall top watching the herd, and an hour will go by, and you will not be able to say what you were thinking.

This is the actual danger, and the preparation for it is mostly done before you arrive. Bring something unfinished with you — a book you are halfway through, a letter you owe someone, a problem you are working. Bring the sense of a life going on elsewhere that needs you back at a certain hour. The shelf offers, very gently, the possibility of setting all that down, the way the tools were set down, and staying exactly as you are. Refuse it. Not angrily. Just keep the watch wound and keep the log and keep walking toward the marker when the log says it is time.

If you travel with a companion, agree beforehand that either of you can call the departure early and the other will go without argument. The silence works on people differently, and the one who is still counting hours must be able to bring out the one who has stopped.

Talk aloud, even alone. Your voice will sound strange — too loud, unswallowed, the only voice on the shelf — and that is exactly why you should use it. It is the one sound there that does not belong to the place, and it will remind you that you don't either.

---

**Leaving**

Go back to your marker at the hour you said you would, whether or not you have seen the far side of the shelf, whether or not you have understood the figures, whether or not you have finished what you came for. Nothing here will be different when you come back, if you come back. That is the one promise the place makes and keeps.

Take a last look before you go. The channels catching the light. The cistern lying flat and silver. The grey birds on the wall tops with their beaks closed. The sun where it always is. Then step off the shelf and into wherever you are going next, and feel the first shadow that lengthens, the first breath of wind that moves the dust, and understand that you have been somewhere that will still be exactly there, exactly so, cutting-dust damp in the bottom of the groove, when everything you know has worn down to nothing.

**A short list**

- Cut-resistant gloves; heavy trousers; knee protection
- Boots with soft sticky soles
- Wool base layers; waterproof shell; wide-brimmed hat
- Two complete changes of dry clothes in sealed dry bags; extra socks beyond reason
- Two mechanical watches; waterproof notebook and pencils
- Sleeping mask; sleeping bag in a dry bag
- All food for the full stay plus a third again
- Water filter; two bottles
- Full first-aid kit including wound closures
- Bright cord and cloth for the arrival marker, and nothing else you intend to leave
- A hard date of departure, written on the first page of the notebook, agreed with whoever is waiting for you
- Something unfinished from home
