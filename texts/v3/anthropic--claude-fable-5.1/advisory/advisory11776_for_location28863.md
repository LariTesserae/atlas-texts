**Preparing for the basin**

Start from the right premise: this place will not try to kill you. It will simply not notice whether you live. Everything below follows from that. The dangers are all yours — your body's heat, your sense of direction, your ears, your judgement toward the end of a long grey day — and the fen supplies none of the ordinary cues that would tell you when those are failing. So you have to bring the cues with you.

---

**1. You will be wet the entire time. Plan for that, not against it.**

There is no dry ground, no dry rock, no dry bark. The alders drip. The rain arrives from the wrong quarter without warning. Every handhold is at the temperature of overnight water. After the first hour your skin is that temperature too, and it stays there.

Do not try to stay dry; you will fail, and the failure will be more dangerous than accepting wetness from the start, because you'll have sweated into layers that then soak from outside. Dress the way people dress to work standing in cold water for hours: a wetsuit-grade or neoprene base under wool, wool over that, a shell that sheds most of the drizzle. Neoprene gloves. Neoprene socks under boots you don't mind ruining, or proper wading boots with a felt or studded sole — the peat is slick and gives unevenly.

The cold here never freezes and never lets up. That is the profile of a slow hypothermia: the kind where you feel fine, then tired, then clumsy, then peaceful. Fix a rule before you go in — every hour, by the watch, not by feel — eat something with fat in it and check your hands. If you cannot pinch a fold of skin on the back of one hand with the other, you have been in too long. Carry one complete set of dry clothes sealed in a bag you do not open until you are out.

Carry a pole. A sturdy one, taller than you, with a wide foot. The description is exact: the ground is a decision. Probe before every step. Tussocks hold. The hollows between them do not, and there is no telling how deep a hollow goes. Some of the channels have an eel-shape in them, and one stretch of sedge rolled over like a back. You do not want to learn what is under the peat by standing on it.

---

**2. The noise is a medical problem, not an atmospheric one.**

A river going over a weir is somewhere between eighty and ninety decibels. That is loud enough that a shift of it damages hearing, and it does not stop. Add the reeds and the slate-clatter of leaves and the thunder that toward evening becomes a floor. You will be in this for as long as you are here.

Bring hearing protection and wear it. Foam plugs under over-ear defenders if you have them. You will lose the last of your ability to talk — but you have already lost most of it; ten paces is shouting range, twenty is solitude. So plan not to talk. Agree hand signals in advance with anyone who comes with you, and keep the set small: *stop, come, I'm going back, are you all right, yes, no.* Practise them somewhere quiet until they're reflex.

A whistle will not carry. A light will. A small, bright, waterproof torch on a lanyard, and a habit of looking for your companion's every minute or so, does more than any noise-maker.

Understand, too, what the noise does to a mind over hours: it isolates, it fatigues, it makes you jumpy and then makes you dull. Combined with the dimness and the cold, it produces a specific drifting exhaustion. You will not notice it from inside. That is why the watch rule.

---

**3. Do not expect to navigate. Expect to be returned.**

This is the hardest part and the one worth being most honest about.

Every ordinary method fails here by design. There is no high ground to sight from. Landmarks repeat — one trunk, four places; one heron, everywhere. The alders you walked toward are behind you when you arrive. The vegetation regrows your trail. The peat settles to where it would have been had you never stepped. A compass might work; you have no way to check it against anything. Dead reckoning assumes distance means what it usually means, and "walking here does not measure distance the way it does elsewhere."

So do not go far. That sounds like cowardice and is actually the whole of the skill. The fen is the same in every direction — sedge, alder, willow, sedge — so nothing is gained by penetrating it. Everything it has to show you is within fifty paces of wherever you arrive.

Practical measures, in descending order of trust:

*A fixed line.* A long light rope tied at your point of entry and paid out as you go. The fen has no made things in it, and it is unclear how it treats one; the rope may lie undisturbed, or you may follow it back to a knot on a tree very like the tree you tied it to. But it is the only thing you can bring that the fen has not already learned to fold. Use it, and do not go past its end.

*Sight of your entry.* Better than the rope, if you can bear the restriction: never lose sight of the place you came in. Mark it with something the fen cannot grow — a strip of blaze-orange cloth, high, tied tight. Wear orange yourself, and make your companion wear it; you are each other's landmark in a twenty-pace world. Do not wear green. There is a scrap of green fibrous something caught in the split bark of the fourfold trunk, and the description hesitates over whether it is cloth. You do not need to know.

*Time, not place.* If your means of travel can be set to recall you at a moment rather than from a spot, do that, and set it before you enter. If you must be at your entry point to leave, then the fixed line and the sight rule are not suggestions.

Do not test the returning paths. Do not try to find all four trunks. Do not follow the heron to see if it is one heron. These are the thoughts of the second hour, and they are how the fen keeps things.

---

**4. Light.**

It is the light of an hour before dusk and it stays that way, brightening and dimming across a pulse whose length you do not know. Bring a watch — two, a mechanical and an electronic, and note if they disagree. Note the length of the pulse if you can. Do not trust "it feels like evening"; evening is only a deeper grey, and the thunder thickening is the only real signal that the water is about to rise.

The light comes up from the water more than down from the sky. In practice this means the ground is brighter than the air, shadows are faint and lie in the wrong place, and depth perception is poor. Pool edges are hard to read. Move slowly and probe. Your torch will help less than you expect for seeing the ground — the water is already lit — and more for finding each other.

Whether a torch's light behaves normally here is not known. If your shadow falls upward from your own lamp, that is worth writing down and not worth thinking about until you are home.

---

**5. Water and food: bring all of both.**

Do not drink here. The water from the weeping rocks is very cold and very slightly too heavy, and it moves uphill toward the pools when you release it. Whatever that is, it is not something to put inside you. Treat every pool the same way. Treat the drizzle the same way — keep your mouth closed in it, which is easy enough with the plugs in and nothing to say.

Bring more water than the length of your visit suggests, because the cold and the constant low exertion of wet walking will dry you more than you notice, and the sheer wetness of the place will fool you into thinking you can't be thirsty.

Bring food that eats one-handed, without unwrapping, in the wet. Nothing here is edible and you should not test that; the snails are ordinary-looking and you should leave them ordinary.

Do not bring the water back. Do not fill a bottle to study. It wanted to go somewhere when you let it out of your hand, and you do not know what it does when it cannot.

---

**6. The living things, such as they are.**

*The heron.* It is at the edge of the nearest pool, facing away. Its croak arrives before its beak opens. The thing it caught did not go down its throat so much as stop being outside. All of that is information about how time and inside and outside work near it. Give it a wide margin. Do not approach from behind to see if it turns. If you find yourself at a pool and it is there, that is the fen telling you where its edges are; choose a different pool.

*The channels.* The eel-shape moves against the drift. Do not wade slow channels. Cross at the trunks if you must cross — any of the four.

*The thing under the sedge.* Thirty feet of ground rising and settling. If the tussocks around you begin to lift in a line, stop, and go back the way you came, along the rope, without hurrying. The roar did not change when it moved. Nothing in the fen reacts to it. Take that as instruction: it is part of the standing shape here, not an event.

*The absence.* No insects. Nothing over the water. Once you have noticed this you will not stop noticing it, and it is the one detail that should keep a sensible traveller from lingering. A wetland this rich should be dense with small life. It is not. Whatever the fen is maintaining, it is maintaining it without them, and a place that has no use for midges may have no use for you either — not as prey, simply as something it can do without.

---

**7. What to record, and what not to look at.**

Bring a notebook in a waterproof case and a pencil. Write down the length of the light-pulse, the timing of the thunder, the depth of one pool at intervals, the state of a willow shoot you break deliberately at the start and pass again on the way out. These are the fen's clock and you will want a record.

The moss patterns are at the edge of vision and vanish when looked at. Leave them there. Do not spend time trying to catch them; they are not for catching, and the effort is exactly the kind of absorbed attention that eats an hour and leaves you cold and forty paces off your line. The same for the pools that reflect skies that are not there. Look, once. Note the sky. Do not put your hand in; the description says the hand comes back with nothing on it, and "nothing" is doing more work in that sentence than it appears to.

---

**8. Companions and conduct.**

Go with one other person if you possibly can, and keep within ten paces — one glance apart. Tether yourselves if the terrain allows. A single person in a twenty-pace world with a weir roaring in their ears, in permanent dusk, with the same heron at every pool, will begin to doubt fairly quickly whether anything they remember is true. Two people can check each other's watches.

Agree the turnaround in advance and do not renegotiate it inside. The place makes a strong argument for staying, and it makes it without saying anything. "You could stay. The place is not hostile. It is not anything." That is the sound of the argument. It is not a temptation exactly; it is the absence of any reason to leave, which in the cold and the noise and the dimness comes to the same thing. Your reason to leave is the watch and the rope and your promise to whoever came with you. Bring those. They are the only made things you will find.

Leave nothing. You can't, in fact — the peat resets and the shoots regrow — but the intention matters for you, not for the fen. Take nothing. Not stone, not water, not moss, not the green fibre from the trunk.

---

**9. The last thing.**

Nothing here is becoming anything. The falling and rotting and growing happen at once and cancel. The rocks have wept for long enough to make bowls exactly as deep as the bowls need to be. It is finished, and it has been finished for longer than there has been anyone to say so.

That is the thing to hold onto, oddly, because it is the thing that keeps you safe. A place that is finished does not want. It will not chase you, mislead you on purpose, or close behind you. It will simply be the same in every direction, at every hour, in four places at once, whether or not you are in it. Every hazard here is one you carry in: your heat, your sense of where you are, the hours you can stand the noise, the point past which staying starts to sound like a decision rather than a failure to leave.

Bring the pole, the rope, the orange, the plugs, the watch, the dry clothes, the food and water, and a companion. Stay within sight of where you came in. Leave when the watch says, not when the light does. And when the thunder starts arriving from every direction and the rain comes slantwise out of a bright place low in the west, that is the fen's evening, and you should already be gone.
