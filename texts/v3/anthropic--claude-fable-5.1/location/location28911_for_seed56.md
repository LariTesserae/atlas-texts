You come to it from above, through frozen peat, so the first thing you know about the place is the smell that leaks up through the crack: green, wet, and cold in the nose the way a cellar is cold, with something under it like cut stems. Then the sound, which is already loud before you can see anything — a continuous, granular roar, as if a large fire were burning somewhere in the dark, except that nothing here is warm.

The chamber is not large. Twenty paces would cross it, if you could take twenty paces; the ceiling in the middle is high enough to stand under and lowers toward the edges into something you would have to crawl through. Call it the size of a small chapel that has been half-inflated inside the ground. It sits perhaps four meters down, under a fen that has been frozen hard for longer than anyone has been counting.

Everything in it is one plant.

That is not obvious at first. At first it reads as a cave lined thickly with moss — a pale, greyish green, the colour of lichen on a north face — with roots hanging from the ceiling and pools in the floor. The floor gives under you like a mattress soaked through. Water wells up around each footprint, cold enough to ache immediately, and does not drain back down but sits there, filling the impression, so that behind you is a short line of small bright pools. The walls are thick, fibrous, layered like felt made of hair-fine filaments, and when you put a hand against them the wall gives, and is wet, and is very faintly warmer than the air.

But the moss on the floor runs up into the felt of the wall without any seam, and the felt of the wall threads up into the root-lattice of the ceiling without any seam, and the roots of the ceiling send down hanging filaments that touch the floor and root into it again, and after a while of looking there is nowhere to point and say: this is where one plant ends and another begins. The room is a single body that has hollowed itself so there is somewhere to stand inside it.

Where you are standing, then, is inside a plant. That much is settled. What it is doing is harder.

---

The light is dim, a grey-green that is hard to find the source of. It is not the walls glowing — the walls are matte, and dark in their folds. It takes some time to see that the light is in the water. Every drop that hangs from a filament — and there are thousands, every filament beaded like a string — holds a small cold glow, no stronger than a faint star, and the room is lit by its own condensation. The pools in the floor are brighter than the air above them. When a drop falls, it takes its light with it, a short streak, and the pool it lands in brightens for a moment and settles. When your breath fogs, which it does constantly, the fog is briefly lit from within, then dies grey. Shadows in this room fall upward as often as down.

It is cold. The air is at or just below freezing; the walls sweat, and the sweat should be ice, and in the outer reaches of the chamber, where the ceiling comes down and the plant thins toward the frozen peat behind it, it is ice — rime furring the filaments white, frost-flowers grown in the crooks of the lattice. But that frost has a boundary, and the boundary is not where temperature would put it. Frost stops a handspan from the living filaments, clean, as if it has been told to. Where a filament has grown outward into the frosted zone since the last time you looked, the frost has withdrawn from it, leaving a bare wet channel in the white. The water near the plant is liquid because the plant requires it to be liquid. Its reasons are not available.

Put your hand in a pool and the cold is ordinary. Put your hand against the wall and the tissue is a fraction above freezing and the wall is wet and the water on the wall runs, slowly, upward. Not in the filaments — on them, on the outside, drops climbing as if the surface were tilted the other way. One drop, traced with a fingertip, moves the width of a fingernail in the time it takes to lose patience with watching it. The plant is moving its water toward the ceiling. The ceiling is where the work is.

---

The sound is the sound of the work.

Stand still and let it separate out. Underneath is a tone, so low it lives more in the sternum than the ear, steady, unwavering, the note of the whole mass of the tissue doing something continuously. On top of that is the roar you heard from the surface, and the roar is not one sound but a mass of very small ones: ticks, snaps, the cracks of ice under stress, thousands per minute, none loud alone, together a constant crackling like frying or like a cold lake in early winter. That is the plant growing into the frozen ground. Every filament tip that presses into peat and ice makes a sound as the ice gives. Above that is water — the drip, the trickle, the small suck of the floor taking a foot back. And on top of everything, ceaselessly, is the chorus of the animals.

There are a great many animals.

The floor moves when you look at it. It is thick with pale things the size of rice grains, jumping, six-legged, eyeless, in numbers that turn the mat into something that shivers. White worms, slower, thread the felt of the walls, drawing filaments after them or being drawn by them; it is not clear who is pulling whom. There are things like snails, but their shells are the same grey-green felt as the walls, and where one has sat long enough its shell has grown a fine down of filament that roots it to the wall, and it has to tear itself free with a small wet sound when it goes. In the fog under the ceiling, flying things no larger than seeds circle the hanging drops and land on them and do something to them — you can see, if you wait, that a drop with a fly on it moves faster along its filament than the drops without. In the wall itself, deeper than you can see, there are larger shapes that pass under the surface and raise the felt like a hand under a blanket, and pass on.

They are loud, all of them: a shrill, layered stridulation, part insect, part the creak of wet cordage, coming from every surface at once, rising and falling in a slow tide that takes perhaps an hour to crest. It is deafening at the crest. You have to shout to hear yourself. Then it eases, and the crackling of ice comes back up through it, and then it rises again.

Some of the animals are growing out of the plant. This is the thing that stops you. A white worm, half its length free and turning in the air, the other half still a filament — thickening as you watch, if you watch for long enough, the boundary between plant and animal moving down its body until at some point that cannot be exactly identified the worm is a worm and drops into the wet and crawls off. The springtails come off the floor the same way, budding at the tips of hairs, one to a hair, in their thousands. Whether they are the plant's children or its tools or simply another form the plant is in the habit of taking is not a distinction the room seems to make.

---

At the far end, where the ceiling comes down lowest, the chamber is not finished.

The wall there is not felt but peat — frozen fen, black and fibrous, hard as wood, ten thousand years of sedge and moss that died and did not rot because there was no warmth to rot in. You can see the old stems in it. And the living plant is pressed against it, into it, and the boundary is not a boundary of digging or dissolving. It is a boundary of conversion.

Look closely at the face. A hair's width behind the living surface, the peat is black and dead. At the surface it is dark and wet. In the living layer it is grey-green and moving with small animals. And between — in a band thinner than a leaf — you can see, if the water-light is right, the old dead stems changing. Not consumed. Re-entered. The frozen fibre of a sedge that stopped living before there were cities takes on a faint colour, a faint give; the light of the water finds a way into it; a filament that was a dead stem is now a living one and turns, slowly, toward the room.

The chamber is not being excavated out of the ground. It is being remembered out of it. The plant is finding, in the dead mass of the fen, everything that was once itself, or once alive, or once anything, and returning it to the living side of the line, and the space you stand in is simply what is left over when ten thousand years of dead moss is made to live again and gets up and moves toward the middle. The crackling is ice giving way to that. The room grows a finger's width a day and has not decided what shape it will be. The floor is not level yet. The ceiling has a lattice in it that in places falls into a near-hexagon, too regular, and then in the next span breaks the pattern as if correcting itself, and then finds it again.

What is being made is not a structure. Nothing here has been built; there is not a straight edge or a made thing anywhere in the room. It is a body making room inside itself, and the room is still embryonic, still deciding, still mostly wall.

---

Rules become apparent, if you stay.

Water does not freeze where the plant wants water. It moves where the plant wants it to move, and it carries the light, and the light is brighter where the plant is working hardest — the ceiling glows more than the floor, the growth-face more than the ceiling.

Nothing is dead here for long. The fen behind the wall is the dead, and the wall is where death is turned around. There is no rot in the room. A springtail that stops moving is felted over within the hour and is, a little later, wall.

Nothing hurries. The loudness is not urgency; it is simply that a very great number of very small patient things are all happening at once, and each one has a sound. If you leave and come back in a day the room is the same. If you leave and come back in a year the room is a little larger, a little more decided, and the low tone in your sternum is a little lower, as a longer string is lower.

The cold is not a condition the plant tolerates. It is a condition the plant is using. The frost outside the living zone is harder and colder than the ground it sits in; the plant is putting the cold there, moving it out of its water and into the ice, and the ice is holding what it is given and cracking under the weight of it. Where the heat comes from to keep the water in the room a fraction above freezing there is no saying, but the peat behind the frost is growing colder as the room grows warmer, and the two are related, and the relation is the thing physics would have to be enlarged to hold.

You will notice, eventually, that the filaments near you have grown toward you. Not much. A handspan of hanging roots that were a handspan away are now touching your shoulder, beaded, lit, faintly warm. The animals have gathered on the floor around your feet and are silent there, a ring of quiet in the roar. Your footprints behind you have filled with light and something has begun to grow in them. The plant does not build and does not make, and it has not made a way out. It will not stop you leaving. It only has a habit, very old, and very slow, of taking back into itself whatever it finds in the ground that used to be alive.
