You come to it from above, which is the only way anyone comes to anything here. A cast-iron stair, its treads worn to shallow spoons, turns down through four landings of glazed brick the colour of old teeth. On the second landing the air changes. It gets heavier and cooler, and it starts to carry water — not the smell of it, exactly, but the fact of it, the way a cellar tells you before you see anything that there is more water in the walls than in the air.

Then the stair lets out onto a gantry and the hall opens.

It is not a large hall. That surprises you, because everything about it is built as if it were large: the vault is brick, four bays of it, ribbed, sprung from squat piers faced in white tile that has gone the colour of cream and then past cream into something greyer. The floor is perhaps forty paces long and twenty across. A cathedral's proportions poured into a chapel's footprint. The vault sweats. Every rib carries a line of droplets along its underside, and when you stand still long enough you can watch one gather, bulge, let go, and fall into the black water of the channel below with a sound that is smaller than you expected.

The channel runs the length of the hall down its centre, a stone-lined race maybe two metres wide, and the water in it is moving, though you have to look at a drifting flake of rust to be sure. It is not black once your eyes settle. It is a very dark green, and clear, and lit from underneath — faintly, unevenly — by four submerged lamps in brass cages that were set into the channel walls a very long time ago and have never been switched off. They give the water the colour of a bottle held up to a dim window. That is most of the light in the room. The rest comes from three incandescent bulbs on long flex, hanging from the vault, each one burning at a low orange like an ember that has decided to be patient.

The machines occupy the two end bays. They are pumps — you know this the way you know a thing is a church — and they are running.

The nearer one is a horizontal engine, a flywheel taller than you are, painted a green that has flaked to show a green beneath it and a red beneath that. It turns. It turns so slowly that at first you take it for stopped, and then the single brass spoke-boss you have fixed your eye on is not where it was. One revolution takes about as long as a slow breath held and released. Each time the piston reaches the end of its stroke there is a sound: not a clank, but a settling, like a heavy man shifting in a chair in another room. Then the long quiet of the return. The far engine is the same, but out of phase, so that the two settlings alternate — one, a long wait, the other, a long wait — and this is the metronome of the place. You count it without meaning to and then stop counting because it does not seem to be measuring anything.

There is no one here. There has not been anyone here for a long time, and the place has arranged itself around that absence the way a room arranges itself around a piece of furniture that has been removed: the shape of the missing thing is still legible in what remains. A tin mug on a ledge, its handle facing the stair. A logbook on a sloped desk, swollen shut. A row of hooks with one oilskin coat hanging from it, so stiff with mineral bloom that it has become a kind of sculpture of a coat. The ironwork is all softened. Handrails have been worn thin at the height of a palm and have grown a skin of pale carbonate everywhere else, so they look like bones that were once handrails. Nothing is broken. That is the thing you keep returning to. Nothing has failed; it has simply been allowed to reach the state it was always going to reach, and then stay there.

And it is full of animals.

Not crowded — they have too much room for that. They are spaced through the hall the way people are spaced through a library. A heron stands on the gantry rail at the far end, grey on grey, so still that you took it for a finial until it moved its head. Another stands in the shallow spill-channel where the far pump's overflow runs, ankle-deep, watching the water with an attention that has nothing to do with hunger, because you look where it is looking and there is nothing to catch. Eels lie in the main channel below the brass lamps, long as your arm, not swimming, holding position with a slow ripple you can only see because the light comes from beneath them. Over the piston housing of the near engine a cluster of small bats hangs in the warmth the metal gives off, and they shift as the stroke comes, and settle as it goes. Moths have gathered on the bulbs — big pale ones with wings like wet paper — and they do not flutter; they have simply landed there and stayed, and because there are so many of them the bulbs are dimmer than they should be, and the light in the room has a moth-coloured cast. On the tiled ledge that runs the length of the wall there are toads at intervals of about a metre, each facing the channel, like a congregation. In the corner where the stair comes down a fox is asleep, and does not wake when you pass, and its fur is beaded with condensation.

None of them make any noise. That is not quite true — the eels make a sound when they turn, a soft belly-drag against the stone, and once a toad speaks, one syllable, and the vault gives it back four times, smaller each time. But they are quiet the way the hall is quiet, and you begin to understand that they are quiet because of the hall, that its slowness has got into them. The heron on the rail takes a single step every few minutes. The bats breathe with the engine.

What grows here grows in the places the light reaches and the water pools. Beneath each hanging bulb there is a circle of moss on the floor, dark and thick as felt, exactly the diameter of the light's fall. From the mortar joints around the spill-channel, small ferns have come out, pale and translucent as celery, leaning toward the brass lamps in the water rather than the bulbs above. That is all. A handful of green things, each in its assigned place, and otherwise brick and tile and iron and water.

The rules of the place take longer to see than the place itself.

The pumps lift water from the channel at the near end and return it to the channel at the far end. There is no outfall. There is an outfall — a great arched culvert in the end wall, iron-grated — but it has been bricked up from inside, and the bricks are the same age as everything else. The hall pumps its own water in a circle. It has been doing so for as long as the mineral has been growing on the handrails. Whether it once drained something, whether the city overhead still needs it, whether there is still a city overhead — the hall does not know and has not been told and has kept working, at the speed of a held breath, because that is what was asked of it.

And the animals have come because of the working. The warmth of the piston housing. The steadiness of the light. The water that never stops moving and never goes anywhere, so nothing in it is ever carried away. A heron standing in a spill-channel in a room forty paces long is not fishing. It is attending. The toads on the ledge face the channel the way things face a fire.

Once, while you are watching, the near engine reaches the end of its stroke and does not settle. It holds there, at the top, for longer than it should — long enough that the bats lift their heads — and you feel the hall notice. Every animal in it is looking at the flywheel. Then the piston lets go, the settling comes, the heron puts its head back down, and the interval resumes as if it had never been interrupted. You have the sense, which you cannot support, that this happens a few times a year and that they all remember every one.

You could stay. The stair goes up, and the hall is not asking you to leave; it is not asking you anything. If you stood on the gantry long enough the condensation would bead on you as it beads on the fox, and the mineral would find the places where you rested your hands, and the heron would step closer, one step every few minutes, until it stood beside you, watching the water it does not need to watch, in the light the moths have made dim, to the sound of two engines taking turns to breathe.
