The basin sits below sea level and knows it. Everything here drains inward and nothing leaves except as vapor, and the vapor doesn't go far. It rises off the ponds in the first hour of light, thickens into a low white lid by mid-morning, and then hangs there for the rest of the day, a ceiling of brine-haze that turns the sun into a pale coin you can look at directly. The light that comes through is even and shadowless and slightly gray, the light of a room with a frosted skylight. It does not feel like noon at noon. It feels like an hour that has no name.

The heat, though, is unambiguous. Forty-one, forty-three, sometimes forty-six in the still weeks. The air is saturated—the ponds see to that—and so the heat doesn't dry you, it coats you. Sweat doesn't evaporate. Metal railings are wet to the touch at dawn and hot to the touch by eight and both by ten, beaded and scalding, and the beads that run off them are already saline because there is salt in the condensation itself. Every surface in the basin weeps a little. The concrete pylons that carry the conveyor line across the flats have dark tide-marks of damp halfway up their height, and above the damp a crust of white, and above the crust a second, older damp where the crust has trapped moisture behind it, and so on, the whole pylon reading like a core sample of a hundred humid years.

Nothing grows here. The brine has made sure of it. Where the ponds have been drained and left, the floor is a pale ochre crazed into plates, and each plate is rimmed with a lip of salt that has grown upward as the water fled, so that the ground has the texture of a very old, very large ceramic glaze. In the shallower channels the mud is red-brown and alive-looking but it's mineral—iron, sulfur, the byproducts of the bacteria that are the only thing resembling life in the water itself. The bacteria are not visible as individuals. They are visible as color. Pond Seven is the pink of raw salmon. Pond Twelve, which is further along the concentration chain, is a deep tomato. The finishing pans are the color of dried blood, and their surface is skinned with a floating crust of crystals so thick that the wind, when there is wind, cannot ripple it.

The works are enormous and almost empty. That is the first thing that strikes a visitor coming down the grade from the rim: how much of the basin has been built on and how little there is to see. The pond system alone covers something like two hundred square kilometers, divided by levees into rectangles so large that from the levee road you cannot see the far side of the pond you're driving along—the haze eats it. The levees are gravel and crushed salt-cake, raised two meters, and they run absolutely straight for eleven or fourteen kilometers before turning at a right angle. Along the tops of some of them run the pipelines, insulated, sweating, painted a green that has gone to chalk. Along others run the conveyors: a single rubber belt on rollers, hooded in corrugated steel, carried on trestles above the brine, moving harvested salt at walking pace toward the washing plant. You can drive alongside a conveyor for twenty minutes and see no other structure, no other vehicle, no person. The belt is moving. The pumps somewhere are running. The place is fully operational and it is nearly deserted.

The settlement is scattered through this grid rather than gathered anywhere. There is a pump station every four or five kilometers along the main feed canal—a brick block with a tin roof, a transformer on a pole, a chain-link fence, and a floodlight that stays on all day because no one ever bothered to put it on a timer and because in the haze it isn't clear when day is. There are the crusher houses, three of them, each the size of a cathedral and each mostly air: a steel frame, a roof, one machine on a concrete plinth inside, doing its work. There is the washing plant, which is the closest thing to a center, a cluster of thickener tanks and a stack and a control building with its windows painted over against the glare. There is the rail spur and the loading gantry and the row of hopper cars that sit there for weeks between trains. There is the housing, sixty or seventy identical bungalows on a raised pad four kilometers from the washing plant, with a canteen and a clinic and a water tower, and maybe half the bungalows have someone living in them. The rest have their doors open. The salt has gotten in and frosted the floors.

Everything is old and has settled into its work. The brickwork of the pump stations has spalled to the depth of a thumbnail, uniformly, and then stopped spalling, as though brick and brine had reached an agreement. The steel of the trestles is not rusting so much as it has rusted; the rust itself has become a stable skin, chocolate brown under the white, and where a bracket has failed and been replaced the new galvanized piece stands out for a season and then goes to match. The levee roads are graded twice a year and between gradings develop the same washboard in the same places. There is a rhythm to the maintenance that is older than anyone working here. The works were laid out before the war—which war depends on who's telling—and have been producing without interruption since, and the whole basin has the feel of a machine that has found its idle speed and will run there indefinitely.

The sound of it is very low. You notice the quiet before you notice what fills it. The pumps are a hum below hearing, felt in the levee gravel through your boots. The conveyor is a soft continuous friction, like a hand rubbing a table, audible only when you're within thirty meters of it. Occasionally, far off, a hopper car couples or a valve is cycled and the sound arrives flattened and late and without direction. The haze does something to acoustics. Everything seems to come from everywhere and from a long way off.

Under that, and over it, and in it, is the sound of the animals.

Because the basin is not empty. It is empty of structure and empty of plants and very nearly empty of people, and it is fuller of living things than any place most of those people have ever been. The bacteria color the water and the brine shrimp eat the bacteria and the shrimp are so dense in the middle ponds that a bucket dipped and lifted comes up as a squirming pink soup, more animal than water. The brine flies breed on the shrimp and on the algal mats at the pond margins, and the flies are the thing you actually see. They lie on the wet salt at the water's edge in a band a meter wide, black, uncountable, and when you walk toward them the band lifts and moves ahead of you and settles again, a living hem that never lets you reach it. They don't bite. They don't land on you much. They are not interested in you. There are perhaps ten thousand per square meter along the shoreline of every pond in the system, and the shoreline of the system runs to several hundred kilometers, and the sound they make together is a fine dry sizzle, the sound of a distant fryer, so constant that the workers stop hearing it within a week and only notice when the temperature drops in January and the flies go quiet and the basin becomes, for a month, genuinely silent and strange.

Then the birds. The birds are why the basin is on maps. They come for the shrimp and the flies and they come in numbers that ecologists write papers about: phalaropes in the hundreds of thousands, spinning in tight flocks on the surface of Pond Nine, each bird turning in its own small circle to draw the shrimp up in a vortex; avocets and stilts wading the shallow pans in lines; grebes rafted so thick on the deeper canals that the water under them can't be seen; gulls everywhere, on every roofline and levee crown and pump-house fence; and in the migration seasons, flamingos. The flamingos stand in the finishing pans in the blood-colored brine, pink on red under white, in groups of several thousand, and they do almost nothing all day but sieve and shift their weight. Their voices are a low goose-murmur that carries across the water. When something disturbs a flock—a shift-change truck on the levee, a harrier coming low out of the haze—the whole mass lifts and the sound of it is a single vast exhalation, and then a rattle of wingbeats, and then they set down two hundred meters over and resume.

The harriers hunt the shoreline. Falcons sit on the conveyor hoods and take the phalaropes in the open. At the drain outfalls, where the concentrated brine runs off through culverts into the settling lagoon, the grebes gather in such density that a fox from the rim—there are foxes, thin and salt-caked, denning under the abandoned bungalows—can walk out along the culvert lip and simply pick one up. There is a rough dog population as well, descended from work dogs, living on dead birds and canteen scraps. There is no vegetation for any of these animals to hide in or eat or nest in, and none of them need it. The bird colonies nest on the bare levee crowns and on the salt-crust islands that form where the pans are shallow, laying eggs directly on the crystals. From the control-house roof you can look across a landscape in which nothing green exists in any direction for forty kilometers, and see more individual living creatures than you could see in a rainforest.

The basin runs on cycles that mostly ignore one another. The pumps run on the production schedule, moving brine from pond to pond as it concentrates. The water table rises after the winter rains and the drained pans re-flood from below, welling up through the crazed floor as clear bitter water that turns pink in a week. The flies hatch on temperature. The birds arrive on latitude and length of day. The levees are graded on the calendar. The salt on the trestles thickens in summer and sloughs in autumn. Every so often a pump station is decommissioned and a new one built a kilometer away, and the old one is left standing, and the gulls take it, and within a decade it is indistinguishable in weathering from the ones still running. There are, by the plant manager's estimate, more abandoned structures in the basin than active ones, and no one has ever bothered to demolish any of them, because demolition costs money and the salt is doing it for free at a rate of about a centimeter a century.

In the evening the haze goes from white to a dull rose, the only time it takes any color, and then to gray, and the floodlights at the pump stations become visible one by one across the flats, each a small smeared halo kilometers from the next. The birds settle. The flies drop to the salt and stop. The conveyor keeps moving, invisible, a sound like breathing. Someone in a bungalow turns on a radio and the sound reaches you across three kilometers of brine as a single unidentifiable note, and then a truck starts, and then the basin is quiet again except for the fryer-sizzle where a few late flies are still awake and the far-off goose-murmur of flamingos standing in the dark in water the temperature of blood, sieving, shifting, going nowhere.
