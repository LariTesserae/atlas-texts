The ground gave way a few weeks ago. You can tell by the edges.

A field of low sedge, waterlogged and dark under a sky the color of wet slate, and in the middle of it a hole about the size of a room, its rim not rounded but cut — the turf hangs over in a lip, roots snapped and pale, the peat beneath it still showing the layered brown of things that were never meant to see air. A few of the roots have started to grow downward, feeling for the new floor. Nothing has slumped yet. The walls are vertical, almost sharp, the way a bite is sharp. In a year this will be a soft-shouldered pond. Right now it is a wound.

You can climb down. It is not deep, perhaps twice your height, but the moment your head drops below the rim the light changes. The overcast that was merely dull becomes a ceiling; the hole becomes a chamber lit only from above, and the walls drink most of what falls in. The peat is black-brown and glistening. It weeps. Everywhere along the cut face, water beads and gathers and runs, not in streams but in a continuous slow sheeting, so the whole wall shines like the flank of something breathing.

And it is loud.

That is the thing you were not prepared for, standing up in the sedge where the wind took everything away. Down here the sound is enormous. Part of it is water: at the base of the north wall a channel has opened, a natural drain the ground was hiding, and water pours out of the peat into a pool that fills the hole's floor, and the pool empties through some throat you cannot see, and the sum of this is a sustained roar, an unbroken exhalation. But under the roar there is something else. A pulse. Deep, regular, felt in the teeth as much as heard. It comes up through the water in the floor and through the soles of your boots.

There is nothing built here. There is nothing built within sight of here, and you know that because you walked in across two miles of flat cold marsh and saw only sedge and standing water and a line of alders on the horizon. But somewhere — north, you think, or under — the district is being drained. Pumps the size of houses turning day and night, lifting the water out of the peat and sending it away in cuts and canals you never crossed, and the peat carries their rhythm the way a drumskin carries a hand. This hole is theirs, in a sense. The water table fell, the ground lost the water that held it up, and a void that had been quietly forming for a century decided all at once to become a place. The engines opened it without ever knowing it was there.

So the chamber throbs with distant industry. The weeping walls weep in time to it — you watch a bead swell and drop, swell and drop, and the interval is the interval of the pumps. Perhaps you are imagining that. Perhaps not. The pool at your feet has a skin of ripples that never resolve, standing waves that shiver but do not travel, held in place by a vibration that comes from everywhere.

It is cold. Not the sharp cold of frost but the sodden cold of a place that has never been dry, where the air is so full of water that it settles on your sleeves in gray fur. Your breath hangs. The walls fog. Where the light reaches the upper part of the cut, moss has already arrived, a thin green haze on the black, and in the wet crevices something paler — liverwort, perhaps, or the first pale threads of a fungus feeding on the roots that died in the collapse. Along the rim, where the turf overhangs, the sedge has flopped inward and hangs down like hair, and from it drip a thousand drops in no rhythm at all, so that the pumped pulse and the roaring drain are laced through with a random high patter.

For all this, the chamber is mostly empty. That is what strikes you after the sound. It is a hollow — air and water and raw earth — and the life in it is sparse and sudden. Nothing sits still here. Things arrive, do what they came for, and leave.

A frog, then, on a ledge of peat at the waterline, its throat working. You hear it before you see it, and only because its call rides just above the pitch of the drain. Then another, on the far wall, answering. Then you realize the sound you took for one component of the roar is actually many — that the pool is ringed with them, dozens, and their chorus has bent itself into the register of the machinery so completely that you cannot pull the two apart. They came for the new water. They came within days of the ground opening, from the ditches and the sedge, and now the fresh pool is thick with spawn, gray clouds of it drifting in the eddies, being carried toward the throat of the drain and away to who knows where.

Midges rise off the water in a column that leans with the draft and disperses at the rim. A wren, absurdly small, drops down the wall in a series of hops, seizes something, is gone. Once, at the drain's mouth, where the outflow roars out of the black, you see a long body flex in the current and vanish — an eel, moving through the hidden waterways that the pumps have turned into a labyrinth of new routes, using this brief open chamber as a way-station between one darkness and another. The place is a junction. The animals pass through it the way sound passes through it: continuously, densely, without remaining.

Above, the sky moves. Cloud comes over low and heavy and for a minute the chamber goes almost dark, then a rain squall arrives, hard and brief, and the whole cut face streams and the pool rises visibly and the roar deepens and the frogs go quiet. Then it passes. The light comes back, dim as before. The frogs resume. Somewhere a piece of the rim, softened by the squall, lets go and slides — a wet collapse, a slap, a slow spreading stain in the pool. The walls will not be sharp much longer. The place is aging while you stand in it.

There is a smell of iron and rot and something almost electrical, a thin ozone tang that you decide comes from the sheer amount of water being torn and aerated at the drain. Or from the pumps. You do not know how far the pumps' influence reaches. You begin to suspect that everything here — the frogs' pitch, the standing ripples, the weeping in time — is a form of accommodation, that the marsh has spent a generation learning to live inside an engine it has never seen, and that this hole is simply the first place where the arrangement has become audible.

You stay longer than you meant to. It is not comfortable. The cold gets into your knees, the sound gets into your skull, the wet gets into everything. But there is a completeness to the chamber that is hard to leave: a single raw room, cut fresh from the ground, roaring with unseen labor, thick with life that will not sit still, and slowly, minute by minute, softening toward the ordinary pond it will become. When you climb out the wind takes the sound away all at once, as if a door has shut, and the marsh lies flat and silent under the slate sky, and only the faintest tremor in the sodden ground reminds you that the engines are still turning, somewhere, drawing the water down.
