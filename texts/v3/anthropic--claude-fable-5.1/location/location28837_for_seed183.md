**The Rain Shelf**

It begins as a gentle rise, if you can call anything gentle at this altitude. You've been climbing for so long that the ground has stopped being ground and become a sequence of surfaces — slab, gravel, slab, a scarp of cracked grey stone, another slab — and somewhere in that sequence the valleys dropped away below the cloud and did not come back. What remains is a country of the upper air. A plateau, or a shelf, or several shelves stepped against each other, bleached almost white, running off in every direction until they blur.

It is wet here. Not with rain, though rain comes; with the sky itself. The cloud sits a hand's breadth above the rock for most of the day and the rock sweats under it. Every surface is filmed. Water gathers in the pores of the stone and comes back out again as beads, threads, a constant seep that darkens the undersides of ledges and drips from them in curtains. Where the slabs tilt, thin sheets of water slide across them, so shallow you can see the grain of the stone through the moving skin. Where they dip, there are tarns — dozens, hundreds — ranging from the size of a footprint to the size of a village square, all of them clear to the bottom, all of them cold enough to numb a hand in a breath, none of them holding anything alive.

Nothing lives here. Not a lichen on the rock, not a moss in the wet cracks, not the smallest scrim of algae in the pools. The stone is clean the way bone is clean. It is a mineral country, cool and bright and washed, and the only colors are the greys of the rock, the darker greys of wet rock, the blue-black of standing water, and the enormous, borderless white of the light.

---

The light is the first thing that tells you something is wrong.

It is very bright. The cloud that hangs over the plateau is thin enough to burn through and the sun comes down as a diffuse glare with no single source, so the whole sky is a lamp. That much is ordinary for high places. But watch the shadow under a boulder for a while. It does not lie in one direction. It pools, or it splits — two shadows, three, leaning away from each other like the spokes of a wheel — and if the cloud thins enough to show the sun, you may see the sun more than once, in more than one part of the sky, each disc pale and burning and unbothered by the others.

The people who came up here, long ago, seem to have understood this. Their cairns are everywhere. Stacked flat stones, waist-high, sometimes taller, weathered so their edges have gone soft and their wet faces gleam. They are spaced perhaps a hundred paces apart and they do not run in lines. They run in loops, in spirals, doubling back, and at first you take this for confusion. It isn't. Follow one cairn to the next and you arrive, in some part of the country you did not walk to. The land folds. A line of sight across a flat pan of slab will show you, at its far end, the back of your own shelter, or a tarn you left an hour ago, or a place you have not yet been but will recognize when you get there. The cairns don't mark a path across the plateau. They mark a path through it — the one route by which walking produces something like the distance it should.

---

The shelters are small. Low ovals of dry-stone wall, roofed once with slabs and now mostly open, the roofs slid off in one direction or another and lying where they fell. Inside, on ledges cut or chosen for the purpose, there are tools: flakes of a dark glassy stone that does not occur anywhere on the shelf, hand-sized cores, a few scrapers with edges still keen. No metal. No bone, no leather, no cord — anything that had once been alive has gone, and only the stone and the arrangement of stone remain. In one shelter there is a flat rock with a hollow ground into it, and the hollow holds water, and the water is very still.

Nobody is here. Nobody has been here for a long time. But the shelters are not few. They are scattered across every part of the country the cairns lead to, hundreds of them, in ones and twos, sometimes clustered at the edge of a large tarn as if around a well, and their persistence suggests that people came and went for generations, and that they came for a reason. What reason, the stone does not say.

---

It is loud. You would expect silence from a place with no life and no wind to speak of, and there is wind, but the sound is mostly water. Water dripping from a thousand ledges into a thousand pools, each drop with its own pitch. Water sheeting off tilted slabs and falling a hand's height to the next slab, a continuous hiss that fills the air like a held breath. Water moving underneath the stone, in channels you can't see, a low churn felt in the soles of the feet as much as heard.

And under all of it, the ringing.

The rock rings. Some of it — the paler stone, the stone that makes up the columns — rings when the drip strikes it, a clear glassy tone that hangs in the wet air and does not fade as quickly as it ought to. In the column fields the effect is overwhelming. These are places where the shelf has weathered into close-set pillars, thick as a body, standing so near each other you have to turn sideways to pass, hundreds of them running for a mile or more, each one wet, each one struck from above by the drip off the cloud, each one sounding. The tones overlap and beat against each other. It is like standing inside a bell that has been struck and will not stop.

There is also, sometimes, another sound in it. Speech, or something with the cadence of speech, arriving from no direction. The cairn-builders' voices, perhaps, folded through the same seam in the country that folds the sight-lines — arriving late, from a long way off, from a place a hundred paces away that is also a thousand years ago. You cannot make out words. You are not sure there are words. The tone of it is calm.

---

Weather comes in cycles you can learn. In the morning the cloud sits low and everything drips. Toward midday it lifts, sometimes clearing entirely, and then the multiple suns show themselves and the shadows fan out and the whole plateau blazes, every wet face throwing light back, so bright that the far shelves dissolve into a shimmer you cannot look at. Later the cloud comes back up from below — you can watch it rise out of the drowned valleys, pour over the rim of the shelf, and roll across the slabs like a tide — and with it, sometimes, real rain, hard and cold and brief. At night the temperature drops toward freezing and the seeps go still and a skin of ice forms on the smaller tarns. In the morning the ice cracks, the drip resumes, and the ringing begins again.

The folding shifts, too, but slowly, at the pace of the weather rather than the pace of a breath. A sight-line that showed you the back of your shelter in the morning may show you open slab by evening. The cairns account for this. Where the fold moves, there are two cairns, or three, standing close together, differently weathered — one much older than the others — and you understand that the builders did not lay out their path once. They laid it out again and again, over centuries, as the country moved under them, and that the country moved slowly enough for the path to keep up. This is what it means for the place to be established. The breakdown is not a wound. It is a climate. It has been like this long enough for the stone to weather around it.

---

Walk the cairn-path for a day and you find the large tarn at the shelf's center — or at what the cairns treat as its center, since geometry means little here. It is as wide as a small lake, perfectly still, perfectly clear, and very deep, and it is ringed on all sides by shelters, more of them than anywhere else, their fallen roofs lying in the shallows.

Stand at the edge and look down. The water is empty and the bottom is stone and the light comes through it in the same shattered, multi-sourced way it comes through the cloud, so the bottom is lit from several angles at once and casts no shadow at all. Look longer and the bottom is not the bottom. It is the surface of another tarn, seen from below, and above that, cloud, and above that, the pale wet shelves of a country very much like this one, with cairns on it, and small ruined shelters, and a figure standing at the edge of the water looking down.

The figure does not move when you move. It is not you. It is only standing where you are standing, in the place this place folds into, and the two of you are looking at each other across a seam in the world that the cairn-builders, with their flakes of dark stone and their patient stacked walls, spent generations learning to walk beside without ever crossing. Or perhaps they crossed. Perhaps that is where they went.

The drip falls from the ledges. The columns ring. The cloud, on some schedule of its own, begins to rise.
