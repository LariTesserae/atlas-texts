The first thing to understand about this place is that it is not finished.

You come up onto it from below, over a lip of rock that has not yet decided on a shape, and the ground spreads away from you in every direction with the color of a thing that was recently molten. Not red — that stage is over — but the black-brown of cooled iron, with a bloom of grey across it where the surface has begun to oxidize. The rock is ropy in places and blocky in others, and between the blocks there are gaps that go down farther than you would want to test with a dropped stone. Nothing has rounded anything here. Every edge is the edge the rock had on the day it stopped moving. There is no soil. There is no sand. There has not been time.

The air is thin enough that your body notices before your mind does. The sky sits closer than it should. Cloud comes across the plateau at roughly the height of your knees and then, a minute later, at roughly the height of your face, and you understand that you are not so much under the weather as inside it — that this ground is high enough to be a floor for cloud rather than a thing cloud passes over. The light that gets through is the light of an overcast late afternoon that never becomes evening and never becomes noon. Everything casts a shadow so faint you have to look for it.

---

The water is the strangest part, at first.

It should be dry up here. It is cold — cold enough that your breath hangs and your fingers stiffen inside their gloves — and the wind should scour whatever moisture the rock holds. But the rock is warm underneath. Not hot. Not steaming in the dramatic sense. Warm the way a wall is warm on the shaded side of a house in the afternoon, warm from something held inside it rather than shone on it. And the cloud, coming across cold and saturated, meets that warmth and lets go of what it carries.

So every surface weeps. Beads form on the underside of every overhang and grow until they fall. The vertical faces of the larger blocks are dark with a film of water that runs continuously into the cracks, and if you put your ear to one of the deeper fissures you can hear it collecting somewhere below, a small hollow sound of drops arriving in a pool that has never seen light. In the shallow depressions the water stands, skinned over in the morning with ice that is gone by midday, then skinned again. Some of these pools are the size of a hand. A few are the size of a room. All of them are the exact black of the rock beneath them and would be invisible except that they hold a dim reflection of the cloud, a patch of grey slightly lighter than the ground.

Where the warmth from below is strongest — and you find these places by the way the cloud thins and lifts above them — there is a ghost of vapor rising, torn away by the wind before it gets anywhere. The rock there is dry and faintly ticks as it cools. A meter away it is wet again.

Nothing here is a river. Nothing has had time to become a channel. The water simply arrives from the air, sits, seeps, freezes, thaws, and goes back into the air. It has not begun the long work of moving the mountain. It is only just learning the ground.

---

Almost nothing grows.

You have to get down close to find it. In the lee of the larger blocks, where the wet lasts longest and the wind is broken, there are patches of something that is either a lichen or a very early moss — a grey-green stain, no more than a few centimeters across, so thin that you cannot tell whether it has a thickness at all. Farther on there is a cushion of true moss the size of a fist, dark green and swollen with water, sitting in a crack like something planted there. You do not see another for a long time. Then a place where a few hundred of them cluster on a slope that faces out of the wind, and the effect is of a very old man's beard, patchy and unexpected.

There is one flowering plant, or something that intends to be one. A rosette of thick leaves, reddish underneath, pressed flat against the rock as if it had been stepped on. You see it once. You are not sure you would find it again.

That is the vegetation. A stain, a few cushions, a rosette. Against the scale of the plateau — and the plateau goes on for kilometers, rising and dipping in broad swells that you can see because the cloud lifts and parts and lets you see them, though never for long — the green is so sparse it reads as an accident.

---

And yet the place is full of animals.

This is the thing that takes longest to understand, because you don't see them at first. The ground is empty in the way that only fresh rock is empty: there is nothing on it, nothing crossing it, no track, no scat, no burrow. You walk for a long time and see nothing living that is larger than the moss. The silence is a wet silence, made of drips and wind and the small crack of contracting stone.

Then you crest one of the broad swells and the ground ahead of you is moving.

It is birds. Thousands of them, on the ground, spread across a shallow basin where the warmth from below is strong enough that the rock is dry and the snow that falls each night is gone by dawn. They are small, dark, uniform, and they are sitting — every one of them — a body's length apart from every other, in a lattice that covers the basin floor as far as the cloud lets you see. When the cloud lifts a little more the lattice continues. It goes down the far side of the basin and up the next swell and stops at the line where the ground goes cold.

They are not nesting. There is nothing to nest with. They are simply here, on the warm rock, in the thin air, and when you get closer you see why: the warmth brings up insects from the cracks, small flies that emerge in the vapor and are stunned by the cold a moment later, and the birds are picking them off the rock as fast as they come. It is the quietest feeding you have ever seen. A rustle, the click of a beak on stone, an occasional low note passed between two of them. No calling. Not one takes flight while you watch. They have flown a very long way to get here and they are not going to spend anything they don't have to.

Beyond the birds there are other things. In the deeper fissures, where you thought there was only water, there are small mammals — you hear them before you see them, a scrabbling that goes silent when you approach. They come out at dusk to work the edges of the colony for whatever the birds miss or drop or leave when they die. You find one dead bird and around it the rock has been cleaned with an efficiency that suggests many mouths. Overhead, once, something large passes through the cloud without showing itself, and every bird in the basin goes still at the same instant, and stays still for a slow count of thirty, and then resumes. You do not learn what it was.

So this is the shape of the ecology. Warmth from below draws insects out of the raw rock. The insects draw the birds across whatever distance they have crossed. The birds draw the scavengers out of the fissures and the hunter down out of the cloud. None of it depends on the plants, which cannot yet feed anything. All of it depends on the mountain's heat, which the mountain is losing, slowly, day by day.

They are living on a fire that is going out. They know this the way animals know things: not as knowledge, but as the sense that the season is finite and the thin margin of safety will close. In a few years — ten, twenty — the ground will be cold and the flies will be gone and the birds will not come here anymore. In a few hundred years the rock will have begun to break down and the moss will spread and some other assembly of creatures will find the place useful for some other reason. The birds are here for the beginning. They are the first tenants of an unfinished building.

---

Now the other thing. The thing that does not fit.

You will be standing on the plateau in the wet grey light, with the birds silent in their lattice and the water running down the faces of the rock, and you will hear an engine.

It is a long way off and it is above you, which should be impossible given how high you already are, but there it is: a steady industrial drone that swells and fades over a period of several minutes, moving across the top of the cloud from one horizon to the other. Once a day, sometimes twice. You never see the aircraft. You see only the effect, which is that on the days when the overcast is thin enough there is a straight white line scored across it, unraveling slowly at the edges, drifting east. The only straight line in the landscape. The only thing here that anyone made.

And when the cloud lifts at evening and you can see farther than you have all day — the plateau falling away in swells to a distant rim, and beyond the rim the tops of other mountains, older, with snow on them and the beginnings of real valleys cut into their sides — there is a light in the northeast that is not sunset and not a star. It sits low on the horizon in the direction the aircraft come from. It is the color of sodium, faintly orange, and it does not flicker. It is a very long way away; a day of walking at least, probably more, down off this rock and across whatever lies between. It goes on all night. It is bigger than a town light. It is the kind of light that means something is running.

You never learn what it is. A mine, perhaps, or a plant, or a port on a coast you can't see. It doesn't matter. What matters is that it exists, that the people who made it fly over this plateau without landing, that the birds sitting on the warm rock have almost certainly seen that light from the air on their way here and flown past it without stopping, that the water condensing on these stones has almost certainly, at some point in its long life, been steam in a boiler or a cooling tower or the wet plume from a stack. The plateau is untouched. Nothing has been built on it. No one has cut, moved, or even, as far as you can tell, walked upon this rock before. And it is not remote. It is only high, and new, and — for the moment — of no use to anyone.

The birds are proof of this, if you think about it. They did not come here from a wilderness. Every one of them, in the fine hollow of its leg, carries the metal of somewhere else — a few of them literally, a ring you notice on a dead one, stamped with numbers you can't read in the dim light. They came here across farmland, across roads, across whatever glows in the northeast, and they chose this place because among all the places available to them it was the one where the ground was warm and there was nothing on it yet.

---

Stay long enough and you see the cycle turn.

Night comes, and with it the cold deepens and the cloud drops and settles onto the rock as a snow so fine it is more like frost falling. The pools skin over. The weeping stops, or slows to something below hearing. The scavengers come out and work the colony's edges in the dark; you can hear them, and hear the occasional brief flurry when they find something. The birds do not move. They sit in their lattice in the falling frost and the warmth from beneath keeps the rock around each of them bare in a small dark circle, so that from above — if you could see from above — the basin would look like a sheet of white paper punched with thousands of black holes.

Morning, and the cloud lifts an arm's length and the frost goes back into the air. The rock ticks and steams. Flies come up out of the cracks. The birds begin, in silence, to eat. Water starts down the faces of the blocks again. Somewhere in the deep fissures the drops resume their count. Overhead, the engine.

There is one more thing, and it is easy to miss because it happens so slowly. The ground itself is not still. Every few days there is a sound from below — not a rumble, more the sound of a very large door closing in a distant part of a very large house — and some part of the plateau shifts. A crack widens a finger's width. A block settles. A pool drains overnight and another one, that wasn't there before, fills. The birds feel these things before you do and go still, and then resume. The mountain is not done being made. It has cooled enough to stand on, but not enough to stop moving, and the shape you see is a shape it is still in the middle of taking. Nothing here has been worn down. Everything is still being put up.

That is the place. A high raw floor for cloud. Warm rock under cold air, weeping water it will spend a thousand years learning to carry away. Almost no green. Thousands of small dark birds sitting a body's length apart, feeding quietly on a warmth that is leaving. Above the cloud, once or twice a day, an engine, and at the edge of night a light that never goes off. Nothing built. Nothing worn. Everything, in its way, just arrived.
