**The Pith**

The first thing is the light, and the light is wrong for being underground.

You are below the surface — far below it, by any reasonable measure. Above you is a thickness of living tissue that you could not walk through in a week, and above that, somewhere, the sun. But the tissue is white. Not the white of paint or bone but the white of the inside of an apple, of pith, of the inner rind of a fruit, and it is saturated with a clear cold sap, and it passes light the way snow passes light: not through, exactly, but *into*. Every part of it glows. The sun hits the outer skin of this thing and is swallowed and then re-emitted a hundred kilometers down, from every direction at once, soft and total and shadowless and so bright that the eye cannot find a place to rest.

There are no shadows here. A hand held in front of the face is lit from beneath as well as above. A crevice is as bright as the wall it opens in. Distance is very hard to judge, because nothing recedes into darkness; a wall a hundred meters away and a wall ten kilometers away are the same white, only slightly softened by the air between. People who live here — and some do — learn to read distance by the fog.

---

It is cold. This is the second thing, and it takes longer to arrive than the light. The tissue is full of water that has come down from the surface and been filtered through kilometers of fiber, and it is water that has never been warmed. Sap beads on every surface and does not evaporate. Where a draft moves across a wall, the beads freeze into a fur of small white needles, and then the wall grows underneath them and the needles crack off and fall, tinkling, into the void.

Your breath does not steam so much as sink; the air is very still. When it moves, it moves because the place itself is moving.

---

You are standing on a floor — a shelf, really — of dense white fiber at the bottom of a cavity, and the cavity is the size of a country.

This is the third thing, and it is the hardest to hold in the mind. The vegetation here is not a forest. There are no trunks, no leaves, no ground with roots in it. There is one plant, or one plant-mass — no one who lives here uses a word that distinguishes — and it has grown over and through and down into an entire continent's worth of what used to be rock, and you are inside it. The rock is gone, or so deep it doesn't matter. Everything you can see, everything you can touch, is *it*: the shelf under your feet, the walls, the immense pale vaults overhead, the ribs and buttresses and hanging cords of fiber that span the space like the inside of a gourd scaled up past sense.

And the space is *empty*. That is the strange marriage here. The plant is everything and the plant is almost nowhere; it has hollowed itself. For every cubic meter of tissue there are ten thousand cubic meters of cold bright air. You could walk for a day across the floor of this chamber and touch nothing but the floor. The nearest wall is a white smear at the edge of seeing. Overhead there is a ceiling, you are told, but you have never seen it — only a place where the brightness stops being a color and becomes a condition.

The people who live here call these spaces *breaths*. Not as metaphor. They believe, with some evidence, that the plant made them by breathing.

---

The chambers have weather.

This is what happens when you make a room large enough. Cold air pools in the low places and warms slightly against the sap-wet walls, and rises, and the risen air meets colder air pouring down from the vaults, and fog forms — thick banks of it, sliding across the floor in walls a hundred meters tall, lit from inside so they glow like enormous lanterns. Under the fog, the sap-beads on the floor swell and join and run in threads toward whatever is lowest. That is where the water is: not lakes, not rivers, but pockets. A hollow the size of a cupped hand, brimming. A long seam in the floor with a finger's-depth of water lying in it for a kilometer. Places where a tear in the wall has bled, and the sap has pooled, and skinned over with ice, and been buried by new growth, and now lies as a lens of clear cold water inside the white, visible as a faint darkening.

Sometimes the fog thins at the right height and you can see rain — not falling from the ceiling, which is too far, but forming halfway down, condensing out of a cold draft and dropping the rest of the way. It rains on you out of clear brightness. The drops are so cold they hurt.

---

Now the fourth thing, which is the reason no one stays anywhere.

The plant is growing. Not at the pace of plants — the pace of hedges, of vines, the pace where you look away and come back in a season — but at a pace you can watch. Put your hand flat against a wall and hold it there. After a minute you will feel the wall push back. After ten, your hand will have moved. Stand in a crevice too long and the crevice will begin to hold you.

And the growth is not even. It surges. Somewhere in the mass, some deep signal fires, and a wall that has been still for a day bellies outward in an hour, fibers splitting with a sound like slow cloth tearing, new white tissue swelling out of the split, wet and glistening and already beginning to frost. A chamber that was one room becomes two as a partition grows across it. Two chambers become one as the wall between them thins and stretches and finally rips open along its whole height, and the pressure difference — because the air in a sealed breath is never quite at the same pressure as its neighbor — drives a wind through the gap that can knock you flat. The people call that a *mouth opening*. It is the loudest thing that happens here. It happens perhaps once a day somewhere within hearing, a long low groan followed by the roar of air, and then the quiet closes back over everything like water.

Everything here is sharp because everything here is new. There is no erosion, because nothing has existed long enough to erode. The edges of a tear are ragged, bristling with torn fiber, hard enough to cut. The floor is ridged with growth-lines that have not been walked smooth. The buttresses have the raw, extruded look of something squeezed rather than shaped. In a week, all of it will be somewhere else, or gone, absorbed back into the mass, and the plant will have made new edges just as sharp. Nothing here is old. The oldest thing you can find is the light.

---

Little lives here, and what does is pale.

There is a thing like a slug the length of your forearm, clear as glass, that moves across the walls eating the frost. You see it only because its interior organs are faintly amber, and because the frost behind it is gone. There are small hard beetle-like things that live in the tears, in the fresh sap, and die when the tear closes, and are entombed, and stay visible for a while as dark specks deep in the white before the tissue grows too thick to see through. There is something that flies. It is rarely seen and never seen well, a grey slip across the brightness far overhead, silent, gone. People argue about whether it is one animal or many.

And there is a large thing. It is slow and it is very rare and it comes up out of the deep floors — out of the breaths below this one, and the ones below those — and it grazes on the walls, taking bites the size of a house, and it goes back down. Where it has fed, the wall heals over in a day. Nobody has seen it well either. The people mark where it has been with a particular sign, three short vertical cuts, and avoid the spot for a while, not because it is dangerous but because they consider it rude.

---

This is the fifth thing: the signs.

They are everywhere, once you learn to see them, and they are almost nothing. Three cuts. A spiral. A row of small wooden pegs — not wood, nothing here is wood; hardened sap, cast in a mold of packed fiber and set in the cold until it rings — driven into a wall in a rising line, a ladder to a ledge that no longer exists. A ring of stakes on the floor, a camp, the fiber inside the ring pressed flat by sleeping bodies and already lifting again as the floor grows. A shallow basin scraped into a low place to catch the condensation, half-filled, frost at its rim, the tool that scraped it lying beside it: a length of dense fiber with a thorn lashed to the end, the thorn from some part of the plant that grows thorns, far away or deep down, traded hand to hand.

There is nothing else to make tools from. There is no stone. There is no metal. There is, after a fashion, no wood. Everything the people have is the plant: fiber twisted into cord, cord woven into cloth and baskets and the walls of the small pale tents they carry, sap cast into pegs and blades and bowls, torn edges harvested and dried into something that will hold a point for a few days. They are skilled. They are also always leaving. A tent stays pitched for three nights at most. A route marked with cuts is a route for a week. The great scattering of small structures across the floors of the breaths is not a settlement; it is the trail of people who have never once stood still.

You come across a place where someone has begun to carve the wall. Not a sign — a picture, or the start of one, a long curved line and a set of shorter ones radiating from it. Then the growth-ridges cut across it, the line bent and thickened where the tissue swelled under it, and then it simply stops, absorbed. Whoever did this stood here for perhaps an hour. It was, for them, a long time.

---

Far off — a day's walk, or three, you cannot tell in this light — there are two figures moving along the base of a buttress. They are small and pale and they are walking away. Every so often one of them stops and bends to the wall and straightens again, and you understand they are cutting a sign, and that by the time you could reach it the sign would mean nothing, because the wall it is on will have moved.

The fog comes between you. When it passes, they are gone, and the buttress is a different shape.

---

Rules of the place, as far as they can be given:

The light is the sun. It is always day here, because the sun is always somewhere above the surface and the tissue holds its light like a bruise holds blood, slow to take it and slow to let it go; the interior brightens and dims by only a little between what the surface would call noon and midnight. The people count time by the frost, which forms and cracks off in cycles they know.

Down is toward the old rock. Down is warmer, slightly. Down, the breaths are older — meaning a month instead of a week — and larger, and darker by a degree, and the walls are thicker and the sap runs slower and the big thing lives there. Up is toward the skin. Up, the tissue is thinner and the light is unbearable, and the breaths are small and open and close so fast that nothing can live in them, and beyond them is the surface, which no one here has seen, and which they describe as a place where the plant has no inside.

Nothing you make will last. Nothing you leave will stay where you left it. The floor you sleep on will be a wall by the time you are old. A water pocket you find today will be sealed tomorrow, a clear lens buried in white, and someone a year from now will cut into the wall for another reason entirely and find it and drink it and not know it was yours.

And the plant does not care. That is the last rule, and the one the people say most often, in the flat tone of people stating the temperature. It is not hostile. It is not hospitable. It is doing something — growing, breathing, opening its mouths — that has nothing to do with them, at a scale that has nothing to do with them, and they live in the gaps of it the way frost lives on a wall: briefly, brightly, and then somewhere else.

The cold comes up through your feet. Somewhere a long way off, a wall groans and gives and the wind comes, and the fog leans all in one direction, and then everything is still again, and white, and bright, and new.
