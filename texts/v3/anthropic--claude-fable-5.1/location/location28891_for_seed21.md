The shelf is not old enough to have a name.

It came up out of the side of the mountain within living memory—the rock is still warm under the hand, still ticking. A ledge of dark stone perhaps three hundred paces across, tilted slightly toward the drop, held against the mountain's flank at a height where the air should be thin and cold and is instead thick, hot, heavy with water. You breathe it and feel you've breathed a little of the ground.

The heat comes from below. That is the first rule here. The sun does its work overhead, ordinary and hard, but the rock gives back more than the sun puts in, and so everything here that touches the ground is warm, and everything that stands away from the ground—the underside of a fern frond, the shaded face of a boulder, your own forearm—runs with condensation. The whole shelf sweats. The rock weeps. Beads gather on every edge and fall, and the falling is most of what you hear: an unsynchronized, patient dripping, from a thousand places at once, that never resolves into rhythm.

---

The stone is young in the way that stone is young—sharp-cornered, unweathered, black going to rust at the fractures. Where it broke when it rose, the faces are clean and angular and catch the light like slate. There are no rounded edges here yet. Nothing has had time to soften. You can see the individual bubbles frozen into it, the places where it cooled too fast and split. Walking on it is like walking on a thing that was recently alive.

Water sits in the fractures because it has nowhere to go—the rock is too new to have worn itself channels. Small pools, hot as bathwater, lie in every crack with a skin of mineral bloom on them, white and faintly blue. Between pools, the ground steams. Not dramatically. A thin lifting haze, close to the surface, so that the shelf seems to be exhaling and the air just above it wavers.

Into this the plants have come, and come fast.

They are colonizers, the first generation, and they grow the way first generations grow—too quickly, too green, greedy with the wet heat. Tree ferns already twice a person's height, their trunks fibrous and black, unrolling new fronds the color of unripe limes. Horsetails in dense bristling stands, ribbed and jointed, each one a small tower. Mosses so thick and saturated they give under the foot like a mattress and release warm water when pressed. Something with broad, almost rubbery leaves that stands in the pools with its roots in the mineral scum and seems untroubled by the temperature.

But they have not filled the shelf. That is what strikes you when you stand still and look. The vegetation comes in islands—a stand of horsetail here, a clump of ferns there, a single broad-leaved thing alone in the middle of an open plain of ticking black rock. Between them, bare stone, bare pools, steam. As if the plants had arrived by parachute and grown up exactly where they landed and were not yet in touch with one another. The green is intense but scattered. Most of the shelf is still dark and open and empty and warm.

---

You could walk it in a quarter of an hour, end to end. It has edges—that is the second rule. The mountain rises behind it in a wall of the same black rock, unclimbable. In front, the shelf ends in a lip and then a drop, and the drop is filled with cloud, so that you cannot see how far down the world resumes. To either side the ledge narrows and pinches out against the flank. It is a room. A room with a floor of hot stone and a ceiling of hard sky and one wall missing, and the missing wall is filled with white.

Twice a day the cloud climbs.

It comes up out of the drop in the late morning and again toward evening, thick and slow and body-temperature, and it rolls across the shelf until you can see perhaps ten paces. Everything drips harder. The ferns bow with the weight of it. The dripping becomes a soft continuous sound, a sound with no gaps in it, and the steam off the rock and the cloud off the drop become the same thing, and for an hour the shelf is inside a wet white lung. Then it thins. It goes back down the way it came, or burns off upward, and the sky reappears very blue and very close, and the rock, freshly soaked, ticks louder as it dries.

---

The things that do not belong are few, and you notice them precisely because there are so few.

The first is a line.

It runs the length of the shelf from the mountain wall to the lip—a seam in the rock, no wider than a finger, perfectly straight. Not straight the way a fracture is straight, which is to say approximately, with wobbles and steps. Straight the way nothing on a mountain is straight. If you kneel and sight along it, it does not deviate by the width of a hair across three hundred paces. Its edges are polished. Its depth is uniform. Its floor, when you look down into it, is a dull gray that is not the color of the rock and is not the color of anything else you can name.

Water does not cross it.

This is difficult to describe in a way that conveys how simple it looks. Rain falls on both sides. Condensation runs down the rock on both sides. But where a rivulet approaches the seam, it stops. Not pooling—stopping. The water stands up in a small clean lip a few millimeters from the edge, holds itself there against the slope, and goes no further. On the uphill side of the seam the ground is slick and running; on the downhill side, a hand's breadth away, it is comparatively dry. The line divides the shelf into a wet half and a less-wet half, and the plants know it: the ferns crowd the uphill edge and stand back from the downhill edge, and along the seam itself, on the wet side, a bright green rind of moss has grown in a straight stripe, as if someone had painted it.

Whoever put the seam there did not need it to be a wall. It is more like a rule—a place where one condition ends and another begins, and the water is simply obeying.

---

The second thing is the cold stone.

It stands near the middle of the shelf, alone in an open stretch of warm black rock: a block roughly the size of a small house, the same dark material as everything around it, but cold. Not cool. Cold enough that condensation on it freezes into a fur of white frost that grows, and thickens, and sheds itself in little collapses, and grows again. Cold enough that the steam off the surrounding ground bends toward it and falls down its faces as a cascade of visible air. Around its base is a ring of ice-water pooled in a moat, and in the moat float shards of ice, and this in a place where the ground everywhere else is too hot to lie on.

The block has no seams. It has no mechanism that you can find. It is not hollow—you can rap it and it answers like solid stone. The cold does not come from anywhere; it is simply the block's condition, in the way that warmth is the shelf's condition, and the two conditions meet at its surface and negotiate in frost.

Nothing grows on it. Nothing grows near it. It is the emptiest place on the shelf, a circle of clear ground and low fog and the constant faint crackle of ice forming and letting go.

---

The third is a hole, and it is the one you will not stand close to for long.

It is near the mountain wall, a circular opening in the rock perhaps two paces across, and the walls of it go down smooth as poured glass, perfectly vertical, without a mark. There is no spoil around it. No rim, no debris, no sign that anything was ever removed to make it—as though the rock had simply been told to be absent there and had agreed. Warm air rises out of it steadily, warmer than the shelf, and with it a faint, even light, dull orange, from somewhere far below. Not a glow. A light with a source, the way a lamp has a source, though you cannot see the source. When the cloud comes up the hole exhales a visible column that stands up straight and does not disperse until it is well above your head.

You drop a pebble in. You wait. You do not hear it land.

You drop another, and this time you count, and you stop counting.

---

The fourth is the only thing on the shelf that a person you'd recognize could have made.

It is a shelter. Someone built it against the mountain wall not far from the hole, out of what the shelf had to offer—fern trunks lashed together, a roof of the broad rubbery leaves layered against the drip, a floor of moss laid over the warm rock so that the whole thing would be heated from below. It is small. One person could lie down in it. Inside, on a shelf of stone, a few things are arranged: a cup of the white mineral bloom, dried to powder. A bundle of horsetail stems tied with fiber. A flat piece of dark rock on which someone has scratched a straight line and, beside it, a circle.

It has not been used for a while. The roof has begun to grow—the leaves, still alive when they were cut, have put down roots into the moss floor, and now the shelter is slowly becoming a plant. But not for so long that it has fallen. The lashings hold. Whoever stayed here stayed a while, and looked at the seam and the hole and the cold block, and made a note, and left. Or went down the hole. Or was here when the shelf rose and has been here ever since, and is somewhere in the cloud right now, watching.

---

There is one animal.

You see it first on the far side of the cold block, in the frost: a lizard the length of your forearm, dark, with a broad flat head, moving very slowly across the ice as if it enjoys it. Later you see it—or another—at the seam, pressing its mouth to the standing lip of water on the wet side and drinking from the edge, where the water stands up and will not fall. Later still it is on a fern trunk with its throat pulsing, and the throat is a color you cannot immediately place, a shifting bruised iridescence that seems to have the dull gray of the seam's floor somewhere in it.

It is the only thing that moves that is not water or cloud. It does not appear to be afraid of you. It does not appear to be interested in you either. It goes from the cold to the wet to the warm in a slow circuit, unhurried, and you begin to suspect it is not cooling itself or warming itself but simply visiting the conditions, one after another, the way you might make a round of a garden.

You never see two at once.

---

In the evening the cloud comes up again and the shelf closes.

You sit with your back to the fern-trunk shelter and the rock warm under you and the drip thickening into a hush, and you can no longer see the lip or the drop or the cold block, only the nearest ferns bowing under the weight of the air and, off to your left, a column of faintly orange breath standing straight up out of the ground.

The rock ticks. Somewhere a shelf of frost lets go and slides into its moat. Water runs downhill toward a line it will not cross, and stops, and stands there, patient, obeying.

It is a very small place. It is not finished. Whatever made it made only these few things and then left the mountain to do the rest, and the mountain is doing it—slowly, wetly, with ferns—and has not yet caught up.
