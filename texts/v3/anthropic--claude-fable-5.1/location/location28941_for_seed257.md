**The Upper Cistern**

Begin with the pressure on the ears, because that is what arrives first. Not much of it. Not the crushing weight of the true deep, only the mild, insistent squeeze of six or seven fathoms of water, which is to say this is a small drowning, a domestic one. The water is warm. It is warm the way a body is warm, and it is warm all the way through, no cold layer waiting beneath, and this is the first thing that is slightly wrong, because there is no sun here to heat it and no fire beneath it that anyone has found.

Above the water there is nothing. That should be said plainly. You are high — the air above the ceiling of this place is thin and cold and full of stars, the kind of altitude where lichen gives up — but you will never see it. The cistern was built on the summit, or the summit was built into the cistern; at this age the distinction has softened. Somewhere up there is a lid of stone slabs, and somewhere below the lid is a hand's width of trapped air that has not been exchanged in a very long time, and below that is the water, and in the water is everything.

---

The walls are made. Every surface is made. There is no bedrock showing anywhere, not in the floor, not in the corners where a lazy builder might have let the mountain do the work. Someone fitted these blocks by hand, without mortar, each one dressed with stone hammers so that it seats against its neighbors with a joint you could not slide a fingernail into. You can feel the tool-marks if you run your palm over the stone: shallow crescent dents, thousands of them, the patient percussion of someone who had no metal and all the time in the world. The blocks are dark. They may have been pale once. In the near-total dark you cannot tell, but under your hand they have the slick, closed texture of stone that has been wet for longer than it was ever dry.

The chamber is not large. A tall person could cross it in eleven strides if there were anywhere to stride. It is roughly square, and its corners have been worn — not by current, there is no current, but by the slow chemistry of warm water and by the passage of ten thousand generations of small bodies going in and out of the same crevices. The ceiling is vaulted in the crudest way, blocks stepped inward course by course until they meet, a corbel vault of the kind that was invented independently on every continent by people who wanted a roof and had only stone. It sags. It has been sagging for so long that the sag has become its final shape. It will not sag further. Everything here has arrived at the form it will keep.

---

Light: there is almost none, and what there is does not come from above.

Along the seam where the floor meets the walls there is a faint, cold luminance, patchy, the color of a bruise healing. It is not steady. If you watch a single patch for the span of a long breath, you will see it dim and brighten, dim and brighten, in no rhythm you can name. This is the bacterial mat that carpets the lower courses of stone, and it is the reason anything lives here at all. It feeds on something in the warm water — sulfur, iron, some mineral tithe the spring pays as it seeps up through the floor — and it glows because of some accident of its chemistry that no one has ever had light enough to study. It is not a plant. Nothing here is a plant. Nothing green has ever grown in this room, and the mat is the closest thing to a garden the place will ever have.

By its glow, once your eyes have given up hoping for anything better, the room begins to reveal its population.

---

They are everywhere. That is the shock of it.

The walls are not walls but a slow crawl of pale, blind, jointed things — amphipods the length of a knuckle, colorless as boiled rice, packed so close along the stone that they touch, that they climb over one another, that they form a second skin over the first. They move with a stiff, deliberate gait, antennae swinging, and they graze the mat, and where they have grazed the glow is a little dimmer, and where they have not it is a little brighter, so that the walls carry a faint, shifting map of appetite.

Between them, in the joints of the stonework, live flatworms as thin as wet paper, arrowhead-shaped, that unspool from the cracks and hang in the water feeling for the amphipods' cast shells. Snails the size of lentils, their shells worn to translucence, clamp themselves to the ceiling in clusters so dense the corbels look upholstered. Something like a shrimp with no eyes and long, trailing legs picks its way across the floor with the exaggerated care of a person crossing a room full of sleeping children.

And there are the salamanders. Not many — perhaps a dozen in the whole cistern, though they are hard to count because they are the same color as the water and because they do not move unless they have decided to. They are as long as a forearm, white, with the vestigial eyes of things that gave up on light a hundred thousand years ago, their gills a frilled ruff at the neck that stirs faintly, faintly, like a candle flame in a room where no one is breathing. They hang in the middle of the water at odd angles, head-down, sideways, as if gravity were a rumor they had heard but not confirmed. Every few hours one of them will drift to the wall, close its jaws on an amphipod, and drift back to the center of the room to wait. They can wait for years. Some of them have.

The density is not frantic. Nothing here is frantic. It is the density of a place that has reached its carrying capacity and stayed there, generation after generation, every niche filled and every mouth accounted for, a whole economy running at a walk in the dark.

---

Sound.

There is the sound of your own blood, which the pressure makes louder. Beneath that, if you hold very still, there is a sound like sand being poured through a very narrow gap, far away: the combined click and scrape of a hundred thousand tiny appendages on stone. It never rises above that. No fish here to flick a tail, no current to sigh through a gap, no drip from the ceiling because the ceiling is underwater. The salamanders make no sound at all. Once, perhaps every few days, a block somewhere in the walls settles by the width of a hair, and the whole room receives a single soft *tock*, felt more than heard, and every amphipod on every wall stops moving for the space of a heartbeat, and then goes on.

That is the loudest thing that happens here.

---

The water rises.

Not visibly. Not in a lifetime. But the spring in the floor gives more than the joints of the stonework can weep away, and so the trapped air under the vault has been growing thinner for as long as the cistern has been sealed. The high-water mark is written on the upper courses in a band of dead mat — a pale, unglowing line where the bacteria climbed and were exposed and died, a fraction of a finger's width above the line from the century before it. Perhaps in some thousands of years the last of the air will be gone, and the room will be a solid block of warm water and stone and pale life with no surface at all, and nothing that lives here will notice, because nothing that lives here has ever needed a surface.

This is what the builders could not have intended, and what the place has decided on anyway.

---

There is one more thing, and it belongs to the category of slightly wrong.

In the middle of the floor, under the crawl of shrimp and the patient snails, there is a single block that is not like the others. It is larger, and it is set flush, and its upper face is not pecked with tool-marks but polished — worn smooth by something, a hand or a hoof or a stream that no longer exists, long before it was laid here. And it is warmer than the water around it. Not by much. Enough that the salamanders, on the rare occasions they descend, descend to it, and rest there with their bellies against it, one at a time, never two together, as if there were an order to it that they all understood.

The spring comes up around its edges. The mat will not grow on it. In all the time this room has been filling, nothing has ever grown on it.

You could put your hand on it. It would be warm, and smooth, and it would feel like a thing that had been touched before, by a great many hands, for a great many reasons, none of which are left.

Above you the vault holds its final shape. The walls breathe with pale, blind bodies. The water climbs toward the ceiling at the pace of stone. And the summit, somewhere in the thin cold air outside, goes on being the highest place for a hundred miles, with no one on it, and this warm drowned room at its heart, full.
