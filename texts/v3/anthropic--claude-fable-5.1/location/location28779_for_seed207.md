The way in is a crack in the floor of an old cellar, or what was a cellar before the building above it fell down and was carted off. The crack is wide enough for a person turned sideways. It goes down about four meters through split bedrock, and the whole way down the air is moving with you — not a draft exactly, but a slow settling, as if the ground below is a lung that only inhales.

At the bottom the crack opens into a room. It is not large. If you stood in the middle and reached out you would touch the walls on either side, and if you stood upright your head would push into the ceiling. It is perhaps five meters long, rounded at both ends, roughly the shape of a kidney, and it is the coldest place for a very long way in any direction.

That is the first strange thing, and it is strange only if you know how ground behaves. Four meters down, rock should hold steady at the annual average of the surface — cool, cellar-cool, the temperature of stored apples. This room is at the edge of freezing and has been for as long as anything has been in it. The cold does not come from outside. Nothing here is refrigerating in any way that leaves evidence: there is no exhaust, no vent, no place where the removed heat goes. Heat comes in through the crack on the warm air, arrives here, and is gone. The engineers who could explain where it went have not been available for some centuries.

The room is full of plants.

Not a few. The walls, the ceiling, the floor, the ends, the underside of the one flat lip of stone that might once have been a bench — all of it is grown over, and grown over again, so that the room is noticeably smaller than the stone that contains it. If you put your hand out flat against the wall it sinks in to the wrist before it finds anything hard, and what it finds first is not rock but the compacted felt of older growth, layers of it, dead and half-dead and living, each generation rooted in the one below. The surface layer is soft and very wet and cold enough that your fingers ache within seconds. There are mosses, or things that behave like mosses: tight cushions, and long trailing kinds that hang from the ceiling and touch your face when you move. There are flat overlapping scales that would be liverworts if there were light to make them green. There are things with strap leaves, a finger long, that stand out from the walls in dense ranks, and when you brush them they move together like the teeth of a comb. None of it has any color anyone has confirmed. In here everything is the same color, which is none.

Because there is no light. That should be said plainly. It is not dim. It is the kind of dark in which the eye, after a while, begins to manufacture faint grey shapes out of nothing, and you learn to distrust them. If you carry a lamp down, the room is revealed as pale — bone-pale, ivory, the white of things grown in cellars — every surface beaded with water, the water catching your lamp in ten thousand points so that the whole chamber seems to be holding its breath and sweating. Turn the lamp off and it is gone again, and the plants, which have never needed it, go on doing whatever they do.

What they do is grow toward the cold. This is the second strange thing, and it is smaller than the first. Every leaf, every strand, every cushion is oriented — not to the crack where the warmth and air come in, but away from it, toward the far end of the room, where the growth is thickest and the temperature drops the last half-degree to the edge of ice. They are feeding on the gradient. The room takes heat out of the world, and something in the plants has learned to sit on that current and take a share of it as it passes, the way a mill sits on a river. Whether they were made that way or found their way to it, the effect is the same: they are the only lush thing for a mile in any direction, and they live in the dark on cold.

At the far end, under the deepest growth, is the thing itself. You have to dig for it. Pull away the trailing stuff, push aside the strap leaves, press through the felt, and your hand meets a surface that is perfectly smooth and not quite the temperature of anything else. It is a ring, or part of one — a curved band of some material, wide as a forearm, set into the stone as if the stone had been poured around it. It has no seams. It has no moving parts, or none that move on a scale you could feel. It does not hum. It gives off nothing, which is exactly its function: it is a place where energy goes and does not come back, and the room is only the shadow that such a thing casts.

There are a few other made things, and they are ordinary by comparison. Near the bottom of the crack, half-buried in growth, is a rectangular plate of dull metal fixed to the rock with three of four bolts. Whatever was written or stamped on it has been worn to a few raised bumps you can read only with a fingertip, and they mean nothing. A short length of cable, sheathed in something that has stayed supple, comes out of the wall at knee height and goes back into the wall a meter along, and neither end can be found. On the floor, if you kneel and feel carefully among the cushions, there is a small hexagonal thing about the size of a coin, loose, that has never corroded and that nothing grows on. People who have found it have generally put it back. And the flat lip of stone that might have been a bench has, at one end, a shallow socket cut into it, square, clean-edged, waiting for something that was taken away or never installed.

The room is damp rather than wet. It has no standing water except in pockets — a hollow in the bench-stone, a few cups where the felt has sagged, a shallow trough along the base of one wall where the drips collect. Warm air comes down the crack, meets the cold, and gives up its moisture onto everything at once, so that the plants are never dry and the stone where it shows is always sheened. Drops gather on the hanging strands until they are heavy enough to fall. This is most of what you hear: an irregular ticking of water onto felt, onto stone, into the small pools, never quite settling into rhythm. Sometimes, when the surface above has had a hard frost and the incoming air is colder than usual, the pools grow lids of ice a millimeter thick, and hours later, when the air warms again, the lids lift and crack with a sound like a knuckle. Under all of it there is a very faint, very low tone that you notice only when you have been still for a while, and you cannot decide whether it is the room or your own blood.

There are no animals. This is not a matter of degree. There are no insects in the felt, no springtails in the pools, nothing that has ever been seen to move on its own. Spores and seeds must come down the crack on the air — they came once, or the plants would not be here — but nothing that walks or crawls has stayed, and it is not clear whether that is because of the cold, or the dark, or because whatever the ring does to heat it also does to something else that small creatures need and plants do not. The absence is complete enough to feel intentional. It probably isn't.

The place is old and has stopped changing in any way that a single life would notice. The felt on the walls is deep enough that its lowest layers are centuries compressed, and the front of living growth advances into the remaining space by perhaps a millimeter a year, so that the room is closing, very slowly, like a wound. The ring's pull has a cycle — a little stronger in some years, a little weaker in others, on a period that nobody has stayed long enough to measure — and the plants track it, thickening on the far wall in the strong years and thinning back when it slackens. Nothing in here is in a hurry. Nothing in here is in trouble. Whatever this was for, it has finished being for that and become a place instead: a cold, dark, crowded, quiet room a little way under the ground, breathing in and never out, growing a garden no one planted toward a hole no one can see.

When you climb back up the crack the air gets warmer with every meter, and at the top the cellar feels, for a moment, like a heated room, and your hands do not stop aching for some time.
