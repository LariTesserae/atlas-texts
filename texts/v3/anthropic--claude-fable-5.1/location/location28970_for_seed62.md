The first thing is the light. It comes from everywhere at once — from the white sky, from the pale rock, from the undersides of leaves — so that nothing here casts a proper shadow. A shadow, when it exists at all, is a slightly less bright patch a few centimeters wide, huddled directly under whatever made it. You squint for the first hour. After that your eyes give up trying to darken the world and simply accept it.

The land is new. That is the second thing, and it takes longer to understand. The ridges have edges you could cut a hand on. The great tilted slabs that make the sides of the basin are not weathered, not rounded, not grooved by any stream; they are the raw fracture faces of something that broke and lifted not long ago, still showing the mineral glitter of the break. Where two slabs meet there is no soil in the seam, only a fine grey grit that has not yet decided to become anything. The ground rings faintly underfoot — a stone sound, but you feel it more than hear it. It is a place that has not had time to soften.

And yet it is thick with growth. This is the contradiction that keeps you standing still. Out of every crack, along every ledge, up every fracture line, the vegetation has come — not as pioneers, not thin and scrappy, but dense, layered, pressing. Ferns with fronds the length of an arm overlap one another so closely that the stone beneath is visible only as a suggestion. Cushion plants have mounded up over boulders in domes a meter across, tight enough that you can press a hand on them and feel the resistance of a mattress. There are shrub-trees, none taller than a house, with trunks that divide and divide again into a crown of small silvery leaves, and they grow so near to one another that their crowns knit into a continuous ceiling in places, so that you walk beneath a low bright roof of leaves with the white light coming through in a thousand points. Everything is pale. The greens are the greens of things that have too much light and are trying to reflect it away: grey-green, lichen-green, the blue-green of a glass bottle held to the sky.

The water is never quite water. It never rains, or almost never. Instead, the air holds a great weight of moisture that it cannot decide what to do with, and toward what would be morning — the light barely changes, but the temperature dips — every surface in the basin beads. The leaves are jeweled. The fracture faces run with a film thin enough to read the rock through. The ferns bow a little under it. Then the air warms by a degree, and it is gone: taken back, breathed in by the plants, absorbed into the grit. There are hollows in the slabs where it pools for an hour, fingernail-deep, and then those too are dry and bright. The whole basin drinks like this, once a cycle, and the only trace of it by midday is the smell — mineral, cold, a little metallic — and a coolness that clings under the shrub-canopy long after the open rock has dried.

You begin to see the machines after you have stopped looking for them.

They are scattered across the basin at a spacing of perhaps a kilometer, though the basin is wide enough that you can count fifteen or twenty of them from any high ridge. Each is a column, three or four stories tall, finned along its whole length like a radiator, standing straight up out of the rock on a footing of poured grey stone that is the only smooth surface for miles. They are the same color as the sky. The fins are beaded with condensation longer than anything else — the plants are dry before the columns are, and the columns stay dripping into the bright part of the cycle, and beneath each one there is a permanent dark patch of wet grit and a mound of the most vigorous vegetation anywhere, growing right up the footing and into the lowest fins. From the base of each column a pipe leaves at ground level, sheathed in something like glass, and runs off in a straight line across the slabs regardless of the terrain, stepping down the fractures on small trestles where it has to, always heading in the same direction: down, toward the lower end of the basin, where the ridge is notched and the pipes all converge and pass out of sight.

Above the columns run wires. A single line of them, on masts of a lattice so slender that at a distance they are only a grey pencil stroke against the white, crosses the whole basin from one wall to the other and leaves over the far ridge. The wires do not sag as much as wires should. Where they pass a column, a thinner wire drops to it, and enters the top.

None of this makes a sound. That is the rule you learn slowly and then all at once. The columns do not hum. The pipes do not tick or gurgle. The wires do not sing in whatever passes for wind here, because there is no wind; the air stands in the basin like water in a bowl. You put your ear against a column, and the metal is cold and faintly vibrating, at a frequency below anything you can hear — a pressure on the eardrum, a sense of something enormous turning very slowly — but no sound. You put your ear against the pipe and there is a movement in it, a slow crawl, like something being pulled along an inch at a time. The plants make no sound either. The ferns do not rustle because nothing moves them. Your own footsteps are absorbed into the cushion plants and returned as nothing. When you speak, the words leave you and go a short distance into the brightness and stop.

The machines are drinking the same water the plants are. This is what the columns are for, or all that they seem to be for: each cycle the moisture beads on the fins, runs down them, gathers in the footing, enters the pipe, and creeps toward the notch in the ridge. There is no factory here. The factory is elsewhere, downhill, wherever the pipes go. The basin is a catchment. A field. The columns stand among the ferns like the pumps of some very patient orchard, and the plants have gathered around them because the columns are the last place the water stays.

It is possible the plants came for the machines. It is possible the machines were placed among the plants. It is possible — you think this on the second ridge, looking back at the basin and its twenty grey columns and its single pencil-line of wire — that the land itself was lifted for this, that the sharp young slabs were tilted up into the cold bright altitude so the air would condense here and nowhere else, and the ferns and the cushion plants and the silver shrub-trees came afterward and filled every seam the lifting had opened. The rock is that new. The plants are that thick. Nobody has been here to tell you which came first, and there is no one to ask.

There are almost no animals. In two cycles you see one: a small grey bird, the color of the columns, sitting on a fin and drinking from the beads, not moving when you approach, not moving when you touch the metal a meter from its feet. It looks at you with an eye like a drop of the same water. Later there is a snail, or the shell of one, wedged in a fern-crown, very large, very white, and you cannot tell if it is alive. That is all. No insects rise from the cushion plants when you press them. Nothing lives under the leaves. The basin is full to the brim with green and empty of anything that moves through it except you and, once, the bird.

Change happens here on a schedule you can feel but not watch. The ferns are longer than they were, you think, on the second morning; the dark patch under the nearest column has grown by a hand's breadth; a fracture in a slab that was hairline is now wide enough for a fingertip, and there is a seedling in it, pale as a sprout under a stone. The light does not dim. The air does not stir. The water comes and beads and goes. Somewhere down the pipes, past the notch in the ridge, something is being made of all this slowness, and the making takes so long that you would have to stay a lifetime to see one product finished. You do not stay. You cross the second ridge with the brightness on your back, and the last thing you see when you turn is a single fin, high on the farthest column, still glittering wet long after everything else in the basin has dried.
