Start above it, because that is where you would arrive: a field of shale and cropped grass, low, near the bottom of a wide basin where the roads all tilt gently toward the same point. The point is a hole. Round, about nine meters across, edged in stone so recently broken that the fractures still have their bloom of pale dust and the corners could cut you. Nothing has weathered here. A season, maybe two. The grass stops a meter short of the lip as though it had been told to.

The hole is full of water to within a hand's width of the rim, and the water is cool and impossibly clear, and it is completely full of a city.

You would see this first as texture. Where you expected the dark of a flooded shaft you get instead a dense white-grey lattice going down out of sight, every part of it hard-edged, every part of it touching every other part. Spires, ledges, colonnades no wider than a finger, stairwells that turn eleven times and open onto galleries, cisterns inside cisterns, ducts, weirs, bridges between bridges. It reads as urban immediately and without translation: there are streets. There is a grid that becomes a radial plan lower down and then becomes something else. There are districts. It is a settlement built at a scale that could fit in a courtyard, and it fills the entire shaft so tightly that there is nowhere in it you could put your hand.

The light is ordinary daylight, which is the first strange thing. The sky is overcast and thin, and yet the light at depth is the same as the light at the surface, neither dimming nor bluing. The structures are doing this. Whatever they are made of takes light in at the top and hands it downward from surface to surface without loss, so that a gallery twenty meters down is lit like a kitchen on a grey afternoon. Look for shadows and you find them, but they fall the wrong way, sideways, from sources that are the walls themselves.

The material is ceramic to the eye, matte, faintly translucent at the thin edges, the white of a new tooth. It has no grain and no seams. Where two elements meet they simply become each other. Touch is not possible, the water is too full, but a dropped pebble from the rim never reaches anything; it is met a few centimeters down by a small extruded cup that closes around it, and the cup migrates sideways along a channel, and the pebble is somewhere in the plan now, being used.

The city is being built as you watch. That is the second strange thing, and the one that takes longest to accept, because the growth is not at the edges. There are no edges. It grows from the inside, in the packed middle, by making room where there is no room. A wall thins to a membrane and a duct pushes through it. A stairwell that was climbing decides to descend and its treads reverse one by one, top to bottom, in a wave. A whole ward of small domed chambers folds flat like a set of paper cups and is replaced, in the same volume, by a single long hall with a barrel roof, and the hall is already carrying water before the domes have finished going.

And the sound. You hear it before you see the city, if you come across the field: a low broad roar that rises out of the ground, with a great deal going on inside it. Water carries sound and this water has a great deal to carry. Underneath the roar is the working of thousands of small sluices opening and closing, a dry clatter like a hall of typewriters. Above it are tones, long ones, produced where currents run through flutes and hollow spans, and the tones drift against one another and beat. Sometimes a whole quarter of the city locks into a chord for a few seconds and the surface of the shaft trembles into standing rings. Then it breaks up again into the traffic sound. Standing at the rim you feel it in your sternum. Lean your face close to the surface and it is like putting your ear to the side of a ship.

There are no animals. Not one. The water was sterilized when the shaft was cut and it has stayed sterile, because everything that enters it is met and cupped and taken into the plan. There is no film on the surface, no midge, no water boatman. The only life is a faint green in the uppermost few centimeters along the north edge, a filamentous algae that can just survive in the thin margin between the last stone and the first architecture, and which the architecture, for reasons of its own, tolerates. A few strands. That is the whole biology. Further down there is nothing you could call alive except the city, and you would not quite call the city that either.

What the city is for becomes clear, slowly, if you watch the movement rather than the shapes. The shapes are not the point. The point is what flows. Every channel is carrying water and every channel is carrying it somewhere particular, and the somewheres change. A current comes down a boulevard on the east side, splits at a roundabout of six weirs, one branch taking a long spiral through a residential-looking quarter of tiny stacked cells, the other dropping straight through a shaft to a district you can't see, and both arrive, as far as anyone can tell, back at the top, cooler by some fraction of a degree, and go round again. The city is a settlement for currents. Its citizens are flows, and it houses them, and it rebuilds itself constantly to house them better, according to some rule of comfort or efficiency that is not visible from outside. When a flow grows, its street widens. When two flows keep meeting and slowing each other, a bridge appears and one goes over. Districts fall out of favour and are dissolved and their material is carried in grey threads to where it is needed, and the threads themselves are carried by currents that have, presumably, been persuaded.

That is the physics no one has described. Not that ceramic can flow like this, though it shouldn't; not that light can be passed hand over hand to depth, though it isn't supposed to. It is that the city knows where the water wants to go and is always slightly ahead of it, and the water, in turn, seems to know where the city is going to be. Cause and effect at the nine-meter scale have been folded into each other. Drop a second pebble and the cup that catches it has begun forming before the pebble enters the water.

The temperature of the shaft is a steady twelve degrees. On warm days a thin fog stands over the surface. On cold days the surface never skins with ice, because nothing in it is ever still long enough.

At the very top, in the north quarter, there is a plaza. It is the one open volume in the entire structure, a flat white floor about the size of a dinner plate, a hand's depth below the surface, ringed by low walls. Nothing is built on it and nothing crosses it. Currents come to its edges and stop and turn back. It is the only place in the city where the water is quiet, and if you watch it long enough you understand that it is being kept. Whether for something arriving or something departed, or as the one thing the city holds still so that it has something to measure itself against, there is no way to tell. The algae grows along its northern wall. The roar goes on all around it. And the plaza sits in the middle of all that packed machinery like a held breath, lit like a kitchen, cool, exact, and waiting.
