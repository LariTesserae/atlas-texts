There is no light, so begin with the floor.

It is warm. Not the warmth of sun-heated stone, which fades from the top down, but an even, interior warmth, the same a hand's width below the surface as at the surface, the same a mile on. It gives slightly underfoot, the way the back of a hand gives, and it is wet in the way a hand is wet after rain: not standing water, but a film, a sheen, water held in the skin of the thing. Press down and water rises around the boot. Lift the boot and the water is gone, drawn back in before it can pool. The floor is not soil. There is no soil anywhere. It is a mat, fibrous and tight-woven, and when the fingers work into it they find it goes down and down, denser, warmer, and wetter, and never reaches ground.

The air is the same temperature as the floor. It is saturated. Breathing is like breathing through a cloth soaked in warm water, and the cloth tastes of something green and something else — hot resin, wet iron, the smell of an electrical cabinet left closed too long. Nothing moves the air. It does not need to move.

The first column is found by walking into it.

It is smooth, and ribbed, and rises out of the mat with no flare at the base, the way an extruded thing rises, not the way a tree does. The ribs run vertical and their edges are sharp — not cutting, but crisp, as if they had been pulled from a die that morning. Nothing has worn them. No wind has rounded them, no water has channeled them, no growth has bulged them. A hand laid flat between two ribs feels the same interior warmth as the floor and, under it, something that is not sound: a steady pressure that comes through the palm and up the arm, the pressure of a fluid moving inside under load. It does not pulse. It does not vary. It moves at a rate, and the rate is the rate.

The column is wide enough that walking around it takes some time. There is another column beyond it, and another. They stand at intervals that are not regular but are not random either — the spacing of things that have found their distance and stopped. Between them, at chest height, at head height, at heights beyond reach, run the conduits.

The conduits are what a hand finds when it reaches into the dark and expects nothing. They are cool-skinned — no, they are the same warmth as everything; the skin only seems cool because it is smoother than the columns, smooth as glass — and they are perhaps as thick as a thigh, some thicker, and they run level from column to column, entering the columns without seam. The fluid inside them can be felt moving. It moves in one direction in one conduit and the other direction in the next. Follow a single conduit by touch and it goes column to column to column, a hundred columns, and then it enters one and does not come out on the far side, and a different conduit leaves that column at a different height, and the fluid in it is moving the other way.

The vanes hang above.

They cannot be seen. They are found when water lands on the face — a single drop, warm, slightly heavy, tasting of salt and copper — and then a second drop, from the same place, after the same interval. Reaching up finds an edge. The edge is thin and straight and as sharp as the column ribs, and the surface behind it is broad and faintly ridged, angled a few degrees from vertical, hanging from a stalk that joins a column somewhere overhead. It is a leaf, in the sense that it is flat and grows from a stem. It does not gather light. It gathers water. The air is so wet that water forms on any surface, and the vane's ridges run it down into a channel at the midline and the channel runs to the stalk and the stalk runs into the column. The drop that landed on the face was one that missed. There are not many that miss.

That is where the water goes, then. It comes out of the plant as vapor, somewhere, and thickens the air, and settles on the vanes, and runs back in. In a place this wet there should be the sound of dripping, and there almost is: the sense of dripping, the expectation of it, and then nothing, because the drops are caught before they fall, and the few that fall land on the mat, and the mat makes no sound.

There is no sound. Speech goes a short distance and stops, absorbed. The columns do not echo. If two people stood a column apart and shouted, each would hear the other faintly, as if through a wall. There is a pressure, sometimes, in the chest and the ears — a slow deep displacement, a fraction of a second, and then release — that is felt rather than heard, and may be a valve somewhere in the columns opening on its interval, or may be the whole mass shifting its weight by the width of a hair. It comes, and it goes, and it comes again after the same time.

And there is the faint thing in the eyes.

Not light. Not enough to see by, not enough to cast a shadow, not enough to tell the hand in front of the face. But where two columns stand close, or where a conduit runs near a rib, sometimes — after a long stillness — a thread of violet appears along the gap, there and gone in less time than it takes to know it was there, and the afterimage is the only proof. The hair on the arm lifts near the columns. Touch two conduits at once, one with each hand, and the fingers ache faintly. Something is being carried in the fluid or along the skins that is not only water, and it is being carried at a potential, and the potential is not small. It is only that it is contained. Everything here is contained.

Move away from the first column and there are columns for as far as anyone could walk. The mat does not rise and does not fall. The lowland — and it is a lowland, low and flat and deep, the floor of a continent — does not end. Walk for a day and the columns are the same, the conduits at the same heights, the vanes dropping their occasional missed drop. Walk for a year. The temperature does not change by a degree. There is no day here because there is no light, and there is no season because there is nothing to have a season. Somewhere far above the vanes, where the tops of the columns end, or do not, the water that leaves the plant as breath rises and becomes cloud, and the cloud is the sky of this place, and it rains back down onto the vanes, and the vanes send it into the columns, and the columns send it through the conduits, and the conduits return it to the columns, and the columns breathe it out. The weather is the plant's. The rain has never fallen on anything else. From very far above — if there were anywhere to stand — it would be a dark continent under a dark lid of cloud, with nothing lit, and the cloud would be the same shape tomorrow.

Nothing lives here but the thing itself. Nothing crawls on the mat, nothing has ever chewed a vane, nothing burrows in the columns, nothing has laid a spore on the wet warm surfaces where a spore would surely take. The plant has no flowers. It has no seeds. It does not reproduce because it is not finished with being what it is. It does not decay because nothing here has learned to decay it. The edges are sharp because nothing has ever touched them but water, and the water is its own.

And the fluid goes around.

That is the thing that takes longest to understand and cannot, in the end, be understood. There is a rate of flow in the conduits and a pressure in the columns and a charge in the skins, and a vapor rising and a rain falling, and all of these are the movements of an industry — the pipework and the pressure vessels and the condensers and the long steady load of something being processed at scale, a refinery the size of a continent running at full throughput in the dark. And nothing comes out. There is no product. There is no intake. Nothing is delivered and nothing is shipped. The fluid tastes of salt and copper going one way and salt and copper coming back. The columns hold what they hold. The plant refines itself and returns itself to itself and the rate is the rate and has been the rate, and the ribs are as sharp as the first hour.

It is possible to sit down on the mat, in the warmth, in the wet, in the dark, with the pressure of the flow coming up through the floor, and wait for something to change. The waiting is the only thing in the place that has a duration. Hunger will eventually give the time. Nothing else will.
