The sound arrives first, and it arrives from below.

You would expect to hear it at a distance, to walk toward it and have it grow. It does not work that way. There is a point on the approach — the road slopes down through fog, the fog thickens, and somewhere in the descent the sound simply becomes the condition of the air, as if you had walked underwater. It has no direction. It is in the road, in the soles of your boots, in the hollow of your chest. After an hour you stop hearing it as sound at all and begin experiencing it as a kind of weather: a pressure, steady and slightly variable, that the whole plain is held inside. People who have stood in it say the silence afterward is what deafens them.

---

The plain is low. It is lower than anything around it — lower than the sea it was taken from, if there ever was a sea; the horizon in every direction rises very slightly, a lip of darkness against darker cloud, and you understand without being told that you are standing in a basin that something is keeping empty.

The ground is made. That is the first strange thing and the one that never stops being strange. Kneel and put your palm flat on it and it is not soil, not stone, not anything that formed on its own. It is a crust: clinker and furnace-ash and brick-dust and slag, pressed together into a substance the colour of a cold stove. Kick at it and a plate of it lifts, and beneath is more of the same, a slightly different colour, a slightly different grain. There is no bottom to it that anyone has found. Bores sunk to look for bedrock came up with layer after layer of the same manufactured rubble — poured, dumped, raked, poured again — shading from grey to black to a deep rust-brown far below, older and older fill, the discarded weight of works that no one now remembers the purpose of. The plain was not built *on* anything. The plain *is* the building. Whatever was under it once has been buried so deep it has stopped being relevant.

And it is flat. Not flat the way a floor is flat — flat the way a very old riverbed is flat, or a salt pan, or the bottom of a dried lake. Everything on it has been worn down to the same level. Where there were once heaps and tips and embankments there are now only broad, low swells, so gentle you cannot see them, only feel them in your calves as the ground tilts and untilts under you across a quarter of a mile. Every edge is gone. Kerbs, sills, the lips of channels, the corners of foundations — all rounded, softened, sunk. If you pick up a fragment of brick it has the shape of a beach pebble.

The strange part is that there is no river here to have done this, and no wind to speak of, and no ice. The plain has been eroded by the sound.

---

This is the rule of the place, and once you know it you see it everywhere: the noise is not a by-product of what happens here. It is the agent. It is what the plain is made by.

The engines are what make the noise. There are not many of them. In the whole of the basin — a day's walk across, more than a day, the far side lost in fog even by such daylight as there is — there may be a dozen, spaced miles apart, each one a low hill of iron and brick with a chimney or two rising off it into the cloud. You see them as denser darknesses against the general dark, each with a faint smear of light at its base: the amber of an inspection lamp, the dull orange of a firebox door left ajar, the yellowish glow of a gauge that is still lit though nobody reads it. Between them there is nothing. Between them there is only the plain, and the fog, and the sound.

Each engine is a pump. That is all they are and all they have ever been, though they were not always alone here, and the works they once served — the foundries, the sheds, the yards that dumped this ground for centuries — are gone, worn down into the ground itself. The pumps stayed because the pumps could not stop. The basin fills. Water comes up through the made ground from below and down out of the air from above, and the whole plain is a shallow dish that would flood to the rim in a season if the engines did not lift it out, over the low dark horizon, to wherever it goes.

So they run. They have run for longer than anyone can put a figure to. The beams are worn concave where the bearings sit; the bearings have been replaced so many times the brass castings have taken on the shape of their own wear, like the stone steps of a very old church. The pistons have hollowed the cylinders and the cylinders have been re-bored and the re-bored cylinders have been hollowed again. Everything about the engines has been shaped by their own running, and they run on, and the running is the sound, and the sound is a fist of pressure that beats on the whole basin without pause.

Put your hand on the ground near one and you can feel it. Not a tremor — a beat, slow, felt through the wrist. Two or three strokes a minute, the great beam rising and falling somewhere inside its housing, each stroke a concussion that passes out through the made ground like a wave through a drumhead. Walk half a mile away and the beat is still there, faintly out of time with the beat from the next engine, and the next; and at the points where the waves meet and cross the ground hums with something higher, an unrelenting middle tone, and it is this — the sum of a dozen engines' worth of shaking, overlaid and interfering, running for centuries — that has taken the plain apart grain by grain and laid it flat.

Every clod of fill has been shaken against every other clod until they fit. Every rise has been jostled down into every hollow. The bricks did not crumble; they were vibrated smooth, the way a stone is smoothed in a tumbler. The plain has settled into the shape that the sound wants it to be, which is the shape of least resistance, which is level. It has nearly arrived. It is at the end of its geology. There is very little left for the sound to do to it, and it goes on doing it anyway.

---

It is cool here, and wet, and the two are the same thing.

The air over the plain is always a few degrees warmer than the ground — the engines exhaust steam and the fog that fills the basin is half made of it — and the ground is always cold, sunk as it is below everything else, sun never reaching it through the cloud. So the air weeps. Every surface sweats. Iron is beaded with water; brick is dark and slick; the fog condenses on your face and on the back of your hands and runs. Lean on anything and your sleeve comes away soaked.

Where it collects it stands. There is no soil to drink it and no slope to carry it off, so the low places — and everywhere is a low place — hold sheets of shallow water, some a few paces wide, some as broad as a field, none deeper than a boot. They are black and perfectly still and perfectly not-still: the surface of every pool is shaken by the beat from below into patterns. Standing waves. A grid of tiny peaks and troughs, or concentric rings, or something more complex — hexagons, spirals, patterns that shift when one engine falls a fraction out of phase with another — and the whole pool holds the pattern like a photograph and then, at the next beat, changes it. Where the fill under the pool is denser the pattern is finer. Where a channel or a foundation lies buried the pattern shows it, a ghost of old structure drawn on the water in rippled lines. You could map the buried plain by its pools, if you had the time and a light.

You do not have much light. What there is comes from three places. From the engine-houses, in smears. From the cloud, which is faintly lit from somewhere far beyond the rim of the basin, a rust-coloured pallor that never brightens and never quite goes out, so that the fog above you is dim and the fog around you is darker and the ground beneath is darkest of all. And from the water itself: in the shallowest, oldest, most undisturbed pools there is a slime that gives off a faint green glow when the beat is strong, so that in the moments after a big engine's stroke the plain flickers, very far away, with a spatter of pale points, and then goes out.

---

Things grow, though not many things, and not easily.

Along the drainage channels — the man-made ditches that carry water toward the pumps, worn now into shallow smooth-sided swales — there are reeds. They stand in rows, sparse, waist high, grey-green where the gauge light catches them. They do not rustle, or if they do you cannot hear it; they shiver constantly with the beat and have grown thick and short-jointed as if in response to it, more like stems of wire than plants. Between the reeds on the wet brick there are lichens, black and yellow, in spreading crusts. On the leeward flanks of the engine-houses, where the heat leaks through the walls, there is moss in dense wet cushions, and once in a long while a stunted willow, never taller than a man, its trunk bent away from the nearest chimney.

What is odd about the vegetation is where it grows. Not where the water is — the water is everywhere — but where the sound is least. The reeds line the channels not because the channels are wet but because the channels sit at the nodes of the interference pattern, the lines where the beats of neighbouring engines cancel and the ground holds still enough for a root to keep hold. Between the nodes, in the antinodes where the shaking is strongest, nothing grows at all, not even lichen. The plain is striped with life along invisible lines of quiet, and the stripes drift very slowly as the engines age and their rhythms change, and the reeds die out along one line and come up along another, a few paces over, across a century.

There are no animals. None. No birds — nothing flies into a sound like this, and nothing that tried would find a place to land that did not beat at it through its feet. No fish in the pools; the water is too shallow and too cold and full of ash. No insects. There is not so much as a midge over the reeds. Whether the sound drove them out or the ash poisoned them or there was simply never a way in, the effect is the same: this is a place in which the only moving things are water, steam, and iron. You realise after a while that you have been listening for something — a call, a splash, a rustle — through a wall of noise that would never let you hear it anyway, and that even so you know the plain is empty, the way you know a house is empty when you walk into it.

---

It is not still. That is the last thing to understand. The plain has reached its shape but not its rest.

Water rises. It seeps up through the fill from whatever is below — from the sea, from old sumps, from the sheer weight of made ground pressing water out of itself — and it falls out of the fog, and the pools spread across the plain by imperceptible degrees until they touch and join and a shallow lake stands over acres of clinker. Then an engine somewhere on the far side quickens: you feel it before you can locate it, a change in the beat, a fourth stroke a minute, a deeper concussion. The water in the channels begins to move, slow at first, then with a visible current, and the great lake unjoins into pools again, and the pools shrink, and the ground shows dark and steaming between them. This can take a day. Then the engine slows, and the seep begins again. There is no season to it — it happens as the water demands, on the water's schedule — but it is a cycle, and the plain breathes with it.

Beyond the rim, sometimes, something is answering. A pressure change in the fog, a shift in the pallor over the far cloud. Whatever the engines lift the water into has its own rhythm, and on some nights the beat of the pumps labours, as though pushing against a head that has risen, and the fog thickens and the seep quickens, and you understand that the engines are not draining the basin so much as holding a line, and that the line is old, and that it was drawn by people who are as thoroughly gone as the sheds they worked in.

The engines are wearing out. This is also a process, and it is also slow, and it is also under way. Every stroke takes a little more from the bearings. Every so often — years apart — one falls silent, and for a while there is a hole in the sound, a soft place in the pressure, a stretch of plain where the pools go flat and the reeds grow thicker and a person could almost think. Then the water rises there, and the plain in that region begins very slowly to fill and drown, and either someone — something — restarts the engine, or the neighbouring ones take up the load, their beat deepening, their strokes coming harder. No one has seen who tends them. The lamps are lit; the fireboxes are fed; the brass is worn to the shape of hands. The fog is thick.

And underneath all of it, the compaction goes on. The beat that keeps the water off the plain is the beat that shakes the plain lower, a hair's breadth at a time, into the water. The engines make the ground that needs the engines. The basin sinks; the pumps run harder; the basin sinks. It has been arriving at its final form for longer than anyone remembers, and it is nearly there — flat, cold, wet, dark, empty, made — and the last stage of the arriving is the sound, which fills the bowl from rim to rim and will not stop until the bowl is finished, and no one knows what the plain is when it is finished, only that it will be quiet, and that the quiet will be the same thing as the flood.

Stand on it long enough and you stop trying to hear. The pressure in your chest becomes a kind of company. Far off, a green flicker runs across the water and goes out; nearer, a reed shivers in a line of stillness you cannot see; the beat comes up through your feet, and comes up through your feet, and comes up through your feet.
