The first thing you learn is that the ground is younger than you are.

It's a shield — a great low dome of stone lifted almost two thousand metres above whatever lies beneath the haze, so broad that its curvature is the horizon. There is no mountain here; the whole land is the mountain, and you stand on the crown of it. The air is thin enough that your breath goes short on any slope, and the sky is that deep, unmixed blue that only comes when there's nothing between you and the vacuum but a few kilometres of gas. Daylight is plain and full and comes from a plain sun. Nothing about the light is strange. Everything it falls on is.

The rock has the look of something that stopped mid-sentence. Ropes of it, thick as your body, lie coiled and looped across each other where they flowed and then didn't. Domes swell up like bread that never finished rising. In places the surface is glass — black, faintly iridescent, unmarked — and you can see the frozen turbulence in it, the eddies and the drag lines, as if you were looking down into a river that had been photographed rather than stopped. There are cracks, but the cracks are clean. Their edges are as sharp as the day they opened. Nothing has touched them. No rain has ever fallen here; no wind moves; no frost has ever wedged them wider. Whatever process made this ground has ceased without finishing, and the ground simply remains in that unfinished condition, warm, with the exact posture of a thing about to cool.

It is warm underfoot. Not hot — you could sit on it — but warm the way a machine is warm an hour after it's been switched off, except this has been switched off for a very long time and the warmth has not gone. Put your hand flat on the glass and the heat is steady, sourceless, uniform. It does not fluctuate through the day. It is the same at what you would call noon and the same in the long red light before the sun goes down.

You walk for an hour and see nothing but stone.

Then, far off, the first grove.

---

They are hard to judge for distance at first because there's nothing to scale them against. From ten kilometres they look like a stand of dark trees. From two they are clearly not trees. From within, they are the strangest thing you have ever stood inside.

The trunks are tall — thirty, forty metres — and columnar, and their surfaces are not bark but something ribbed and jointed, dull grey-green, segmented into sleeves that fit one over the next. They are enormously thick at the base, flaring into the rock rather than rooting in it: the stone and the stem grade into each other with no clear boundary, as though the plant were a place where the lava had decided to keep going upward. Nothing branches until very high, and then the crown opens into wide, stiff, overlapping plates, dark on top and pale beneath, angled toward the sun with a precision that never changes because the sun's arc here is always the same and the plates have long since found their best position and stayed there.

The grove is dense — you can't see out of it once you're properly inside; the columns crowd close, twenty or thirty of them in a space the size of a town square — and it is the only dense thing for a day's walk in any direction. That is the pattern of this whole country: lushness in knots, and between the knots, nothing. You can stand on a rise and count the groves scattered across the shield like islands, each one a dark, crowded, fervent little thicket, each one separated from the next by kilometres of naked glass. There is no undergrowth. There are no seedlings at the edges. The groves are exactly as large as they are.

And they are working.

That's the word that comes to you, standing among them. Not growing — working. Put your ear to a trunk and there is a sound in it: not sap moving, but something more like a slow cycling pressure, a deep breath held and let out over the length of a minute, so low it's more felt than heard. The jointed sleeves of the stems shift against each other by fractions — you have to watch for a long time to be sure — with a rhythm as regular as a governor. Where the stems meet the rock there are vents, dark slits between the flaring buttresses, and from these the air rises warm and dry and faintly sweet, with a smell like hot resin and machine oil. Bend down and you can hear it: a thin, continuous exhalation, quieter than a whisper.

Break a plate — they are brittle at the edges — and what comes out is not water. It is thick, amber, almost clear, and it beads and holds its shape and does not soak into the rock or evaporate. It sits there on the black glass, perfectly round, catching the light. There is no water in this place, none in the ground and none in the air and none in the things that grow. The groves run on something else. They draw whatever the warm stone exhales through those slits at their base, and they take the sun through those unmoving plates, and somewhere in the pressure-cycling dark of their columns they turn the one into the other and store it as this — this heavy, golden, non-evaporating oil that is their blood and their fuel and, as far as you can tell, their purpose. The groves are refineries. They were not built. Nobody made them. They grew, or they emerged, in the same unfinished instant as the rock, and they have been running ever since, and they do not appear to produce anything they do not also consume.

---

You keep expecting the place to give you more than it does. You look for tracks and there are none — the glass would show them, and it shows only yours. You look for insects in the grove and find nothing on the trunks, nothing among the plates, nothing in the warm exhaled air. You look for dead groves, or half-groves, or the scar where a grove once stood and failed, and there are none. Nothing here has died, because nothing here has lived in the sense of coming into being and passing out of it. The groves are the same age as the rock. They will be this age tomorrow.

Between the groves there is only the quiet. It is not silence — silence would be a presence; this is more like a room where a sound has been removed. Your footsteps ring a little on the glass and stop dead. Now and then, far away, there is a single clear tick, like a cooling engine block — the sound of some stress in the stone resolving, though nothing ever seems to change. You wait for the next one. It comes after ten minutes, or twenty, or an hour, from a different direction, and it is exactly the same tick.

The light lies flat and honest on all of it. In the evening the shield turns the colour of a heating element, deep red, and the groves stand up out of it black and motionless, plates tilted, breathing their slow cycle, exhaling warmth into air that never carries it anywhere. Then the sun goes and the sky is black and the stars are hard and very close, and the ground is still warm, and the groves are still working, and nothing has happened.

You realise, sometime in that night, what kind of place this is. It is not a landscape that has been finished and is now weathering back down. It is not one that is building toward something. It is a landscape caught in the act of arriving — the lava still ropes and swells, the groves still cycle and exhale, the cracks still gape clean and new — and it has found, in that arrested arrival, a balance so complete that it never needs to move again. Every process here has met its counter-process exactly. The heat that rises from below is precisely the heat the groves take in. The gas the rock breathes out is precisely what the columns swallow. The oil they make is precisely what they burn. There is nothing left over. There is no surplus for a river to carry, or a wind to lift, or an animal to eat. There is no waste, and so there is no time.

You will leave a trail of footprints across the glass. In a place where nothing else ever changes, that will be the only clock.
