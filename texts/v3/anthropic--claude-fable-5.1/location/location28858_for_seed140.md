**The Basin at the Top of the Worn Mountain**

You climb for most of a day to reach it, but the climb is gentle, because the mountain has almost stopped being a mountain. What was once a peak has been ground down into a broad, low dome of grey stone, rounded like a knee under a blanket. There are no cliffs. There is no summit to speak of. Grass and low scrub have taken the slopes wherever a little soil has collected, and rain has cut only the shallowest grooves, and even these are old and softened at the edges. The rock has settled into the shape it will keep until the end.

Near the top the grass thins, and the dome flattens out into something close to a plain — a lens of bare stone a kilometer across, faintly convex, warm underfoot in the afternoon. And near the middle of that plain, laid on the stone as if poured there, is a disk of perfect black.

It is perhaps three hundred meters across. It has a hard edge. It does not shine, and it does not reflect the sky, and in the low sun it casts no shadow because it *is* a shadow — a place from which no light comes. Standing at its rim you feel a mild dizziness, as when looking down a well, except that the black surface lies a little above the level of the stone, a few handspans over the lip, like a meniscus. Cloud drifts across the mountaintop most afternoons, and where the cloud passes over the disk it simply is not seen; the vapor goes into the black and comes out the other side unchanged.

Warm air rises from it. It smells of wet leaves and iron.

---

**The threshold**

The edge of the black is not a wall. Put a hand out and it goes in. There is no resistance, no temperature change at the moment of crossing, no tingling. The hand is gone up to the wrist and you can still feel your fingers moving. Withdraw it and it is wet — a fine dew has formed on the skin in the time it was inside.

Carry a lamp in and the lamp keeps burning. You can feel its heat on your face, hear the small tick of the flame or the whine of the battery. It gives nothing. It is not that the light is absorbed, in the way black cloth absorbs light and grows warm; the lamp housing does not heat any more than it would outside. The light is simply not produced, or is produced and goes elsewhere, or is produced and immediately ceases to be the kind of thing that light is. Nobody who has studied the place has been able to say which. Instruments brought inside record that their light-emitting components are drawing power. They record nothing else. When you bring the instruments back out, they work.

This is the one impossible thing about the basin. Everything else that follows is only strange because of it.

---

**Inside**

Once you are over the rim the stone under your boots tilts downward, and you understand that the black disk is a lid over a bowl. Beneath the surface of the dark there is a hollow in the mountaintop — the one part of the ancient peak that did not wear away, because it was never a peak but a cavity, a crater or a cut, and the stone around it has slumped down to meet it. The walls of the bowl are steep at first and then ease, and the floor is far below.

The darkness is total. Not the darkness of a moonless night, where the eye adjusts and finds grain and gradient; there is nothing for the eye to do. After a while the eye stops trying, and this is a relief, and other things come forward.

The first is the air. It is warm — noticeably warmer than the mountaintop outside — and thick with moisture, so saturated that it condenses on everything cooler than itself, which includes you. Within minutes your face and hands and clothes are beaded. Breathing is like breathing in a greenhouse at dawn. There is no wind. The air moves only as air moves in a still room, by convection, by the slow turning-over of warm and cool.

The second is the sound.

---

**The chorus**

The basin is loud. It is not the loudness of a machine or a waterfall, though there is something of both in it. It is the loudness of a marsh at night multiplied by the acoustics of a stone bowl — every call reflected off the curved walls, returned, layered on itself, so that the whole hollow rings with a continuous dense weave of living noise.

There are things like frogs, hundreds of them, each with a low resonant note that they repeat at intervals, never quite in time with one another, so that the bass of the chorus is a rolling irregular pulse that you feel in your chest. Above them, a shrill continuous rasp like cicadas, rising and falling in long slow waves across the bowl as if a single enormous animal were breathing. Above that, sharper sounds: clicks, chirps, short high whistles, some so high they are barely audible, coming from the open air of the basin's interior and moving — passing overhead, swinging around the walls, dropping toward the floor.

And under everything, water. Dripping from a hundred places at a hundred rates; running in thin sheets over stone; the deeper sound of drops falling into a pool. The stone of the walls weeps. Everywhere the air touches it, it is cooler than the air, and the air gives up its water to it, and the water gathers and slides down.

Stand still long enough and the sound arranges itself. You come to know where the walls are by the way the frog-notes return from them. You come to know that the open middle of the bowl is genuinely open — the whistling things pass through it with nothing in their way — and that the walls are where the life is.

---

**The walls**

Put a hand on the stone and it is not stone. It is a growth — dense, soft, cool, wet, like moss but deeper, so thick that your fingers sink to the knuckle before they find rock. It covers the walls in an unbroken pelt from the rim down to the floor. In places it hangs in ropes and curtains, some as thick as an arm, swaying very slightly in the convection. In places it forms shelves and lobes that give under pressure and slowly recover. It smells green, sharply green, and slightly of something mineral, like the smell of a struck flint.

It is lush. That is the word, even though no light has touched it in longer than the mountain has been round. It grows faster than moss should, and it grows in the dark, and it is fed by the thing that keeps the dark.

The best understanding anyone has is this: light does not vanish without cost. Whatever stops it at the rim must do something with what it stopped. Some of that becomes warmth — the walls are warm under the growth, warmer than the floor, warmest of all near the rim where the boundary lies — and the warmth is what keeps the basin a greenhouse at the top of a cold mountain, and what makes the air condense on every surface. But not all of it becomes warmth. Some of it becomes something else, something that leaks from the stone into whatever is pressed against the stone, and the growth has learned over an unthinkable span to live on that. It does not photosynthesize. It does something for which there is no word, and it does it very well.

The growth is not uniform. Crouch and run your hands over it and you find textures — fine and velvety in some places, coarse and fibrous in others, and here and there small rigid structures like the fruiting bodies of fungi, cool and smooth and brittle, which break with a soft snap and leave your fingers slick. There are cavities in the pelt, mouth-sized to head-sized, warm and wet inside, where things live. Put your hand into one and something small and quick leaves it.

---

**What lives here**

The basin is abundant in the way a coral head is abundant: the surfaces are crowded, and the volume is empty.

The frogs — they are called frogs for convenience — live in the growth of the lower walls, near where the seepage collects. They are cool and smooth and heavy in the hand, larger than a fist, with skin like wet silk and no eyes at all, only slight depressions where eyes might once have been in an ancestor that had use for them. They do not flinch from touch, only from vibration in the stone. They will sit in your palm and call, and the call is far too loud for their size.

The rasping things are in the middle walls and are hard to find. They fall silent when you come near and begin again when you move on, so that you walk inside a bubble of quiet through the surrounding noise, and can hear your own breathing and the drip of water off your sleeves. Once or twice one has been caught: something long and jointed and light, with a body like folded paper and legs that grip. They eat the growth. There must be enormous numbers of them.

The flying things are never caught. They pass overhead with a soft ripple of displaced air and a stream of clicks so rapid it becomes a buzz. They are the hunters of the basin, taking the rasping things off the walls, taking the small quick occupants of the cavities, maybe taking the frogs. They navigate the empty middle of the bowl by sound, and they have done so for so long that they have become expert in a way that is almost unsettling: they never touch a wall, never touch each other, never touch you, even when you stand at the rim with an arm outstretched and one goes by close enough that you feel the wind of it on your fingers. They know the shape of the basin better than the basin knows itself.

There are others. Things that live in the pool at the bottom. Things that live inside the growth and are never seen because nothing here is ever seen. Now and then a sound that is none of the familiar ones — a low groan from the far side of the bowl, a scrabbling — and then the chorus closes over it.

None of them have eyes. There has been no reason.

---

**The floor**

The way down is long and slow because it is done by hand. The growth gives good purchase. The slope eases as you descend, and the walls of the bowl curve out toward a floor that is not really a floor but a long shallow trough, sloping toward one side, where the seepage collects.

Here the sound changes. The frogs are dense and near and the bass of their calling is enormous. The water noise gathers into the sound of a real pool, small waves against a soft shore. The pool is warm — warmer than the air, so that it steams, though of course you cannot see the steam — and shallow at the edges, and its bottom is thick with the same growth as the walls, gone slick and rotted in the standing water. Wade in and things brush your legs. The water tastes of iron and of something faintly sweet.

Where the floor is not pool, it is drifts of soft decay: growth that has died and fallen from the walls and lain here composting for ages, spongy, sinking under weight, warm inside. Reach down into it and it is hot. There are bones in it. Small ones, mostly. And here and there, larger.

---

**What was built**

The basin was made. That much is certain — the black lid did not happen. But whoever made it is not here, and what they left is very little.

A stair. Cut into the wall on the northern side, a switchback of steps descending from rim to floor. The steps are almost gone — each one worn to a shallow undulation in the stone, softened past use, buried under the growth so that you find them only by the rhythm of your feet as you climb. Whoever cut them expected to walk here in the dark, or expected the dark to be lifted, or did not expect the dark at all.

A platform near the top of the stair, a shelf of level stone with a raised lip, ten paces across. Standing on it you are in a place where the sound of the basin arrives from all sides at once, perfectly balanced. It might have been made for listening.

A doorway, or the memory of one — a rectangular opening in the wall at floor level, waist-high, half-filled with drift. It leads into a passage that goes back into the mountain for as far as anyone has followed it and does not become anything else.

An object. On the floor at the western end, half-sunk in the compost, there is a thing about the size of a person curled up. It is neither warm nor cool — it is exactly the temperature of the air, which nothing else here manages — and it has a surface like glazed pottery that is somehow unpleasant to keep touching, not painful, just wrong, so that the hand withdraws on its own. It has no seams. It makes no sound. It has been there long enough that the growth has surrounded it but not long enough that the growth has grown over it, which is the same as saying that the growth will not grow on it. This is the only object in the basin that might be the machine, or a piece of the machine, or the machine's mark. Nobody knows.

Scattered elsewhere: a few worked stones that could be paving; a length of something metallic and unrusted, bent in a curve; a socket in the wall near the platform, a fist-sized hole with polished sides, empty.

That is all. The place that made itself dark did not fill itself with much else. It was built to be mostly empty and, unlike most built things, it has remained so.

---

**The slow change**

The basin has one motion, and it is very slow.

The lid is descending. Measured against the worn steps of the stair — the only reference that has lasted — the black boundary is a little lower than it was when the steps were cut, which is to say it has come down by perhaps the height of a person in the whole span of the stair's existence. Whether this is because the mechanism is failing, or because it was always meant to sink, or because the mountain itself is settling under it, cannot be told from inside. What can be told is that the band of stone between the old rim and the present boundary is bare — the growth stops exactly at the dark. Above the line, in the day, there is only lichen.

So the basin is drawing in. Each century a little less volume. The air a little warmer, for the same energy in a smaller space. The chorus a little louder, for the same animals in a smaller bowl. The pool a little deeper, for the same seepage with a little less wall to spread across. The animals do not notice. The growth does not notice. Only the bare band widens, one finger's width in a lifetime.

There will come a time — very far off; the mountain may have worn away first — when the lid reaches the floor. There is no imagining what happens then. Maybe the darkness simply lies on the stone like a black coin on the flattened top of a dead mountain, and the frogs and the flying things and the green pelt are gone, and the coin stays warm.

Or maybe the machine, whatever it is, was made to do exactly this, and the sinking of the dark is not a dying but a closing — the long, careful shutting of something that was opened once, long ago, for reasons that were left on the northern stair and worn away with it.

---

**Leaving**

Climbing back up the stair you feel the boundary before you reach it: a coolness on the face, a thinning of the wet. The chorus does not fade behind you because the walls carry it up; it is as loud at the rim as at the floor. Then your head is out, and the light comes in like a slap.

The mountaintop is exactly as you left it. Evening, most likely; the low sun on grey stone; a thread of cloud sliding over the black disk and being briefly lost. You are wet to the skin and smell of iron and green. Your hands are stained something the color of tea.

The disk lies on the stone, three hundred meters across, roaring faintly, giving up its warm breath into the cold. It is a few handspans above the rock. It is a little lower than it was.
