You come down to it, though it doesn't feel like descending. The land has been lowering itself for a mile in increments too slight to register in the knees — a slope of a hand's width every hundred paces — and then the reeds close in on both sides of the causeway and the air changes, and you understand that you are at the bottom of something. Not a valley. A shallow bowl in a wet country, no more than a few hundred yards across, with a rim you could step over. That is enough. In a landscape this level, a few feet of depth is a whole world's difference. Water that elsewhere sits a foot below the turf here sits at the surface and a little above it, and everything that stands in the bowl stands in water, or in ground that is water with a habit of holding its shape.

The works fills the bowl. It is the only thing in it.

---

Brick, first. A dark red that has gone nearly black in the places that never dry, which is most places. Six or seven buildings, or perhaps three buildings that have been extended so many times the distinction is lost — engine house, boiler house, a low range of sheds with tall louvred vents in the roof, a chimney that leans very slightly and has leaned that way for so long that its lean has become a fact of the sky rather than a fault. The buildings share walls. Between them run alleys no wider than a cart, and above the alleys run pipes, and above the pipes run walkways of iron grating, and above the walkways more pipes, lagged in canvas that has rotted and been rewrapped and rotted again, so that the wrapping is layered like bark. Nothing here has been built once. It has all been built, and then built onto, and then repaired in the pattern of the repair before, until the distinction between construction and growth is one you stop insisting on.

The ground between the buildings is not ground. It is cobble in places, then stone flag, then iron plate laid over a channel, then open channel with the water an inch below the lip, running slow and brown and warm. There are leats everywhere. They cross under the alleys and reappear. Some carry water toward the engine house and some carry it away and it is not obvious which, because the flow in all of them is so slow that a fallen leaf would take a minute to travel its own length. The channels are lined in brick that has softened at the edges into something like the edges of a very old loaf.

---

The engines run.

That is the first thing about the sound, and for a while it is the only thing: the works is loud in the way that a river is loud, not as an event but as a condition. You stop hearing it as noise within a few minutes, and then you begin to hear it as a set of parts. Beneath everything is the beam engine, one great stroke every four or five seconds — a rising groan, a pause at the top that is almost silence, then a descent that ends in a soft enormous thud you feel through your feet and the water feels through the bowl. On that slow pulse rides everything else: a flywheel somewhere going round with a sound like breath through teeth; the tick and chatter of a governor; steam escaping from a dozen points in long hisses that overlap and never quite synchronize; and water — water falling from spouts into channels, water sucked through valves, water dripping from the undersides of the walkways in a rain that does not depend on the sky.

The sound has a shape. Standing in the central alley you can hear where the engine house is with your eyes closed. Standing at the rim of the bowl you can hear the whole works as a single low thing, like a held chord. It does not stop. There is no indication it has ever stopped. The brick around the engine-house vents has been shaken for so long that its mortar has taken on a fine crazed pattern, a map of a vibration a century and more in the making.

---

The light is what the steam allows. The sky above the bowl is low and even, a lid of grey, but you rarely see it directly, because the works exhales continuously and the exhalation, cooling, hangs. It does not rise as steam does in cold places. It is too warm here and too wet; the air is already full. So the works sits inside its own breath, a fog that thickens in the morning and thins toward the middle of the day and thickens again as evening comes on, never lifting entirely. The light that comes down through it is grey-white and directionless. Shadows are soft to the point of not existing. Everything is lit from everywhere, dimly.

Against that: small points of other light. Furnace doors, when they stand open, throw a wedge of orange across wet cobbles and make the fog above them glow briefly the colour of the inside of a shell. Electric lamps hang in wire cages under the walkways, wired along the pipes, and they are on — they are always on — and each one wears a beard of condensation and lights a sphere about six feet across and nothing beyond it. Walking the alleys is walking from one small lit room of air to the next through grey between.

Every surface is wet. Not damp; wet. The brick sweats. The iron sweats. The lamp-cages bead and drip. If you stand still your own arms bead. Water condenses on the undersides of things and gathers and falls and the falling is part of the sound and the sound is part of the water. There is a rock outcrop at the north edge of the bowl — the only natural stone in the place, a low shoulder of grey limestone the works was built up against — and it weeps. Water comes out of its face in slow threads that have polished channels into it, and on the polished channels a black film of something living catches the lamplight.

---

That is the thing that needs longest to see: how much of this is alive.

It is green. It is very green, once your eye adjusts to a green that is not lit by the sun. Moss on everything horizontal and most things vertical — deep cushions of it on the tops of the channel walls, a thin velvet of it on the brick faces, a bright startling emerald on the warm pipes where the lagging has failed, because the pipes are warm and never dry and the moss has found that it likes that better than anything. Ferns in every mortar joint that is wide enough, hart's-tongue and maidenhair and the small polypodies, their fronds hanging down in rows along the courses of the brick so that the walls look ruled in green. Liverworts on the flags, flat and dark and glossy, so that in places you cannot tell where the stone ends and the plant begins.

And larger things. Horsetail in the channels, standing in the slow water in ranks. A fig, or something with a fig's habit, has come up in the angle between the boiler house and the shed range and put its trunk through the iron of the lowest walkway, which has bent to hold it, and its leaves are broad and lobed and drip from their points in a steady tick. Willow has established on the embankment rim and leans inward, toward the warmth, its branches hung with a grey lichen that is fed by the fog and has never needed rain. Where the alleys widen into anything like a yard, the cobbles have gone entirely under a low forest of rush and sedge and a soft-leaved plant with a purple stem that you don't recognize, thigh-high, close-set, with a smell when you brush it like crushed tomato leaf.

Nothing has been cut back. That is clear. But nothing has been allowed to close the alleys, either — the paths remain walkable, exactly walkable, the width of a person carrying something. The vegetation stops at the edge of the walked line as if it had reached an understanding. The moss on the handrails is worn through in a strip at hand-height. The valve-wheels are bright where they are gripped and green everywhere else.

---

Look for anything moving that isn't water or steam or the engine and you will keep looking.

There are no birds. You notice it late, because the sound of the works is enough that you don't expect to hear birds, but then you realize you haven't seen one, not on the chimney, not on the willow, not anywhere in a place where a heron should be standing in every leat and starlings should be nesting in every vent. There are no fish in the channels — you look, and the water is clear enough in the still places, and the bottom is soft silt and brick rubble and there is nothing over it. No frogs in the sedge. No snails on the wet brick, no midges in the fog, no spider-webs strung under the walkways where every walkway in the world has spider-webs.

The plants are not troubled by it. They are the kinds that do without: mosses and ferns and liverworts and horsetail, spore-bearers, water-and-wind things, plants that were here before there was anything to be pollinated by and would be here after. The fig sets no fruit. The willow, when you look closely, is all one sex. The purple-stemmed plant in the yards flowers, small and pale, and the flowers stand open in the drip and the fog and close again unvisited and drop.

It is not that the works is hostile. It is that nothing else came, or nothing else stayed, and the works and the green have been going on together for so long without a third party that the absence has become part of the arrangement. It is very quiet, ecologically, inside all that noise.

---

Somebody tends it. You do not see them. But the lamps are lit and the furnaces are stoked — there is a stack of black peat bricks under a tarpaulin in the shed range, cut from the fen beyond the rim, and the stack is neither full nor empty. The bearings on the beam are dark with fresh oil. A rag hangs on a valve-wheel where a hand left it. There is a bootprint in the silt at the edge of a leat, and the water is standing in it and has not yet begun to soften its edges, which in this place means it is less than an hour old.

Whoever they are, they are doing the same things that have been done here since the mortar was wet. The works is not producing anything, so far as you can tell. It pumps. It lifts water out of the bowl and puts it into a channel that runs out through a cut in the rim, and the water goes away into the reedbed, and the reedbed gives it back through the ground, and the rock weeps it and the fog condenses it and the channels bring it around again. The engine burns the fen and the fen grows back at the rate the engine burns it. Whatever the works was for, it has become something else: a slow heart in a wet place, keeping a level.

You can feel the level. Stand for a while and you become aware that the water in the channels is not simply slow but breathing — rising a finger's width over an hour, falling a finger's width over the next — and that the breath is the engine's, the sum of ten thousand strokes, the bowl filling and being emptied and filling in a cycle too long to see and too steady to doubt.

---

Toward what the light says is evening, the fog thickens until the far end of the central alley is gone. The lamps become the only fixed points. The fig ticks. The engine groans and pauses and comes down. Somewhere a valve opens, and a new hiss joins the others and finds its place among them, and the sound of the works reorganizes around it by some fraction and settles.

The brick will be a grain softer in the morning. The moss on the warm pipe will be, by some measurement nobody makes, a little further along its length. The water will have gone round once more.

It has been like this for longer than it was ever anything else. It is not waiting. It is not ruined. It is simply what a place becomes when it is left with only water and green and one slow machine, and enough time to stop being anything but itself.
