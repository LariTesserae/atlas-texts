The room comes before anything else. There is no approach to it — no path, no slope, no last hard scramble over scree — because the mountain that ought to hold it up has not been finished yet. You are simply inside, stooping, and the walls are close enough on every side that your shoulders learn their shape before your eyes do.

It is about the size of a small kitchen. The ceiling is low and uneven and in one place not there at all. The air is hot in the way a bakery is hot, hot from the inside of the materials rather than from any sun, and so wet that breathing feels like drinking. Everything drips. Water gathers on the underside of the ceiling blocks in beads the size of fingertips and lets go without warning, and where a drop lands on the wall it does not run off — it sinks in, and the stone around it goes briefly darker and softer, like bread taking milk.

Because the stone is not finished being stone.

You can see this if you look closely at the blocks. They are cut — there are tool marks, the shallow scallops left by a wooden wedge driven and levered, driven and levered — but the cuts are too clean. Nothing has ever worn here. The corners meet at angles that are exactly what they are and have never been anything else, and this is unpleasant to look at for long, the way a word repeated too many times stops meaning. Press a thumb into the face of a block and it yields a little, warm, resisting the way clay resists at the point just before firing. Pull the thumb away and the dent stays for a moment, then fills, then is gone, and the surface has not remembered.

The blocks are grey-green. In the dim they seem to carry a faint light of their own, not enough to read by, a glow like the belly of a hot pot in a dark room, but greener, the green of things that grow rather than things that burn.

And things do grow. That is the second thing about the room, after its smallness: it is packed. There is no empty volume in it. Between you and every wall there are ferns, unfurling from cracks that should not be wide enough to hold them, fronds still tight and knuckled at the tip and already the length of your forearm. Liverworts lie flat and glossy over the lower courses like something spilled. A vine as thick as a wrist has come in through the gap in the ceiling and gone into the wall on the far side — into it, not along it — and where it enters, the block has closed around it as neatly as flesh around a splinter. There is a moss on the wet places that is so bright it looks lit. When you shift your weight the leaves press against your back, your cheek; there is always a leaf against some part of you. The plants are wet the way the stone is wet. It is not clear, in this room, which of them is the substrate and which the growth.

The floor is not floor. It is more block, laid rougher, and between the blocks the water has pooled to the depth of a finger and is warm.

Then the noise arrives, or rather you become able to notice that it has been there all along, because it is the kind of loud that is so total it registers first as pressure. Hammering. Not one hammer. Mallets, wooden by the sound — a dense, dull, dead sound, no ring — striking in an uneven multitude from every direction at once, so that the room is not so much surrounded by the work as suspended inside it. Beneath the hammering there is a lower thing, a long groan that has no rhythm, the sound of enormous weight settling into a position it has not quite agreed to. That is the mountain. It is being built underneath the room, course by course, to hold the room up where it already is. The room came first. The room is the summit, and the summit has to be kept at the top, so the builders raise the ground beneath it and the whole thing climbs, and every block laid below sends up through the walls a shudder you feel in your teeth.

There are no builders in the room.

There are tools. A mallet with a head of some wood so heavy and dark it looks wet even where it is dry, its handle worn pale where the hand goes. Wedges, split from the same wood, with their driven ends frayed to brushes. Two baskets, coiled from split root, one full of grey grit that steams very slightly, the other empty and wet inside. A length of vine rope. A thing that might be an antler or a rib, pointed, used for scoring lines. Nothing of metal, except — half under a fern at the base of the wall — a small copper ring, green with wet, too small to be a bracelet, its purpose gone.

The tools are in use. You can hear them. You put a hand flat against the nearest wall and feel the blows landing on the other side of it, close, no more than an arm's length away, and you understand that the other side of this wall is not outside. There is no outside yet. On the other side of the wall is the wall being made, and the hands making it are in the stone, or are the stone, or arrived before they were needed and have been working ever since in the space between one block and the next.

Sometimes a blow comes from a direction in which there is no wall.

The gap in the ceiling is the one place light comes in, and the light is grey and comes from very close. If you stand under it — you must stand crooked, the plants will not let you stand straight — you can see that what is above the room is not sky in the way sky is usually meant. It is sky, certainly: the colour is right, that thin cold high grey of a summit at dawn with weather below. But it is a hand's width away. You could reach up and touch it, and if you did your fingers would come back wet and warm, because the sky, like the stone, is still fresh, still condensing out of whatever it is that things condense out of, and it has not cooled. The ceiling blocks are being set into it. That is what the ceiling is: sky, cut into courses while it is soft, lifted, fitted. The gap moves. You watch a block that was not there arrive along one edge of the opening, sit, weep, set. The gap is smaller. Later — though *later* is doing uncertain work here — the gap will be somewhere else, opened again by the hands you cannot see, because the room is not being finished, it is being kept.

You wait for something that is not the room to move, and for a long time nothing does.

Then a snail. Just one, on the underside of the vine where it enters the wall, a snail with a shell the colour of the copper ring and about the same size, moving across the vine's skin at the pace of a snail. It has been there the whole time. It is, you come to feel, the only thing in the room that is exactly one age. The ferns are older than the cracks they grow from; the blocks are older than the ground beneath them and younger than the marks upon them; the sky is younger than the room it roofs. The snail has been alive for as long as a snail has been alive and no longer, and it makes its way along the vine leaving a track that the wet immediately erases, the only erasure this place has ever seen.

It is the room's weather that undoes you finally. In the heat and the drip, a cloud forms at head height — small, a cloud the size of a sleeping dog, grey and complete with a flat bottom and a rounded top, hanging among the fern tips and moving very slowly toward the gap in the ceiling as though it means to leave. It rains, a little, on the liverworts beneath it. Where the rain falls the liverworts thicken audibly, a faint wet crackle under the hammering. The cloud reaches the gap and does not go through. It stops against the underside of the sky the way a hand stops against glass, and flattens, and is absorbed, and the sky where it went in is for a moment darker and softer, and then it is not, and the surface has not remembered.

The hammering does not pause. The mountain groans beneath and rises a fraction. Somewhere under your feet, the ground that will one day be the world's highest ground is being laid by hand, warm and wet and cut with wood, so that this small crowded green room, this room made first of all things and then never left alone, can go on being at the top of it.
