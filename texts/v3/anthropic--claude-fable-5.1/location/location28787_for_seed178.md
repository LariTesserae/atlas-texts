The valley begins where the fjord ends, and it is still deciding where that is.

At the mouth the tide runs in over gravel that was under a kilometre of ice forty years ago and has not yet learned to be a beach. The stones are sharp. They have not been rolled long enough to lose their edges, and they cut boot soles and the pads of the dogs that come down here in the dark to work over what the water leaves. The water leaves a great deal. The river is in flood, as it is most nights in this season, and it comes down brown and roaring with the whole freight of the upper valley in it — silt, crushed rock, torn moss in rafts the size of tables, whole trees with their root plates still gripping a wedge of soil. The trees are young, none of them older than the deglaciation, but some are already twenty metres tall and they hit the fjord like felled masts.

It is dark. It has been dark for two months and will be dark for another one. Not the dark of a room but a thick, working dark with things in it: the sky is a lid of cloud lit faintly from within by something you cannot place, a bruise-colour that is not dawn and does not move. Under it the valley is layers of black — the black of the forest, the wet black of the cliffs, the moving black of the water with a grey scum of foam riding on it. Snow does not stay. It falls, it lies for an hour on the moss, and then the air off the fjord comes up warm and wet and takes it, and the whole valley drips. The cold is not the clean cold of deep winter. It is a cold that is always a degree or two from freezing, that gets into wool and stays there, that condenses on the inside of a hood and runs down the neck. Everything sweats. The rocks sweat. Water comes out of the fresh cliff faces in a hundred small weeping seams, stains the stone dark, freezes into a rind by morning and lets go again by afternoon, and the rind comes off in sheets and takes a skin of rock with it. This is how the cliffs are being made. They are very new. They were the sides of the glacier's bed and they still hold its shape — smooth, grooved, scored in long parallel scratches — but they are already cracking, already shedding, already growing the first stubble of alder in every ledge that will hold a fist of dirt.

The noise is the first thing and the last thing. It is not one noise. It is a stack of them, and the lowest is the one you feel rather than hear: a long note, somewhere below what an ear can properly resolve, that comes down the valley from the ice front and gets into the bones of the face. That is the work. The other noises are what the work makes. Above it is the river, which is not a sound so much as a pressure — a continuous white roar with detonations in it where boulders roll and knock, where a bank lets go, where a tree jams sideways across a channel and the whole flood piles against it and finds a new way. Above that is the wind in the canopy, and there is a great deal of canopy for the wind to be in. And above that, threaded through all of it, the birds, who do not sleep because there is no night to sleep in, only this long dark, and who shout across it constantly: geese in their thousands on the flats, kittiwakes on the new cliffs, something small and endlessly repetitive in the alders that sounds like a stone tapped on a stone. You do not speak here. You put your mouth against a hood and shout, and the other person nods, and mostly you use your hands.

The forest is the wrong forest for the latitude. Everybody who comes here says so, in the tone of someone remarking on the weather. It is temperate rain-forest, the kind that ought to belong to somewhere far south with mild winters and a long grey growing season, and it has come up in forty years to a density that would take four hundred anywhere else. The soil under it is a black spongy loam a metre deep in places, lying directly on raw glacial rubble, with no transition between them: you can push a hand down through the loam and feel it stop against angular gravel that has never had a root in it. The loam was laid. That is the word people use — not grown, laid — and they use it the way they'd say a road was laid. Something in it builds on itself faster than any peat, holds warmth from somewhere, and everything that touches it grows. Alder first, then willow, then the tall conifers with drooping tips that shed water like tents, and under all of it a moss so thick and so saturated that walking on it is like walking on a wet mattress; it takes a footprint and holds it, and the footprint fills with water, and in the dark you find your way by these small mirrors of not-quite-light. Ferns to the waist. Fungi in shelves and ranks, pale and enormous, on every fallen trunk. The whole thing steams faintly where it is warmer than the air, and the air is full of the smell of it — rot and resin and cold mud, and behind those the mineral flatness of ground-up rock, which is what the river smells of, which is what everything smells of when the wind comes down from the ice.

The forest is also being destroyed. This is the other thing the valley does. Every night the river takes some of it — a bank collapses, a channel jumps, a stand that was on dry ground at midnight is a gravel bar by morning with the stumps still in it, upside down, roots to the sky. Every night the cliffs shed onto it, and a fan of fresh rock spreads out through the alders and buries them, and by the end of the week there is a haze of green on the rockfall and the alders are coming back up through it. Terraces form on the inside of the meanders and are cut away on the outside. Lakes appear behind ice dams and drain in an afternoon with a sound like the end of the world, leaving a plain of fine grey silt with drowned trees standing in it, and the silt goes green in ten days. Nothing here is more than a few years from having been something else. The map that was made in autumn is wrong by spring, and no one bothers to correct it; the surveyors put in stakes and the stakes wash out, and the stakes are found downstream in the fjord, and put in again.

The stakes are most of what is built here. That, and the things that go with them. Walk up the east side, where the ground is a little higher and holds, and every few hundred metres you pass one: a rod as thick as a thumb, dull grey, driven into the loam or the rock or, in places, standing in the river itself, in the full force of the flood, unmoving, with the water piling against it in a white collar and going round. They do not vibrate. They do not lean. Whatever they are made of does not care about the river, and when the bank they were driven into is gone they simply stand in the air where the bank was, and the flood goes past them. Between some of them, where the valley narrows, there are lines — cables, you would say, except that they go from a stake on one side to nothing on the other, straight out into the dark over the water and stopping, taut, at a point with no anchor. The dogs will not walk under them. Nothing else is bothered. Kittiwakes perch on them.

There is a hut on the second terrace, about a third of the way up, and it is an ordinary hut — plank walls, a stove, a roof of tarred sheet, a rack of boots by the door. It is on skids, because it has had to be moved twice. Behind it, half in the alders, is the platform that came before the hut, a slab of something smooth and warm to the touch that sits a hand's width off the moss and does not touch it, and that anyone who has been here more than a season stops noticing. A drying rack of caribou bone leans against one edge. Someone has scratched a tally into the slab with a knife and got nowhere, and then painted the tally on instead. Further up there is a bridge, or a piece of one: a span that leaves the east bank at a good height and stops in the air over the middle of the river, sheared clean, its end fifty metres from anything. The river that it crossed is no longer under it. The river is a hundred metres west now, and the bridge stands over a gravel bar where geese nest, and in the flood the gravel bar goes under and the geese go up in a screaming cloud and settle again on the bridge.

The birds are everywhere. There is no quiet season in a place where the light does not change, and the geese in particular have decided that the flats at the valley mouth are theirs; there are so many that the ground moves, that walking through them is like walking through a grey surf that closes behind you. Ptarmigan in the willow scrub, white, invisible until they go up under your feet like grenades. Ravens in the tall trees, in pairs, watching. Eagles on the new cliffs, and the cliffs themselves alive with kittiwakes and fulmars in every fresh crack, ten thousand of them, a continuous rising and settling that from across the valley looks like the cliff breathing. On the ground the tundra animals and the forest animals are mixed together in a way that has not been sorted yet: hare, vole, a marten that lives in the hut roof, foxes in two colours, and once, coming up out of the fjord onto the gravel in the dark, a bear that had swum from somewhere and stood dripping in the roar and looked at the light in the hut window and went on into the alders. Salmon came up the river three years ago and no one knows how they knew. They hang in the eddies behind the standing stakes, dozens deep, and the river carries them up and sets them down again, and the foxes take what the flood strands.

Insects, even in this cold. Something like a midge in clouds so thick in the lee of the cliffs that you breathe them. They come up off the wet moss in the dark and go for the eyes.

Follow the river up. Past the hut the terraces steepen and the forest thins — not because the ground is worse but because it is newer, and the loam has not been down as long — and then you are on rock again, sharp fresh rock in fans and drifts, rock that was inside a mountain until the ice took it and inside the ice until the ice let it go last week. The noise changes here. The river is still under everything, but the low note is closer and you can feel it now in the ground, in the soles of the feet, a slow grinding hum with a shape to it, rising and falling like breath. The air gets colder and wetter at once. Fog lies on the rock in a layer the height of a man, coming off the ice, and you walk with your head in clear dark and your legs in it. Then the fog thins and the ice front is there.

It is a wall. It runs from one side of the valley to the other, two kilometres of it, and it is the only thing in this place that gives off light — a low cold blue coming from inside the ice, unsteady, brighter in some places than others, moving. It is not a clean face. It is a face that is being taken down. There are notches in it, and shelves, and places where it has been cut back a hundred metres into a bay, and the light is brightest at the working edges, where the note is loudest, where the ice is shaking. You can see it shake. Slabs the size of houses tremble along their length and then let go and come down in a fall that you see a full second before you hear, and then hear with the whole body, and then the water comes up out of the base of the fall in a brown wave, and the wave is what will be in the fjord in an hour. Meltwater comes out from under the ice in a dozen places at once, some of it in fountains, some of it in vents that are dry one moment and roaring the next. The rock at the foot is scoured clean and wet and blue-lit and there is nothing on it but the stakes, standing in the ice-fall zone as if the ice-fall zone were a meadow, and along the base of the wall, where the light is, a line of small dark shapes that might be more of them or might be something else, and that nobody goes close enough to check.

No one knows what does it. This is not a mystery, exactly; it is just not information anyone here has, in the way that no one who lives by a road knows how the road was made. It is understood that the ice is being taken back, that the taking is a job, that the job goes on whether or not anyone watches. That the note is part of the job. That the loam and the forest are part of the job, laid ahead of the ice like a carpet unrolled toward a door. That the stakes mark something — a line, a schedule — and that the valley is meant to look, eventually, like the valley further down, and then like something further down again that no one alive has seen. In the meantime the ice comes down in thunder and the river changes its mind every night and the forest grows and drowns and grows and the birds shout over all of it, and the hut on its skids sits a little further up the terrace than it did last year, and someone keeps the stove in and paints another mark on the slab.

It does not stop. That is the thing to know about the place if you know one thing. There is no hour of the dark when the note drops out, no hour when the river is quiet, no hour when nothing falls. Lie in the hut with the stove roaring and the wool steaming and you feel the note in the boards under your back and the floor lifts a hair with each big fall and settles, and the geese are shouting and the roof is being hammered by rain that was snow a minute ago and will be sleet in another, and the marten is moving over your head, and somewhere very close a whole bank of moss and alder and black loam and a nest of something and a stake that did not move is going into the water with a sound like a door slamming on the world, and outside the small window there is a blue light on the underside of the cloud that does not flicker like fire and does not go out.
