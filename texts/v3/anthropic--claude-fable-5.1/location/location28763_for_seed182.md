The way down is a stair cut into cooled rock, and the stair is wet. That is the first thing: the wetness, before the heat, before anything is visible. The steps have not been walked long enough to be worn, and their edges are still crisp, still faintly glassy where the tool went through, so that a hand on the wall for balance comes away with a bead of blood as often as a bead of water. Then the heat arrives, not from below but from ahead, as if a door had been opened onto a room where bread has been baking for a very long time.

The room is a cavern roughly the size of a cathedral nave, and it did not exist eighty years ago. Its far wall is the reason it exists and the reason anyone is here. That wall is a tongue of intrusive rock — a slow, thick body that pushed up from somewhere beneath and stopped, or nearly stopped, and has been cooling ever since. Cooling, but not cooled. It still gives off enough heat to feel on the face from forty paces, and in its deepest cracks, low down near the floor, there is a dull orange that is not a lamp. That orange is the oldest light in the place. Everything else has been arranged in relation to it.

The rest of the light is electric, and there is not much of it. Filament bulbs in wire cages, hung on cords that run everywhere overhead like a second, tidier root system, most of them switched off. The town cannot afford to burn them all at once, and nobody has ever found a reason to. Two or three are lit at any hour, chosen by some rota nobody has written down, and they make small yellow pockets in an air that is otherwise the colour of a green bottle held up to a distant window. Underfoot, occasionally, the orange.

The green is the plants. It should not be possible for anything to grow here, and for a while nothing did; then a seed came down the stair on someone's coat, or in the crack of a crate, or on a draught from the surface through some fault too narrow for a person, and it found the warmth and the perpetual drip and an entire cavern of nothing to compete with. That first plant is probably still here somewhere, buried under its descendants. Ferns the height of a standing man grow out of the seams between stone blocks. A fig has come down through the roof — its roots are visible first, a curtain of them, pale as candle-wax, and then, following them up with the eye, the swelling where the trunk has jammed itself into the fracture it descended through and is patiently, over decades, widening it. Moss covers every surface that is not either scalding or in daily use. Something with leaves like wet leather has taken over the whole of the upper gallery on the east side and has to be cut back from the lamp cords once a season, and the cuttings are dried against the hot wall and burned for the little extra heat nobody needs but everyone likes. The ecology is not complex. It is a handful of species that happened to arrive, each grown to the limit of what heat and water and dimness allow. There is nothing here to eat them.

Almost nothing. A moth, grey and slow, that seems to breed in the fig roots and dies in the lamps when the lamps are on. A gecko, pale to the point of translucence, that lives only on the hot wall itself, on the sections warm enough to be uncomfortable to touch, and is seen perhaps once a month by anyone. Nothing else. No birds have found the stair. No rats. The quiet has a particular quality because of this — it is not the quiet of a wild place, where the silence is full of small movements, but the quiet of a place that has never been fully alive, only occupied.

The town occupies it completely. There is no floor left in the cavern that is not built on, and where the floor ran out they built on what they had built. It is a settlement that has been assembled entirely from the inside: there was no room to stand back and plan it, so it grew the way a coral does, each addition wedged against the last. Brick where they could bring brick down; more often blocks of the cavern's own rock, cut fresh and sharp and laid without mortar, the joints sweating. Stairs go up through the floors of rooms and come out in other rooms. The walkway along the upper gallery is a plank laid on the shoulders of two buildings that were never meant to touch. Nothing has a front. Nothing has a back. From any point in the town it is possible to see into at least four rooms that are not the room one is standing in, and so the people have stopped, over two generations, distinguishing very sharply between inside and out.

And the town works. This is what the heat is for. The wall drives everything. Where it is hottest, near the floor, iron pans of water sit directly on the rock and steam; the steam is caught in hoods of hammered copper gone green with the wet and led through pipes to a row of engines that are the largest objects in the cavern after the wall itself. They are low-pressure machines, single-cylinder, enormous and patient. Each one takes something like four minutes to complete a stroke. The piston travels up almost imperceptibly, hangs, and comes down with a long exhalation and a soft clank of the flywheel — and then nothing, for four minutes, but the hiss of the leak in the third coupling that no one has fixed because everyone likes the sound. The engines run presses and rollers and a drying gallery where sheets of something — felt, or paper, or a fibre pulled from the leather-leaved plant — are hung in the hot draught until they stiffen. What the town makes goes up the stair in bundles and does not come back. What the town makes is not the point.

The point is the wall, and the wall is moving.

Not fast. A finger's width in a year, perhaps; a hand's breadth over the time it takes a child to grow tall enough to work the presses. The intrusion has not stopped after all — it is still, very slowly, being pushed from beneath, still advancing into the cavern it made, still adding fresh rock at its face, rock so young it has a skin like cooled glass and breaks with edges that cut. The town has known this from the start. The first row of buildings, the ones that stood nearest the heat when the heat was worth standing near, have been taken. Not crushed, exactly — nothing so sudden. Encroached upon. A doorway that was once a doorway is now a rectangular recess in dark stone, half a metre deep, with the hinges still bolted to its jamb. A little farther along, only the top course of a window is visible, a stone lintel breaking the surface of the rock like the back of a swimmer. Someone's iron bedframe protrudes from the wall at knee height, two of its legs already inside, the rest of it glowing very faintly at the joints when the lamps are out.

They do not fight this. They move. Every year the row nearest the wall is dismantled — the useful stone carried through the town on people's backs, because there is no cart that could get through — and laid down again on the far side, against the cold rock, where the ferns have to be cut back to make room. The town creeps across the floor of the cavern at the pace of the wall. There is a mark cut in the stone of the stair, at the height of a man's chest, that shows where the wall stood when the stair was cut. It is a long way from the wall now. There are people alive who remember the mark being near.

So the whole settlement has a grain to it, a direction, like a tree or a glacier. The newest buildings are at the back, in the cool and the dark, raw-cut and clean and fern-encumbered. The oldest are at the front, near the light and the heat, blackened and sweating, and then older than those, and then the wall. To walk from the stair to the face of the intrusion is to walk forward in time, through the town's whole history, to the present, which is a surface of glass-skinned rock too hot to touch that will, tomorrow, be a finger's width closer.

The air is heavy and never still, though nothing in it could be called a wind — only a continuous drift, hot from the wall and cooled against the roof, so that the roof drips constantly and the drips are the loudest thing in the place for most of every hour. They fall onto moss, onto copper, onto the standing pans where they crack briefly into steam. Where they land on the hot rock itself they leave nothing — no stain, no mineral — because the rock is too new to have anything to give up. The whole cavern smells of iron and wet fern and something else underneath, faintly sweet, that is either the fig or the town's own manufacture and that no one who lives here can smell anymore.

At what passes for night, the rota switches off the last bulbs, and the only light is the orange in the wall's low seams and the paler orange where the bedframe and a few other captured things have warmed through. The ferns stand black against it. The engines breathe. Somewhere the gecko moves over stone that would burn a hand, at a temperature it has no name for and finds entirely comfortable, and the wall, with nothing watching it, advances by a distance that cannot be seen.
