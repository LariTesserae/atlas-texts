Start with the noise, because it arrives first and never leaves.

It is a roar that has no edge to it — not a wave breaking and withdrawing but a single held syllable, low and wide, the sound of a great deal of water falling a short distance over an enormous surface. You hear it from the switchbacks below, when the trail is still in trees, and you feel it in the ground before you understand what it is. By the time you come out onto the shelf it has stopped being a sound. It is the medium. You speak inside it and your voice goes nowhere. People who live here for a season say the roar goes quiet after about a week; what they mean is that they have stopped being able to hear anything else.

The shelf is perhaps four hundred paces across, a rough oval of white stone set into the side of the mountain a little above where the clouds like to rest. Behind it the slope continues up, dark with dripping forest and then bare. In front of it the world drops away into a soft grey nothing that is sometimes cloud and sometimes, for an hour in the afternoon, a view of valleys so far down they look painted. The shelf itself did not exist in the way it does now a few hundred years ago. It is still, in the strict sense, being made.

The water comes out of the mountain along the back wall of the shelf in a line of springs — no single mouth, but a seep the width of a village, hot when it emerges, steaming into the cool air, and so saturated with dissolved stone that it cannot hold it. Within a few feet of open air the water begins to let the stone go. It lays it down on whatever it touches: the ground, the previous day's stone, a dropped tool, a fallen branch, the edge of a footprint. Over years this becomes a terrace; over centuries, a shelf with a rim; over long enough, a mountain wearing an apron of its own dissolved interior, hardened again on the outside. The stone is the color of the inside of a shell. Where it is a week old you can mark it with a thumbnail. Where it is a decade old it rings under a stick. It has no grain and no bedding you can see, only faint growth lines like the inside of a tree, and it has never once been worn down by anything. There are no gullies here, no rounded corners, no scree. Every edge is as the water left it, sharp or lipped or fluted according to how the water was moving when it set. This is the youngest country you will ever stand on.

The water itself is not loud on its own. It is loud because of the arrangement. The shelf has been built up into a set of broad shallow steps — the terracing is partly the water's own habit and partly deliberate, as we will come to — and the outflow from the back springs runs across every one of them in a sheet a finger deep and forty paces wide before spilling off the lip to the next. Forty paces of water, dropping a hand's height, a hundred times over across the whole shelf, all at once, forever. That is the roar. It does not vary. There are no floods here and no droughts. The springs come from too deep to notice weather. The sheet flows at the same depth in every season, and a person who marks the level with a scratch on a stone will find, a year later, that the scratch has been buried under a thin new skin of white and the water is exactly where it was.

Where the water runs, nothing grows. It is too warm at the top and too full of mineral throughout, and anyway the ground is being reburied daily. But the shelf is not all wet, and the air is. The hot springs steam continuously into cold air, and the cloud that lives around this altitude comes and goes through the valley below, and between them the whole place exists in a state of perpetual condensation. Every surface not being actively encased in stone is beaded. Every leaf drips. And so on the ridges between the water channels, on the drier rims and the walls and the old settled stone that the flow has abandoned, the vegetation is extravagant. Moss so thick and deep that it gives like a mattress, in a dozen greens. Tree ferns with trunks a man could not put his arms around, their fronds heavy with water, bowing. Filmy ferns that are a single cell thick and look like wet green tissue paper laid over the rock. Small hard-leaved shrubs with white flowers that never seem to open fully in the damp. Everything is cool to the touch and slightly cold to sit on. The light is good — this high, above most of the murk, the overcast is thin and bright and the white stone throws it back up at you — and the greens are the specific, saturated, almost alarming greens of a place where nothing has ever been dry.

There is very little living in it. This is worth saying because the lushness suggests otherwise. There are no fish, because there is no water an animal could live in; the sheet is too shallow and too warm and too alkaline. There are no frogs for the same reason. There are a few birds — small dark ones that move through the tree ferns in silence, or in what passes for silence here, since you could not hear them if they sang. Occasionally a large slow beetle. Once in a long while, in the dry moss high on the back wall, something with fur that no one has ever seen clearly. Mostly, the shelf is plants and stone and water, and the absence of anything else is part of its character. It is a place where things do not so much live as accumulate.

And then there is the town.

It is not obvious at first that the terraces are anything but terraces. The steps the water runs over are too regular, but the water makes regular things. It is when you look at the rims — the low lips at the edge of each step, over which the sheet spills — that you begin to see the courses. Someone laid these. Someone set stone on stone, without mortar, in lines, and the water came over them and coated them and fused them and made them look like nothing anyone ever did. Then look again at the back wall, and the walls that come forward from it at right angles, and the ones that cross those. The shelf is gridded. There are streets. They are the dry ridges between the channels, and they are paved with the same white, and they run in a plan.

This is the settlement, and the settlement is the point of the place, and the point of the settlement is that it is not finished and is not meant to be finished for a very long time.

The idea, as far as anyone can explain it, is this. The stone is laid down by the water at a pace you can watch if you are patient — a skin a day, a finger a season, a course of blocks swallowed in a lifetime. A wall built here is a wall that the mountain will eventually take and make its own, incorporate, thicken, and make permanent in a way no wall built anywhere else can be permanent. So the town is being built ahead of itself. The people who work here — and there are never many; on most days you could count them on your fingers, and on many days you could not find one — set out walls in the plan, dry-laid, from blocks of older stone cut off the back wall with mauls and wedges of a harder rock carried up from far below. They lay foundations, doorways, the bottoms of stairs, the channels that will one day be conduits, the basins that will one day be cisterns. They lay them in the water's path, deliberately, and then they leave them, and then the water does the rest. A wall laid this year will be a stone wall in a hundred years and part of the mountain in a thousand. The builders will not see any of it. They are laying out a city for a population that does not exist yet, on the assumption that when the stone has finished with it, people will come.

So the town is empty. That is the first thing and the last thing about it. There are, across the shelf, the beginnings of perhaps two hundred rooms, in blocks, along streets, around what will be squares, and not one has a roof and not one has a second story and in almost none has anyone ever slept. You can walk the streets for an hour and meet nobody. The rooms are knee-high or shoulder-high, open to the sky, some of them half-full of running water a finger deep, some dry and carpeted in moss, some in the process of turning from a thing someone made into a thing the mountain is making. The scale of the plan is a real town — you can see where the wide street will be, where the steps will go up to the upper quarter, where the channel branches to feed what must be intended as a public basin — and the amount of anyone in it is a few figures far off, bent, moving slowly, unable to call to you and unable to hear if you called.

Those few live in a cluster of low huts on the driest corner of the shelf, roofed in fern thatch and tree-fern trunks, which they move every few years because the stone finds anything that sits still. This is the rule the place has taught them and which they hold to with more gravity than any law: the water does not distinguish. It will take a wall, a tool, a boot, a body. A maul left overnight in the sheet has a skin on it by morning. A basket left a season is a white basket-shaped stone. There are, on the back wall, high up where the earliest work was done and long since abandoned to the springs, shapes in the smooth flowstone that were once a ladder, a coil of rope, and — the workers do not like to point to it — something with the outline of a sleeping man. Whether it is a man or a story about a man does not matter to how they behave. Nothing is left in the water that is not meant to be taken. Everything that is meant to be taken is left in the water, and then let go of. You lay the stone, you step back, you do not touch it again. Touching it again means you have not understood.

There is very little else that happens. The sun comes up behind the mountain and reaches the shelf late; the cloud below rises in the afternoon and sometimes covers everything so that the roar is the only proof the world still exists; the cloud sinks; the light goes. The workers cut stone, in the roar, without speaking, since speech is useless, using a small set of gestures that a newcomer picks up in a week. They eat in their huts. They lay a course or two of blocks in a day, perhaps, and then stand looking at it with the particular expression of people who have just given something away. In a good year they finish a room's footings. The room will not be habitable for longer than any of them, or their children, or their children's children, will live. There is no sign that this troubles them. The shelf itself is only a few centuries into being a shelf. Everything here is at the beginning. That is what they came for.

If you stand at the rim, at the lip of the lowest terrace where the last sheet of water goes over into the grey, and put your hand flat on the stone, you can feel two things. One is the vibration of the whole shelf humming with the fall. The other, if you leave your hand there long enough, is that the stone under it is very slightly warm, and that when you lift your hand the palm comes away chalky, and that the mountain has begun, without malice and without pause, to consider making you part of the town.
