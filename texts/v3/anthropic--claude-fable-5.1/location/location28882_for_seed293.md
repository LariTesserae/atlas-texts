Begin with the ground, because the ground is the only thing here that has stopped.

It is a plain, and it is as flat as a thing can become without being water. Whatever hills this country had have been carried off grain by grain over a span so long that the number wouldn't help. No stone larger than a knuckle survives. The surface is silt, packed to the softness of old soap, and over it lies a film of water never deeper than a boot's welt and never quite absent. Press a palm down and water rises around the fingers, clear, faintly warm, and when you lift the hand the impression stays for a while before the mud sighs shut. The horizon is level in every direction to the degree that the eye distrusts it. Nothing interrupts it. Nothing has interrupted it for an age.

Then the settlement, in the middle of all this, doing the opposite.

It occupies a rough circle perhaps four hundred paces across, and you know where it begins because the ground changes underfoot: a low raised lip, ankle-high, of something dark and dense and fibrous, packed like peat, compressed like coal, running in a ring. It is dead plant matter. It is very old dead plant matter. Everything inside the ring has been dropping its castoffs onto this rim for a length of time you can begin to estimate by how far your boot sinks — not at all. The rim does not give. It is the settlement's only permanent product.

Inside the ring: thirteen structures, maybe fourteen, spaced out across the shallow water with wide emptiness between them, a hundred paces or more from one to the next. Each is the size of a barn, a chapel, a large house. Each is made entirely of plant. There is no mineral construction anywhere — no stone, no metal, no baked thing. The walls are trunk-fiber woven at a density you would need a saw for, and the roofs are leaf, and the floors where you can see them are root-mat pressed smooth, and none of it is holding still.

That is the first thing that takes a while to accept. A wall is rolling up. Not falling, not being dismantled — rolling, tightly, the way a frond unfurls but reversed, the way a tongue retracts. Where the wall was there is now a column, glossy and green-black, sweating, and where the column meets the roof the roof is already correcting for the loss of support, thickening, sending down a new lattice with a sound like a bundle of wet rope being twisted by many hands. It takes about the length of a slow breath to see that this is happening everywhere. Every structure in the ring is being unbuilt and rebuilt continuously, by itself, at the speed of weather.

The sound of it fills the whole circle. It is a creaking that has no rest in it — the fibrous complaint of tension being taken up, then released, then taken up somewhere else — and under the creaking a pumping, a heavy rhythmic suck, which is water being drawn up out of the flat ground faster than any plant should be able to draw it. You can watch the pools around the base of each structure drop a finger's width while a wall goes up, and refill when it comes down. Over this, dripping: every surface condenses. The air is warm and thick enough to drink and the structures are cooler than the air, and so water beads on every leaf and runs down every column and falls in steady patter into the shallows below, so that inside the ring it is always, softly, raining upward from the ground and downward from the buildings at once.

Look for the mechanism and there is none. There are no seams, no joints, no hinges, no visible source of the effort being expended, which is enormous. Cells here do not divide; they move. Fiber that was structural a moment ago goes soft and is drawn back into the body of the thing, and where it is drawn back there is a brief bluish light at the cut edge, faint, the color of a gas flame seen through milk, and then it is gone and so is the material. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is fetched from outside. Each structure appears to build itself from itself and from the water and from something else you cannot see and the sums do not come out. A stair goes up the inside of one structure — you can see it through a gap that was not there before — and the stair makes two full turns in a space where there is room for one, and then the gap closes and you are left uncertain whether you saw it.

They talk to each other. This becomes clear if you stand in one place long enough to get wet through. When the nearest structure drops an entire side, the one across the water from it, a hundred paces off, begins to raise a matching side within a few moments, as if answering. A ripple of adjustments travels around the ring — a roof here shifts its pitch, a doorway there narrows to a slit and reopens facing a different way — and then settles, and then somebody starts again. There is no visible signal. There is an argument going on that has been going on since before the rim was a hand thick.

The spaces between them are the quiet part, insofar as anything here is quiet. Open water, ankle deep, over black silt, with a low moss growing in it that is the only vegetation in the ring not engaged in the argument. It grows flat and slow and is the color of dull brass. Standing in it you feel how large the empty parts of this place are relative to the built ones, and how deliberate that might be — each structure with its own generous margin, its own moat of stillness. Or perhaps not deliberate. Perhaps they simply cannot bear to be nearer to each other than that.

And it is full of animals.

The animals are the part that makes it a place rather than a phenomenon. Wading birds stand in the shallows on legs like reeds, spearing at things too small to see, and they are entirely indifferent to what is happening above them. Herons have a way of standing on a roof that is being unmade beneath their feet and simply stepping, one foot at a time, onto whatever has come up to replace it, without seeming to look down. Smaller birds nest in the walls and are turned out of their nests every hour and rebuild in the new walls with a fury of chatter that is one of the loudest things in the ring. Frogs live inside the fiber — in it, embedded — and when a wall rolls up they come out with it, dozens at a time, dark and glossy and wet, and drop into the shallows and swim to the next structure and climb in wherever it has opened. They have a call like two wet stones knocked together and there are so many of them that the knocking is continuous. Insects in low clouds. Something with a shell the size of a spread hand, many of them, moving purposefully across the mud between structures carrying tufts of fiber in their mouthparts; you cannot tell whether they are stealing or delivering. Something long and pale and boneless moving inside a translucent section of wall, visible for a moment as a shadow, and then the wall thickens and it's gone.

The animals do not seem engineered. They seem to have arrived, and stayed, and adapted to the churn the way shore animals adapt to the tide — not by fighting it, by living in the rhythm of it. They are the only things here that behave as if this is normal. Which it is. Which it has been.

It is worth trying to find someone. The structures are shaped as if for occupancy — there are corridors at shoulder width, and shelves at the height a body would sit, and rooms whose proportions are unmistakably rooms — but the rooms open and close like mouths and the shelves rise and are swallowed, and no one is ever sitting on them. A door dilates in the nearest wall and shows an interior lit by a warm green light through leaf, a floor of packed root, a shape in the far wall like a bed being drawn up out of the fiber, half-formed. Then the door is a column. Perhaps the residents are inside and never visible from outside. Perhaps they are the frogs. Perhaps the occupants moved on, or were never here, and what you are watching is a house preparing rooms forever for a household it has forgotten the shape of and is trying, each time, to remember. Perhaps the structures are the residents and the rooms are how they think.

Step back out over the rim, onto the flat.

From outside the ring, with the sound dropping off behind you faster than it should, the settlement looks like a single event — a knot of dark green motion on an infinite bright wet plain, roofs going up and down like something breathing hard, birds lifting off and settling. Beyond it in every direction the plain goes on, worn to nothing, and you begin to think about what the plain is made of, and how deep, and how it came to be this flat, and what it must have been compressed from. The rim under your feet is very old. There must, once, have been other rings.

This is the last one still going. The land is finished. The land has been finished for longer than there have been words for it. In the middle of it, in one small circle, something has decided not to be finished yet, and is building and unbuilding its houses in a warm drizzle of its own making, out loud, with an audience of herons, to an argument nobody remaining can hear the terms of. The water rises around your boots and settles. A wall comes down across the way with a long tearing sigh, and another one, already, is going up.
