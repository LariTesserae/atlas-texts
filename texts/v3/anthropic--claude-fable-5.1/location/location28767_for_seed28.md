The plain gives no warning. It is low, warm, the color of unfired clay, and it runs flat in every direction under a sky that has been scoured to white. Nothing grows on it. Nothing moves on it. You would walk across it for a day and think it the emptiest place you had ever been, and then you would begin to hear something under your feet — not through the air, through the soles — a pressure, a tremor that arrives before any sound, as though the ground were a drumhead and something were leaning on it from below.

Then the sound itself, rising as you walk. And then the edge.

It is a hole in the plain roughly the size of a market square. Its walls go straight down, pale limestone slabbed and weathered to the softness of old soap, and the drop is not far — perhaps four or five stories — but the moment you look over you understand that the plain was never the place. The plain is a lid. The place is underneath.

---

A city fills the hole.

It fills it the way a seed fills a pod: completely, and with nothing to spare. The walls of the sinkhole are the outer walls of the city; the buildings have been cut into them, built out from them, stacked against them, so that from above you look down onto a nest of roofs and terraces and stairs descending in irregular rings toward a small flat floor at the bottom, an open space no larger than a threshing yard, paved in the same pale stone. There are arches. There are galleries that run along the walls at several levels, colonnaded, their columns squat and hand-shaped, each one a slightly different thickness. There are doorways — hundreds of doorways — and every one of them is open. There are no doors. There is no sign there ever were.

The stone is white. The light comes down into the hole from directly overhead, unbroken, and it strikes every surface and rebounds, so that the shadows in the galleries are not dark but merely less white, and the whole city seems lit from within, a bowl of glare. You will squint here always. The inhabitants, when you see them, squint too, or wear strips of woven fiber across their eyes with a slit to see through.

And everywhere, out of every joint between the stones, out of the cracks in the treads of the stairs, from the tops of the columns and the lintels of the empty doorways, grow the stems.

---

They are not like plants you know, but they are plants. They are hollow, jointed, upright, and they are the color of bleached straw with a faint glassy sheen, as though they had been dipped in a thin syrup that dried clear. The tallest reach the height of two men. They branch rarely and then only at the top, into a spray of finer tubes. They have no leaves. They have no roots that anyone has been able to find; where a stem has been pulled from a wall, there is only a shallow cup of powdered lime and a whiff of something like hot pepper.

They do not drink. There is no water here to drink — no well, no cistern, no seep at the bottom of the hole, and the sky over this plain does not rain and has not in living memory. What the stems live on is heat, and light, and — the people of the city will tell you this with their hands, since no one can speak here — the sound.

Because the sound is the stems.

---

It works like this. The sun comes over the lip of the sinkhole in the morning and falls on the highest terraces first. The stone warms. The stems warm. Inside each hollow stem is a resin, amber and thick in the cool of night, and as it warms it thins and expands and begins to move, and the moving resin drags air through pores in the stem walls, and the stem begins — very faintly, one stem alone would be no louder than a held breath — to hum.

There are not one stem. There are, by the reckoning of someone who once tried to count from the floor, something over forty thousand.

By mid-morning the upper city is singing. The sound is a chord — not a pleasant one, not an unpleasant one, a dense unresolved cluster of tones that shifts as the sun moves and different quarters warm. By noon the light has reached the floor and every stem in the hole is awake and the sound has become something else. It stops being a thing you hear and becomes a thing you are inside. It presses on the chest. It makes the teeth ache. The air itself seems to thicken and stand. Loose grit on the pavement dances in small patterns and drifts toward the walls and collects in the corners of the doorways in fine ridged drifts like frost. Speech is impossible; you could scream into someone's ear at arm's length and they would see your mouth move and nothing more. The people of the city do not try. They have a language of hands, quick and small, made to be read in glare.

And here is the strange part, the part that makes the city what it is rather than simply a hole full of loud grass: the stems grow toward the sound. Not toward light, though they need light; not toward warmth, though they need that too. Where the sound is loudest, they grow thickest and tallest and most lushly, in dense pale groves that lean inward. Where the stone deadens the sound — behind thick walls, in the closed cells — they thin and dwarf and eventually die.

So the city was built to be loud.

---

You see it once you know to look. The arches are not arches for passing under; they are tuned. Each gallery is a slightly different depth and the columns are spaced to trap and throw the tone. The stairs descend in spirals not because spirals are convenient but because a spiral well rings. The small round floor at the very bottom is a focus — stand there at noon and you will not be able to stand there; the sound folds the knees. Around that floor, the stems grow densest of all, a pale forest ten deep, their tops brushing the first gallery, and it is from those stems that the people take what they take.

What they take is the resin. At dusk, when the stone begins to cool and the stems quiet and slump — they do slump, visibly, losing perhaps a hand's width of height as the resin thickens — the people come down with knives of flaked stone and small copper hooks and open the stems near the base and draw out the cooling resin in long threads and wind it onto spindles. It hardens to a clear amber that can be worked warm, drawn into cord, pressed into bowls, laid in sheets across a doorway to make a pane that lets the light in and keeps the worst of the sound out. It is also — chewed slowly, a fingernail's worth — the only thing here that is like drinking. It is faintly sweet, faintly hot, and after it the mouth does not want water for a day.

Every night the stems are cut. Every day they grow back the distance they were cut. The city has been doing this for as long as the walls have been smooth, and the walls are very smooth.

---

You will notice, at some point, how few people there are.

The city could house a thousand. Every doorway opens into a room, every room has a sleeping ledge cut into its back wall, and there are rooms in tiers all the way down, hundreds of them, each with its open mouth toward the glare. Almost all are empty. Not abandoned — swept. The drifts of dancing grit are brushed out each morning. But there is nothing in them. No bedding, no pots, no tools left lying. The people of the city own their knives and their spindles and their eye-strips and little else, and there are, at most, two dozen of them, and you will rarely see more than one at a time — a figure on a far terrace, cutting; a figure asleep in a doorway on the shaded side, a pane of amber propped across the opening; a figure walking the floor at midday, slowly, with the deliberate unsteady gait of someone moving through deep water, which is how everyone walks down there.

Whether the emptiness is loss or design, they do not say. It may be that there were never more. It may be that the city was always meant for the stems, and the people are the stems' attendants, and a few is enough.

There is one other kind of living thing. It is a beetle about the size of a thumbnail, dull copper, with a domed carapace that rings faintly when tapped. It lives inside the stems, in the hollow above the resin line, and it is the only creature that seems able to bear the noon. You will not see it out of its stem except at dusk, when it climbs to the branching tips and sits there, hundreds of them, small dark points catching the last high light, and does nothing at all for the length of time it takes the shadow to climb the far wall. Then it goes back in. The people leave the beetle stems uncut. They can tell which ones, though you cannot.

---

In the night, the city is almost silent. Almost. The stone gives back its heat slowly and the last stems on the west wall mutter until well after dark, and then there is nothing but the sound of your own blood, enormous after the day, and the tick of the stone contracting, and the very high whine of forty thousand hollow tubes settling into their cool shapes. The stars over the mouth of the hole are hard and few. It is warm still. It will be warm until an hour before dawn, and then the first light will touch the eastern lip, and a single stem up there will begin to breathe.

You can climb out. There is a stair for it, a long one, unroofed, that follows the inner curve of the wall and comes up onto the plain some distance from where you first looked over, and if you climb it before noon you will feel the sound drain out of your body step by step, like a fever leaving. At the top the plain is exactly as flat and warm and empty as it was. You will be able to hear again — a wind, your own footsteps — and the hearing will seem thin and unconvincing, a rumor of sound.

Below you the city will go on being lit and being loud, cut each dusk, grown each dawn, tended by its handful, held in balance with its pale hungry grass, a room in the floor of the world where noise is a kind of weather and the weather never breaks.
