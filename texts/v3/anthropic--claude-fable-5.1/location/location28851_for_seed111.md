The ridge is new. That is the first thing to understand about it, and it takes a while to understand, because a mountain is not something you expect to be new. But this one is still coming up out of the earth at a rate you could almost measure with a stick and a patient life — a fingernail's width in a year, more in the years when the ground shakes — and the summit ridge has not had time to be worn into anything. Its edges are edges. The stone breaks along planes that shine, faintly, a fresh grey-green like the inside of a snapped bone, and where the wind has not yet dulled them these planes will cut a hand. There is no soil anywhere. There is no rounding. The whole crest has the look of something that was broken open recently and left.

About forty years ago — the lichens say roughly this, the pale ones, a few coins of them just beginning on the older surfaces and none at all on the new — a slab the size of a house shifted on the crest. It did not fall. It leaned. It leaned against its neighbor and stopped, and under it a cleft that had been a crack became a room.

---

You enter from the south side, along the ridge, where the crest thins to a blade you can straddle. The opening is a triangle taller than a person and no wider than a person's shoulders at the base, tapering to nothing overhead. The wind coming over the ridge finds this triangle and makes a note in it. Not a howl. A low, sustained, reedy tone, a single pitch that swells and falls with the gusts but never quite stops, like someone blowing across the mouth of an enormous stoneware jar. On some days it is almost musical. It is always loud.

Just inside the mouth is the only part of the place that ever sees sky, and it is a strange kind of daylight even here — the light arrives bounced off the inner faces of the slab, grey and directionless, strong for perhaps two hours around noon and then gone. In that light a little vegetation clings: a cushion of something dense and dark green in the angle of the floor, a few strings of moss on the wall where the seep runs, an orange crust on the stone at knee height that is either a lichen or a mineral bloom and has never been examined closely enough by anyone to say. That is all the growing green in the place. Past the first three or four strides there is not enough light to make chlorophyll worth the effort, and the plants know it and stop.

On the floor near the mouth, if you look down and your eyes have adjusted, there are some flakes of a darker stone than the ridge is made of. Six or seven of them, thin, curved, with the ripple marks of having been struck off a core. Beside them a patch of the floor is scorched — greasy black, the residue of animal fat burned in a shallow hollow of rock. Someone came here. Someone came here with a stone that was not from here, and knapped it, and burned a lamp for a while, and left, and there is no telling whether that was five years ago or five hundred, because nothing rots at this altitude and nothing grows over anything. There are no marks on the walls. There is nothing built. Just the flakes and the burn, and a very faint suggestion — it may be imagination — that the flakes were laid down in a line, pointing inward.

---

Then the room.

The cleft widens behind the mouth into a space about the size of a small chapel, perhaps twelve strides long and half that wide, and tall — the two leaning walls meet somewhere overhead in a dark you cannot see the top of. The floor slopes gently down and away from the entrance, so that as you go in you also go slightly under. The stone underfoot is not scree. It is the original fracture surface, bare and hard, with a thin skin of something over it that is soft and gives a little and is difficult to think about for long.

The air changes. Outside, on the ridge, the air is the thin scouring cold of extreme height, so dry that lips crack in an hour. In here it is cool — only cool — and damp. Damp the way a cellar is damp, the way a barn is damp in winter with the animals in it. Your breath does not feather out white as it did outside. The walls, where you can touch them, are wet, and the wet is not running from anywhere; it is condensing out of the air onto the stone, beading, joining, and every so often a drop lets go from somewhere overhead and falls with a small distinct sound into the dark.

And the dark is full.

---

It takes time to understand this too, because the eyes fail here in a way they don't quite fail anywhere else, and because the mind resists it. The walls are not walls. The walls are covered. Every surface, from the floor to as high as you can reach and, by the sound, much higher, is layered with bodies — furred, folded, pressed against each other and against the stone in ranks two and three deep, hanging in some places and clinging in others and in the deepest corners simply heaped. They are about the size of a spread hand. They have wings, or something that folds like wings. They are warm. If you put your palm flat against the wall — and you will, before you understand what you are touching — it comes away with the heat of a living animal and a scent like old wool and mineral salt and, underneath, something sweet and faintly rotten.

There are thousands. There is no counting. The room is not a room with animals in it; the room is the animals, with a hollow at the center where the air is.

They are not asleep exactly. They are doing something slower than sleep. Each one breathes perhaps once in a minute, and the breath goes on for a long time, a soft extended sigh, and when several thousand of them do this on their own separate slow schedules the sound they make together is continuous — a deep, layered, susurrating roar, like surf heard through a wall, like a field of grain in a strong wind, that fills the chamber completely and mixes with the wind-note from the mouth until the two are inseparable. You cannot speak over it without raising your voice. There are other sounds inside it: small dry clicks, the occasional creak of a folded wing shifting against its neighbor, and once in a while, from somewhere up in the unseeable top of the cleft, a single thin rising whistle that is answered, many seconds later, from a different place.

---

Here is what is strange about them, and it is not the number.

They move. Very slowly, so slowly that if you sat in the dark for an hour you might not be certain you had seen it, the whole mass is rotating. The animals on the outside of the layers, exposed to the cooler air of the hollow, work their way inward over days, and the ones deep against the stone, where the wall's heat and their neighbors' heat keep them, work their way out. A single individual makes the full journey from surface to wall and back in something like a month. The colony as a whole turns itself over, like a body rolling in sleep, and it is this endless patient churning that keeps any one of them from dying of the cold. Nothing directs it. They are not awake enough to decide. It happens the way a pot of water finds its own slow convection.

And they are the same animals. All of them. Not the same kind — the same individuals. There are no young here, no small bodies, no bones on the floor except the very few that make up the soft skin underfoot. Whatever they are, they came into this cleft when it opened, forty years ago, riding the same wind that makes the note in the mouth — blown up from somewhere far below, from valleys they may not remember — and they found this pocket of leaning stone and went into it and slowed down and have not, in any sense that matters, come out. Their metabolisms at this altitude have dropped to something that barely qualifies as living. They will go on this way for a long time. A very long time. They are waiting, or they are not waiting for anything and this is simply the shape their lives have taken. The moisture on the walls is their breath. The warmth is theirs. The room is cool rather than frozen because they are in it; without them it would be a crack of black ice in a dead ridge.

The mountain rises under them. Over their long slowed lives it will lift them, imperceptibly, higher into the thinning air, and they will slow further, and the roar of their breathing will drop by degrees in pitch, and it will take longer and longer for the whistle in the roof to be answered.

---

Deeper in — you feel your way, the walls of fur pressing closer on both sides as the cleft narrows toward the back — the floor becomes wet in earnest. Condensation from the whole height of the chamber runs down the leaning stone behind the bodies and collects at the bottom in a shallow pool perhaps two strides across, black and utterly still except for the ripples when a drop hits it. The water is cold and tastes of iron and of something else. Around its rim the soft skin on the floor is thickest, and here, only here, something grows: pale filaments, fungal, threading up out of the muck in the total dark and in places forming small dense pads the size of a thumb, faintly luminous — not enough to see by, just enough that when you have been in the dark for long enough you become aware of a scatter of dim green points at your feet, like a reflection of stars in water that has no sky over it.

The animals nearest the pool are the ones that have come outermost in the rotation. They are the coolest, the most nearly awake. Sometimes one will unfold — this takes a long time, a wing loosening from the mass with a faint tearing sound where the fur has matted to its neighbors — and drop, and open, and glide down across the pool to the far side of the hollow and fold itself into the wall there, and the sound of the wingbeat in the wet air is enormous, a single soft concussion that echoes off the leaning slabs and stops the roar for half a second before it closes over again.

If you stayed long enough, you would see that the far side of the hollow is where they are going. Slowly, a body at a time, over years, the colony is migrating deeper into the cleft, following some gradient of warmth or damp or darkness that only they can feel, toward the place at the very back where the two walls close on each other and there is no more room. What they will do when they get there is not clear. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the cleft will have opened further by then. The ridge is young, and it has not finished breaking.

---

Back at the mouth, coming out, the wind-note takes on the quality of relief. The grey light hurts. The cushion plant is still there in its angle, the flakes are still in their line, and the ridge falls away on both sides into an enormity of thin air and snow-shadow, and the sound of the roar recedes behind you until, three strides along the crest, it is gone completely, cut off by the stone, and there is only the wind and the sense of something breathing in the mountain where you cannot hear it anymore.
