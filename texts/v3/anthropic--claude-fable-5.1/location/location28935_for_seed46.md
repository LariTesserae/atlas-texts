High on the dome, where the rock is still learning to be rock, there is a hollow the size of a small room.

You wouldn't call it a cave. Caves are old; something has to be taken away to make one. This is the other thing: a bubble that was in the lava when the lava stopped, a vesicle the size of a chapel that the mountain simply forgot to fill. The dome around it is months old at most. Its surface still has the skin lava gets when it cools too quickly — ropy, glassy, the color of a burnt pan — and nothing has scratched it yet. No frost has gotten into it. No rain has ever landed here, and none is expected; the air at this altitude is thin and mineral and smells of struck matches. The sky is a very pale, very ordinary blue.

The opening is a tear in the skin, low, and you go in on your knees.

Inside it is hot. Not the heat of sun on stone but the deeper kind, the heat of something that hasn't finished happening. It comes up through the floor and sits against your face like a hand. The floor itself is smooth, obsidian in some places and in others a dull sulfurous yellow, and you notice fairly quickly that the boundary between the two is not fixed. Where you looked a moment ago and saw black glass, there is now a yellow bloom the size of a plate, and it is not that it grew; it is that the rock has changed its mind about what it had been all along. The hollow is still deciding its own mineralogy. You can watch it do this if you have the patience, and patience is the only currency here.

The vegetation is what stops you.

There isn't much of it, that's the first thing — perhaps eleven individuals across the whole chamber, with wide dark distances between them. But each one is enormous for its space, luxuriant, overfull, the way a single fern in a bottle can seem like a jungle. They are not green. They are the colors of things that come out of a foundry: verdigris, rust-orange, the oily blue of tempered steel, a yellow so bright and dry it hurts. Their leaves are heavy and pleated and have the sheen of enamel. If you touch one — you will want to — it is warm and slightly gritty, and it does not give at all.

They have no water in them. Nothing here does. Whatever moves inside those stems is molten sulfur, or a low-melting alloy, or something that hasn't been given a name yet because it has never been anywhere a name could reach. They draw heat from the floor through roots that are less roots than solder, spread flat and silvery across the glass, and they use the difference between the floor's heat and the chamber's cooler air the way a turbine uses a river. They are engines. That's the plain fact of them. Each one is a small power station that happens to be shaped like a plant, and the shape is not a disguise — it is simply that the two words, at this altitude and this temperature and this early in the world's formation, turn out to mean the same thing.

Nothing built them. There is not a straight edge or a made thing anywhere in the hollow; no one has been here to leave one. But if you sit long enough you start to see the industry in them regardless. One near the back wall has a bulb at its center, faceted, that revolves — very slowly, perhaps a quarter-turn an hour — and it is a flywheel, storing the day's heat against the night's. Another has hollow stems arranged in a manifold, and something moves through them in a pulse you can feel more than hear. A third has fruited, if that's the word: dull grey ingots the size of a thumb, hanging in clusters, each one identical, each one heavy in the hand and warm and utterly featureless, the product of a process that has no one to sell to. When one drops it makes a small clear tick on the floor and then lies there. There are a few dozen of them scattered about. None are the same age. None have corroded.

The quiet is the quiet of a transformer room. There is a hum you cannot locate, low enough that you feel it in your teeth, and under it the occasional tick of cooling stone, and the rarer tick of a falling ingot, and that is nearly all. Your own breathing is the loudest thing here. There is no wind in the hollow. Outside, at this height, there is always wind, and you can hear it going over the skin of the dome like a hand over a drum, but it does not come in.

No animals. Not one. Not an insect, not a mite in the folds of the enamel leaves, nothing that eats or is eaten. Whatever relationship these plants have with their world is entirely with the rock and the heat and each other, and it has not occurred to any of it that there could be a third party.

And then the hollow starts to show you what it is, which is a place that hasn't finished being a place.

It happens at the edges first. You measure the chamber by eye — twelve paces across, say, four high at the center — and you walk it and it is twelve paces, and you walk back and it is fourteen. The far wall is not further away; there is simply more of it than there was. In the corner where the light from the opening does not quite reach, the floor and the wall have not yet agreed on which of them is which, and a leaf from the nearest plant lies flat against a surface that is in some sense both. A shadow falls from the flywheel plant in a direction the light does not come from, and the shadow is a little too sharp, as if it had been decided before the plant was, and the plant grew to fit it.

You count the leaves on the yellow one nearest you. Twenty-three. You look away and back. Twenty-three, but not the same twenty-three; one that was pleated is now smooth, one that was outermost is now inside, and you cannot say that anything moved. The plant is not being coy. It is that the plant, like the floor, like the dimensions of the room, is still a range of possibilities that has been narrowed to a very small range but not yet to one. Everything here is like a photograph still in the bath — the image is there, recognizably, and getting sharper by the hour, and it is only that the fixing has not been done. Some details will be lost. Some will be kept. The hollow does not seem anxious about which.

This is the slowness of it. Nothing here happens at a speed you could call an event. The rock decides its color across an afternoon. The flywheel turns its quarter-turn. The seam in the floor where black meets yellow moves a finger's width in a day, and in a month one of the plants will have finished committing to its exact number of leaves, and in a year — perhaps — the chamber will have settled its width. The sun comes through the tear in the skin and crosses the floor and leaves, and the plants swell almost imperceptibly in the heat and slacken almost imperceptibly in the dark, a tide with nothing wet in it. The ingots accumulate.

You understand, sitting there with the heat coming up through your palms, that you are early. Not early in the day but early in the sense a fossil is late — you have arrived before the place has finished being made, and you are watching the last stage of its manufacture, which is the stage where the world stops being many things and becomes one. The plants are already nearly certain. The floor is nearly certain. The walls are the least resolved, and the corner where nothing has yet been decided is where the hum is loudest.

When you leave, on your knees again, the tear in the dome's skin is a little narrower than it was, or you are a little larger; it comes to the same thing. Outside the wind is exactly where you left it. The dome goes on cooling under the pale sky, rope after rope of black glass, and somewhere beneath the surface there are, you now suspect, other bubbles — a hundred, a thousand, each with its own small factory of the unnamed, each unhurriedly working out what it is going to have been.
