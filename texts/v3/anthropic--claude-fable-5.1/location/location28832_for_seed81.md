**The Condensing City**

Start with the heat, because the heat is what you notice before your eyes adjust.

It comes up through the soles of your boots. The plateau is basalt, black and pocked and old, and somewhere not far beneath it something has been warm for a very long time. Not volcanic in the eruptive sense — nothing here has flowed in living memory, or in the memory of anything that lived. The rock has settled into its heat the way an old house settles into its foundations. It has stopped arguing with it. Put your hand flat on the ground and you feel what you'd feel on the lid of a kettle a few minutes after it's boiled: not painful, but not something you'd leave your hand on.

The air is thick. At this altitude it should be thin and dry and cold, and it is thin — you feel the shortness in your chest, the small extra effort of every breath — but it is neither dry nor cold. It is saturated. The mountain exhales. From ten thousand cracks and vents and the pores of the stone itself, water comes up as vapor, meets the high cool sky, and cannot decide what to be. So the whole plateau sits under a lid of its own breath: a ceiling of cloud perhaps two hundred meters up, grey-white, faintly luminous from a sun that is up there somewhere but never resolves into a disc. The light that reaches the ground is the light of a room with the curtains drawn. Noon looks like dusk. Dusk looks like the last minute before you'd normally switch on a lamp.

---

The city came for the water.

That is the only explanation that survives looking at it. Nothing grows here — nothing green, nothing with leaves, nothing that photosynthesizes, because the ground is too hot and the light is too weak and the water, though everywhere, is loaded with dissolved minerals that would crust any root shut in a season. But water at altitude is worth having, and here it comes for free, endlessly, out of the mountain's throat. Someone built the machinery to catch it.

The condensers are the first thing you see and the last thing you lose sight of. They stand at intervals across the plateau, kilometers apart, each one a tower of riveted iron perhaps sixty meters tall, flared at the top like a trumpet mouth turned to the sky. Their surfaces are ridged with cooling fins, thousands of them, and every fin is beaded, every bead swelling and running and dropping. The towers weep constantly. At the base of each one a catchment basin, then a channel, then the channels join into troughs, and the troughs into the great ceramic-lined aqueducts that run in straight lines across the black rock toward the plateau's edge and whatever lies beyond it. The aqueducts are the spine of the place. They are raised on arches — real Roman-looking arches, which is somehow more surprising than the iron — and they hum faintly, a sound you feel in the breastbone more than hear, from the water moving inside them.

Between the condensers there is the city proper, if "proper" is a word that applies. Blocks of buildings stand in clusters that are not quite streets. They are built low, of the same basalt as the ground, cut and fitted without mortar, and their roofs are copper gone to a soft matte green-black in the wet. Every roof is a gutter. Every wall has a channel at its foot. The architecture is entirely organized around not letting a single drop go anywhere it wasn't meant to. Doorways have thresholds like small dams. Windows have sills that slope inward, toward cisterns.

And the walls sweat. Warm stone, saturated air: the surfaces run with condensation as though the buildings were exerting themselves. In the dim light every vertical face gleams. The city looks like it has just been rained on, permanently, though you have never seen a drop fall from the sky and you suspect it does not.

---

Now the emptiness.

It is not ruin. That is important. Nothing is broken. The copper is not stripped, the iron is not rusted through, the aqueducts run full. The furnaces are lit — you can see the low orange of them through grated vents at the base of certain buildings, and it is the only warm color anywhere. The machinery works. Pumps cycle somewhere below the streets with a slow submarine thud, once every forty seconds or so, and after each thud a fresh sheet of steam breathes from a vent and rises to feed the ceiling.

But there is no one. Walk from one condenser to the next — an hour's walk, more in this air — and you will not pass a single figure, not a single lit window in the domestic sense, not a voice. The buildings are not boarded. Their doors are open or unlocked. Inside they are bare and clean and warm, with the dripping quality of a bathhouse after closing. Some contain rows of iron beds bolted to the floor with no bedding. Some contain long tables. Some contain nothing but a cistern, full to the brim, its surface trembling with the pulse of the pumps.

It's possible the city was never meant to hold people in the way cities do. The infrastructure so vastly exceeds the habitation that you begin to think the buildings were an afterthought — barracks for the crews who tended the condensers in shifts, and the crews were small, and the shifts were long, and at some point the machinery became reliable enough that the shifts stopped coming. Or it's possible they are simply elsewhere. The plateau is large; the cloud hides its edges; the aqueducts go somewhere. You could be in the wrong district. You could be in the wrong century.

What's certain is the scale of the space between things. You stand in a plaza cut from black rock, wet and shining, sixty meters across, and there is nothing in it. Not a cart, not a barrel, not a bench. The far side is fog. Above you the cloud-lid glows its featureless grey. Around you, at the very edge of hearing, the city runs itself.

---

Life, such as it is, keeps to the water.

In the catchment basins, where the condensate pools before it enters the channels, there are mats. They are the color of tarnished brass and they have the texture of wet suede, and they grow on the submerged iron in patient rings. Bacterial, or something simpler still — they need no light, only the warm mineral broth and the slow flow. They are the closest thing here to a plant, and they are nothing like one. Where they've had decades they've built up terraces, tiny stepped formations of precipitated stone, each terrace a lip over which the water spills in a film. Some of the basins have become, without anyone intending it, small ornamental gardens of orange and cream and rust-red stone, growing at the speed of drip.

And in the aqueducts there is something else.

You will not see it often. It lives inside the channels, in the dark, in the fast warm water, and it comes to the surface only where the channels open into settling tanks. It is long — a meter, sometimes two — and pale, almost translucent, so that the lamplight of the furnace-grates shows the dark thread of a gut running its length. Eel-shaped, but soft-bodied, with no visible eyes and a fringe along its underside that ripples continuously. It holds itself against the current without effort. When it surfaces it opens a mouth like a slit in wet cloth and takes a breath, audibly — a small wet gasp — and then submerges again. The mats, presumably. Or whatever lives in the mats. There is a chain here, short and strange and closed, and it runs entirely inside the plumbing.

Whether it was here before the aqueducts, or came with them, or arose in them, is a question the city does not answer.

Occasionally something moves against the cloud-lid overhead — a shape, darker grey on grey, gliding without wingbeats, perhaps riding the thermal updraft from a condenser mouth. It never descends. You never see it clearly. The plateau is too hot for anything with feathers to nest on, so it must roost somewhere at the cold edge of the cloud, and come here only to feed on the vapor-rich air, or on the pale things when they surface, or on nothing, for reasons of its own.

---

The strangeness is in the rhythm.

The city breathes, and after a while you realize it breathes in a pattern, and after a longer while you realize the pattern is not mechanical. The pumps thud, the vents exhale, the steam rises, the condensers weep, the water runs — and the interval between one cycle and the next is not fixed. It lengthens toward what would be evening. It shortens in the dim hours before what would be dawn. The whole plateau slows and quickens like something sleeping and waking, and the machinery follows, not leads.

You begin to suspect the mountain was not merely tapped. It was *fitted*. The condensers sit where the vents were, the aqueducts follow the old channels, the furnaces are dug into chambers that were already warm. Whoever built this did not impose a city on the rock; they read the rock and gave it iron edges and copper gutters and let it keep doing what it was already doing. The city is the mountain wearing clothes.

And it seems, by any measure that would satisfy an engineer, to be running at a small but steady surplus. The aqueducts are full to their brims. In places they have begun to overflow — a thin sheet of warm water spilling over the ceramic lip and running down the arch and across the plaza and into a channel that leads it right back to the basin it came from. The city is drinking its own tears, over and over. Whatever the water was being sent *to*, at the far end of the aqueducts, either no longer needs it or has stopped taking delivery, and no one has come to close the valves.

So the surplus accumulates. It accumulates as cloud, which thickens; as mineral, which builds its little terraces; as heat, which the stone holds and holds. Slow processes. A city that has become a landscape and a landscape that has become a very slow, very patient event. Established. In its own state. Not waiting for anyone in particular.

---

Leave by the aqueduct, because it's the only line in the fog you can trust.

Walk alongside it toward the plateau's rim. The condensers thin out, then stop. The buildings stop. The black rock goes on, wet and warm and empty, until the ground begins to fall away in front of you and the cloud, having nowhere left to sit, tears open at the edge.

For a moment, cold air. Real light — hard, high-altitude light, painful after the dimness. Below, a long way below, the mountain's flank drops through belts of grey into something that might be a valley, might be another cloud. The aqueduct goes over the edge on its arches and descends, and you can see it running for a kilometer, two, before it enters the mist and is gone.

Behind you, the lid closes again. The heat comes back up through your boots. Somewhere back in the fog, the pumps thud once, the vents exhale, the city takes a breath, and the towers go on weeping into their basins, patiently, for no one.
