The saddle between the two peaks was not there last year. The old maps show a gap — a notch of air where the ridge sagged and wind poured through — and now there is a shelf, floor-flat, about the length of a large church, and the wind has to go around.

You come up onto it from the north, over scree that behaves the way scree should: loose, sharp, frost-split, grey with lichen, full of the long slow story of a mountain being taken apart. Then the scree stops. There is a line, clean as a tidemark, and beyond it the rock is dark, smooth, warm to the touch, and wet, and it has no story at all. It was made this week.

---

The shelf is bounded on both sides by the old peaks, which lean over it like two people watching something being poured. Their faces are cracked and pale. The new stone between them is almost black, with a faint blue-green sheen where the mist beads on it, and it lies in broad shallow terraces, each terrace a few centimeters higher than the last, stepping up toward the middle of the saddle. The steps are still being laid. If you kneel at the edge of the highest terrace and watch the lip of it, you can see it thicken. It does not flow. It arrives. Water films the rock, the film goes cloudy, the cloudiness settles into stone, and a new film slides over it.

The whole shelf hums. It is not a subtle sound. It comes up through your boots and sits in your back teeth, a low chord with several notes that don't quite agree, and every few seconds a layer somewhere finishes setting and the sound cracks — a sharp report, like a limb snapping in cold, though nothing here is cold. The cracks come from different places. You cannot predict them. Over an hour the pattern starts to feel like breathing, and then stops feeling like anything you can name.

---

What is doing this is not visible in the way a machine is visible. There are objects on the shelf, and they are clearly placed, clearly made, but they do not look like they are working. A spindle of dull metal, waist-high, standing alone on the second terrace with its base already an inch deep in new rock. A ring of something like glass, laid flat, half-swallowed. Three slender posts in a rough line near the southern peak, each topped with a lens that shows nothing when you look into it — not your reflection, not the sky, a flat absence, like looking at the back of your own eye. They are cool. They do not hum on their own. They seem more like markers than engines, the pegs that hold a tent down rather than the tent.

The best guess anyone has offered is that the shelf is a well. The air up here is wet — the process makes it wet, pulling moisture toward the saddle from both valleys until the whole gap sits under its own permanent cloud, a grey lid that keeps the light dim and directionless even at noon — and the water carries whatever the water carries. Silica, lime, iron, the dissolved shavings of the mountain. Something in the placed objects, or in the space between them, sorts it out. The water gives up its load and the load becomes stone, and the giving-up releases heat. That is why the shelf is warm. That is why the mist never clears. That is, perhaps, why it hums: the sound of a great deal of material being asked to change its mind about what it is, all at once, over and over.

Nobody has offered a guess about why.

---

The warmth is why the animals are here.

Above the tree line, on the old rock, there is almost nothing — a pipit, a hare, the wind. On the shelf there is more life per square meter than anywhere for fifty kilometers in any direction. Newts the color of wet rust lie in the thin sheets of water on the lower terraces, hundreds of them, so still that you take them for stains until one moves. Small dark birds, some sort of chough, have found that the terrace lips hold heat through the night, and they roost along them in rows, and at dawn — a grey brightening under the cloud, no more — they all lift at once and the noise of it is added to the noise of the shelf, and for a moment the hum is gone under wings. Flies in slow columns over the pools. A fox has been seen at the edge, on the old scree, not crossing the line, watching. Snails on the wet posts. A single heron, absurd at this altitude, standing on the glass ring with its feet in a finger's depth of water, patient as furniture, taking newts.

The vegetation has not caught up. It cannot; there is no soil, and the ground it would root in is younger than the seeds. The older, lower terraces near the northern edge have a grey-orange crust of pioneer lichen, and in two hollows something like moss has taken hold and is already partly buried, its top few millimeters still green, its base sealed in stone. A single tuft of grass, blown up from somewhere, has seeded in a crack that is filling in beneath it. It will be gone by autumn — not dead, entombed. The place is not hostile to plants. It is simply faster than they are.

---

This is the part that keeps people coming up the scree.

The stone sets around whatever is on it. The newts leave newt shapes. The choughs' feet leave forked prints on the terrace lips by the thousand, each night's roost pressed into a fresh layer, so that the rising steps of the shelf are stacked with bird tracks the way a ledger is stacked with entries. There are feathers in the rock. There are the whole bodies of insects, and a fish — a fish, with no water within a day's walk deep enough to hold it, dropped presumably by the heron, now a perfect grey relief on the fourth terrace with its mouth open. Near the spindle there is a hare, or the upper half of one, cast in the stone at a run. Whether it died there or was simply too slow to notice the ground rising under it, no one knows. The rock has no opinion.

It is a fossil bed in which nothing is old. Erosion has not touched it; the wind that would sand these prints down over ten thousand years has not had ten hours. Every mark is as sharp as the moment it was made. You could come back next season and the layer you stood on would be a meter down, and the layer you stand on then would have your boot-prints in it from today, sealed and lightless, and the layer above would have a heron's.

People have started leaving things. A coin. A ring, deliberately, on the terrace where it would be taken quickest. A folded paper, which the water reached before the stone did and which set as a smear. There are marks scratched into the fresh rock that the next layer covers — a name, a date, the outline of a hand — and the scratching is easy, because for the first hour the new stone is soft as chalk. After that it is stone.

---

Toward evening the cloud thickens and the light drops to a dusk that has no sunset in it, only less. The hum changes register — lower, or perhaps just louder against the settling of the birds. Water runs off the shelf in every direction, down the scree, dark and silty, carrying with it the mountain's leftovers and the smell of warm minerals, which is something like a cave and something like a struck match. The lenses on their posts are the last things to hold any light, though where it comes from is not clear. The heron leaves. The newts do not.

In the night the cracks come more often. If you stay, you hear the shelf climbing.

It has not finished. It does not seem to be building toward anything — no shape is emerging, no wall or vessel, just more shelf, wider, higher, the terraces marching up toward the height of the two peaks as though the mountain had decided to heal a wound it did not have. Whether it will stop when the saddle is level, or go on past it, or whether the objects on it are the whole of the apparatus or only the part that happened to fall here — none of this is known. What is known is that the ground is new every morning, warm, wet, printed with the feet of everything that slept on it, and that the hare in the fourth terrace is still running, and that the hum does not stop.
