**The Notch**

Start with the air, because there is more of it than anything else.

The place is a gap in a summit ridge — a saddle worn between two horns of dark rock, perhaps forty paces across and sixty long, floored with something that is not quite soil and not quite peat. On either side the horns go up another hundred meters, and to the north and south the floor of the gap simply ends. There is no slope. There is a rim of wet grass, and then there is nothing, and the nothing goes down for a distance the eye refuses to measure. On clear mornings you can see the tops of cumulus far below, spread out like a second ground. Most of the time you can't see them, because the cloud is here, inside the gap with you.

That is the first thing to understand about the Notch: it is a room, but the room is mostly empty, and the walls are made of weather.

---

**The floor**

Underfoot, the ground gives. Centuries of moss have laid down a mattress over the bedrock — sphagnum in the low center where water gathers, and on the higher margins a stiff carpet of cushion plants, dense green hemispheres the size of ottomans, so tightly packed that a hand pressed on them barely dents the surface. Between the cushions the rock shows through in ribs of dark slate, tilted on edge, weathered into a soft grey nap. Everything is beaded. Water does not fall here so much as *arrive*: the cloud passes through and leaves itself on every surface, and the surfaces weep. Lichen the color of old copper streams down the horns in long vertical stains where the seepage has run for so many centuries that the rock is grooved to hold it.

The vegetation is stranger than it should be at this altitude, and the reason is the cloud. Below, on the exposed shoulders of the mountain, there is only frost-shattered scree. Here in the sheltered gap, wrapped in permanent wet air, cooled but never quite frozen, things grow that have no business at this height: giant rosette plants with leaves like felt, their dead lower foliage hanging around the stems in shaggy brown skirts; tree-heathers gone bonsai in the wind, trunks as thick as a thigh and no taller than a person; a tussock grass whose blades are so drenched they clatter faintly when the wind bends them. Everything is trimmed at roughly the same height, the height of the wind's hand.

It is cool. Not cold — the cloud is a blanket as much as a bath — but cool enough that your breath shows, and cool enough that nothing rots quickly. A fallen heather trunk in the sphagnum has been lying there so long it has become part of the geography, moss-covered, with a rosette plant rooted in the bend of it.

---

**The sound**

You will not hear the Notch as quiet, ever, though there is almost nothing solid in it.

The wind comes through the gap because it has nowhere else to go — the ridge is a wall and this is the only door, and the whole atmosphere of one side of the mountain is trying to reach the other. It does not howl. It is lower than that, a standing roar with a body to it, a sound you feel in your sternum, and it has a pitch. The eastern horn has a fluting in its face, a vertical slot the width of two hands, and when the wind is strong enough the slot speaks — a long, unwavering tone somewhere below a human bass, coming from the rock itself. It stops when the wind slackens, and the silence when it stops is briefly appalling.

Underneath the wind, the water: a constant fine ticking of drops from a thousand leaf-tips, and at the lowest point of the saddle a small rill that gathers and spills over the southern rim, thin as a rope, and is torn into mist before it has fallen ten meters.

And over all of that — this is what makes the place *loud* — the birds.

---

**The birds**

The Notch is a door for the wind and therefore a door for everything that rides the wind. Swifts use it the way a river uses a gorge. They come through in pulses, low and fast, dozens at a time, so close overhead that you hear the tear of their wings before the shrieking reaches you, and then they are gone over the far rim and out into the void and the cloud takes them. A minute later another pulse. They never seem to come back through. The traffic is one way, east to west, all day, thousands of them, as though the sky on the eastern side were slowly emptying itself into the sky on the west.

Choughs live on the horns. They are the loud ones — black, red-legged, with a call like a hinge — and they own the gap the way gulls own a harbor. They tumble off the ledges and let the updraft catch them and hang there, twenty of them at once, riding the air a meter above the rim with their wings barely moving, screaming at each other. Their whitewash streaks every ledge on the western horn. Their nests are wedged in the seepage cracks, and the young call from inside the rock like something buried.

Something larger hunts the choughs. It is rarely seen; usually only the alarm is seen — the whole flock lifting at once and pouring away downwind, and a shape passing through the cloud too fast to name. Then a scatter of black feathers on the moss, and the flock reassembles within the hour, no quieter than before.

Lower down, among the cushions, there are things with fur — a colony of rock-dwelling rodents, stout and short-eared, that whistle when the shadow of a wing crosses them and vanish into the slate. The whistles are constant. Every twenty or thirty seconds, from somewhere, a whistle and a scuffle.

In the wet hollows, when the cloud is thick and the light is low, a small frog calls. Only one, it seems, or one at a time. Its voice is a single dry click repeated at long intervals, and it is impossible to locate.

---

**The made things**

People have been here. Not many, not often, and not for a long time, but the marks are unmistakable once you see them.

A ring of flat slates set on edge in the moss, about three paces across, near the western horn. Fire-blackened stones in the middle, long cold. The ring is old enough that the cushion plants have grown over the outer edge of several stones and sealed them in.

Against the base of the eastern horn, where an overhang keeps off the worst of the drip, a shelter: a low wall of stacked slate, dry-laid, no mortar, leaning into the rock to make a space a person could lie in but not sit up. The roof is a single flake of stone that must have taken several people to lift. Inside, in a niche, a bundle of dry tussock grass tied with a twist of hide — bedding, or tinder, or an offering. It is not damp. This is the only dry thing in the Notch.

Steps. On the southern rim, where the drop begins, someone has cut notches into a rib of slate — eight of them, descending toward the edge and then simply stopping at a ledge the width of a foot. Below the ledge, cloud. What the steps lead to is not visible. Perhaps it was once.

And a post. A single trunk of tree-heather, stripped of its bark and grey with age, set upright in a cairn of stones at the highest point of the saddle's floor. Chough feathers have been jammed into cracks in its wood, some old and bleached, some still glossy. Whether the birds put them there or someone else did is not a question the place is interested in answering. The post leans slightly west, the way everything here leans.

---

**The cycle**

Nothing about the Notch holds still, and yet nothing about it seems to change. This is the paradox of established places: enormous motion, no drift.

The day has weather in hours. At dawn, if the eastern sky is clear, sunlight comes level through the gap and lights the cloud from inside so that the whole room glows amber and the drops on every leaf catch fire. Within the hour the sun heats the eastern face, the air starts to rise, and cloud boils up out of the void below the southern rim and fills the gap like water filling a bowl. By mid-morning you cannot see the far horn. The birds go on flying through it regardless.

Around midday the wind often shifts and strengthens, and the cloud tears — great rents open and close in seconds, and through them you see the sky's blue, or the second cloud-floor far below, or once in a while the summit of the neighboring mountain, impossibly distant and apparently level with you. The rock sings. The choughs shout. The swifts come in pulses.

In the afternoon it may rain properly for a while, hard, loud on the cushion plants, the rill over the southern rim doubling and then trebling. Then it stops, and the cloud thins to a white brightness, and everything drips, and steam lifts off the slate.

Evening brings the cloud back down, thick and cold and still, and the wind drops, and the horn stops singing, and for a while the only sounds are the drip, the rodents whistling less often, the frog clicking, and the last few swifts passing overhead unseen, their cries arriving from a direction that has no shape.

Then night, which is total. Inside the cloud, in the dark, the Notch is a room without walls or floor or ceiling, in which you can hear water moving on every side.

And then the level light again, and the room glows, and the whole thing starts over — as it has, going by the depth of the moss and the grooves in the rock and the cushion plants sealing the old fire-ring, for a very long time.

---

The strangeness of the place, if it has one, is only this: that so much should pass through it, and so little stay. The wind, the water, the birds, the cloud, the people who cut the steps — all of them are on their way to somewhere else. What remains is a gap. A door propped open at the top of the world, roaring with everything going through it, and behind the roar, an emptiness that has been here so long it has grown a garden.
