**The Hollow**

From the plateau it reads as a second range risen behind the first: a long pale swell against the stars, smooth where the true mountains are serrated, and wrong in a way that takes a while to place. It has no snowline. Nothing has collected on it. Its flanks are the grey of old bone and they go up without a single ledge, without a scree fan at the base, and where they meet the ground there is no talus, no debris apron, nothing shed. It has not been standing long enough to shed anything. Climbers who touch it say the surface is faintly warm and faintly damp and that it hums, and that if you lay your ear against it you can hear something like a distant crowd.

It is a plant. One plant, or one clone of a plant, that began pushing out of the high basin some three or four centuries ago and did not stop. Nobody watched it start. The oldest accounts describe a low dome the color of turnip flesh, and then a decade of silence, and then a wall.

The interior is the thing.

---

You enter through a split. The wall has been growing faster than its own skin can stretch, and along the seams it tears, and the tears are fresh — the edges of the rupture are crisp, laminar, showing the layered grain of the tissue like the pages of a wet book, with none of the rounding that weather gives to any opening it has had time to work on. Some splits are a hand wide. A few are the height of a house. Water beads on the lip of every one of them and falls in a thin curtain, because the air inside is wetter than the air outside, and it condenses the moment it meets the cold.

Then you are through, and the space opens, and it keeps opening.

The floor is the plant. The walls are the plant. The roof, somewhere above, is the plant. But the volume between them is almost nothing — air, dark, and distance. The wall you came through falls away behind you into black; the floor runs out ahead into black; the roof is not a roof but an absence of stars. In daytime, a few of the high splits let down a light like the light under a heavy door, blades of grey that reach the floor as vague brightenings and show you, for a moment, how far away the far side is. It is very far. The Hollow is measured in days. There are people who have walked its floor for a week without touching a wall.

Because there is no stone here. This is the first rule and the one that takes longest to believe. The floor underfoot has the give of hard cork and the sheen of something oiled. It rises and falls in long ridges — growth ridges, each one a year or a season — and between the ridges are troughs where water stands, and in the troughs the plant has grown a different tissue, softer, mottled, that yields under the heel and springs back. There is no sand, no gravel, no soil. Anything that dies here lies on the floor until something eats it or until the floor, growing, folds over it. You can find the shapes of animals two ridges deep, sealed under a skin of translucent grey.

---

It is cold. The basin the plant grew from is already high and the floor of the Hollow lies higher still; the plant lifted its own ground as it rose. Breath shows, if you can see it. The cold has a particular flavor in here — damp, mineral-tasting though there is no mineral, like the inside of a cistern. It gets into fingers. The people who come here wrap their hands in strips of the plant's own inner bark, which holds warmth and never quite dries.

The dampness is not the dampness of a wet place. It is the dampness of a place that is breathing. The walls exhale. Along the lower flanks the tissue is porous, faintly spongy, and warm air moves out of it in a slow steady draft you can feel against the face. The air carries water. It rises. Far up, at the vault, the tissue is dense and cold, and the rising air cools against it and the water lets go. Sometimes as fog. Sometimes as a fine drizzle that falls for hours over one part of the floor while the rest stays dry. And sometimes — when the outside weather is wrong, when a warm front stalls against the eastern flank and the walls all breathe at once — it gathers into rain. Real rain, straight down through half a kilometer of dark, so loud on the floor you cannot hear the person beside you. The Hollow has its own weather because it is large enough to have it. It takes a day for a weather to cross.

Where the rain collects, in the troughs, there are pools. Some of them are the size of a room and a few of them are lakes, black and still, and things live in them. Where the pools overflow, water runs along the ridge-lines in channels the plant has grown to carry it — smooth-bottomed, a little warmer than the air, and always in motion, and always moving toward the low places, where it drains through the floor and is not seen again. The plant is drinking its own rain.

---

The sound is the second rule.

It never stops. Underneath everything there is a tone — a low, wide, chest-felt hum that comes from everywhere and nowhere, that you stop hearing after a day and then hear again with a shock when it changes pitch. It is the wind. Outside, the wind off the range crosses the splits in the wall, and the whole Hollow is a resonant body, and it sounds the way a bottle sounds when you blow across it — except the bottle is the size of a country and the note is at the bottom edge of hearing, and when the wind veers, the note shifts, and the shift moves through the floor and into the bones.

Over the hum: dripping. Constant, at every scale, from a single bead ticking somewhere close to the roar of a whole seam letting go at once far off. Over the dripping: the plant itself. It grows fast enough to be heard. Along the walls it creaks and pops — sharp, woody reports as the skin tears another finger's width, and long groans as some deep layer takes strain. Sometimes there is a sound like a tree falling that goes on for a minute and then stops, and nothing has fallen, and the wall is simply a little larger.

And over all of that, the animals.

---

They came in through the splits, and they stayed, and now they are of the place. In the dark you mostly hear them. There are grazers — long-legged, heavy-bodied things descended from something that once ate lichen on the plateau — that move in herds across the floor and strip the soft tissue from the troughs, and their feet on the corky ground make a hollow, wooden drumming that you hear an hour before you hear their breathing. They have almost no eyes. They call to each other in a deep repeating cough that carries a long way, and when a herd is close the calls come from every side and you cannot tell how many. Hundreds. Sometimes many hundreds.

There are things that fly. They live in the seams of the roof and come down in the wet hours and pass over the floor in loose flocks, clicking; the clicks are dense and dry and very fast, and when a flock is overhead the sound is like gravel poured onto a table. They take the small things that swarm in the pools. Occasionally one falls and you find it — pale, soft-furred, lighter than it should be, with a face that is mostly mouth and ears.

And there is something that hunts the grazers. Nobody who goes into the Hollow has seen it clearly. It is heard: a herd's coughing changes to a scream and the drumming scatters and there is a single long low call that is not the wind, and then, a while afterward, a wet tearing. In the morning — if there is a morning, if a split is near enough to give one — there is a place on the floor that has been fed on, and the tissue around it has already begun to close.

The floor eats what the predators leave. That is the third rule. Nothing is left on the ground for long.

---

People come. Not many. There are perhaps a hundred at a time, in the good season, and they come for the sap — the plant weeps a thick clear syrup from any cut, and the syrup is sweet and does not spoil and burns hot when it is old — and for the fiber, and for the inner bark, and some of them, it seems, for the sound. They have been coming for a few generations. Long enough to have marked the place, not long enough to have changed it.

Their marks are small. Along the walls near the larger splits there are ladders of notches cut into the tissue, climbing to the tapping-heights, and where a notch is old it has begun to heal over from the edges and the ladder has to be recut, so the walls near the entrances are patterned with generations of stairs, each a little more closed than the last. There are tap-holes with plugs of bark hammered in. There are shelters: low, single-roomed, built of plates of the shed outer skin propped and lashed with fiber cord, standing alone on the floor at a day's walk from anything, and most of them empty. A few have been there long enough that the floor has begun to grow up around their footings and they lean.

There is no metal. There is no fire that anyone trusts; the tissue is too wet to burn and the sap burns too well, and the people who work here have learned to be cold rather than to light anything inside the walls. Tools are made from the plant's hardest layer — a dense, dark stratum found where the floor has been growing for a long time — split into blades and shaped by grinding against itself. They are good enough. They cut bark. They will not cut the floor.

And there are the wayfinders. On the open floor, where there is nothing to steer by, people have set up posts — lengths of cut stalk driven into a trough, wrapped in pale bark so that a faint light from a high split will catch them — and strung between the posts, sometimes, a line of cord, so that a person moving in the dark can hold the cord and walk. Most of the lines are broken. The herds go through them. Some of the posts have been lifted by the floor's growth and stand at angles, higher than they were set, their bases sheathed in new grey tissue, so that following a line of them you can read how much the place has risen since someone last walked it. In a few decades of markers you can see the world get taller.

---

It is young. That is what the whole place says, if you stay in it long enough to stop being afraid of the dark and the noise. Nothing has been rounded. Nothing has settled. The splits in the wall are as sharp as the day they tore; the ridges on the floor stand at their full height; the water runs in channels the plant made for it last year and will remake next year. There are no ruins here, because nothing has been here long enough to become one — only shelters standing or leaning, only ladders half-healed. The animals are still the animals of the plateau, pale and altered but not yet strange. The whole ecosystem is a few centuries old and everything in it is still finding out what it is.

And it is still rising. Stand still on the floor at night — at what would be night — with the hum in your chest and the drip on every side and a herd somewhere drumming toward you or away, and you can feel the hollowness of the place, the sheer volume of nothing that it encloses, and under that, slower than any of it, the sense that the floor is not a floor but a lid, and that beneath it the thing that grew all this is still growing, and has not decided how large it means to be.
