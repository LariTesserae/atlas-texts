There is a shelf on the mountain where the rock is warm.

Not warm the way stone is warm after a day of sun — this is warm from beneath, a steady body-heat that does not change between noon and the hour before dawn. You notice it first through the soles of your feet, then through your palms if you kneel, and then you notice that you have stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing your own temperature. The shelf is perhaps sixty paces long and thirty deep. Behind it the mountain goes up in a wall of grey-green rock. In front of it the mountain goes down into cloud. There is no way to arrive except from above, and no way to leave except back the way you came, and the cloud below is so constant that the shelf has the quality of a room with one wall missing, opening onto nothing.

The air coming up from the valley is cool. The stone is not. Where they meet, the air gives up its water. So the shelf sweats. Every surface holds a skin of moisture that is always forming and always sliding off, and the rock has been polished by this for so long that it has no edges anymore, only curves that pass into other curves. Water gathers in the low places and finds its way in threads to the lip and goes over into the cloud without a sound. In the mornings, when the valley air is coolest, the shelf makes its own mist — a knee-high layer that stands and thickens and then, as the day comes on, simply thins into nothing, having gone nowhere. This is the only weather here. It has been the only weather here for a very long time.

You expect, in a place this warm and this wet, to be crowded by growth. You are not. The shelf is mostly bare stone — wide, gleaming, wet stone — and the plants stand in it the way furniture stands in a large room that has been mostly emptied. There are five or six of them, if you count generously. You could name each one. You could walk around each one and see it from every side and see the bare wet stone behind it.

The largest is a tree that is not exactly a tree. It rises from a fissure near the back wall, a single grey trunk as thick as two people, and at the height of a house it stops rising and instead spreads, flat, into a canopy like an open hand held palm-down over a candle. The leaves are enormous and leathery and hang in overlapping plates, and their undersides are white with condensation, and from their tips water drips in a slow ring onto the stone below, where it has worn — over what must be centuries — a shallow circular groove, exact as a compass-line. Nothing grows inside the ring. Nothing grows for several paces outside it. The tree has cleared a space around itself the way a cathedral clears a square.

Further out along the shelf there is a stand of tree-ferns, three of them, leaning together though not touching, their fronds so heavy with water that they hang nearly vertical. Their trunks are fibrous and black and give slightly when pressed. A single cushion plant, dense and dome-shaped and the intense green of something that has never known drought, has established itself on a knob of rock near the lip, where the mist is thickest; it is the size of a sleeping body and warm to the touch, warmer than the stone, and you cannot tell if this is because of the stone or because of something the plant is doing. There is a fringe of moss along one seam in the back wall where the seep is heaviest. There is a low tangle of something with red stems and no leaves, only stems, forking and reforking, that spreads across a corner of the shelf like a diagram.

That is all.

The rest is the stone, and the stone is beautiful in the way of things that have been finished. It shows no strata. It has weathered into rounded basins and saddles and long slow ridges no higher than an ankle, and the wet makes it look like it is moving even though nothing here moves faster than a season. The surface is not uniform: in the low places it is dark and glassy, in the high places it is faintly bloomed with mineral — thin white rings where water has stood and dried and stood and dried, the same rings in the same places, the shelf drawing its own contour map of itself over and over. In one basin the rings have built up into a lip, a delicate raised crust the thickness of a fingernail, which the water now has to overtop to leave. Given enough time the basin will become a bowl, and then a pool, and then the wall of it will fail and the water will find a new low place and begin again. That is the fastest thing that happens here.

It takes a while to understand what is wrong with the place, and then you understand it all at once.

Nothing is eating anything.

There are no insects. There is no whine near the ear, no small bodies on the wet leaves, nothing walking the stone. There are no birds — you would have heard a bird a hundred times by now in any other lush warm place at this altitude. No lizard on the warm rock, which is the strangest absence of all, because the rock is so obviously a place for a lizard to be. No spider-silk between the tree-fern fronds. When you look closely at the great leathery leaves of the tree, they are whole. Not one has been chewed. Not one has a hole in it, a scar, a gall, a fungal spot, anything. They are only old — you can tell which are old because the white condensation-bloom on their undersides has become permanent, a chalk that does not wipe off — and they age and they fall and that is the whole of what happens to them.

And they do fall, and then they lie there. Under the tree, outside the drip-ring, there are leaves on the stone that must have fallen years ago. They are intact. They have gone grey and then paler grey and have begun, very slowly, to soften into a kind of felt, and the water moving over them has carried some of them a hand's width downslope, but they have not rotted in the way leaves rot. There is nothing here to rot them. There is only water and time, and the water is patient and time is enormous, and between them they take a fallen frond apart at roughly the rate that the rock takes itself apart, which is to say you will not see it happen.

The plants have arranged their lives around this. Nothing here flowers — or nothing that you would call a flower. The cushion plant produces, at its crown, small hard grey nodules that swell over years and then split and release something like dust, which the mist carries an arm's length and sets down. The tree does not seem to make seed at all. It makes itself again: where a leaf-tip drips long enough on a fissure, and the fissure is right, a root comes down from above, a grey cord feeling its way to the stone, and when it lands it thickens. There are two of these cords hanging from the canopy now, one of which has not yet reached the ground and sways, very slightly, in the up-draught. The other landed a long time ago and is becoming a second trunk. In this way the tree may eventually walk the whole shelf, one trunk at a time, over a span of time that has no human name. The ferns lean because they are leaning toward the places where they will eventually root from their own bowed tips. The red-stemmed thing does not seem to reproduce at all. It is simply larger than it was.

The silence is total in a way that silence in the living world almost never is. There is the drip from the tree, which is regular and soft and which you stop hearing. There is, when you put your ear near the moss, a sound at the edge of hearing that might be water moving inside the rock or might be your own blood. That is all. The cloud below absorbs everything — no wind-sound comes up out of it, no river, nothing. You can hear your own joints when you stand. You are, on this shelf, by an overwhelming margin, the loudest thing that has ever been here.

One more thing, and it is the thing that will follow you back up the mountain.

Near the base of the back wall, where a seam of darker stone runs through the grey, the rock breaks differently. It comes away in flat plates, each one edged, some of them edged finely enough to cut. They lie in a small talus below the seam, a few dozen of them, dark and wet and glinting. Most are irregular. One is not. One is the length of a hand and has a rounded back that fits the palm and an edge along the other side that is not quite straight but is straighter than water and gravity would make it, with a pattern of small parallel facets along it, each facet the shape a blow would leave. It is heavy. The edge is keen. There is no way to tell how long it has lain there, because nothing here leaves marks of duration — no lichen on its upper face, because there is no lichen; no rust, because there is no iron; no stain, because the water is clean and takes nothing with it and leaves only the same faint white rings it leaves on everything.

It may be nothing. Stone shears along its own lines and sometimes the lines are regular. The seam is the kind that would make tools whether or not anyone ever came to take them, and it has all the time in the world.

But you will pick it up. Everyone who finds this shelf picks it up, and turns it over in the warm wet air, and looks along the edge, and then looks around the empty room of stone — the great flat-handed tree, the leaning ferns, the green dome breathing warmth at the edge of the cloud — and listens, for a while, to nothing.

And then most people put it back exactly where it was.
