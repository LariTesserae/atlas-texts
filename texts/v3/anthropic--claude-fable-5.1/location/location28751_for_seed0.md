Start with the noise, because the noise arrives before anything else does, before the eyes have adjusted, before the feet have found where the ground is.

It comes up out of the valley like heat comes up off a road. Not a sound but a pressure. Frogs, mostly — you learn that later — but at first it is simply a wall, a solid thing with a grain to it, and standing at the lip of the basin you can feel it in the bone behind your ear and in the wet skin of your palms. Under the frogs there is a lower layer that turns out to be insects, and over the frogs a higher one that turns out to be bats, and threaded through all of it a percussion that will take you an hour to identify as the sound of things falling: fruit, branches, bodies, water. The valley is loud the way a waterfall is loud. It is not producing sound. It is made of it.

And it is dark. Not cave-dark, but close. There is no moon, or there is a moon somewhere above a lid of cloud that never quite lifts off this place, and whatever light comes down through it is eaten twice — once by the cloud and once by a canopy so thick that it is less a roof than a second ground suspended overhead. What is left is the color of a bruise. You see shapes. You see that something is moving and that something else is moving behind it. Edges you have to take on faith.

---

The ground is young. That is the first thing your hands tell you, before your eyes can. The rock is black and it is sharp — not weathered-sharp, the accidental sharpness of a split cobble, but sharp the way glass is sharp, the way a thing is sharp when nothing has ever rubbed against it. This is lava that cooled within living memory, or within the memory of the oldest trees, which is not very long here. The skin of it still has the ropy folds of the last motion it made. Where it broke it broke into blades. Rain has not had time to soften anything. There are no rounded stones, no gravel, no soil in the ordinary sense, only a black crust with cracks in it, and in the cracks a dark red-brown paste of rotted leaf and ash and frog.

Here and there the crust has failed. The lava ran in tubes, and the roofs of the tubes have given way in places, and so the valley floor is pocked with collapse pits — some the size of a room, some the size of a ballfield — with vertical walls and rubble floors twenty or forty feet down. Water gathers in them. Not much. There is no river here, no stream you could name; the rock is too new to hold a channel and the rain runs straight down through the cracks and is gone. But the pits catch it. Each one holds a black pond at the bottom, warm, still, skinned with fallen petals, and each pond is a separate loud country of its own with its own frogs, its own pitch. Walk the valley at night and you cross from one chorus into another like crossing between rooms in which different radios are playing.

The air is wet without ever quite raining. Condensation stands on every surface. The rock sweats. The leaves drip continuously, not from weather but from themselves, and a fine warm mist hangs at knee height that you can wade through and that closes behind you. Occasionally the cloud lid does let go and there is a burst of rain, hard and brief and warm as bathwater, and for its duration the noise doubles because everything in the valley responds to it at once, and then it stops and the noise falls back to its ordinary roar.

---

The vegetation is a riot of first-comers. Nothing here is old. Nothing here has had time to be old. The forest is what arrives when a place has been scraped back to bare mineral and the seeds and spores come in on the wind and find warmth and water and no competition, and everything that lands grows as fast as it can before something else does. So there are tree ferns with trunks like shaggy pillars, thirty and forty feet tall, their fronds meeting overhead and making that second ground. There are vines that have gone up the tree ferns and out along the fronds and down again in loops. There are things with leaves the size of doors, waxy, held out flat to catch whatever light the cloud lets through. There is moss on the moss. In the cracks of the lava, where the red paste is, whole gardens have come up — heliconia, ginger, things with flowers the size of a face that open in the dark and smell like rot and are covered in beetles.

It is dense in the literal sense: there is very little air. You cannot take a step without pushing something aside, and what you push aside is usually alive in two or three ways — the frond, the vine on the frond, the frog on the vine, the mite on the frog. Every surface is occupied. Every occupied surface is being contested. You put your hand out to steady yourself and the bark under it moves, because it is not bark but a sheet of insects, and they pour off around your fingers and reassemble below.

---

Which brings you to the animals, because the animals are the valley, in the same way the noise is.

Frogs, first, since they make most of the sound. Hundreds of kinds, or dozens of kinds in hundreds of thousands. Thumbnail frogs in the leaf axils. Frogs the size of a fist sitting on the sharp lava with their throats blown out white. Frogs stacked on frogs at the pond edges in the collapse pits, in mounds, in seething slow-motion heaps, breeding. You cannot walk without stepping on them. They do not move for you. They are too many to move, and too loud, and too busy.

Then the insects, which are not so much a population as a weather. Moths come through in waves, in dense drifts, drawn to nothing since there is nothing here to be drawn to, just moving. Beetles in the flowers, beetles in the paste, beetles falling from the canopy in a steady patter you feel on your shoulders and hair. Something like a cicada, or occupying that place — it is hard to see — screaming from every trunk at a frequency that makes your fillings ache. Ants in rivers across the lava, lit faintly from within, or so it seems in the dark, a bluish line that turns out to be a fungus they are carrying.

And the bats overhead, which you never see and never stop hearing, thousands of them, more, coming out of the lava tubes at dusk in a rope of bodies that takes twenty minutes to pay out and then spending the whole night in the canopy eating the moths and the beetles and the fruit, shrieking, shitting, dropping half-eaten things down onto the frogs below, who eat those.

Land crabs are climbing the tree ferns. Something in the dark that grunts like a pig and is not a pig is turning over the frog heaps with a snout. Birds that should be sleeping are not sleeping; there is too much to eat. A snake as thick as your leg is draped across the lip of a collapse pit, and it is entirely covered in frogs, who do not seem to mind, and it does not seem to be eating them. It is not clear what is eating what. It is not clear that it matters. Everything is eating everything and at the same time everything is breeding, all at once, on the same night, and the noise is the noise of that.

---

There is a thing that happens in the valley that you will notice only if you stay.

The gray-headed birds — heavy, crow-sized, with a ragged crest — come down to the lava at the edges of the collapse pits and pick up flakes of the black glass. They are selective. They turn a flake in the beak, drop it, take another. They carry the ones they keep into the tree ferns, and there they use them. The land crabs go up the tree ferns at night in armies, and the gray-heads pin a crab with a foot and drive the glass into the seam of the shell and lever it, and the crab opens. Hundreds of them at it. The sound of it, under everything else, is a fine hard clicking, like someone knapping stone very far away.

The flakes fall. In the morning — if there were a morning here, if the cloud ever let in enough to call it that — the lava beneath certain tree ferns would be found littered with glass edges that look worked, that look chosen and used and discarded, that look like the leavings of a camp. There is no camp. Nothing has been built here and nothing will be. The gray-heads carry nothing home, keep nothing, arrange nothing. They select and use and drop. The valley is full of tools and empty of makers in any sense that leaves a mark. The one intelligence at work in the place leaves behind only a slightly higher concentration of good edges at the feet of certain trees, and the rain moves those, and the frogs cover them.

---

The ground is warm. You feel this through your soles and then, if you kneel, through your knees. It is not the warmth of a warm night. It is coming up. In the deeper collapse pits the warm ponds steam faintly, and here and there in the black crust are cracks that breathe a heat you can feel on your face from a yard away, with a smell to them of struck matches and boiled egg. The rock that made this valley is not finished. It is not far down.

And the valley knows. This, if there is a rule to the place, is the rule: nothing stays. The trees are fast-grown and shallow-rooted and they fall — you hear them go, four or five in a night, a long creaking tear and then a crash that silences a hundred yards of frogs for a breath before the chorus rolls back in over the gap. Collapse pits open. There is one at the lower end of the valley that was not there a season ago and is now full of water and already full of frogs. The slopes shed rock. The fern heaps rot at a rate you can smell. The animal population is not a population but a series of floods, each species surging through in its season in numbers the valley cannot hold and then crashing, so that the frog year is followed by a crab year and the crab year by a plague of the gray-heads and then the frogs again, and never the same balance twice. The place is being made and unmade around you continuously. It is not an ecosystem yet. It is the argument that comes before one.

You could stand at the lip and watch, if there were anything to watch with. You could stay one night, or many, and the noise would not stop and the dark would not lift and the ground would not settle, and at the end of it you would carry out no more than you brought in — some cuts on the hands from the glass, a smell you cannot wash out, a ringing.

And behind you, as you climbed out, the sound would drop away in layers, the frogs last of all, a wall becoming a roar becoming a hum becoming something you could mistake for your own blood, and then the valley would go on without you, as it does, loud in the dark, building nothing, keeping nothing, alive past all reason on a floor of glass.
