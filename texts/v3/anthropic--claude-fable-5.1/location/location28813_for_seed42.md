The lake sits in a bowl of granite two thousand meters up, where the only weather is wind and the only trees are a day's walk downslope. From the rim you would see a disc of dark water, perhaps four hundred paces across, ringed with grey boulders and old snow. Nothing on it. Nothing much reflected in it. You would not think to look under.

Under, the basin is green to the floor.

Not green with algae, not the soft murk of a pond. One plant fills the lake. It anchors in the rubble of the bottom and rises in columns as thick as a torso, ribbed, cold to the touch, hollow at the core. Its stems are the colour of a bottle held to the sun. Where they reach the surface they do not break it; they stop a hand's breadth beneath and spread into flat plates that lie under the water like a second, softer ceiling, and the light that comes through them arrives already tinted, already slow. The bottom is not stone. It is the root-mat of this one organism, generations of it, compressed into a spongy floor several meters deep that gives underfoot and springs back. Nobody has dug through to bedrock. Nobody has needed to.

The water is clear the way high water is clear, and cold the way it is cold: a few degrees above freezing at the bottom in every season, cool enough at the surface in late summer to make the teeth ache. Sunlight at this altitude is strong and there is little in the water to scatter it, so the basin is bright at noon, a green brightness with hard edges, and the shadows of the columns fall long and blue across the floor. It looks like a hall of pillars. It looks like a forest in which someone has removed every branch and left only trunks.

The columns are far apart. This is the first thing you notice after the colour: how much water there is between things. A dozen strides of open floor between one stem and the next, sometimes twenty. The plant does not crowd itself. It seems to have arrived, long ago, at the spacing it can afford on the light it gets, and it has held that spacing ever since. New shoots come up rarely and only where an old column has died and softened and folded. The whole basin has the settled look of a thing that stopped changing before anyone was there to watch it change.

And then, among the columns, the buildings.

They are made of the plant. There is no other material in the lake to make them from, apart from the granite rubble along the shore and a few kinds of bone. So the builders, whoever they were and are, learned to work the stems. The technique is visible everywhere once you know to look for it. A young column is bent while it is still supple and lashed to its neighbour with cord twisted from its own fibre; the two grow into each other over decades and become an arch. Arches are lashed to arches. Old columns are hollowed and scored so that their walls thicken on one side and thin on the other and the stem curls as it grows. Nothing here was cut and stacked. Everything was persuaded.

The structures are small. Round-chambered things, three or four to a cluster, the clusters set well apart from one another with open floor between. There are perhaps thirty chambers in the whole basin, and paths between them that are only paths because the root-mat has been worn down along certain lines by centuries of passage. Some chambers are old enough that the seams between the original stems have vanished under bark. Some show the fresh pale wounds where scoring was done this year, with a tool that could only have been a flake of stone or a sharpened rib.

The chambers hold air.

This is the thing the plant does that made the town possible. In sunlight it breathes out oxygen, and it does so not in a diffuse way but in a steady leak from pores along the upper stems. The builders long ago learned to grow a stem into a closed dome, seal its top, and let it fill. A dome the size of a small room will take a summer to fill and, once full, will stay full, because the plant keeps leaking into it faster than the air escapes. Each chamber has a low mouth at the floor where you swim in and come up, gasping, into a bell of still green-lit air that smells of cut grass and iron. The walls sweat. Sound inside is close and dead, like being under a blanket. From within, the lake outside is a shifting brightness through the translucent wall, the shadow of a fish crossing it now and then like a hand passed over a lamp.

Outside is the opposite of quiet.

Water carries sound well, and the basin is small and hard-walled, so nothing that happens in it goes unheard. In the morning it is the fish: dense shoals of a pale, blunt-headed char that drum, a low throb from the swim bladder that dozens of them produce at once and that you feel in the sternum. In the afternoon, when the light is highest, it is the plant itself. Every pore along every column lets go its oxygen at once, and the whole lake ticks and hisses and crackles, a sound like fat in a pan, like dry leaves burning, rising to a sustained fry by mid-afternoon and falling away as the sun leaves the rim. Under that, all day, the grazers: thousands of snails and a flat-bodied amphipod that live on the columns and rasp at them, and the rasping in aggregate is a grinding sigh that never quite stops. Now and then something structural gives, a column shifting under its own weight against a neighbour, and the whole basin fills for a moment with a groan like a hull. In winter, when the surface freezes, the ice adds its own voice, splitting and complaining above the plant's ceiling, and the sound comes down through the water fierce and metallic. It is never silent here. The chambers are the only refuge from it, and the people, if they are people, seem to spend the loud afternoons inside.

The animals are everywhere and the animals are not afraid. Char hang in the open water in the middle of the columns like lamps left on. Newts the length of a forearm, mottled the colour of the root-mat, sit in the crooks of the arches and do not move for hours. Eels, or something like eels, live in the softening cores of the dead columns and come out at dusk to work the floor. Small clawed things pick along the seams of the buildings, cleaning them. Once or twice a day the ceiling-plates part and a bird comes through, a diver with a body like a stone and a neck like a spear, and it works the length of the basin with its eyes open and takes what it takes and goes back up, and the char scatter and reform behind it and the drumming resumes. The lake does not seem to register any of this as disturbance. It has room.

The edges of the town are the edges of the plant. Where the basin floor climbs toward the shore and the light through the shallows is too harsh or the winter ice grinds too close, the columns thin and stop, and past them the bottom is bare granite, cold and pale and empty, sloping up to the air. No one builds there. Nothing lives there but the amphipods, thinly. The town is bounded by its own substrate, a green island in a grey bowl, and it is entirely possible to swim its whole circuit in the time it takes the afternoon crackle to rise and fall.

What is odd, if anything is odd, is the patience of it. A chamber begun now will hold air in the builders' grandchildren's time. The arches took a hundred years. The paths in the root-mat are the record of more footfalls than the present number of chambers could ever account for, which suggests the town was once larger, or once fuller, and has settled to this. There are no signs of that being a loss. The spacing between the columns is the spacing between the buildings is the spacing between the people, and all of it has the air of a thing that has found the distance it can hold and is holding it, deliberately, in cold clear water, with the light coming down green and the whole basin frying softly in the sun.
