The first thing is the altitude, and you feel it in the blood before you see anything: a thin sharp air, a sky darker than it should be at midday, a sun that gives light without much comfort. By every rule of height this should be a place of frost and lichen and bare rock. It is not. It is warm. The warmth comes up from below.

You are standing in a bowl in the summit of a mountain that did not exist very long ago. Geologically it is a child — pushed up in a few thousand years rather than a few million, and still rising, a few millimeters a season. The rock shows it. Nothing here has been softened. The rim of the bowl is a ring of fractured black and rust-red stone with edges that would cut a hand, faces still holding the glassy skin of the day they cooled. There are no scree slopes worn to gentleness, no rounded boulders. Where a block has fallen it has fallen recently and lies where it landed, angular, unlichened on its broken face. Elsewhere the world's mountains are being worn down. This one is still being made.

The bowl is small — you could walk its floor in ten minutes if the floor let you, which it does not. Perhaps three hundred paces across, walled on all sides, with a single notch in the eastern rim where the ground drops away into cloud. The cloud is the horizon. Below the notch there is only white, moving slowly, and once in a while it thins enough to show a ridge some impossible distance down and then closes again. The bowl is a room with one window, and the window looks out on nothing.

Inside, it is a garden, or a jungle, or something between the two. The heat rises through the ground in a hundred places — through cracks, through gravel that steams faintly when the air cools, through pools that never freeze and never go still. The wet, warm rock breathes upward all day, and the cold air of the altitude takes that breath and turns it instantly back into water. So it is raining almost always, but softly, and from no great height: the cloud forms a few meters over the treetops, sags, and lets go. Drops hang from every leaf edge. The rock walls weep in long dark stripes. Everything you touch is beaded. Your clothes are wet through within an hour and you will not be cold.

The vegetation has taken this gift and crowded into it. Tree ferns with trunks as thick as a person, their fronds meeting and interlacing overhead so that the bowl is roofed in green at about the height of a second storey. Under them, giant rosette plants with leaves like grey felt, each one taller than you. Under those, mosses in a depth you sink into to the ankle, in reds and golds and a green so saturated it looks lit from inside. Ferns on the ferns. Bromeliads clamped in the crotches of the tree ferns, each one holding its own cup of rain. Nothing has room. Stems lean against each other. A climbing thing with heart-shaped leaves has taken possession of an entire section of the northern wall and is working its way along the rim. You cannot see more than a few meters in any direction and you cannot take a step without moving something living aside.

And it is loud. That is the strangest part, once you have adjusted to the rest — that a place so enclosed and so high should have no silence in it at all. Some of the sound is water: the drip from ten thousand leaf points, the small waterfalls that spill off the rim wherever a stripe of weeping rock gathers itself, the pools that boil at their edges and knock stones together. But most of it is steam. The mountain vents. Along the western wall there is a line of fissures where superheated vapor comes out under pressure with a sound like a continuous exhale through clenched teeth, and above it, where the vapor hits the cold, a roar that rises and falls with the wind. You speak to a companion by shouting. You quickly stop speaking.

Someone came here for this. That is what the structures are for.

They are not many, and you find them one at a time, half-swallowed. First a steel pipe, forearm-thick, wrapped in rotted insulation, running out of the moss at knee height and disappearing into the ferns. Follow it and it leads to a wellhead — a heavy valve assembly bolted to a concrete pad, painted once in industrial yellow, the paint now mostly a substrate for moss. The pipe hums. Something is still moving through it. Farther along, a squat cabinet on a plinth with a glass gauge face fogged white from the inside, its needle pinned. A length of cable strung on porcelain insulators from the wellhead toward the notch, sagging, one stanchion leaning at forty degrees where the ground it was set in has lifted and tilted. There is a small block building with a corrugated roof near the eastern rim, one door, no windows, and inside it a transformer the size of a wardrobe that is warm to the touch and gives off a low, insistent note under the roar of the vents. Whether it is warm because it is working or because everything here is warm, you cannot say.

There was a plan, once, to take the mountain's heat down the slope in pipes and wire and turn it into something useful far below. The plan seems to have been small — a survey station, a pilot bore, not a plant — and then it seems to have been left. Not destroyed. Not finished. Left. The pipe still carries steam because the well was never capped, and the steam has gone on making its way to the end of the pipe, where it exits a broken flange in a hissing white plume and has, over years, grown a bright green cone of moss and liverwort on the rock beneath it — the only spot in the bowl where something grows faster than the ferns.

What has happened since is mostly this: the mountain has kept rising, and everything bolted to it has come slightly out of true. The concrete pads sit a hand's width higher on one side than the other. A survey marker — a brass disc set in a drilled hole, stamped with an elevation — is wrong by an amount that would take an instrument to detect but is real, and grows. Nothing here is fast. The vents do not erupt, the pools do not surge, the ferns do not visibly move. But if you came back in a decade the wall of the bowl would be a little taller, the notch a little narrower, the pipe a little more bowed, the moss a little further up the transformer housing. The place changes at the speed of geology and the speed of moss, and those turn out to be nearly the same speed.

Animals are few. This is too high for most things, too enclosed for the rest, and the bowl's living world is overwhelmingly green rather than moving. But not entirely. There are small dark flies that live over the warm pools and never seem to leave them. There is a single kind of bird — you hear it before you see it, a thin descending note that somehow cuts under the roar, and then a small brown shape, streaked, working the underside of the fern fronds for insects, entirely unconcerned by you. Perhaps a dozen of them in the whole bowl. They nest, as far as you can tell, in the gap behind the corrugated roof of the transformer house, where it is warm and dry, and they have done this for enough generations that the metal of the roof edge is polished bright in one spot from their feet.

Once, in the east notch, with the cloud thinned for a moment, you see something larger — a dark wingspan riding the updraft where the mountain's warm breath meets the cold — and then the cloud closes and it is gone and does not return.

At the end of a day here the light does not so much fade as get lost. The cloud thickens, the sun somewhere behind it goes orange and then is simply absent, and the bowl fills with a grey glow that seems to come from the vapor itself. The vents go on roaring. The transformer goes on humming. The birds go quiet and the drip does not. Warmth comes up through your boots. Above the fern canopy, the sky at this height turns the deep violet it only ever turns on the roof of the world, and a few stars come out over a jungle, and the mountain, as it has every night since it was born, grows very slightly taller under you in the dark.
