Start with the mountain, because the mountain is the only thing that stays where you put it.

It is high. You know this from the cold of the rock before you know it from anything else — the stone under your palms is that particular altitude-cold, thin and mineral, the cold of places above where weather usually bothers to go. But the water around the stone is warm. Bathwater, bloodwater, a heat that has nothing to do with the sun. It comes up from below, or it comes from the water itself, which seems to be generating its own temperature the way a body does, and the mismatch — cold bones, warm flesh — is the first sign that the place has not been assembled by the usual rules.

You are on the summit of something and you are underwater. Both are true and neither is a metaphor.

---

Call it a basin. A shallow crater, perhaps two hundred paces across, ringed by a broken lip of dark rock, and filled — overfilled — with water that does not stop at the lip. The water goes up. It stands over the crater the way a lens stands over a page, a domed, held, impossible sea sitting on the crown of a mountain in the sky, and you are inside it, moving slowly across the crater floor with your lungs doing something that is not quite breathing and is not quite not.

Light comes from somewhere above, but it has been through a great deal of water on its way to you, and what arrives is a dim green-brown, the color of the inside of a closed eye. You can see perhaps ten body-lengths. Beyond that the world dissolves into suspended grit and the memory of shapes. It is a small place, and the murk makes it smaller, a room with walls of turbidity.

And it is loud.

Not loud the way a waterfall is loud, or a storm. Loud the way an organ is loud when you stand inside its casing. The water is full of sound to the point of saturation — a grinding, a tearing, a bell-depth boom that is not periodic and never resolves, layered with hisses and the long cracking of stone that is being made and unmade faster than stone should permit. Sound in water is felt as much as heard, and here it is felt in the teeth, the sternum, the small bones behind the eyes. It presses. There is no gap in it. When you try to remember silence, you find you cannot reconstruct what it was.

---

The floor of the basin is young. This is the word the geologist in you keeps reaching for: *young*. Every edge is a cut edge. The rock has not been here long enough to be smoothed by anything; it has the crystalline sharpness of something that was liquid a minute ago and is now, unwillingly, solid. Fresh basalt, or something that resembles it, black and glassy and ridged, still faintly ticking as it cools — you can hear the ticking under the roar if you press your ear to it, a hundred tiny fractures adjusting.

But it is not cooling from a volcano. There is no vent. The stone is coming from the water.

You watch it happen, once you understand what you are watching. A cloud of the warm murk thickens ahead of you — gathers, darkens, takes on a heaviness — and then with a sound like a door slammed inside a cathedral it *sets*. It becomes rock. A ridge, a step, a fin of black stone six feet tall, thrown up out of nothing in the space of a breath, sharp as broken glass along its crest. The concussion of its making rolls through the water and shoves you back a pace.

And then, thirty heartbeats later, the water takes it apart. Not by wearing it down — nothing here lasts long enough to be worn — but by unmaking it, the stone going soft and cloudy and dispersing back into the warm fog it came from, with a long descending groan.

This is the sound. This is all of the sound. The whole basin is doing this, everywhere, all the time. The water is thinking in stone, and thinking badly, restlessly, throwing up a shape and discarding it and throwing up another, and every thought is a detonation.

---

Some of the shapes are recognizable.

This is the part you did not want to notice, and now cannot stop noticing. Among the fins and ridges and blunt cairns, the water sometimes makes a *thing*. A flight of five steps, going nowhere, very well proportioned. An arch, low enough to stoop through, with a suggestion of a keystone. Once, off to your left at the edge of vision, what is unmistakably a doorframe — lintel and jambs and a sill — standing alone on the crater floor for the length of a long exhale before it clouds and goes.

The water has seen buildings. Somewhere, somehow, it has learned the grammar of them and it is practicing, the way a child draws the same house over and over. Nothing it makes has a purpose. The steps lead to nothing. The door opens onto more water and more roar. But the making is fluent, and it is getting more fluent — you are almost certain the arches are becoming truer as you watch, the proportions settling, as though it is remembering rather than inventing.

There are a few things it has not unmade. You find them in the low places, half-buried in the grit that constantly falls out of the water: a length of dressed stone with a chiseled groove down one face, worn genuinely smooth by real time. A step that has an *edge* — an old, rounded, foot-hollowed edge. A basin within the basin, a shallow bowl cut into bedrock, the kind of thing people once knelt at. These are older than the water's imitations. These are the originals. Whatever shrine stood on this summit before the sea came to sit on it — and something did — the water has been studying its bones, and has loved them, and is trying to say them back.

You put your hand in the little stone bowl. It is full of warm water like everything else. There is nothing in it.

---

Living things do not do well here, and you understand why: nothing can root in stone that will not exist in a minute. Yet a few things have found a way. On the oldest fragments, the ones the water leaves alone, there are strands — black, filamentous, a finger's length, streaming in the invisible currents that the constant making and unmaking throws off. They are not many. A dozen tufts in the whole visible basin. They are the only softness in a place built entirely from edges, and you find yourself grateful to them out of all proportion.

There is one animal.

You never see it properly. You see a paleness pass at the limit of vision, a long low body, unhurried, and then the same paleness passes on the other side of you at the same moment, and then it is ahead of you and also behind you, and you realize there is one animal and it is in several places, or the place has several *heres* and the animal is in all of them, and that these are not different statements. It does not approach. It does not seem to be afraid of the concussions. Once it passes close enough that you can see it has no eyes and does not need them; it is steering by the roar, reading the sound-shapes of stone that does not exist yet. It has been here long enough to have learned that. You have not.

---

The rules, as far as you can gather them:

Up is negotiable. The lens of water above you has a surface, you assume, but when you try to rise toward it you find yourself instead approaching the crater floor from a new angle, as though the basin were the inside of a sphere and every direction eventually became down. The mountain is the only fixed reference, and even it is being rewritten at the edges, growing new spurs, losing them.

Sound arrives before the thing that makes it. This you confirm after the third or fourth time a ridge slams into being exactly where, a moment earlier, you heard it slam into being. The water is loud with the future — the roar is not the sound of what is happening but of what is about to, a half-second of prophecy that no one asked for and that cannot be turned off. This is why the sound never gaps. Something is always about to be made.

Heat is a kind of attention. Where the water is warmest, it is about to build. You learn to feel a rise in temperature against your face and move aside, and the stone that then appears would have gone through you, and you would have been part of the wall for the thirty heartbeats before the wall dispersed.

The originals are safe. The water never builds over the true old stones, the worn step, the little bowl. It makes its copies at a respectful distance, and leaves the space around each real relic open, a small clearing in the storm. You stay near them.

---

Somewhere in the long murk of standing there, with the roar pressing your ribs and the green-brown dark thick around you, you notice that the water has begun to build something larger.

It is happening at the center of the basin, where the crater floor dips lowest, and it is happening slowly, for here — slowly meaning across minutes rather than seconds. A cloud gathers and does not slam into stone. It hangs, thickening, considering. Its warmth reaches you from twenty paces off. The roar around it has changed key, dropped, become a single sustained note that you feel in your knees.

Then the shapes begin to come out of the cloud, and they come out in order: a foundation, a course of walls, a rank of narrow openings that in any other world would be windows. Pillars. A lintel too wide to have been made by anyone, but made in the manner of someone. It is building the shrine. Not a step or an arch of it — all of it, whole, from the memory of the bones you are standing among, a black glass reconstruction of a place that must once have stood in air and light on a high mountain and had people in it.

It is almost right. It is almost exactly right. The proportions are true and the doorway faces the direction a doorway should face and you can see, in the dim, the raised sill of the inner room where the bowl would go.

Then the roar that has been holding the single note begins to descend, and you know — you *hear* — what is about to happen, a half-second early, the way you always do here. The walls fog. The pillars soften. The whole almost-shrine dissolves back into the warm dark with a sound like an enormous weight being set down very gently, and the water is quiet for the length of one breath, the only quiet there is in this place, the pause between forgetting and beginning again.

And then, somewhere off to your right, a cloud begins to thicken, and the roar comes back, and you feel warmth on your face, and you step aside.
