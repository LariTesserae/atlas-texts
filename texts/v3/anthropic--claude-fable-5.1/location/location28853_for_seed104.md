The first thing you have to accept is that this is a summit.

Not a sea floor. Not a drowned valley. The ground here, when you finally reach it, is the top of something — the highest broad shoulder of a continent that was once a range of mountains and then, over a span of time too long to be honestly called time, was worn down to a single continental swell. What remains is a tableland the size of a hemisphere, rising almost imperceptibly toward a middle nobody has found, its surface rounded and soft as a knuckle under skin. The rock is the oldest stable rock there is: a dark grey-brown, faintly warm to the touch, pressed so completely that it has no grain. It does not break. It has finished breaking. There are no boulders, no scree, no cliffs — those belong to a younger world. Here the erosion has run all the way to its conclusion and stopped, and the land lies in its final form, which is nearly flat.

And it is underwater. All of it.

The water goes up further than sight can follow. It is warm, blood-warm, and it is not resting on the plateau the way a lake rests in a basin; there is no basin. It is simply that the sea is here, at this altitude, occupying the whole volume above the highland as if it had always been the medium in which the mountains grew. Your ears know something is wrong. There is pressure — the dense, held-breath pressure of deep water — and at the same time there is the thinness of great altitude, a faint shortness in every breath you take. Both are true. The water is as heavy as any ocean and as high as any peak, and the body cannot decide which fact to believe, so it believes both and aches slightly.

This is how the world is here. Distinctions have eroded the way the rock has eroded. Sea and mountain were once separate categories with an edge between them, and the edge was worn away, and now they are one thing that the old words don't fit.

---

The light comes down brown-green and thick, and it comes from more than one direction.

Far above there is a surface — you can sometimes see it, a pale sheet of moving brightness impossibly high, the way one sees the sun through closed eyelids. But there is also a second glow, lower, off to what might be west, lying inside the water like a bank of lit fog. And in places the sea floor itself gives off a faint mineral luminance, a cold ember light that rises a few metres and fades. None of these is strong. The whole plateau lives in a permanent late dusk, the dimness of a room where the lamp has been turned down and left. Colours survive only as hints: the rust of the rock, the deep viridian of the forests, occasionally a shocking small yellow that turns out to be a spore-cap or a scrap of old shell.

The forests are the second thing you have to accept.

They are not kelp, though they resemble it in the way a cathedral resembles a tent. They are rooted directly into the ancient rock — the only thing that grips it — and they rise in dense columns forty, sixty, a hundred metres, their fronds broad and soft and heavy, lying against one another in overlapping layers so thick that in the interior you move by parting them with both hands, and they close behind you without sound. They are warm-blooded, or something like it; the water inside the stands is a degree or two warmer than outside. Their surfaces are furred with smaller growths, and those with smaller still, so that every square metre holds a hundred lives pressed together, feeding on one another's shed cells, near-touching, saturated. This is a lush place. It is one of the lushest places there has ever been. It is just that nearly everything alive here is a plant, or close enough that the difference has worn off too.

The stands go on for hundreds of kilometres. From above — from as far up as one can rise before the pressure and the thinness make it unwise — they read as a dark continental fleece, moving very slowly in a single direction, as though the whole plateau is being combed.

---

There is weather.

A sea this large, at this height, makes its own. Long currents pass over the tableland like fronts, bringing water that is a shade cooler, or saltier, or that carries a fine grey silt that settles on the fronds and is shed a day later in a slow collective shrug. There are tides, but they don't move in and out; they move down and up. Twice in a long cycle the entire body of water settles a little, pressing closer to the rock, and the forests bow; then it lifts, and they stand. In some seasons the upper water clouds with a bloom of something pale and the light fails to a near-dark for weeks, and in the forest interiors nothing can be seen at all, only felt — the warm soft weight of the fronds, the small resistances of things living on them.

Rain falls here. This should not be possible and it is not, in the usual sense. But scattered across the plateau are pockets of the old sky — remnant volumes of air, some the size of a room, some the size of a valley, held in the water like bubbles in glass. They sit in hollows of the rock, or drift slowly a few metres above the forest canopy, their undersides silvered, their insides dim and dry. Inside them it is cold, and inside them rain falls upward from the water surface below and gathers on the ceiling and, when it is heavy enough, falls back down as water again. The forests avoid them. Where a pocket has drifted over a stand and settled, the fronds beneath it are bleached and stiff, and when the pocket moves on they are slow to recover.

The sound of the place is the sound of pressure: a hum below hearing that is felt in the chest, and over it, almost nothing. Fronds slide against fronds and make no noise. The tide's descent is silent. What can be heard, occasionally, is a single soft knock — rock against rock, though there is no loose rock — repeated at intervals of several minutes, from no fixed direction. Nobody has found its source. It may be the plateau itself, still settling by fractions after all this time. In the air pockets the silence is different: sharp, with edges, the silence of a room that has just emptied.

---

Animals are rare and large.

You may go for days through the stands without meeting anything with a face. Then a shape passes, slow, at the limit of the dimness — long, pale, without visible eyes, twenty metres or more, moving through the canopy tops with the fronds parting before it and closing behind. It does not hunt in any way that can be seen. It seems to feed by passing. Where it has gone, a stripe of the forest lies flattened and faintly grey and does not right itself for a season. There are perhaps a few dozen of these on the whole plateau. They do not appear to notice anything smaller than themselves, which is everything.

There is a second kind — small, quick, glass-clear, in the air pockets only, where they cling to the underside of the water surface and drink the upward rain. They may be the only fauna here that still lives in a single medium. They are said to die the moment they touch water, and to have never done so.

---

Nothing was ever built here — not here, not in this place as it is now. But things were built on the mountains, before the mountains were worn down and the sea climbed to meet them, and some of those things have lasted longer than the stone they were cut from.

You find them scattered, one every few days of travel. A flight of eleven steps rising out of the flat rock and stopping in the water, leading to nothing, their treads worn concave. A doorway — two uprights and a lintel — with the forest pressing against it on both sides and nothing behind it but more forest. A low wall, forty metres of it, straight as a rule, made of the same dark rock as the ground and indistinguishable from it except for being raised. A ring of stones the size of a small room, the interior floor of which is perfectly bare, no growth, no silt, as though something is still being kept out. Once, a single carved face, upturned, mouth open, the features worn smooth as a pebble, at the very bottom of one of the sky pockets, with the upward rain running over it.

These are the marks of people who stood on summits, in thin cold air, and looked down at seas that were far below them. Their works have been kept exactly where they placed them. It is the rest of the world that moved.

---

There are rules, or what remains of rules.

*Depth and height are the same measurement.* One descends to the plateau and one climbs to it, and both are correct, and if one keeps going up through the water one eventually arrives at the rock again. Not the same rock. Similar.

*Distance is unreliable in the stands.* A thing seen through the fronds at arm's length may take an hour to reach. A stand entered at its edge may be left, at its edge, having crossed nothing. The forest is not doing this deliberately. It is simply that "here" and "there" have worn thin in the same way "wet" and "dry" have, and in the densest places, where the most lives are pressed into the least volume, the thinness shows.

*The sky pockets are older than the sea.* They are not being made; they are only being lost. Each one that collapses is gone for good, and each one that drifts to the edge of the plateau and over it — nobody has seen the edge, but the pockets must go somewhere — is gone too. In time the last of them will fail, and the last upward rain will fall, and then this place will have no air in it at all and will be, finally, only one thing.

*Nothing here is dying.* The forests grow and are grazed and regrow; the tide falls and lifts; the currents pass. But the plateau is not on its way anywhere. It is not becoming. It has arrived at the place where becoming stops — the oldest stable state, the flat warm dimness that everything wears down into if given long enough — and what happens now is not history but circulation. The weather turns. The great pale grazers pass. The knock repeats.

It is not a dead place. It is a place that has finished. There is a difference, and it is the difference between a ruin and a summit, and here, where everything else has been worn to one thing, that difference alone is kept.
