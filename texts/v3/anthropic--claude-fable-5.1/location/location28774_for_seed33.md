The first thing to understand is the height. Not the number, which would mean nothing, but the condition: this plateau stands so far above the rest of the world that the rest of the world has become a floor. Cloud lies below it, unbroken, white going grey toward the edges, and it does not move the way cloud moves. It moves the way a sea moves in a very old painting. Standing at the rim you can look down on weather the way you would look down on a field, and once or twice a day a bulge rises in it, slow as breathing, and subsides.

The air is thin enough to hurt. It is cool rather than cold—there is not enough of it to carry cold with any conviction—but it takes the warmth out of you steadily, from the surface inward, the way dry stone takes warmth from a hand laid on it. There is almost no water. What water exists is frost, and the frost never melts and never lifts. It lies in the shadows of things and has lain there so long that it has taken on the texture of the stone beneath it, a white grain over a grey grain. You can scrape it with a fingernail and it does not come away wet. It comes away as powder, and the powder does not fall.

---

The plateau is stone. Pale layered stone, buff and ash and a faint rusted pink where iron has come to the surface, laid down in beds so regular that at a distance the whole plain looks ruled, like a page. The beds have been worked by wind for a length of time that is not really available to thought. Every edge is rounded. Every face is fluted. The wind has taken the soft bands and left the hard, and the hard bands stand out as ledges and sills and shelves, and the shelves have become, over the ages, floors.

That is the word that arrives first: floors. Then walls. Then, walking further in, with some reluctance—doors.

Because the plateau is a city.

Nobody built it. There is no mark of a tool anywhere, no chisel-bite, no joint, no mortar. The stone is one stone. The walls grow up out of the bedrock without seam and the lintels are the same bed as the sills and the whole thing has the continuous grain of a single formation that has never been broken. And yet it is a city, unmistakably, and it is no more possible to see it as anything else than it is possible to see a face in a cliff and then not see the face. There are streets. They run in long straight lines toward the horizon and cross at angles that are not quite right and continue. There are thresholds, worn into a dip at the centre where feet would fall. There are windows, square-cut and deep, with the light coming through them wrong. There are stairs.

The erosion here does not make rubble. That is the first rule, or the first place where a rule is missing. Elsewhere in the world wind takes stone and leaves it in slopes and heaps and fans. Here the wind takes stone and leaves it in rooms. A softer bed hollows out and the hollow is a chamber, with a flat floor and a flat ceiling and a doorway on the leeward side exactly where a doorway should be. A ridge of harder rock weathers down and does not become a spine or a fin but a wall with a coping, running true for four hundred paces and then turning a corner. The plateau has been eroding toward architecture for as long as it has been eroding at all, and it has arrived. This is the settled form. The wind removes as much each century as the mountain has to give, and what stands between them is a town that has always been exactly this size.

---

It is very large and very empty.

The buildings stand alone. Each one—a hall, a tower, a low house with a single room—is separated from the next by a distance that is too great for a city and too small for anything else: two hundred paces, three hundred, of bare fluted stone, with the street running between them straight and purposeless. It is as if the town were drawn at the correct proportions and then the paper stretched. You can stand in the middle of a crossroads and see eight or nine structures at various distances, none of them near, all of them clearly belonging to one another, and between them nothing at all.

The emptiness continues inside. The interiors are larger than the walls. Not dramatically—nothing here is dramatic—but a room that should be six paces across is nine, and the far wall, when you reach it, has the faint too-close look of a wall that has just now arrived. The corners hold frost. The floors hold dust in a skin so even that it must never have been walked on, and when you cross it you leave no mark, and when you look back at the door you came through it is a door in the middle of the wall, and the wall has no other door, and it opens onto the plateau, and it always opens onto the plateau. Every doorway in the city faces outward. You do not enter the buildings through them. You are inside, and then the door is how you leave.

Some of the structures are taller. There are towers—stacks of chambers, one on another, each floor a harder bed of the formation and each hollow a softer one—that rise fifteen and twenty stories above the plain, and the upper stories are older than the lower. This is visible. The stone at the top is more worn, more rounded, more nearly finished; the stone at the base is still sharp in places, still becoming. The city is being built from the top down. The wind starts at the sky and works toward the ground, and the ground is the newest part.

---

The light is dim, and the dimness has a quality to it that takes some time to name.

The sun is present. It sits in the sky, a small hard disc without glare, and the sky around it is a blue so dark it is nearly the colour of the stone in shadow. There is not enough air to scatter anything. The sun's light goes where the sun points it and nowhere else, so that every surface is lit from one side and black on the other, no reflection, no fill, no softening, and the shadows are so complete that things placed in them cease. A hand held in shadow is not a dark hand. It is an absence in the shape of a hand.

And the light is not enough. That is the other thing. It falls at full strength on the lit faces and they are still dim, as if the light arrived here having already been spent on something else, as if this were the last of it. The stone glows faintly rust and ash and gives back less than it receives. Colours are present but withdrawn. You can see for a very long way—to the far towers, to the rim, to the cloud-floor beyond—and none of it is bright.

The sun does not move much. It is not that the day is long; it is that the day is not a process here. It is a condition, and the condition persists. Occasionally the shadows are somewhere else than they were, without having travelled.

---

The silence is not the absence of sound. It is the failure of sound to arrive.

There is wind—you can see it, in the dust that skates along the flagstones in low sheets, in the dry lift of powder off a sill—but you do not hear it. A stone kicked loose and dropped into a chamber falls and lands and there is nothing. Your own footsteps do not come from your feet. They come from elsewhere in the city, from a street two hundred paces off, a moment late, and they are not always your footsteps. Sometimes what arrives is something else. A stone dropped long ago. A hoof on a threshold. Once, from very far off, a long slow settling, like a wall lying down, and nothing anywhere in the visible city has changed.

Sounds here are made and then they go somewhere and wait. They come back when the city has room for them. This is a kind of weather.

---

There is other weather. The city is in motion; it is only that the motion is at the pace of something with a very slow heart.

The dust falls. There is no rain—there cannot be—but at intervals the air fills with a fine pale grain that comes down without wind, straight, out of the dark blue, and lies on everything, and then some hours later lifts again and goes back up. Nothing is wetted. Nothing is cleaned. The dust is the city's own stone, leaving and returning.

The frost has a tide. It lies in the shadows, and the shadows are fixed, and yet the frost advances across the flagstones a hand's breadth over a day and then retreats, on a cycle that has nothing to do with the sun. Where it has been the stone is a shade paler. Where it is going the stone waits.

And the buildings come and go. Not quickly. A low hump in the bedrock that was not there is there, one day, and over the following years it rises, bed by bed, and hollows, and becomes a house, and stands for a time that might be centuries and might be an afternoon, and is worn down again from the top, and is a hump, and is level ground. You cannot see this happen. You can only see that the city you crossed a while ago is not the city you are crossing back through, that there is a tower to the east now where you remember a gap, that the crossroads has one more street than it had, and that all of it is old, all of it is worn, none of it is new.

---

There is a crust on the north faces of some walls. Grey-green, very thin, granular, with an edge that is not quite straight. It might be mineral. It might be something simpler than anything that has been called alive—a pattern that spreads the way life spreads, along the grain, favouring shade, avoiding the frost, without any of the other things life does. It does not grow toward the light, because the light is not worth growing toward. It is more extensive on the older stone. On the very oldest, the tops of the highest towers, it covers everything, and there it has a texture, a low relief, ridges and cells, that from the ground looks almost like writing and from the top looks almost like a map.

---

The animals are few, and each is the only one.

There is a pale long-legged thing, hare-sized or a little more, with the general shape of something that browses and nothing to browse. It stands in doorways. That is what it does. You see it at a distance, motionless, in the deep frame of a door with the black interior behind it, and you look away and look back and it is in another door. It has two shadows, and one of them is on the wrong side. It has never been seen to eat, or move, or make a sound, and the frost is disturbed where it has been.

There is a bird that flies through walls. Grey, dove-sized, with a flight that is more falling than flying, a controlled descent that never quite reaches the ground. It comes out of the face of a tower forty feet up and drops in a long line across the street and into the face of another, and the stone does not mark it. There is only one. You know this because you have never seen two, and because the city is the kind of place where, if there were two, you would.

And there is something that hunts.

You do not see it. What you see is the street. A street that was three hundred paces from wall to wall is, when you look again, two hundred and fifty. The buildings on either side are where they were. The distance between them is not. The pale thing stands in a doorway at the far end and the doorway is coming closer, or the far end is, and the pale thing does not move, and then the street is a street again, the full width, empty, and the doorway at the end is a doorway with nothing in it, and some while later, from far off, there arrives the sound of a hoof on a threshold.

It has never caught it. Or it has caught it many times and there is only ever one. The pale thing stands in another door. The city narrows, patient, elsewhere. It has been going on for as long as the wind has been making rooms, and both of them are worn smooth by it.

---

There is a place near the middle—if the city has a middle; it has a place where the streets are longest and the buildings furthest apart—where the stone is dished. A shallow basin, a bowl a hundred paces across, floored with the same layered rock, the beds visible as concentric rings going down. It is the one place where the erosion has worked downward. The rings are worn to a polish. At the bottom, where the youngest and hardest bed lies exposed, there is a flat disc of dark stone about the width of a door, and it is the only surface on the plateau that holds a reflection.

Not of the sky. The sky in it is bright.

You can stand on the rim of the bowl for a long while looking at that. The dust falls, and lifts. The frost is a hand's breadth further out than it was. Far off, on a tower you do not remember, the grey crust has spread another ring. Your footsteps arrive from somewhere behind you, unhurried, and stop when you stop, a little late, and then one more.

The city goes on to the rim in every direction, and past the rim there is only the cloud, and the cloud is very slowly rising.
