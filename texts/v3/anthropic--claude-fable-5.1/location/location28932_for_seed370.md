The approach is the last natural thing, and even that is uncertain. A shelf of dark rock at an altitude where the sky has thinned to a hard, colorless blue — but the shelf has been paved. Every yard of it. Flat fired tiles the color of old liver, set edge to edge without mortar, running out to the drop on three sides and, on the fourth, rising into the structure. There is no soil between the tiles. There is no grit on them. Whatever was here before the paving is under it and has been under it for so long that the question stops meaning anything.

You hear the place long before you see it, and you see it long before you understand its size, because there is nothing near it to compare it to. It is not large. That is the first correction the eye makes on arriving: this thing that has been roaring at you for an hour is perhaps the size of a barn, a granary, a small chapel. A squat dome of stacked clay, mud-brown gone red-brown gone almost black at the windward face, its surface worn to the softness of a river stone. No edge on it anywhere. No seam sharp enough to catch a fingernail. It looks poured, but it was not poured. Get close and the coursework is still legible under the wear — thousands of hand-sized bricks, each one slightly different, each one pressed into shape by someone's palms and dried in a sun that presumably still comes up somewhere below this altitude. On the lee side, low down, there are shallow grooves in the clay worn by ropes: hauling marks. They are the only record of the labor. They are also worn round.

The sound is a single tone. That is the second correction. From a distance it seemed like a roar, a chorus, wind in a gorge — but standing on the paving at the threshold you understand that it is one note, or one chord so stable it has fused into a note, and that it has not varied by the width of a hair since you first heard it. It does not swell. It does not breathe. It is loud in a way that stops being loudness: your chest picks it up before your ears, your molars vibrate at some sympathetic frequency, and the small bones in your face hum. Speech is impossible. Not drowned — simply irrelevant, as if the air here is already fully occupied and has no room for another voice.

The wind is why. On this shelf, at this height, the wind arrives from one direction only, warm and dry and at one speed. It has done so always. Nobody built here because of the view. They built here because of the wind, the way one might build a mill on a river — except a river floods and dries, and this does not. It is a warm wind. It comes up over the lip of the plateau having crossed some enormous sunlit lowland out of sight, and it carries that heat with it and nothing else. No moisture. No seeds. No dust, because whatever dust it once carried it has long since carried away, and whatever dust the structure once shed has gone the same way. The surfaces are clean the way bone is clean.

There is an opening on the lee side, low, a crouching height, worn to an oval by the passage of bodies that have not passed through it in a very long time. Inside, the light is the color of the walls — a brown amber, arriving through the windward slots and reflected so many times off curved clay that by the time it reaches you it has no direction. You cannot see a shadow. You cannot see very far, either, because the interior is full.

This is the third correction. You expected a hollow. There is no hollow. The dome is packed, floor to vault, with vessels — jars, throats, long-necked bottles, gourds of clay, some the size of a fist and some you could stand inside — every one of them hand-built, coil on coil, the finger-marks still faintly there under a glaze that is not glaze but ten thousand years of moving warm air. They are stacked and nested and wedged against one another with no space between, mouths all facing the same way, toward the slots where the wind enters. Only a few passages remain, narrow and low, threading through the mass. You go on hands and knees. The clay under your palms is warm, the warmth of a body that has been lying in the sun, and it is warm all the way through; the whole structure has come to the temperature of the wind and stays there.

Each vessel speaks. That is what the note is. Each mouth catches the same steady stream of air and sounds its own pitch, and the pitches were chosen — you can feel that they were chosen — so that together they collapse into this one immense unwavering tone. Hold your ear to a small jar and you can almost separate its thin whistle from the mass. Move your head an inch and it is gone again, folded back in. There are no beats, no interference, no drift. Somebody tuned this. Somebody spent lifetimes tuning this, shaving a lip here, adding a coil there, and then at some point it was finished, and it has been finished ever since.

There are no bones in here. No tools left behind, no votive things, no scratched marks on the walls that you can find in the dimness. Whoever made it did not stay to be part of it, or if they did, the wind took them the way it took the dust. Nothing lives in the chamber. Nothing could — there is no water, nothing to eat, and the air itself is a single solid pressure that would not permit a heartbeat to find its own rhythm. Even the lichen that colonizes every high stone elsewhere in the world is absent. The clay is too smooth, too warm, too clean, and it has been too clean for too long. The place is not dead so much as prior to death, or past it; it has settled into the one state it can hold forever and is holding it.

After a while — how long is hard to say; the tone erases duration the way it erases speech — your ears give up. They stop reporting the sound as sound. What you are left with is a silence of extraordinary density, a silence with a grain, a silence you could lean against. Inside it, small things become audible: your own pulse, the click of your knees on the clay, the dry sound of your breath. These are the only changing things in the room, and they are yours, and the structure does not acknowledge them.

You might try to alter something. Turn a jar. Pull one from the stack. You will find you cannot: they have fused where they touch, not with mortar but with time, the clay surfaces pressed together under their own weight until the boundary between one vessel and the next is a suggestion. The whole interior is one object now, a single hand-built thing made of ten thousand hand-built things, and it has one voice.

Outside again, blinking in the thin hard light, the sound reassembles itself into a roar and then into a note and then into weather, a fact of the place, like the altitude. The paving runs out flat to the drop. The warm wind pushes at your back with exactly the force it pushed with when you arrived. Nothing here is waiting for anything. It was built to make a sound and it makes the sound, and it will go on making it after the last of the bricks has worn to a lens and the vessels inside have gone to a single ringing lump, because by then the difference will not matter. It is already, in every way that counts, finished.
