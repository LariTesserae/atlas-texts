The plateau sits at the height where clouds usually form, so it is more often inside weather than under it. This is a warm wetness, not a cold one: the air at shoulder height is the temperature of skin, and it holds so much water that surfaces sweat without rain ever falling. You feel it first on the backs of your hands.

The ground is young rock. Not soil, not yet — a broad shield of dark basalt that came up as liquid recently enough that its surface still remembers being a fluid. It has ropes and folds in it, places where a skin cooled and was dragged by what moved beneath. Its edges are unweathered: where it has cracked, the cracks are clean and the faces inside them are glassy, with corners you could cut yourself on. Nothing has had time to round anything. Where the flow stopped it stopped in a lip, a ten-meter wall of black that ends the plateau on its eastern side and drops, more or less vertically, to a lower shelf of the same material. There are several of these steps, one below another, going down out of sight into the cloud.

The rock weeps. Everywhere along the fracture lines and the flow-fronts, water comes out of the stone — not in streams but in a slow, steady filming, a sheen that darkens the basalt to a wet black and beads at the undersides of the ledges and lets go one drop at a time. The whole plateau is a sponge of cooled lava with water moving through it from somewhere above, and the water finds every crack and comes out clear and slightly warm. It collects in the hollows. There are hundreds of pools, most no larger than a table, some the size of a courtyard, each one with a rim that is exactly the shape the lava took when it stopped. The pools are full to the brim and stay that way. They receive exactly as much as they lose.

The vegetation lives on the water and ignores the rock. It cannot root in basalt, so it grows where the seepage has left a little mineral silt, in the fissures and along the pool margins, and there it is dense and extravagant: tree ferns with fronds two meters long, sedges the color of new brass, moss in such thickness that it takes the shape of the rock beneath it and then improves on it, swelling into pillows. Each of these gardens is a small island. Between them is bare stone — ten meters, thirty, a hundred — dark and lightly shining and empty. Standing on the open flow you can count the green places around you like ships. They do not join. The plateau is generous with water and stingy with everything else, and the plants have settled for what they can get.

The town takes the same approach.

It occupies the seam where the upper flow meets the one below it, where the wall of the flow-front provides both shelter and a source: water comes out of that wall in quantity, and people have cut into the basalt beneath the seepage to make channels, and the channels feed cisterns, and the cisterns are the reason there is anything here at all. The construction is the rock. Every wall is dry-laid basalt, the blocks split along the natural fracture planes so their faces are flat and sharp, fitted without mortar and holding by weight and care. Where a lintel is needed there is a beam of tree-fern trunk, dark and fibrous. Roofs are thatched with the long sedge, laid thick and already black with damp. There is no metal anywhere. The tool marks visible on the cut channels are the marks of stone on stone — broad, patient, slightly irregular, the record of someone working a very hard material with a harder one and a great deal of time.

It is a town and not a village because of its organization more than its size. It steps down the flow-front in terraces, each terrace a level shelf built out from the wall on retaining courses of stacked basalt, each with its own row of houses and its own channel running along the back, drawn from the seeping cliff and carried in a groove cut into the terrace floor. The channels are stepped as the terraces are: water leaves one level through a notch and falls in a thread to a basin on the next, and from that basin runs along the next channel, and so on, five or six times, until the last terrace drains into a pool on the shelf below that is the same pool it has always drained into. There are stairways between levels, cut into the rock where the rock permitted and built where it did not. There are wider spaces that function as squares — flat pans of lava flow left unbuilt, swept clean of ash and silt, with a cistern at the center of each. The whole arrangement is legible from above. Someone laid this out.

No one is in it. Or no one is visible; the two are not the same thing, and the town does not tell you which. The steps are swept. The channels run clear and their grooves show no silt. Bundles of cut fern are stacked in the dry angle under an eave. A round basalt grinding stone sits in a doorway with its handstone beside it. Everything is in the condition of having been used a moment ago. But there is no one, and no sound of anyone, and the doors — where there are doors, which is not everywhere — are a matting of sedge tied to fern poles, and they hang still.

That absence extends past the human. There are no birds on this plateau. No insects hum over the pools; nothing skates on them; nothing has laid eggs in the moss. The tree ferns are not eaten. The sedge is not nested in. The water in the cisterns is perfectly clear because nothing lives in it to cloud it. The rock is too new, perhaps, and too high, and too isolated by the cloud; whatever would need to arrive here on wing or leg has not arrived. The town's builders came up and stayed, and nothing followed them. It gives the place a particular quality, hard to name until you have stood in it a while — the quality of a stage set after the performance, when the set is still lit.

What sound there is, is water. Not much of it. The seepage does not run, it drips, and a thousand drips at a thousand slightly different intervals produce a sound too diffuse to locate — a faint, continuous, irregular ticking that seems to come from the air itself. The channels carry a whisper. Where water falls from one terrace to the next it makes a small clear note that repeats. That is all. The cloud absorbs the rest. If you spoke, you would hear your voice and then nothing, not even an echo off the flow-front, because the wet moss on the wall takes it.

The light is the light of the inside of a cloud, which is to say it comes from everywhere and casts no shadow. It is bright — surprisingly bright; the overcast is thin and high, and the sun is somewhere behind it — but it has no direction. The black rock takes the light and gives back a soft gleam wherever it is wet, which is everywhere, so the whole plateau has a low, even shine. The pools are mirrors with nothing to reflect but white. The green of the fern islands is very green.

And it does not change. This is the last thing you understand about the place, and it takes the longest, because you have to wait for it. The cloud does not lift or lower. The seepage does not increase or slacken; the pools stay at their brim. The channels carry the same thread of water from the same notch. The mosses are the same wetness. No wind comes across the flow to move the fern fronds. Nothing grows visibly, nothing dies, nothing falls, because nothing is here to fall and the rock is too young to shed. The town has reached the shape it was going to reach and holds it. Somewhere the water enters the plateau from above, and somewhere below the cloud it leaves, and between those two places it has found a course through the stone and the channels and the cisterns that satisfies it exactly, and there is no reason for anything to be otherwise. You could come back in a year. The drip you were listening to would be the one you left.
