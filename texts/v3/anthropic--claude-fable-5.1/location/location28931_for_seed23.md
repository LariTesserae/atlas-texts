It is a bowl. That is the first thing, and it never stops being the first thing: a high shelf of dark rock, scooped, walled on three sides, open on the fourth to a drop and to sky, and you can walk from one wall to the other in the time it takes to eat something. The walls are wet. All of them, everywhere, from the lip down to the floor, a thin film of water moving over the rock so continuously that the rock looks varnished. In places it gathers into threads. In places it breathes out as mist and hangs. The stone underneath is almost black, and the water on it is almost white, because the light here is not a thing that arrives from one direction. It comes off the snowfields above the rim, off the sky, off every wet surface, and it fills the bowl like a liquid of its own. There is no shadow that lasts. You squint. You keep squinting. After a while you realize your eyes are not going to adjust, because there is nothing to adjust to; the brightness is the condition.

It is cold in a way that has nothing to do with the light. The air is thin and does not hold heat, and the water on the walls is a degree from freezing and always will be. Ice forms at the edges of things — the undersides of ledges, the north face of a stone — and melts, and forms again, within the same hour. You can watch a rim of ice grow along a crack and then retreat, like something breathing.

The floor of the bowl is a settlement, and the settlement is the floor. There is no bare ground left. Every part of the bowl that is not weeping wall is stacked stone: low walls, higher walls, rooms roofed with slabs, rooms roofed with hide gone stiff and pale, passages a shoulder wide, ledges where one house has been built on the back of another. It is dense the way a rookery is dense. Nothing is more than an arm's length from anything else. And it is dry-stone throughout — no mortar, no timber except a few grey poles that came from somewhere very far below and are treated as precious — because there is nothing here to make mortar with and no fire hot enough to burn lime, and because, you come to understand, mortar would be pointless.

The ground moves.

Not fast. Not in a way you could see if you stood still and stared at one spot. But the whole floor of the bowl is saturated, the water table is at the surface or above it, and the freeze-and-thaw works the gravel under the stones the way a tongue works a loose tooth. A wall that was level at dawn has a lean by midday. A room floor humps. Water that came out of the ground in one place stops, and starts, clear and cold, from under a doorstep on the other side of the bowl. Somewhere a slab roof slides a hand's breadth and the room below it is open to the sky. Somewhere else the gravel settles and a whole passage drops half a step, and the walls on either side, no longer supported by each other, fold gently inward. This is happening everywhere, all the time. The settlement is never finished and never was, and every wall in it is being taken down and put back up by someone, or by something, at any given moment. It has the energy of an anthill. If you could see it from above and speed it up, it would seethe.

And it is nearly silent.

That is the strangeness that takes longest to accept. All this motion, and the sound of it is: drip. The click of a stone set on a stone, carefully. Water running under gravel, which is less a sound than a pressure in the ears. The thin air carries nothing. The wet rock absorbs what the air does carry. The people — there are people, you see them everywhere once you know how to look, crouched in the passages with a stone in both hands, moving between rooms with a slab across their shoulders — do not talk while they work, and they are always working. If they speak at all it is a syllable, low, to the person next to them, and the sound falls flat on the wet stone and is gone. There is no wind in the bowl. There is no wind because the walls are too high and the bowl too small, and the water on the walls does not fall so much as slide.

The stones are the second thing you notice, after the light. They are all rounded. Not river-rounded — river stones are round the way eggs are round — but rounded the way a tool handle is, the way a stair is worn where the foot lands. Every stone in the settlement has been lifted and set down so many times, by so many hands, over so many centuries, that it has taken the shape of being handled. They fit the palm. They fit each other, loosely, in the several ways each one has learned to fit. A child could find the place where a stone goes. There are no new stones; the walls of the bowl are too hard and too wet to quarry, and nothing comes up from below. What is here has always been here and has been the whole town a thousand times over.

Everything living here is in the walls. The gaps between stones are wet and cold and out of the light, and so they are full: pale salamanders with red gills, packed two and three to a crevice, that do not move when you look at them; a kind of small dark frog whose skin is always beaded with water and whose eyes are the only thing in the bowl that does not glitter; snails with shells worn thin as fingernail, grazing something invisible off the wet stone; and birds — small, black, red-legged, in a number that seems impossible for the space — that stand on every wall-top and roof-edge and ledge, close as a row of stitches, and shift and resettle as the walls beneath them shift and resettle. They do not call. When a slab slides they rise all together in a soft blur and come down somewhere else, and that is the loudest thing that happens all day, the sound of several hundred wings folding.

The people rebuild around the animals, not through them. You see a woman lift a stone from a leaning wall, look into the gap it leaves, and choose a different stone from the pile because a salamander is in the first place she meant to set it. This is not sentiment; you sense it is closer to method. The frogs move before the ground does. A room whose frogs have left is a room whose floor is about to hump, and the people watch for that. The birds crowd thickest on the walls that will stand longest. Nobody explains this. Nobody explains anything. But when a passage begins to drop, there is already someone at each end of it with their hands out, waiting for the stones to lean into them.

Water wells up in the middle of a room and the room is let go. Its walls are taken apart in an hour, stone by stone, and carried — no farther than a few steps, there is nowhere to go — and stacked into the walls of the rooms beside it, which thicken, and the new spring runs clear and shallow over the place where the floor was and the salamanders come out into it and lie there, red gills open, in the terrible light. By afternoon the spring will have found another way out and the gravel will be firm again, and someone will start a wall there. Not the same wall. Something.

There are things that do not fit. Frost that forms on a wall-top in the shape of the wall that stood there yesterday, and melts. A pool that holds still enough, in all this motion, that the light passes into it and comes out the other side hotter, and the stone beneath it is warm to the hand — the only warm thing in the bowl — and the frogs know about it and crowd it and the people never build there. A slab that has been the roof of a room for as long as anyone alive remembers, that has never slid, that the birds will not stand on.

Toward the open side of the bowl the walls thin, the passages widen, the stacking goes low and loose and then gives out entirely, and there is the lip: a few strides of bare wet rock, then air. From there you can see nothing below but light coming up through cloud. Behind you the settlement leans and drips and remakes itself without a sound, every wall in it a decision made a hundred times a day, every stone in it older than the shape of the town, and the birds shift on the roof-lines like a single black hem being pulled through wet cloth, and nothing ends and nothing stays.
