You come up onto it from below, through a scree that has not moved in a thousand years, and the first thing you notice is that you can no longer hear your own boots.

The place is a bowl set into a shoulder of the mountain — a few hundred paces across, walled on three sides by dark rock and open on the fourth to a drop that goes down into cloud. The rock is a basalt gone brown with age, its faces worn to a smoothness that suggests wind rather than water, because there has never been water here. The air will tell you that before your eyes do. It is warm, much warmer than it should be at this height, and so dry that your lips split within the hour and the split does not bleed; it just opens, like a seam in old leather. Nothing in this bowl has been wet in living memory. Nothing in it needs to be.

The floor of the bowl is riddled with vents. They are small — most no wider than a fist — and they exhale. The mountain is breathing out something warm and heavy and faintly sweet, a gas that hangs in the low places and makes the light go amber and thick. This is why the place is dim even at midday: the bowl fills like a cup with its own exhalation, and the sun comes down through it as though through smoked glass. Shapes lose their edges. Distances lie. You will not see the far wall clearly until you are standing at it.

And out of the vents grow the pipes.

They are the vegetation here, and they are lush in the sense that they are enormous and thriving and there is nothing else — not a moss, not a lichen, not a blade — but they are not close together. Each pipe stands alone above its vent with a wide skirt of bare rock around it, like trees in a park that someone has been tending for a very long time. Perhaps two hundred of them in the whole bowl. They are hollow, pale, the color of old bone or antler, and they rise from the vents as a single stalk that thickens and branches only near the top, where the branches themselves are hollow and open-ended. Some are the height of a person. The oldest are three or four times that, and they lean, and they have the ridged, weathered look of things that have been standing in a hard wind for longer than the wind has been keeping count.

They do not photosynthesize, or not much. They eat the mountain's breath. The gas goes up through them and something in their pale walls takes what it needs and lets the rest out through the open branches — and this is where the noise comes from, because the mountain does not breathe evenly.

It breathes in surges. There is a pressure somewhere below, some slow chamber that fills and spills, and every few minutes — never the same interval twice — the vents all strengthen at once, and the two hundred pipes speak.

It is not a chord. There is no attempt at harmony in it. Each pipe has its own bore and its own length and its own history of damage, and each one sounds at whatever pitch its body allows: shrieks, hoots, a bass moan from the great leaning elders that you feel in your sternum before your ears register it, a chattering from the young ones that is almost a rattle. The surge lasts perhaps twenty seconds and the sound of it fills the bowl to its brim and comes back off the walls redoubled. You cannot speak through it. You cannot think through it, the first several times. Then the surge ebbs and the pipes trail off unevenly, the elders last, and there is a quiet that is not really quiet — a hiss from every vent, the ticking of stalks cooling and contracting — and then it builds again.

The surges are what make the pipes grow. A pipe grows only while it is sounding. You can see it, if you stand close to a young one: in the twenty seconds of a surge the open ends of its branches extend, visibly, a finger's width, pushing out new pale material that is soft for a moment and then sets. A strong surge might add a hand's breadth to a healthy pipe. And the material is brittle once set, and the growth is not even, and so the tall pipes are always in the process of coming apart. A branch grows too long for its wall and snaps during a surge and falls with a hollow crack you can hear even through the din. A whole crown will shear off an elder in a high wind and go bounding down the bowl, ringing like a dropped bell, and by the next day the raw stump will have begun to grow new branches at new angles and the pipe's voice will be different — higher, or split into two notes where there had been one.

So the bowl is never the same shape twice. Fallen sections lie everywhere on the bare rock between the standing pipes, and they weather quickly, going from bone to chalk to a fine white grit that drifts against the walls and fills the cracks. Walk across it and you are walking on a thousand years of collapsed music. The whole place is in a fever of making and unmaking, and yet it has plainly been doing exactly this since before anything looked at it, and it has settled into the doing the way a river settles into its bed.

The animals live in the noise.

There are a great many of them for such a small place, and you see them mostly during the surges, because that is when they move. The most common are the reed-mice — that is what you would call them, though they are not mice; they are something with too many joints and a skin like gray suede that clings to the pipe stalks and gnaws them. They eat the fresh growth while it is still soft, in the seconds after it extrudes, which means they must be at the branch-tips during the surge, and they are, dozens of them on a single elder, riding the shrieking crown like sailors on a mast in a gale. When the surge ends they go still, flattened against the stalk, and you can look directly at one and not see it.

There are things that eat the reed-mice: long, low, six-legged, with wide flat heads and no eyes that you can find. They hunt by sound, and they hunt during the surges, when their prey is moving, and they seem to be able to hear the difference between a pipe's voice and a pipe's voice with something scrabbling on it. They are silent themselves. You will see one cross the bare rock toward a sounding pipe at a flat run, and the surge ends, and it stops as though switched off, and stands there in the hiss, waiting.

There are swarms, too — small hard-bodied flying things, glittering, that rise out of the fallen debris when a surge begins and pour up through the open branches of the pipes and out the top, riding the exhalation. Thousands at once. They give the pipes a second voice during the loud parts, a shimmering rasp above the fundamental. What they are doing in there no one has worked out. Feeding on the pipe's interior, perhaps. Breeding in the warm dark of the bore. When the gas slackens they fall back down through the pipes and out onto the rock in a glittering rain, and the flat-headed hunters eat some of them, and the reed-mice eat some, and the rest crawl back into the white grit and wait.

Higher up, on the rock walls, things with membranes hang in the crevices, and drop and glide during the surges, and you never see them clearly. Something large lives at the open edge of the bowl where the drop begins, and comes up in the evenings when the light goes from amber to bronze; you know it only by the way the reed-mice go quiet on the pipes nearest the edge, and by a track in the grit like something heavy dragging a fringe.

And there are the marks of people, though not many.

Someone has been here. Not lately, perhaps, or perhaps lately and briefly, because whoever comes to this place cannot stay: the dryness would kill them in days, and there is nothing here to eat that a person could eat. But they came, and they came back, and they did work.

There is a stone hut against the north wall, small enough that two people could not lie down in it without touching, built of the brown basalt fitted dry without mortar, its roof a single slab that someone must have levered up from below with great labor. The doorway faces into the bowl. Sit in it during a surge and the sound is somehow worse — focused, the hut acting as an ear.

There are cairns. Eleven of them, scattered widely across the bowl, each one built on the bare rock beside a particular pipe. The pipes they mark are not the largest, or the oldest. Stand by a cairn and wait for a surge and listen to its pipe, and you begin to understand: these are the ones with a *true* voice. Whatever that means. A steady tone that does not waver in the middle of the surge, or a particular interval between the pipe's two notes, or a pitch that carries clean over the rest of the din. Someone came here and listened to two hundred screaming things and chose eleven and marked them, and the marks have outlasted the choosing, because several of the cairns now stand beside stumps, or beside pipes that have plainly regrown into a wholly different voice, and nobody has moved the stones.

And there are the cuttings. Along the base of the west wall lie sections of fallen pipe that have been worked — cut clean at the ends with something hard and sharp, the bore scraped smooth inside with a tool that left fine spiral marks, holes bored along the length at careful intervals. Some are finished. Some were abandoned mid-work, and a few have gone to chalk and crumble when you touch them. A stone chisel lies among them, its edge dulled. A coil of cord, some kind of gut, brittle as straw. They were making instruments. They came to this shrieking place at the top of the world, where nothing can drink and everything is coming apart, and they sat in the din and sawed at its bones to make something they could carry down and play.

You will think about that on the way down. But not while you are there. While you are there you will mostly be waiting, as everything in the bowl waits, for the next breath — feeling it come up through the rock a moment before you hear it, the vents deepening their hiss, the reed-mice already scrambling for the branch-tips, the swarm lifting out of the grit like a thrown handful of glass — and then the pipes open their throats, all of them, and the dim gold air goes solid with sound, and for twenty seconds the place is entirely itself, and you are only something standing in it.
