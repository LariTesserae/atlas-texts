The first thing you learn is that you have to stand still for a long time before there is anything to see at all.

It is a hollow under a warm country. Somewhere above, there is a plateau of dry rock where the sun does the work of a kiln for half the year and the wind does the rest, and long ago the ground gave way into an old gas pocket in the basalt and the roof came down in one piece, and settled, and stayed. What remains is a chamber the size of a barn, or a small chapel: perhaps twenty paces across, the ceiling a little more than twice a person's height at its peak and sagging toward the edges until the walls and floor meet in a curved seam you could run your hand along. It is enclosed. There is one way in, a slot at the eastern end where the rockfall left a gap, and the passage beyond it turns twice before it reaches anything like day, so that what arrives here is not light but the memory of light — a greyness you can perceive only with the edges of your eyes, and only after your eyes have forgotten what they were made for.

The rock is dark. It was dark before it was buried; it is the rusted black-brown of a place that cooled fast from great heat. Its surface is not smooth. The pressure of the fall left long conchoidal shells where sections sheared off, and those shells have a faint waxy sheen that catches the not-light and gives it back as something even fainter. Where the walls have stood a long time undisturbed, a skin of mineral has crept over them — a white bloom, powdery to the touch, that tastes of chalk and metal if you are foolish enough to lick a finger. This is where the water is. Not standing, not dripping, but resident in the rock the way salt is resident in the sea. The air is dry enough to crack lips. And yet, if you lay your palm flat against the north wall and hold it there, after a while your skin comes away with the very faintest impression of coolness, of exchange, as though the stone has been drinking in the dark for ten thousand years at a rate too slow to notice.

The floor is dust. It is the fine, dun-coloured dust that comes from rock that has been ground against itself and then never disturbed; it lies in a level sheet with no ripple in it, no track, no scatter. When you take a step it rises around your ankle and hangs, and is still hanging there when you look back an hour later, because there is nothing to move it. This is the character of the place. Whatever falls here lands where it lands and remains.

The warmth is the second thing. It is not the warmth of a fire or of sunlight but the average of all the days above, worked down through the rock and released without variation: the body-heat of the land. It is the temperature of a held hand. It does not rise or fall with the season, and if there is a day and a night up there, this room does not know it. Sit long enough and the boundary between your skin and the air becomes hard to locate.

There are living things, few and strange. From the seam in the ceiling near the east end, where a crack lets the plateau's shrubs send their roots questing down for whatever moisture the deep rock hoards, three or four roots have broken through and hang in the air like ropes left behind. They are pale — not white, but the bleached grey-gold of things that have never needed colour — and they are dry and papery to the touch and have sent out fine lateral threads that lie against the rock and follow the mineral bloom, drinking whatever it gives. They do not sway. There is nothing to sway them. In the dust beneath them, at the drip-line that never drips, a single seed has come down at some point and germinated on the strength of its own reserves and grown a stalk as long as a forearm, colourless and thin as a wick, with two leaves the size of thumbnails folded shut. It is not dead. It is not going to grow. It is simply in the state a plant takes when it has spent everything and can neither move forward nor fall. It may have been like that for a year. It may have been like that for twenty.

Across the chamber, as far from the roots as the room allows, there is a second thing: a low crust of lichen on the wall at the height of a knee, dry-grey, brittle, no bigger than a spread hand, of the kind that lives on almost nothing and grows by a hair's width in a human lifetime. It is the one clear evidence that this place has a history — that it has been just like this for long enough for the slowest living thing to have found it, settled, and made no hurry.

Nothing else moves. There are no insects. No bats use the passage, because the passage leads to a plateau with nothing on it to eat. The dust bears no track but the ones you make. The silence is nearly total, and you notice, after a while, that it is a silence with a floor to it: your own blood, an interior tide you have never heard before, and beneath that a stillness so complete that the rock itself seems to be listening. Once, perhaps twice in a long watch, there is a tick — a grain of the mineral bloom letting go of the wall and falling to the dust — and it is as loud as a dropped coin.

At the eastern end, near the entrance, half sunk in the dust with only its spine showing, there is a stone that does not belong to the basalt. It is a fist-sized piece of grey flint, and one edge of it has been struck: three flakes taken off in a row, a clean working edge that has never been used, or was used once and set down. There is no other mark of anyone. No ash, no bone, no scratch on the wall. Whoever carried it in did so with it in their hand and left with their hand empty, and the dust has closed over the place where they knelt, and whether that was last spring or before the roots came through the ceiling there is no way at all to know. It is the only object in the room that is not exactly where the world put it, and it is very nearly there.

This is a place that has finished happening. The cooling has cooled; the fall has fallen; the water has gone as far into the rock as water goes. Everything present has arrived at the condition it will keep. To stand in it is to be the only thing there with a temperature of its own, the only thing raising dust, the only clock — and to feel how quickly a room like this would absorb that, too, if you stayed: the breath slowing to the rock's, the eyes ceasing to search, the small commotion of a person subsiding, grain by grain, into a floor that has been waiting all along to be level.
