Start with the fact that it is finished.

Whatever raised this highland—continental collision, mantle plume, or some deliberate act now long past caring about—did its work and stopped, and then the wearing-down did its work and stopped too. The plateau stands a very long way above what was once sea level, so high that the sky at noon goes to a dark plum color overhead and only pales to a thin bluish white near the horizon, where the last of the air is. Nothing rises above the surface anymore. There are no peaks, no scarps, no gullies. The rivers that might have cut it have not existed for longer than the rock remembers. It is not a plain so much as an ending: the shape a landmass takes when there is nothing left to take from it and nowhere lower for anything to go.

The surface is pale. Not the white of salt but the grey-fawn of ground feldspar and something finer, a mineral flour with a faint green cast in low sun. If you were to kneel and scoop it up it would run between your fingers with almost no weight and leave a metallic smell on the skin, like a coin held too long. It is very cold to the touch, and yet the air—thin as it is—is only cool, a mountain-spring cool, and this is the first small wrongness. At this altitude the air should be lethal. It is not. Something beneath the plateau is keeping the world a few degrees warmer than physics wants it, and has been doing so for so long that the warmth has become part of the geology.

It is quiet here in a way that lowland places cannot be. The air is too thin to carry much. You hear the sound of your own blood, the creak of a strap, the occasional small dry tick from the ground that might be a grain settling or might be something else. A stone dropped from waist height lands with a sound like a whisper heard through a wall. Distance does not sound like anything at all.

---

And yet it is never still.

This is the thing that takes a while to see, because the eye expects a plateau to be static and a plateau this old to be more static than most. But look at the middle distance for a minute without moving and you will notice that the ground has changed. A low ridge that was there is not. A dark patch has migrated forty meters to the left. The surface at the edge of sight is smoking—not with heat, but with a fine moving haze that rises a hand's width and falls again.

The mineral flour is in continual motion. It should not be. There is barely enough atmosphere to lift it, and the wind, when there is wind, is a slow cold pressure that would not stir a feather at sea level. But the grains move anyway, and not in the way sand moves. They gather. They sort themselves by some property that is not size or weight—a grain will leave one heap and cross open ground to join another, alone, trailing a line as fine as a hair. They build. Over the course of an hour a flat area the size of a courtyard will raise itself into a set of concentric low walls, or a shallow spiral, or a series of parallel ridges too regular to be dunes, each one a hand high and sharp-edged as if cast. Then, with no signal you can detect, the structure will lose its conviction. The edges soften. The walls slump and pour back into the general surface, and something else begins to assemble a hundred meters away.

You could stand at one place for a day and watch a hundred of these things rise and fail and never see the same form twice. It is a landscape in a state of continuous, silent turbulence, a storm that has forgotten how to make noise, a city that keeps trying to found itself and cannot commit. From a height—and there are no heights, but from the memory of one—the whole plateau would look like a slow boil. At the scale of a person it looks like a thing breathing.

Nobody knows what the grains are, exactly. The reasonable guess is that they are what is left of something built: the ground-down residue of an engineering that operated at a scale below sight and above understanding, spread now across a continent by ages of thin wind. The material has been reduced past its purpose but not past its habits. Each grain still wants to be near certain other grains and away from others. Each still carries some vanishingly small preference for one arrangement over another. Multiplied across a landmass, these preferences produce weather. That is all it is, probably. The turbulence has no intent left in it. It is the twitching of a very large and very old thing that was made to do something, and has forgotten what, and cannot stop trying.

---

Water exists here in the way rumor exists. In the last hour before dawn, in the shadow of the rare fixed objects, a frost will form—a grey fur no thicker than breath on glass. It never melts. When the sun touches it, it goes straight to vapor and the vapor is gone before it has finished rising. No pool, no seep, no damp. The grains are afraid of it: where frost has been, the mineral flour draws back for the rest of the day and leaves a bare patch of the underlying rock, dark and glassy and unbelievably smooth, like the floor of a place where something heavy sat for a very long time.

Life clings to the frost line the way life clings to anything. Around the bases of the standing objects, wherever the shadow falls the same way each morning, there is a crust. Black-green, brittle, thinner than paper, it grows in rings that follow the shadow's edge and stop where it stops. Whether it is a lichen, or an algae, or something that was once a machine and is now merely alive, is not obvious and does not much matter. It takes a century to grow a centimeter. Some of the rings are wide enough that the objects they surround must have been standing since before the plateau finished flattening. There is nothing else that photosynthesizes here. Everything green on this world is a shadow's margin.

---

The standing objects are the only things that break the horizon, and there are very few of them. You can walk for three days and see none, and then see two in an afternoon.

They are not ruins in the ordinary sense. Ruins imply a building that collapsed, and these did not collapse; they were worn, over a length of time that would have taken the mountains down around them, and they are still here. So they were made of something harder than mountains. Most are plinths—square or round, chest-high, their edges rounded to a soapstone softness by the grain-wind. A few are taller: a stump of a column three stories high with a fluted surface polished to mirror on its windward face. One is a shallow dish set flush into the rock, four meters across, so precisely level that the frost pools in it a tenth of a millimeter deep and reads the sky like a lens for the one minute before it burns off.

The grains avoid all of them. Not by much—a ring of bare rock half a meter wide—but consistently, everywhere, as if the objects are still saying something the material can hear.

Then there are the lines. You do not see them from standing height; they are in the rock itself, not on it, and the rock is under the moving flour most of the time. But when a bare patch opens you sometimes find a groove, a hand wide and a finger deep, ruler-straight, running under the flour in both directions to the limits of what has been exposed. Some of them have been followed. The longest that anyone has traced without losing it ran for two hundred and eleven kilometers and did not bend. Where two of them meet, they meet at right angles. What the grid was for, there is no way of saying. It is older than the flattening. It was cut into the rock when the rock was still under something else, and the something else is gone, and the lines remain, a diagram with nothing left to diagram.

Occasionally the flour gives up a smaller object. A hinge, intact, made of a metal that has no fingerprint of oxidation on it. A lens the size of a fist, perfectly clear, that shows through itself a view very slightly displaced from the view around it, as though it were looking at the same place a few seconds ago. Loose things like these seem to circulate. The same lens has been found twice, eighty kilometers apart, a decade between.

---

There are animals. This is not the same as saying you will see one.

The commonest sign is a track: a wide, shallow smear across the flour, a meter across and so featureless that it might be wind, except that it runs straight through the assembling structures and the structures do not reform behind it for several hours. Whatever leaves it is flat and heavy and moves at night. The one person who has described seeing the maker of the tracks described it as a piece of the ground that stood up, and was not sure, afterward, that they had seen anything. It is grey-fawn. It has no visible eyes. It moves the way the structures move, in slow reorganizations rather than steps, and it seems to eat the structures—or rather, to pass over them at the moment they collapse, and to be slightly larger afterward. It is the only thing here that appears to profit from the turbulence.

There is also something in the sky. Very high, in the last of the air, a shape sometimes crosses the sun: long, thin, unhurried, never lower. It does not flap. It has never been seen to land, and there is nowhere for it to land that is not covered in restless mineral flour. Perhaps it does not need to. Perhaps it is not an animal. It is counted as fauna because it moves against the wind and because it is easier to think of it that way.

Both of these things are so rare that most who have walked here for months have seen neither. The plateau is empty in a way that is nearly total. You can stand at the center of a circle a hundred kilometers across and be the only thing in it with a pulse.

---

The rules, as far as they can be made out:

The ground is always moving and nothing ever gets anywhere. The flour rises into forms and the forms fail and the failure feeds the next forms; the net change over a year is nothing; the net change over ten thousand years is nothing. It is a turbulence held perfectly in place. The plateau finished a very long time ago and this is how finished things behave here.

The fixed things are fixed absolutely. What was made hard enough to outlast the erosion of a continent does not shift, tilt, or weather further. It is simply done. The flour respects it. The frost gathers on its shadow side. The crust grows around it a century at a time.

Warmth comes from below and is not explained. Somewhere under the rock, deep enough that no groove reaches it, something maintains this highland at a temperature at which frost can form and a person can breathe, thinly, for a while. It has been doing this since before the lines were cut. It does not appear to be for anyone. It is possible that the whole plateau—the flour, the plinths, the grid, the warmth—is a single ancient instrument, worn down to its last few functions, still running because no one told it not to.

Sound does not travel, and this is a mercy. If you could hear the flour you would hear a continent grinding itself, softly, forever.

---

Late in the day the light goes long and amber and the assembling structures throw shadows for the first time, dozens of them across the plain, spirals and walls and ridges each casting its own dark copy, and for a few minutes the plateau looks like what it might once have been—a settlement, a field of instruments, a plan in the process of being executed. Then the sun goes under the edge and the shadows go with it and the structures, which cannot be seen collapsing in the dark, collapse anyway. By moonrise the plain is smooth again to the horizon except where the plinths stand in their rings of bare rock, holding the frost, waiting on nothing, older than the shape of the world beneath them.
