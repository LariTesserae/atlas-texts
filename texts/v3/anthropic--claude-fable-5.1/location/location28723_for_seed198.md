You come to it from above, which is the only way anyone comes to it, over a saddle where the cloud sits down on the road like a heavy animal that will not be moved. The road itself is the first sign. It is not laid on the mountain; it is the mountain. Slabs of dark ashlar, each one a different age, patched with brick, patched again with a pale pozzolanic concrete, the whole surface dished by centuries of iron-shod wheels and then re-crowned, and re-dished, and re-crowned. There is no soil at the roadside. There is a wall, and on top of the wall another wall, and the walls sweat.

Then the cloud thins for a moment and you see the valley.

It is not a valley. That is the first thing to understand and it takes a while. Whatever fold in the rock was here originally has been buried so long that nobody keeps a picture of it. What you are looking down into is a bowl perhaps six miles across, its every surface built — terraced, stepped, vaulted, buttressed, cut through with conduits — the terraces so continuous and so old that they read from a distance as strata, as though the whole basin were a geology of masonry. The lowest levels are a thousand feet below you. The highest run up under the cloud on the far side and vanish, still climbing. There is not one square yard of it that was not put there by hand, and not one square yard of it that is bare.

---

The water arrives as weather. It rarely rains hard here; instead the cloud comes over the rim in long slow pours, cold, and settles on everything, and everything condenses it. Every ledge, every cornice, every cable and stanchion is beaded. The walls weep in sheets so thin they look like varnish. Where two walls meet there is always a runnel, and the runnel always goes somewhere — a slot, a spout, a shaped stone lip that hands it off to the next level down. The whole basin is a catchment. That is its purpose and has been its purpose for so long that the purpose has become the shape.

The masonry carries the water to the cisterns. The cisterns are the great dark vaults you glimpse through arched openings at the back of each terrace — you can hear them before you see them, or rather you can hear their particular kind of silence, the cold hum a large volume of still water makes in an enclosed space. From the cisterns the water goes down penstocks, cast-iron pipes as wide as a cart, riveted, painted a red that has weathered to the color of liver, half-swallowed in moss. And at the bottom of each penstock is a wheelhouse, and in each wheelhouse a turbine turns.

Six hundred of them, somebody will tell you, or nine hundred; the number depends on how you count and nobody counts the same way twice, because the number changes.

---

Everything here is green with something. The stone under the moss is grey-black basalt block and a paler dressed limestone that must have been carried up from a very long way off, but you mostly infer this; what you see is moss, deep as a mattress on the horizontal surfaces, and liverwort, and filmy fern in the vertical shade, and on every terrace the cultivated things — tea, or something like it, low and dense and glossy; ranks of a broad-leaved crop that catches the drip; small twisted trees espaliered flat against the retaining walls to leave room. There is no room. That is the second thing to understand. Where a pipe passes through a terrace, the plants have grown around the pipe and the pipe has been re-laid around the plants and the two have settled into a relationship. Where a stair ascends, its treads are worn at the center and forested at the edges. Roots go into mortar. Mortar goes onto roots. In the older quarters the difference between construction and vegetation is a matter of speed rather than kind.

The light is what a wet north-facing forest gets at four in the afternoon, and it is like that at noon. The cloud takes most of the sun and the depth of the bowl takes the rest. Down in the low levels the daylight is a grey-green residue, and the electric lamps — bare filaments under enamel shades, strung on catenary wire along every terrace face — are on all day. They are not bright. They were never meant to be bright. They are just enough to show the edge of the step in front of you and the glitter of wet on the wall. From the rim, looking down into the cloud, they make a constellation that shifts as the fog moves, so that the valley seems to breathe light.

---

You expect noise. This much industry — the penstocks, the wheels, the mills that the wheels drive, the foundries, the belt-shops, the winding-houses that haul cargo up the incline planes — should be a roar. It is not. The first reason is the water: it is all enclosed, moving in pipe and culvert and stone conduit, and stone-borne water makes a hush, not a rush. The second reason is the moss, which upholsters every surface and eats sound like felt. The third is the fog. The fourth is a matter of policy or perhaps of habit so old it has become policy: the machinery is built heavy, slow, and tight. Wheels turn at forty revolutions a minute. Belting is leather and rubber, not chain. Cams are bronze. What escapes from a wheelhouse is a low compound note, more felt in the sternum than heard, and it comes at you from six hundred directions at once so that it has no source at all. It is the sound of the ground.

Over this, small distinct sounds carry a long way. A dropped spanner three terraces below. Water landing in a cistern from a height, a single note repeated at irregular intervals. Someone's cough. The click of a lamp coming on. A bell — always a bell somewhere, one stroke, meaning a gate has opened or closed.

---

And here is the thing that the stillness hides, that you begin to notice only after an hour on the rim, or a day: it is all moving.

Not the cloud, though that moves too, pouring over the far ridge and down the terraces in the way of very slow water, so that whole quarters disappear and reappear while you watch. Not the lamps. The valley. The works themselves.

The water can only go through one channel at a time, and there are always more channels than water. So the gates are always changing. A sluice on the fourth level closes; the terrace below it goes from a sheet of drip to a damp wall in a quarter of an hour; the penstock it fed goes silent; the wheelhouse stops; the mill that depended on the wheelhouse goes dark; and forty men and women who were working in it are already crossing a footbridge toward the next wheelhouse over, where a gate has opened and the wheel is beginning to turn. Nothing is idle for long. Nothing runs for long either. The whole basin is a machine for shuffling a finite volume of falling water through an enormous excess of places for it to fall, and the shuffling never stops.

This is why the number of turbines changes. Wheelhouses are dismantled when the water leaves them for too long — the bronze and iron are worth too much to sit — and rebuilt somewhere the water has gone. Terraces are cut back where a conduit needs to pass and built out where it has been abandoned. There is scaffolding up on perhaps a fifth of the visible surface at any moment: bamboo poles lashed with tarred cord, very old-fashioned against the electric lamps, greened with moss on the side that faces the drip. Cranes — long timber jibs on masonry pivots, cable-hauled — swing masonry blocks from one place to another. Cargo cars on the incline planes go up empty and down full and then up full and down empty, and it is not always clear which is which. Whole stairways are closed and re-routed, so that the way you came down in the morning is not there in the afternoon and a new one has been opened with a chalk mark on the wall.

It is not disorder. From above it looks like disorder, an anthill kicked, a bazaar viewed from a rooftop — thousands of small figures in oilcloth moving on every level, never in the same direction, never resting. But every one of those movements is the completion of something: this gate closes so that one opens; this block is taken down so it can go up over there. The turbulence is the equilibrium. The valley has been like this for as long as it has been a valley, and the churn is what has kept it standing. A terrace that is left alone here does not persist; the drip undermines it, the roots pry it, and it fails. A terrace that is taken down and rebuilt every thirty years lasts forever.

The old quarters are not the ones that have been let alone. They are the ones that have been rebuilt the most.

---

There are almost no animals. This surprises you until you think about it: there is no ground. There are no burrows to dig and no dead wood to rot and no still water outside the cisterns, which are sealed. Birds pass over the rim on their way somewhere else and do not descend. In the tea rows there are small brown moths that never seem to fly, and in the cistern mouths a kind of pale cricket. Once a day, perhaps, you see a cat on a wall — there must be a small population, kept for the granaries, but they are as rare as saints. What lives here is people and plants and the low continuous turning of the wheels, and the fog.

---

Rules, as far as they can be given.

Water is never wasted, and never stored for long. Everything is designed for it to be passing through.

Nothing is owned by the terrace it stands on. A wheelhouse belongs to the water that feeds it, and when the water goes, so does the claim. This makes for a curiously placid people; they have moved too often to be attached to any one wall.

The moss is never scraped. It is understood to be structural. You will occasionally see someone trimming it back from a lamp or a gauge-glass, with the care of a barber.

Every surface must shed water to a surface below it. A pool on a flat is a fault, and someone will come.

There is no bottom. The lowest cistern sends its overflow into a tunnel that leaves the basin under the western wall, and where it comes out nobody in the valley has been.

There is no top either, or none that is finished. The terraces on the far side go up into the cloud, and the scaffolding goes up with them, and the sound of the wheels does not diminish as they climb.

---

Toward evening, if it can be called evening, the cloud comes down thicker and the lamps come on in earnest along the upper levels, and you can see the day-change working its way around the bowl: the bells going one stroke at a time, the gates changing, the dark quarters lighting and the lit ones dimming, the small figures crossing the footbridges in the mist. The basin is doing what it does. It has done it for so long that from the rim you might mistake it for something that had grown, if you did not know that nothing here has ever been allowed to.
