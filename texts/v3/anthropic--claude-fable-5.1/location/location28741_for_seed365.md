**The Shelf at Corrach**

It came down in a single night, within living memory: the whole eastern face of the mountain letting go and settling three hundred feet lower as a broken shelf, wide as a few fields, with a new cliff above it and a new cliff below. The rock still shows it. Nothing here is rounded. The slabs have edges you could cut bread with, faces the colour of wet slate where they split and pale, almost bone, where they haven't yet taken the weather. Between them, where the fall packed grit into the seams, moss has arrived in a hurry — deep, sodden cushions, and ferns unrolling from every crevice, and dwarf rowan and birch no taller than a person, growing crooked against a wind that seldom actually comes.

Cloud comes instead. The shelf sits at the altitude where the mountain wears its cloud, and so the place is always damp without often being rained on. Water forms on the stone. It gathers on the underside of every lintel and drops. It beads on spiderwebs and on the fur of things. The light is the light inside a pearl — grey, with green in it, without direction — and it rarely brightens past that. At midday you can read; at dawn and dusk you can't quite.

Someone built a town here. Or the beginnings of one, or the remains — it is hard to tell which. There are no more than thirty roofed structures, and yet they are arranged with the seriousness of a city: a straight main street with a gutter down the centre, cut into the bedrock and lined with fitted stone; side lanes stepped up the incline; a small square with a raised platform in it; walls that terrace the slope into flat plots; a cistern; two arches. Everything is dry-laid, stone on stone, no mortar, the joints so tight a knife won't go in. The roofs are slabs propped on corbels, or turf over birch poles pegged with wood. There is no metal anywhere that anyone has found — the doors hang on wooden pivots, the shutters are woven willow, the drain gratings are stone pierced with round holes that must have taken days apiece to bore. The construction is layered, too: you can see where an early low wall was built on top of, and that built on top of again, three generations of hands in a place that has existed for perhaps one.

The buildings stand well apart. Between them are the slabs and the moss and the gaps, and this spacing is what gives the place its odd hollowness, its feeling of a town drawn as a diagram. Nothing crowds anything else. You could walk down the main street with your arms out.

And yet it is full.

Every step of every stair has a salamander on it. Black, glossy, patient, lying in the film of water on the stone — one to a step, never two. Snails ascend the walls in slow columns, shells the size of a fist, their tracks glinting. Moths the colour of lichen rest flat on the lintels with their wings open, and there are so many that from a certain angle the doorframes appear to have a soft pale fur. A heron stands in the square on the raised platform. Another stands in the cistern. Hares sit upright in the doorways, exactly where a person would stand to look out at the weather. On the roofs, ptarmigan. In the roof-spaces, owls. Under the drain-covers, when you lift them, voles in their dozens and frogs that do not call — the altitude has taken their voices, or they never had them — and once, in the largest house, a stag, filling the single room entirely, its antlers scraping the underside of the slabs, regarding the door with no interest in leaving.

They do not run. That is the first thing. They turn to look, and then they turn back, and continue whatever they were doing, which is mostly nothing — being present, occupying a place. But their occupation is exact. You begin to notice it after an hour: one animal to a doorway, one to a step, one to a window-ledge, the moths spaced along the lintels at intervals as if measured. The town is not overrun. It is *inhabited*, and it is inhabited at a density that would be considered perfectly civil if the inhabitants were people. When a hare leaves a doorway another arrives to sit in it, sometimes within the minute, sometimes after an hour, but the doorway is never empty for long.

The silence is the second thing. There are hundreds — thousands — of living creatures within a stone's throw, and the loudest sound is water dropping from a corbel into the gutter. The cloud eats noise; that accounts for some of it. But the animals themselves make none. Hooves come down on wet rock without a click. Wings open and close and you do not hear them. Even the snails, which you would not expect to hear, seem to be doing their slow work in a deeper quiet than snails ordinarily manage. Now and then the shelf itself speaks — a *tock* from somewhere in the rock, the mountain still adjusting to what it did, a fresh chip falling from the new cliff above and landing in moss with a sound like a coin in a purse — and every creature in earshot lifts its head at once and then lowers it.

Things happen. That is the third thing, and it takes longest to see. The cloud comes through in tides, thick for an hour, thin for an hour, and the animals move with it: as it thickens, the salamanders climb one step higher, all of them, the whole staircase shifting up a tread; as it thins, they come back down. Rain arrives now and then, brief and fine, and the moths close their wings and the hares withdraw one body-length into the dark of the rooms, and then it stops and they come forward again. An owl leaves the roof-space of the second house and crosses the square and takes a vole from the drain and returns, and the vole does not cry out and the owl does not call and nothing else in the square so much as turns its head. Predation goes on. It goes on the way a transaction goes on in a market — expected, unremarked, part of the order.

And the walls grow. This is the last thing, and the one that will not settle. There is fresh stone on the shelf — a new course on the terrace wall by the cistern, the split faces still pale and unweathered, lichen not yet arrived. No one is seen laying it. There are no people, or if there are they keep to the cloud. Some who have come up and stayed a night on the shelf say they woke to find the low wall beside them a hand's breadth higher than it was, and every animal in the lane facing it. Others say this is a lie and the stone is simply young and the town was simply built by someone who left. The moss does not care which. It is already on the new courses, feeling its way into the joints, taking the fresh rock and beginning, very slowly, to make it old.
