The shelf sits about two-thirds of the way up the mountain, on the north side, where the cloud pools. From below you would not know it was there. From above, if there were an above, it would look like a thumbprint pressed into wet clay: a shallow bowl perhaps four hundred paces across, walled on three sides by rock and open on the fourth to a drop that the cloud fills like milk in a cup.

The rock is new. That is the first thing. It is grey-blue, close-grained, and every face is a fracture face — planes and edges where something recently came apart and has not yet been touched by frost or root or a single winter. The edges will cut a hand. There is no rounding anywhere, no grit collected in the corners, no lichen on the broken surfaces. The rock looks like it was split open this morning and it looks that way at every hour, because there are no hours here that differ from one another.

And it weeps. Water runs from every face in threads too fine to see except where they catch what light there is: a slow, continuous bleeding out of the stone itself, welling from hairline seams and gathering at the lips of ledges into drops that hang, swell, and fall. The floor of the bowl is a mesh of small dark ponds, each one ringed by moss, each one fed by a dozen of these threads. Every fern frond carries a row of beads along its edge. Every hair on every moss capsule holds a bead. The air is at the point of saturation and has been for as long as there has been air; breath comes out white and drifts a little way and then simply stays, a small cloud you leave behind you that does not disperse.

The light is the light inside a cloud: even, grey, sourceless. Nothing casts a shadow. Colours are present but dimmed, the way they are in the last quarter hour before dusk — except that the quarter hour does not end. It is cool enough that skin on the arms tightens. Not cold. The kind of cool that a cellar holds.

Given the altitude and the light, the growth is wrong. It is far too much. The moss is not a skin on the rock but a body of its own, a hand's depth, two hands' depth, a mattress of green so dense and wet that the foot sinks and the water rises around it. Ferns stand thigh-high from the moss, their fronds broad and glossy and dark, the kind of fern you expect in a lowland gorge and not on a mountain shelf where the cloud sits. Dwarf willow sprawls in places, and there are cushion plants in the wetter seams, and a low creeping thing with leaves like coins that has grown over the sharp rims of the rock without softening them at all. There is no dead matter. No brown frond, no fallen leaf, no rotting stem in the ponds. Nothing here has ever died, because nothing has had the time.

You hear the place before you take it in. You hear it, in fact, from some way down the mountain, and you assume at first that there must be a works of some kind up here — a mill, a pumping station, a generating hall — because the noise is that noise. A deep rhythmic thud on a fixed interval, felt in the chest. Over it a spinning whine that never varies in pitch. A hiss of released pressure, regular as breath. Metallic knocks, many of them, out of phase with one another so that they braid into a texture rather than a beat. The whole thing at the volume of a factory floor heard from the yard outside: loud enough that you would raise your voice, not so loud that you would cover your ears.

But there is nothing built here. Not a wall, not a pipe, not a peg. Not one straight line that the rock did not make itself when it broke. The bowl is untouched in the way that only a place no one has ever stood in is untouched. And the noise fills it from wall to wall.

It takes a while to find where the noise is. The eye keeps looking for the machine.

Then, at the edge of the nearest pond, something large and grey-green and glistening — the shape of a frog if a frog were the size of a sheepdog — inflates its throat. The sac swells, taut and pale, and the water level in the pond drops a finger's width. The sac collapses with a wet percussive thud, and the water rises again. Inflate. Drop. Collapse. Rise. The interval is exact. This is the thud you felt in your chest.

Once you have seen one, you begin to find the others. They are everywhere and they are very far apart.

On a slab of split rock thirty paces from the frog, a line of beetles the size of fists stands in a row, spaced as if measured, each one working its mandibles against the stone with a small hard clack, and each clack a fraction after the one beside it, so that the row produces the knocking. Nothing is being cut. The rock is not marked. The beetles have never moved along the slab. Further in, above one of the larger ponds, seven birds hang in the cloud in a slow ring, wings beating in unison, going round and round a centre where there is nothing. The whine comes from them — not from their throats but from the air they move through, which sings under the load. Around that pond the moss tips glow very faintly blue-white, and the air there tastes of a storm about to break, and the hairs on the arm stand up. A fish in the pond beneath them lies motionless with its gills flaring wide and slow, and each time they flare the water hisses.

There is a hare-like animal on the far slope, brown, hunched, that lifts and lowers one hind leg like a rod driving a wheel that isn't there. There are moths, hundreds of them, orbiting in tight columns above points where nothing is lit. There is something under the moss that heaves, once, every few breaths, and settles. There are, when you count, a great many animals — more animals than a shelf this size could feed in a year — and yet the bowl feels almost empty, because each one stands alone at its place with a wide clear space around it, the way parts stand in a hall that was designed for them. Between the stations there is only moss, and water, and cloud. No creature comes near another. No creature eats. None sleeps. None has moved from its position since it took it up. They are not living in this place; they are on shift here, and the shift has no end.

You want to ask what is being made. It is the obvious question in a place that sounds like this. Nothing is carried in. Nothing is stacked or piled or sent away. The ponds never fill despite the water pouring into them from every rock, and the rock never dries despite the water pouring out of it. Look closely at a weeping face and you will see that some of the threads run upward — beads climbing the stone against the pull of the mountain, rejoining the seams they came from, so that the water is not being lost but circulated, and the circulation is the work. Look at the fresh fracture surfaces and consider that they are fresh because they are the product: the shelf is manufacturing the moment of its own emergence, over and over, at a rate exactly equal to the rate at which the moment would otherwise pass. The frog pumps and the beetles knock and the birds spin and the current runs through the moss, and the result is that the rock is always newly broken and the fern is always unwilted and the drop is always about to fall. This is the output. It goes nowhere. It is consumed in the same instant by the place that made it. The equilibrium is total.

Other things follow from that, and you notice them slowly, because they are quiet and the noise is not.

A bird in the ring opens its beak and the call arrives a full second before it does, so that the sound is already in the air and the bird is only agreeing to it. A drop lets go of the fern and you see it fall and you see it still hanging where it was. If you press your hand into the moss and lift it, the moss is undented — not recovered but unpressed, as though your hand had been declined. The little cloud of your own breath, when you walk back through it, folds itself into your mouth again.

The far side of the shelf, across the drop, is where the cloud is thickest, and if you stand at the lip and wait, the cloud does not thin so much as simply run out, and behind it the mountain on the other side is not a mountain. It is a flat grey field, matte and featureless, the colour of the light, going up and down and outward as far as you can see: a surface waiting to be given a shape. The shelf ends where the rock ends, cleanly, at an edge as sharp as any other edge here. Beyond it nothing has been finished. Perhaps nothing has been started. The bowl is a small complete thing set into a large incomplete one.

The frog pumps. The pond falls and rises. The ring of birds goes round its empty centre and the moss glows at the tips and the beetles knock in their row and the hare drives its leg against nothing, and the rock weeps and drinks its own water, and the sound of all of it holds steady at the level of a works heard from the yard — and none of it is going anywhere, and none of it is behind, and none of it will stop, and the cool grey light lies over everything like a tarpaulin that has never once been lifted.
