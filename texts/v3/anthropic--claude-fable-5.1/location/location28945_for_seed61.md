There is a room under the hill that is full of one sound.

You come to it through a crack that has to be taken sideways, and for the last body-length the crack is already roaring, so that by the time you step out into the chamber your ears have given up on anything except the water. It falls from a slot in the ceiling — a slot no wider than a forearm — and it has been falling from that slot at that rate for so long that the fall is not an event. It is a column. Grey-white, thick as a thigh, unbroken, it drops the height of perhaps four people and lands in a basin it has drilled for itself, and the noise of that landing fills every part of the room to the same pressure, like a fluid. There is no place in the chamber where the sound is quieter. There is no place where it is louder. You stop hearing it the way you stop feeling the temperature of your own blood, and after that the room is, in a strange way, silent — a silence with one enormous grain.

The chamber is not large. Twenty paces across at most, roughly oval, higher than it is wide, its ceiling lost in mist. What is large is how much has been built into it.

Every surface below head height has been worked. Not carved out of the rock but built onto it, stone stacked on stone, and the stones are small — none bigger than two fists — so that the construction has the fineness of masonry done by people who had a great deal of time and nothing with an edge harder than flint. Terraces step down from the walls toward the plunge basin in tiers a hand-span high. Cells open off the terraces, square-mouthed, lintelled with flat slabs, each one just too small to enter: you could put your head and one shoulder in, no more. There are dozens of them. Between the cells run channels, also stone-lined, and the channels are the point of the whole arrangement — they take the spray and overflow from the basin and lead it, by grades so shallow they are nearly level, around the room, into cisterns the size of washbasins, over spillways, under little bridges of single slabs, and finally to a low drain on the far side where the water leaves as quietly as such water can. The settlement is a device for receiving the fall. Everything in it is turned toward the column of water the way a village on a coast is turned toward the sea.

Nothing here has a joint anymore. Whoever laid these stones laid them dry, and the mist has done the rest. Every stone wears a skin of calcite, thin as glaze on a pot, so that the walls of the cells and the lips of the channels have gone smooth and slightly translucent, faintly ribbed where the drip ran, the colour of old teeth. The corners have rounded. Where a lintel once sat on two uprights there is now a single arch of stone-and-mineral with a ghost of a seam in it. Here and there the deposition has grown past usefulness — a channel three-quarters filled with its own lining, a cell mouth narrowed to a slot — and you would expect this to mean the place is failing, silting up, on its way to being solid rock. But the water has settled that too. Where a channel grows too narrow, the flow quickens and stops laying mineral; where a cistern brims, its overflow finds the next lip. The room has arrived at the shape where the building-up and the wearing-away are the same rate. It has been finished for a long time. It was probably finished before it was abandoned, if it was abandoned.

The light comes from the wet. On every surface the water touches — which is every surface — there is a film, not quite a growth, that gives off a low grey-green luminance. It is not enough to read by. It is enough to see the whole room at once, without shadow, since every part of the room is its own lamp. The mist carries it. Look up and the falling column is a pale bar going into a pale fog, and you cannot find the ceiling, and you cannot find the slot the water comes from. Look down and the basin is a dim eye with the fall standing in it. The light does not brighten or fade. There is no day here to reach for; the film glows at the rate it glows, and has no reason to do otherwise.

It is cool. Not cold — the water has come a long way through rock and carries the rock's temperature, which is the temperature of a cellar in late autumn, and the air has taken the water's temperature, and the stone has taken the air's. Your breath does not show. Everything you touch is the same degree of cool and the same degree of wet, and after a while your hand cannot tell built from unbuilt.

There are a few things living. In the basin, if you crouch and let your eyes settle, small pale fish hang in the outer ring where the turbulence dies, translucent, finger-length, facing the fall the way the cells do. They do not startle. In the channels there are isopods, white and flat, that pick at the film and are the only reason the film has not thickened into a crust. Once, on a high terrace, a single slug the length of a hand, grey, going somewhere very slowly across the calcite. That is all. No moss. No fern in the spray zone where you would expect fern. Nothing green has ever been down here; whatever seeds reached the crack did not reach the light, because there is no light of the kind a seed wants. The room is mineral and water and a thin skin of glow, and a few animals who have made a treaty with the noise.

The builders left things. Not many, and all of them small, and all of them now half-stone. A bowl in a cell mouth, shallow, chipped from the same rock as the walls, with a rim ground so even that you know it was turned against a stone in the hand for a long time. A pile of flat pebbles sorted by size. A bone point set upright in a niche — an awl or a pin, its tip gone soft under the glaze. Nothing of metal, nothing of wood, nothing woven, or else these things were there once and the room has taken them. The chisel marks on the few dressed slabs are short, shallow, patient: the marks of something being tapped rather than struck. The doorways are too small for a person. The tool marks are the width of a human thumb. You can hold both facts and not resolve them, and the room does not help you.

What the room does tell you is how they lived with the sound. There would have been no speaking here. Nothing could be said out loud in this chamber that could be heard a pace away. So whatever they were to each other, they were it by touch and gesture and the placing of stones — and the stones say enough. Every cell faces the water. Every channel is tended toward a cistern. Every terrace steps down in the same shallow measure. This was not a place anyone hid in. It was a place made deliberately around the one thing in it that would never stop, and it was made so well that when the makers left the thing kept it.

You can stay as long as you like. Nothing will happen. The column will fall, the basin will take it, the channels will carry it around the room to the drain, the film will glow, the fish will face the fall. The mist will lay down its next layer, thin as a breath, on the lintels and the bowl and the bone pin, and on you, if you stand still enough. When you go back into the crack the roar will follow you for a body-length and then cut off, and the ordinary quiet of the hill will feel, for an hour or so, like a kind of deafness.
