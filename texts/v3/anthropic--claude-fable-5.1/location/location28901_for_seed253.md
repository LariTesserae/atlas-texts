**The Cistern at the Top of the World**

Begin with the altitude, because everything else follows from it.

This is the highest place. Not a peak — peaks are for young mountains, and this one is old past the point of having a shape that a peak could be said to belong to. What was once a summit has been ground, over an age that has no useful number, into a broad shallow dish some forty kilometers across, so gently rimmed that if you were to walk its edge you would not know for certain where the edge was. The air outside is thin and knife-cold and nearly empty. Clouds form below the rim, never above it. The stars, out there, are steady points that do not twinkle.

But you are not out there. You are inside, and inside there is no air at all.

---

The dish is full. It has been full for so long that the word *lake* is wrong — a lake implies a shore, a surface open to the sky, a thing that could in principle be drained. This is a cistern. It is sealed. A lid of matted growth, meters thick, closes over the whole basin from rim to rim, and beneath the lid the water goes down and down through a structure of chambers and galleries and shafts until, somewhere below, it meets what remains of the mountain's dead heart. Rain and snowmelt seep in through the lid at roughly the rate that pressure weeps water out through the rim, and so the level has not changed, as far as anything here could tell, ever.

Nothing here is stone. That is the first thing to understand about the place, and it takes a long time to believe. The walls are not stone, the floors are not stone, the pillars and arches and honeycombed partitions that divide the dark water into rooms are not stone. Every surface is plant — a single kind of plant, or a single plant, the distinction has stopped mattering — that lays down its own body behind itself as it grows, the way a reef does, the way a tree lays down heartwood. What you touch is old growth, dead and dense and cool, compressed by the weight of the growth above it, smooth as bone. It is not made of anything the mountain provided. The mountain is buried under it. The plant has built the whole of its own world, chamber on chamber, over the flattened ruin of the rock, and now it is the mountain: forty kilometers of grown architecture, all of it flooded, all of it dark.

---

Dark is also a word that needs adjustment.

There is no light. Not low light, not the slow blue of deep water, not the grey of a night with cloud cover. The lid admits nothing. The plant does not glow, and nothing that lives here has ever needed to see, and so nothing here makes light either. The darkness is total and it is cool — about four degrees, the temperature at which water is heaviest and most content to stay where it is. Your hand in front of your face does not exist. The walls exist only when you meet them.

And yet the place is not silent. This is the second thing, and it is stranger than the first.

Sound carries in water four times faster than in air and very much farther, and the cistern, being a closed body of water inside a structure of hard resonant walls, is an instrument. Everything in it can be heard from everywhere in it, softened by distance but never lost. And it is *loud*. Not violently — there is no crash, no thunder. It is loud the way a cathedral organ is loud when every pipe is sounding at once at a moderate volume: a full, saturating, continuous pressure of sound that comes from all directions and from none.

Part of it is the structure itself. The growth is under strain — its own mass, the weight of the water, the slow thermal pulse of the seasons outside — and it answers the strain with long low groans that travel through the walls and out into the water as a note you feel in your sternum more than hear. Each chamber has its own tone, set by its dimensions, and the tones have not changed because the chambers have not changed.

The rest of it is the animals.

---

They are abundant, and the abundance is the kind you find in the deep ocean rather than in a pond: not crowded but *everywhere*, distributed thinly across an enormous volume, so that in any one room you might find three or four presences in a space that could hold a village, and yet across the whole cistern there are millions.

There are the ribbon-things, flat and pale — pale is a guess; nothing here has color, because color has no meaning without light — that live in the galleries near the lid and graze on the soft new growth where the plant is still laying itself down. They are the only creatures that touch the living part of the plant, and they touch it gently. When they move, they produce a dry papery sound like a page turning, amplified by the water into something you can hear from a kilometer off.

There are the knockers. Armored, many-legged, the size of a hand or the size of a table, they crawl the walls of the middle depths and strike the surface with a hard forelimb, once, then wait, then strike again. The strike returns to them off every wall in the chamber and tells them the shape of the room and where the others are. They have been doing this since before the lid closed. Their knocks are the tempo of the place — a slow irregular ticking that never quite becomes a rhythm and never stops.

There are the callers. Long-bodied, eyeless, soft, they hang in the great open shafts where the water goes down farthest and they sing — a single sustained note each, held for minutes at a time, then released, then taken up again a fraction of a tone higher or lower. Every caller has its own note and holds to it for life, and the notes lie so close together across the whole population that the sum of them is a chord with no gaps in it, a wall of tone that fills the shafts from floor to lid. It is the loudest thing here. It is what you would hear first if you could hear anything through the lid, and it is what the whole cistern hears, always.

There are others. Filterers rooted to the dead walls, mouths open into the slow currents. Small quick things that eat the filterers. Something large that moves rarely through the deepest rooms and is known only by the way the chord bends around it. Nothing here has an eye, or a tool, or a nest, or anything it has made. They live in a made place and make nothing. The plant built the architecture and the animals inherited it, and the arrangement has suited both parties for so long that there is no longer any sense in which either could be said to have adapted to the other. They have simply arrived, together, at the shape they were always going to have.

---

Here is what is unusual about the cistern, beyond the obvious. It does not go anywhere.

Most places carry an argument inside them — the river that is cutting the canyon, the forest that is closing over the field, the coast that is losing to the sea. There is a direction to them. You can stand in them and feel the next thousand years pressing at your back.

The cistern has no direction. The plant is still growing at the lid, but at exactly the rate it is being eaten by the ribbon-things and compressed by its own weight, so the lid neither thickens nor thins. The water comes in as it goes out. The callers die and their notes are taken up by the young, who find the empty place in the chord as if it had been waiting for them. The knockers knock. The groan of the walls has the pitch it has because the rooms have the size they have, and the rooms are finished. The rock beneath was worn flat before the plant ever arrived; the plant built on the ruin until the building was done, and then it stopped, and then it filled, and then it closed, and it has been closed ever since. It is not dying. It is not growing. It is not waiting. It is the oldest stable thing on the mountain, which is the oldest thing in the region, and its whole condition is that of a sentence that has already ended.

If you could stay — if you could hang in the black water of one of the great shafts with the chord pressing on your ears and the cold in your bones and the four-hundred-meter dark above you and below — you would notice after a while that you had stopped expecting anything. That the knocks and the singing and the papery grazing and the deep structural groan had ceased to be events and had become the texture of the medium itself, like the cold, like the pressure. That there was no *before* here and no *after*, only the full dark room and the sound that fills it, all the way to the top of the world.
