The first thing to understand is that nothing here has been built, and yet the whole valley works.

You come to it from the pass, where the last ordinary stone gives out, and the ground beneath you stops being ground. It becomes a mat — knee-deep, then waist-deep, then deeper than you can measure — of a single continuous growth that fills the high valley from wall to wall and runs on for kilometers, folding over ridges, dropping into the side basins, climbing until the cold stops it a few hundred meters below the summits. The mat is gray-green and brown and a color that is nearly slate. Close up it is a thousand different things: a wiry cushion-plant with leaves like fish scales, a lichen that grows in flat plates the size of hands, a rope-stemmed creeper with no leaves at all, a sedge whose stalks are hollow and translucent. But there is no soil between them, and no rock. The plants grow on the plants. Whatever they started on is far down, compressed into a black fibrous peat that has never seen the sky, and above it the living layers are stacked so densely that the valley floor is not a floor but a slow standing wave of vegetation ten, twenty, forty meters thick.

It is cold. Not killing cold — a steady few degrees above freezing at midday, a rime of frost most nights that the plants shed by noon. The air is thin and very clear, and the light is the flat, even light of high overcast, shadowless, so that everything is exactly as bright as everything else and distances are hard to judge. There is a lot of it to judge. The valley is wide enough that the far wall is blue with haze, and the mat runs out toward it in a long shallow trough with side-valleys branching off like the fingers of a hand, each of them filled to the same level, each of them moving.

Because it moves. That is the second thing.

Stand still on the mat — you can, it holds you, it is like standing on a wet mattress packed with wire — and watch any patch of it for the length of a few breaths. Something will happen. A cushion-plant the size of a barrel will split down the middle and both halves will roll outward, exposing a pale interior that browns and tightens as you watch. A stem will rise from a crevice, thicken, and kink over at a right angle. A whole section the width of a road will sag six inches and something else will heave up beside it. It is not wind. There is very little wind. The movement is in the plants themselves: growth and collapse, swelling and slump, racing along at a speed that ordinary vegetation never reaches, so that the surface of the valley is never the same for two hours together. From the pass you can see it happening at the regional scale — ridges of growth appearing in the trough, migrating slowly toward the walls, dissolving. Patches darkening as they die and being overgrown within the day. A shallow lake of meltwater in one of the side-basins shrinking as the mat advances into it from all sides, then reappearing a hundred meters away as a section subsides and fills.

And it is silent. Not quiet — silent, in the way that only very cold, very high places are silent. You hear your own blood. You hear the frost on the outer leaves crackle when you brush it. But the valley itself, with all of that churning, makes no sound at all. Stems part without a tear. Masses fall without a thud, because they fall into more plant, and the plant takes them without complaint. It is like watching a storm through thick glass, except there is no glass.

—

You have to go down into it to understand the third thing, which is that this is a factory.

Not a metaphor. Not built. But the mat, in its enormous age and its enormous turnover, has arrived at something that does what a factory does: takes in raw material, processes it under pressure, moves products through a system, and vents waste. The hollow sedges are pipes. Cut one — it takes a knife, the wall is like green vinyl — and a clear fluid runs out under real pressure, a spurt then a steady flow, cold enough to numb your fingers. It smells faintly of turpentine. Follow the sedge down and it joins another, and that joins a third, and somewhere in the dark of the mat there is a trunk-line as thick as your leg, warm to the touch, pulsing at intervals of about a second. There are hundreds of these. Thousands. They run in the same direction along the valley, uphill, against gravity, with the fluid pushed by the collective turgor of the whole lower mass squeezing itself in rhythm. Where they surface, the fluid weeps from the ends and freezes overnight into brittle pale stalactites, and the ice is what most of the frost on the valley floor actually is.

There are structures — you will want to call them structures — that stand above the general surface. Columns of the rope-stemmed creeper wound so tight around each other they have the density of hardwood, rising ten and fifteen meters and flaring at the top into a cup. Vapor comes off the cups when the air is very cold, in rising threads. The columns are radiators. The mat generates a great deal of heat down in its lower layers where the dead material is being broken down — put your hand into any crevice deeper than your elbow and it is warm, wet, almost hot — and it dumps the heat here, at the surface, into the thin sky. Without them the valley would cook itself from within. With them it holds a temperature that is almost exactly the temperature at which the whole system runs best, and has held it, as far as anyone can tell from the peat cores, for tens of thousands of years.

The lichen plates are something else. They grow only on the upper faces of the columns and on the ridges of growth as they pass through the trough, and they are the only part of the system that is obviously electrical. In the very dry hours after a frost, before the sun warms the surface, they hold a charge — you can feel it in the hair on your arms as you come near, and if you bring a knife blade close to the edge of a plate there is a spark, small and blue, and no sound. Nobody knows what the charge is for. The plates may be doing the equivalent of what a shop floor does when it runs a current through a bath to plate one metal onto another: precipitating something out of the sap that runs beneath them. The plates are always slightly heavier than they should be. Break one and the interior is crystalline, faintly metallic, cold.

All of this — the pipelines, the radiators, the plating beds — is running at full production, all the time. That is what the turbulence is. What looks from above like chaos is throughput. A section slumps because the mass beneath it has been consumed and moved elsewhere. A ridge rises because that is where the pressure of the trunk-lines happens to be greatest this week. The valley churns because it is working, and it has been working for so long that the churning is its settled state; the erosion of a place like this is not wearing-down but wearing-in, the way an engine wears in, until the parts fit each other so closely that the whole thing runs without noise.

—

Water comes in pockets. There is no river, though the trough runs the way a river would. Instead there are the meltwater lakes that open and close in the side-basins, cold and steel-gray and never more than a meter deep, with the mat's leading edge advancing into them as a pale, spongy bank. There is condensation — the whole surface of the valley is beaded with it in the mornings, every leaf holding its droplet, and in the cups of the radiator columns there is always a shallow pool of the stuff, faintly bitter with the turpentine smell. And there is rain, maybe one day in six, thin and driven, that the mat drinks the instant it lands. You never see standing water on the surface for more than an hour after rain. The pipelines take it.

Almost nothing lives here that is not the mat. In a week of walking you might see three things. A small dark bird, some kind of finch, feeding on the crystalline grit that spalls off the lichen plates — it picks it up, works it in its beak, drops most of it. A hare, very pale, that lives somehow in the crevices and comes up only after frost; you see its tracks in the rime far more often than you see the hare. And once, in the deepest side-basin where the mat has grown over a lake and left a shallow cavern beneath, something moving in the dark water under the roots that might have been a large amphibian and might have been a broken pipeline pulsing. The mat does not seem to need animals. It pollinates itself, or does not pollinate at all. Whatever it once was, it long ago stopped depending on anything outside the valley.

—

Some rules, as best they can be worked out.

The mat does not grow above a certain line on the valley walls, and the line is dead level, like a bathtub ring. Above it the rock is bare and ordinary and lichen-spotted in the ordinary slow way. The line is where the cold overtakes the mat's ability to heat itself. In warm years it climbs a meter. In cold years it retreats. It has been doing this for longer than the mountains have had their present shape; there are terraces in the walls, high above the current line, where an earlier and much larger valley-mat must once have stood, and the rock there is polished smooth by a substance that was not water.

Anything that dies inside the mat is gone within a day. This includes plant material and it would include you. The heat and the fluid and the pressure break down whatever enters the lower layers and route it into the pipelines. It is not malicious. The mat cannot tell the difference between its own dead tissue and anything else.

The mat does not cross the pass. You can stand with one foot on stone and one on plant, and the boundary is exact, and it never moves. Something about the pass — its exposure, its drainage, a change in the rock — keeps the system inside. In all the length of the valley there is no other outlet.

The silence is not an absence. The mat absorbs sound. Shout, and the shout goes out into the air and does not come back; there is no echo from the walls because the sound reaches the mat before it reaches the walls, and the mat takes it. Two people fifty meters apart on the valley floor have to shout to hear each other and their shouts arrive small and flattened, as if from much farther away. It is very hard to be in the valley for long without beginning to whisper.

—

At dusk the light does not so much fade as become the same everywhere, a slow even grayness in which the moving ridges of the mat are hard to distinguish from their own shadows, and the radiator columns stand up out of the general surface with their threads of vapor going straight up in the still air. The frost begins. You can watch it come — a whitening from the edges of the leaves inward, silent, until the whole floor of the valley from wall to wall is pale, and the darkness underneath it is warm, and the pipelines pulse, and a section a kilometer away lets go and subsides without a sound and somewhere else a new ridge begins to lift, as it has every night for longer than there has been anyone to see it, working, working, in the cold, in the dark, making whatever it is that it makes.
