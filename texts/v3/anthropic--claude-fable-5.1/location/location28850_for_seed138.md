The ground here is eleven years old and still warm from being made.

It is a flat of black glass and grey mineral crust, perhaps four hundred paces across, lying in a shallow basin that was a lake bed until the vents opened and boiled it dry and then, over a single year, floored it with something new. The surface has not had time to break. It is sharp everywhere: fractured into slabs with edges you could open a hand on, rippled where it flowed, blistered where gas came up through it while it set. Nothing has rounded. Nothing has been worn. Where the crust is thin it rings underfoot like a struck plate, and where it is thick it is silent, and there is no way to tell which is which except by walking.

Water lies on it in sheets. Not standing water in the ordinary sense — the glass is impermeable and nearly level, so every drop of condensate that falls stays where it lands, spreading into a film an inch deep that covers most of the basin and reflects the underside of the sky. The sky is the source of it. The whole flat lives under a lid of steam, low enough that the tops of the taller structures disappear into it, and the steam is always cooling and always coming down as a fine warm rain that never quite stops and never gets heavier. The rocks weep. The pipe racks weep. Every surface has its beaded skin of water and every skin is warm to the touch, blood-warm at the edges of the flat and hot enough to hurt at the middle.

The middle is where the wells are.

---

Six wellheads, spaced wide, each one a squat iron collar bolted to the glass and cased in a lattice of staging. From each collar a run of lagged pipe, wrapped in canvas gone the colour of the crust, climbs onto a trestle and goes off toward the turbine hall on legs of riveted angle iron. The trestles are the streets. There is no other way to cross the flat with dry feet, and no other way to know where the thin places are, so the settlement has grown along them: a shed here, a valve house there, the assay hut, the tank stand, the compressor shed with its two flywheels turning behind louvred slats. Everything stands well apart from everything else. Between the structures there is nothing but the wet black floor and the rain, and the presences of the buildings are thin and separate in the murk, each one an island with its own halo of lamplight.

The lamps are arc lamps, carbon-rod, hung in wire cages at the trestle junctions. They burn a bruised violet-white, and the steam takes the light and diffuses it into a general dimness that has no direction. There are no shadows. There is no horizon. There is a brighter region where the turbine hall is and a dimmer region toward the edges, and that is the whole geography of light. It is not dark. It is the particular dim of a place where the sun exists only as a rumour of a paler patch overhead, sometimes, and where the lamps have burned so long that the operators no longer register them as lit.

Inside the turbine hall — a long brick shed with a sawtooth roof, the only masonry on the flat, its mortar already crusted white with minerals sweating through — two horizontal steam turbines run at fixed load. They have run at fixed load for a long time. The governor is set and sealed. The dynamo beside them hums a fifth above the turbines' note, and from the hall the sound goes out through the open doors and across the water and does not lose itself in anything, because there is nothing for it to lose itself in.

---

The sound is the thing you understand first and stop understanding soonest.

The wells roar. Each collar has a relief valve, and the relief valves are set to weep continuously rather than to hold and blow, so each well produces a steady white hiss the volume of a waterfall, and the six hisses overlap into a single pressure of sound that fills the basin from wall to wall. Under it the turbines hold their long chord. Under that, the compressor's slap and knock. The rain on the trestle canvas is a rustle. The rain on the standing water is nothing at all, a sound too fine to hear. People on the flat do not talk; they stand close and shout, or they use their hands, or they leave chalk marks on the valve house door. After a week on shift the roar becomes a silence with texture. After a month you notice its absence only when you leave, and then it is the quiet that hurts.

And through the roar, everywhere, the animals.

---

They come for the warmth and stay for the flies. The flies come for the water.

Every sheet of standing condensate carries a skin of them: small dark midges that breed in the film, rise in clouds when disturbed, and settle again. At the pool edges where the glass is thick and the water cooler, the algae mats have taken hold — the flat's only real vegetation, orange and green and rust-brown, slick, warm, laid over the black in scabs — and the mats are heaving with larvae. The fly clouds are the pasture. Everything else grazes them.

Swifts, first of all, in numbers that darken the steam. They nest under the sawtooth eaves of the turbine hall in hundreds and work the air over the flat in tight low screaming passes that you feel as a pressure on the face before you see them. They have no winter here and do not leave. Frogs — small, drab, the colour of wet crust — sit in the warm shallows in such density that the edges of the pools are audible as a single continuous rasp even over the wells, and if you shine a hand lamp across the water at what would be night if there were night, the reflected eyes go on as far as the light does. Lizards on the pipe racks, on the lagging, on the sun-warm — steam-warm — iron of the collars, motionless, flicking, motionless. Herons, four or five, standing in the wide open spaces between the buildings, patient as the structures they resemble. A colony of small white terns on the spoil bank behind the cooling pond, where the dredged crust from the pond excavation was heaped and has never been moved. Rats in the assay hut. A snake, once, seen by a night fitter, thick as a forearm, lying along the main steam trunk for its heat, never confirmed and never doubted.

Nothing here hibernates. Nothing here migrates. The food does not fail and the warmth does not fail and so the populations sit at their ceilings, stable, dense, loud.

---

The people are the sparse thing.

Fourteen on the flat at any hour — the number the works requires, no more — and they are rarely visible together. A figure on a trestle, oilskin and wide hat, going from one island of lamplight to the next. Another at the wellhead, reading a gauge that reads what it read yesterday. Someone at the compressor shed door, smoking, facing outward at the rain. They live in the long bunkhouse at the eastern edge where the crust is coolest, in rooms that never dry, and their clothes are the same colour as the crust because the crust gets into everything. They cannot hear each other. They have developed a kind of contentment with this that they do not discuss, because discussing anything requires leaving.

There is one tree. A fig, planted in a half-barrel of imported soil outside the superintendent's office, watered by the sky, leaves gone leathery and dark in the mineral rain. It has not grown in years. It has not died. It stands as a marker of what the flat is not, and the swifts use it as a perch, and nobody has ever seen fruit on it.

---

The rules of the place are the rules of a system that has found its balance.

The wells put out what they put out. The turbines take what they take. The steam that is not used goes up and comes down and runs, eventually, in shallow channels cut for it in the crust, to the cooling pond at the western edge, where it sits until it is the temperature of the ground and then seeps out through the one permeable seam in the basin rim into whatever is beyond. The rain that comes down equals the steam that goes up. The flies that hatch equal the flies that are eaten. The crust does not weather because nothing here weathers — no frost, no wind to speak of under the lid, only warm water that adds a film of mineral where it stands and removes nothing. The sharp edges of the glass stay sharp. The eleven-year-old ground will look eleven years old in fifty years.

There is no night. There are arc lamps, and there is the steam, and the steam holds the light of the lamps at the same violet dimness through whatever the sun does on the other side of it. There are no seasons; the ground's heat swamps the sky's. The shift bell rings on the turbine hall wall — nobody hears it, they watch for the man who comes to relieve them — and the man who comes is doing what the man who left was doing, and the gauges say what they said, and the swifts scream, and the frogs rasp, and the wells roar, and the fine warm rain falls on the black water and does not so much as ripple it.

The logbook in the superintendent's office is a column of identical numbers, and the superintendent, who has been here longer than anyone, has stopped writing the date.
