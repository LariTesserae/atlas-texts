Go down the slope until the blue has almost given up. Not black yet — a grey-blue that hangs in the water like breath on glass — and cold enough that the cold has stopped being a temperature and become a property of the water itself, like its weight. The seafloor here is mud, and the mud is flat, and it goes on being flat for a long way in every direction. Nothing announces the place. You would have to know to look.

What there is to look for is a slab. A pale plate of rock perhaps the size of a door lying slightly canted in the sediment, one edge lifted a finger's width off the mud where the ground beneath it has slumped or been eaten out. The rock is young. Not young the way rock is usually young — a few million years — but young the way a scab is young. It is carbonate, and it was water very recently. It is still, in places, becoming rock; around its lower edge the mud has a stiffness that is not quite mud anymore, a cement forming grain by grain out of the seep-water welling up beneath. The slab is a lid that grew over a leak. Under the lid, there is a room.

The room is not large. If you could put your hand in the crack you could nearly span it. Its ceiling is the underside of the slab; its floor is soft grey sediment, faintly rippled; between the two there is a space about the height of a fist, widest at the center and closing to nothing at the edges where rock meets mud. Through the one lifted edge a little of the outside light gets in, and lies across the floor in a thin blue bar, and everything above that bar hangs in a dimness that your eyes take a long while to learn.

What they learn is that the ceiling is inhabited. All of it.

---

Start with the tubes. They hang from the rock in bundles, white and chalk-white and the dirty white of old teeth, each tube the width of a thread or a wheat stalk and some as long as the room is tall, so that their open mouths hover just above the sediment. They are not scattered. They grow in ranks, and the ranks follow lines in the rock — the fine fractures where the seep-fluid leaks through most freely — so that the ceiling is mapped by them: avenues of tubes along the productive seams, and between the avenues, blank stretches of pale rock where nothing has bothered to settle. The tubes at the center of an avenue are the longest and oldest, thick-walled, crusted with smaller life. At the margins they thin to a few stragglers, young and translucent, barely begun. From each mouth a red plume unfurls and holds itself in the water, feathery, and does not move except when the whole city moves.

Between the tube-avenues the rock is tiled with mussels. Their shells are dark and lie shingled against one another, hinge-up, gape-down, so densely that they have become the surface — you cannot see rock between them, only the black seams where one shell meets the next. In places they are stacked two and three deep, the upper layers anchored to the lower by threads. Where they meet a tube-avenue they stop, cleanly, as if at a curb. Where two mussel-districts meet each other without an avenue between them the boundary is a low ridge of piled dead shells, cemented into the growing rock with a mineral glue the rock itself supplies.

And this is the thing about the walls of this city: they are all being built by the same process that is trying to bury it. The carbonate precipitates wherever the seep-water touches the sea-water, and it precipitates preferentially on surfaces, and every surface here is a body or a house. The oldest mussels are half-entombed, their lower valves already indistinguishable from the rock, only the hinge-end still free to open a crack and feed. The oldest tubes are not tubes anymore but ribs — solid, mineralized, with the animal long gone and the mouth sealed over, standing among the living like columns in a colonnade. The city's architecture is its dead. New life fastens to the ribs and the entombed shells and grows outward from them, so the city thickens downward, toward the floor, a little each year, and the room gets shorter, and no one who lives in it can see that this is happening.

---

The room is full. This is the second thing your eyes learn: the space between ceiling and floor, which looked empty, is nearly solid with presence. Amphipods the size of rice grains hang in the water in their hundreds, not swimming, just holding, all oriented the same way, a slow drift of them toward the center of the ceiling where the fluid rises warmest — warm being a relative word here; a half-degree above the ambient cold, a warmth you could not feel but which they can. Small snails move across the mussel-shingles at a pace that is not visible as movement but is visible as *distribution*: where there were four snails on a shell there are now three and one on the shell beside it, and you did not see the crossing. Pale squat lobsters, no bigger than a thumbnail, sit tucked between tube-bundles with their claws folded to their chests, one to a niche, spaced with a regularity that feels less like chance than like tenancy. Scale worms live inside the tubes alongside the tube's rightful owner. Limpets live on the tubes. Something smaller than the limpets lives on them.

On the floor, the sediment is not bare either. It is furred, here and there, with a white growth — filaments a few millimeters long, standing up like grass in frost, in patches that are brightest directly beneath the fractures in the ceiling. These are the only thing that could be called vegetation and they are not plants; they are bacteria, grown to visible thickness on the chemistry welling up through the mud, and they are the reason the city can exist at all. They are sparse. A few white lawns in the grey, no larger than coins. Around each lawn the sediment is pocked with the burrows of things that come up to graze on it — small worms, tinier crustaceans — and the burrows, too, are arranged, ringing the lawns like the houses of a village around its common.

---

Nothing here makes a sound. This is not the silence of an empty place. It is the silence of a place where sound is not one of the media in which things happen. The tubeworms do not hear. The mussels do not hear. Water this cold carries vibration well but there is nothing vibrating; the amphipods drift without a stroke, the snails glide on mucus, the lobsters keep so still that sediment settles on their backs. Once in a long while a bubble of gas works loose from a fracture in the ceiling and rises the fist's-height to the rock and flattens against it and is gone, and even that — you would have to be pressing your ear to the slab, and there would be nothing.

But the city moves. It has a pulse, and the pulse is the seep's. Every so often — the interval is not regular, and it may be governed by the tide overhead or by something far down in the mud — the fluid coming through the fractures surges. The water in the room warms by some fraction. And all at once the red plumes of the tubeworms lean, together, as a field of grain leans, in whichever direction the flow is pushing them, and the mussels along the avenues open a hair wider, and the amphipods drift faster toward the center and bunch there in a loose cloud, and the lobsters unfold and take one or two steps toward the fracture and pick at the rock with their small claws — feeding on what, it is not clear; on the film of bacteria, on the amphipods too slow to hold position. Then the surge passes. The plumes straighten. The amphipods spread back out. The lobsters fold. All of it without a sound and mostly without visible motion, so that you experience the pulse as a series of stills: the city as it was, and then the city as it is, and the difference between them.

Predation happens in the same way. There is a fish that lives under the slab — one fish, eel-bodied, pale, with a head no wider than a pencil and eyes reduced to dark grains. It lives in the far corner where the ceiling comes down to meet the mud, and it does not appear to move, but the amphipod cloud is thinner on its side of the room and thickens away from it, and every now and then, between one look and the next, its mouth is closed on something. It is the only large animal in the city, and it is possibly the only thing in it that will never be entombed; it has no shell, and it does not stay still long enough for the rock to take it.

---

Follow the light back to the lifted edge, and notice what the light is doing there. Where the blue bar falls across the sediment at the room's mouth, the mud is stiffest. This is the threshold of the room, and it is also where the room is being sealed: fresh carbonate is forming in the sediment along the edge of the slab, a pale crust like the rind on cheese, and it is slowly closing the gap the light comes in by. The crack is narrower than it was. Give it time — not much time, in the rock's terms; a few decades, a century — and the lid will be flush with the floor and the room will be closed and the light will not come in at all. The city will not notice. It will keep growing downward off the ceiling and the floor will keep growing up to meet it and the space between will fill with shell and rib and cement until there is no room, only a lens of white rock in the mud with the shapes of a city packed inside it, tube against tube, shell against shell, all facing the same way, toward a warmth that stopped rising long ago.

And somewhere else on the slope — a few meters off, a few hundred — the fluid will have found another way up through the mud, and a crust will be forming around the leak, and beneath it a hollow, and into the hollow something will drift, and settle, and begin.
