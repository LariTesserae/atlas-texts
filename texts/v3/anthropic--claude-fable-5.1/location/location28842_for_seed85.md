**The Cistern at Ora Hend**

Start with the mountain, because the water needs something to stand on.

The plateau lies at the top of a range whose lower slopes are ordinary — scree, thin frost, wind. Up here the wind stops. Not because there is a wall; there is no wall. The air simply gives out its habit of moving a few hundred meters from the edge of the thing, the way a conversation stops when someone enters a room.

The thing is a body of water about four hundred meters across and perhaps sixty high, standing on bare rock with nothing around it. Its outer surface is vertical, or nearly so, and slightly convex, like the side of a lens too large to see the curve of. It does not slosh. It does not sweat. If you put your hand against it — and you can — it is cool, about the temperature of a cellar wall, and it does not wet you until you push, and then your hand is inside and the water closes around your wrist with a light, exact pressure, the pressure of about thirty meters of depth, though your hand is barely below the waterline and there is, properly speaking, no waterline at all.

From outside, the face of it is black. Not the black of ink or of deep sea, which are both blacks of *distance*; this is the black of a stopped surface. Starlight goes into it and doesn't come back. Lower your face close to the water and you can see a few centimeters in — the rock underneath, some fine dark grit that the founders never bothered to sweep — and then the clarity simply ends, not in murk but in a kind of visual silence. The water is perfectly transparent. It is also perfectly dark. These are not in conflict; they are two different facts about two different things, and the engineers who made the cistern kept them separate on purpose.

—

Inside, then.

Walking in is a matter of stepping through the face and continuing. Breathing is handled — you don't notice how, which is a signature of the period; the good work of that time was always the kind you didn't notice. The pressure stays at thirty meters whether you are on the floor or near the ceiling. The water is 4°C everywhere, which is the temperature at which water is heaviest and most reluctant to move, and someone chose that number with care, because there are no currents in Ora Hend. Not slow ones. None. A grain of the founders' grit, lifted and released, hangs where it was left. Come back in a year and it is there.

You see, a little. The darkness is not total. There are eleven structures inside the cistern and along the base of each one, where the built stone meets the plateau rock, there is a seam that gives off a faint bluish-grey light — the color of the inside of a mussel shell, or of snow at dusk under cloud. It is enough to see the shape of the streets. It is not enough to see your own hand at arm's length unless you hold it near a seam. The light does not carry. It reaches about a meter and a half from its source and then stops, the same way the clarity stopped at the outer face. You come to understand that the water is doing this — that it has been told a distance, and honors it. Beyond a meter and a half the world is inferred rather than seen. You know a building is there because the seam-light draws its base like a chalk outline on a floor.

The eleven buildings are set far apart, on a ground plan that wants to be a grid and isn't. Streets cross at ninety degrees and then one of them, having gone forty paces, arrives at its next crossing slightly before you expected it to. You pace it again and get the same answer. The settlement is small — you could cross it in a quarter of an hour if you didn't stop — but the intervals between buildings are long, and the emptiness between them is a designed emptiness, held open the way a courtyard is. Most of Ora Hend is space. Eleven buildings and perhaps a hundred and fifty meters of black water between each, and nothing in the water. No silt, because nothing falls. No growth on the walls, because nothing lives that could grow. The stone is as clean as the day it was set. Tool marks — fine parallel scores, the width of a fingernail — run across every cut face, and the arrises are sharp enough to notice under a hand. Nothing here has softened. Nothing has had the chance.

—

The material is the mountain. They quarried the plateau itself, a dark fine-grained stone with a faint sheen, and relaid it in courses. Under the seam-light it looks like slate. Touched, it is warmer than the water by a degree or two — or seems warmer; it may only be that it is not cold in the way water is cold. It has no grain. Break a piece (you cannot) and it would break the same in every direction. The founders did something to it, or chose a stone that had already had something done to it by the mountain, and either way it will not weather, because there is nothing here to weather it.

The buildings are simple and tall for their footprint — each one a single room, or a stack of two, with walls perhaps a meter thick and a flat roof. There are openings but no doors, and the openings have no frames, no lintels, no sills. The wall simply isn't there for a certain width and height, and then is again. Inside, the rooms are empty. Not abandoned — empty in the manner of a thing that was never furnished, that was built to *be* a room rather than to be used as one. The floors are the plateau rock, dressed flat. In two of the eleven there is a low bench of the same stone, built into the wall. In one there is a shallow square depression in the floor, a hand-span deep, with a single line cut around its rim. Nobody knows what for. Nobody is here to ask.

The ceilings are high enough that the seam-light doesn't reach them. Stand in the middle of a room and you are in a lit rectangle a few meters wide, with walls rising into black on every side and black above, and the sense — very strong, in the stillness — of being at the bottom of something, though you are sixty meters up a mountain, in a body of water with no top.

Because it has no top. That is the other thing you learn, slowly. Swim upward from any street and the seam-light drops away below you and then there is only water and dark and the unchanging pressure, and you keep going, and at some point you notice you have been rising for longer than sixty meters. There is no surface. You do not come out into thin mountain air. You come, eventually, back down — into a street, the same street or a neighboring one, from above, as though the cistern were folded along a seam you can't feel. The founders needed the water to stand without a container and this is how they solved it: they gave it nowhere to go. Up, inside Ora Hend, curves. Outside, it does not.

—

There are animals. Not many, and only one kind.

You will go a long time without seeing one, and then something will pass across a seam-light at the far end of a street — a length of pale ribbon, flat, perhaps as long as a forearm, moving with a slow lateral undulation that never quite repeats. It has no eyes that you can find. It has a mouth, or a fold that might be one. Its skin is the colour the seam-light is, or takes on that colour when it crosses one, and it is possible that it has no colour of its own at all. There are maybe a dozen of them in the whole cistern. They move through the openings of the buildings, they follow the streets, they rise and — since up curves — come back down. They do not eat, or nothing has ever been seen to be eaten. They are not the founders' pets or the founders' remnants. They were in the mountain, in a spring the plateau once held, and when the water was gathered up and told to stand, they came with it, and the same instruction that keeps the grit from settling keeps them from ending. They are older than the buildings. They will be older still.

Sound: there is almost none. Water carries sound beautifully and there is nothing here to be carried. Your own movement makes a soft displacement you feel more than hear. Once or twice in an hour, a faint tick — the stone, perhaps, though it should have finished settling long ago; or one of the ribbon-creatures brushing a wall. And under everything, if you hold still long enough, a tone at the edge of hearing, low and level, that may be the cistern being kept, the mechanism of the holding, or may be your own blood.

—

It was built recently. That is the strangest thing, stranger than the folded height or the disciplined dark. Everything about Ora Hend — the sharp edges, the visible tool marks, the eleven rooms that were never lived in — says *new*, says *just finished*, says the builders have stepped out and will be back. And that is what it will say in a thousand years, and ten thousand. The cistern was made at a moment when it was possible to make a place and then stop it — to finish a thing and have it stay finished, the water at its heaviest temperature, the light at its permitted radius, the animals looping through their unvarying streets. Nothing arrives. Nothing leaves. The only things that move are the twelve pale ribbons and, occasionally, someone like you, who came up through the dead wind and pushed a hand into the black face and walked in, and will walk out again, and will find on leaving that the grit is exactly where you kicked it, and that the mountain outside has gone on ageing without you, and that Ora Hend has not.
