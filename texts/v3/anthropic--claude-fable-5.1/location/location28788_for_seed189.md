Start with the wall, because the wall is the only thing that will hold still long enough.

It is plant. Not stone that has been grown over, not soil bound by roots — plant all the way through, the way an apple is apple all the way through. Pale where the light lands on it, a greenish cream like the inside of a cut stem, going to something darker and wetter in the folds. If you push a thumb against it, it gives, then pushes back. It is warm. Body-warm, or a little above; the warmth of something working. There is no smell of rot anywhere. The smell is cucumber and crushed grass and, underneath that, a mineral sweetness like the water in the bottom of a flower vase before it turns.

You are somewhere inside. Below the surface, though the surface of *what* is a question the place declines to answer. Above you — if that is above — the wall curves over and becomes ceiling without any seam, and the ceiling curves down on the far side and becomes floor. A hollow. A bubble in the living mass. Roughly the size of a room a person could not quite lie flat in, though that estimate will need to be revised.

And nothing in it.

That is the second thing, after the wall. The vegetation is total — you can't touch anything that isn't plant — and yet the space it encloses is almost perfectly empty. No leaves fallen on the floor, because there are no leaves. No debris, no seeds, no dust. The air itself feels thin of content, as though even the motes have been excluded. You stand in a cavity in a thing that is entirely alive, and the cavity is the one place the life has chosen not to be. Vast space between presences, and you are one of the presences, and it is not clear what the others are.

---

The light comes from nowhere.

This is the first rule that breaks. There is no opening. You look for it — a fissure, a shaft, some place where day leaks through — and there is none; the walls are unbroken tissue in every direction. And still the chamber is lit, evenly, brightly, the way a garden is lit at ten in the morning under a thin overcast. Shadows fall from your hand onto the floor. They fall in a consistent direction. There is nothing in that direction but more wall.

You come to understand, or decide, that the light is the light of a place this hollow *used* to be part of, or is still part of in some sense that doesn't survive being walked into. Some open field, some canopy gap. The plant grew around the light the way it grows around a stone, and sealed it in, and the light didn't notice.

---

It is not quiet.

For a hollow this size, sealed in plant, the sound is enormous. It comes from the walls, from inside them: a low continuous rushing, like standing behind a waterfall or beside a highway in fog. Under the rush, slower, a creaking — long, patient, wooden groans that travel around the chamber and come back to you from the other side. And over both of these, at intervals that are not regular, a sharp wet *tock*, like a knuckle on a melon, from a different place in the wall each time.

The rushing is sap. There is no other word for it. Somewhere in the tissue around you, an amount of fluid is moving that has no business being in a body this size, at a speed that has no business being in a plant at all. Put your ear to the wall and the sound resolves into thousands of smaller sounds, the way rain resolves into drops — a hissing chorus, each strand of it a vessel with something moving through it. It sounds like a river that has been folded up and packed inside a stem.

The *tock* is growth. You see it happen. A place on the wall bulges, slowly, the way a blister rises, and then splits with that knuckle-crack, and behind the split there is more pale tissue, new, glistening, a fingernail's depth further into the room than the wall was a moment before. Or a fingernail's depth further *out* — the chamber grows in both directions and does not bother to be consistent. The edges of the split are sharp, un-softened, the fresh edges of something that has never been touched. Everything here has that quality. This hollow is young. It opened recently. It is still opening.

---

Water, when it appears, appears on the walls first.

The air is damp enough that you feel it in your throat, but there is no standing water, no pool. Instead the walls sweat. Beads form on the smooth places, swell until they can't hold themselves, and run. They run in the direction shadows fall, which is not down — or is down, and the floor is not where you thought. They gather in the folds and creases of the tissue and sit there, dark lenses of water, and after a while are drawn back in. The plant drinks its own condensation. There is a cycle here, short and closed: exhale, bead, run, gather, absorb, exhale. You can watch a whole one in the time it takes to catch your breath.

Sometimes it rains. Not from the ceiling — from a place in the air about chest height, in the middle of the chamber, a patch the size of a spread hand where drops simply begin, fall the remaining distance to the floor, and are gone into the tissue. It lasts a few seconds. Nothing precedes it. Afterward the floor there is darker, and warmer, and the rushing in the walls picks up.

---

Now the size.

You paced it when you arrived — six steps across. Pace it again. Six steps. Turn around and look back at where you started, and the far wall is farther than six steps could account for, a good long stone's throw, the light on it thinner, hazed. Walk toward it and it is six steps. Stand still and it recedes.

The hollow is pocket-scale. It is intimate. It is a single feature, contained, and you can see all of it at once, and you are also aware — without being able to say how — that it is inside something whose size is not on any scale you have a word for. A root, perhaps. This could be a gas bubble trapped in one root of one plant, and the plant could be the size of a country, and the country could be underground, and the light could be coming from the flowers.

Or it could be that "inside" is one of the things that has stopped working here. The walls are all plant. There is nothing that is not plant. The chamber is empty. Both of these are true at once and pressing on you, the total and the void, and the place they meet is the surface of the wall, and the surface of the wall is moving.

---

There is one animal.

You don't see it at first because it is the same color as the tissue it sits on, and because it doesn't move, and because your eye has learned already that anything in this chamber that isn't wall is nothing. It is high on the curve where wall becomes ceiling — a soft, lobed thing, a hand-span across, with no eyes you can find. Slug, or snail without shell, or something that gave up being either. It holds to the wall by a broad flat foot and, very slowly, it eats.

The rasp of its mouth on the tissue is audible under everything else. Small dry strokes. It is the only sound in the chamber that is *small*. Where it has fed, the wall is scarred with pale shallow grooves, and the grooves are already closing — you can watch the edges creep in, the tissue knitting behind the animal as it goes. It is eating a place that heals faster than it can eat. It has probably been eating this exact spot for a very long time.

Every so often the wall splits with its knuckle-crack directly under the animal and lifts it a fingernail's depth without disturbing it. It does not react. It may not be able to tell that anything is happening.

---

The tool is the strangest thing, stranger than the light, stranger than the size, because it is the only thing here that means somebody.

It is embedded in the floor — half its length under the tissue, the tissue healed smooth around it, the way bark heals around a nail. A flake of something dark. Chert, or a dark glass, or a very hard seed. Knapped: you can see the scallop of the strike marks along one edge, the bulb where it was struck from its core. A scraper. A cutting edge, made by hitting one hard thing against another with a hand.

Someone was here. Or someone was in the field this light is coming from, and dropped it, and the plant grew over it and around it and brought it down here along with the morning. Or someone is going to be here, and the hollow is growing toward the moment they arrive, and the tool is the first part of them to get through.

Look at it long enough and the wall around it puts up a new bulge, and splits, and the split runs in a clean line right along the edge of the flake — the sharp edge — as though the plant were being cut from the inside by a blade no one is holding.

---

The chamber keeps working.

The rush in the walls rises and falls with a rhythm too slow to be breath and too fast to be a day. The creaking goes around. Water beads and runs the wrong way and is drunk back. The rain arrives at chest height and stops. The far wall recedes and the near wall advances and the animal is lifted, grooves closing behind it. Every few minutes a whole patch of the wall goes a shade greener, a wave of color passing over it and away, and in its wake the tissue is faintly translucent for a moment and you can see, or think you can, the shapes moving in it — dark long shapes going by at speed, the shapes that make the rushing.

Not vessels. Not roots. Not fish.

The light stays exactly as it is. Ten in the morning, thin overcast, a garden you can't reach, coming from a direction that is only wall. The plant has grown around it and forgotten to let it end.

You put your palm flat against the tissue, warm, working, and feel it push back, and understand that if you stay long enough it will heal around you too — kindly, without malice, a fingernail's depth at a time — and that the chamber will still be exactly the size it is, and still empty, and still lit, and still, very loudly, alive.
