You hear it a long way before you see it, and for most of the walk you assume it is weather.

The plateau is dry the way old bone is dry. Pale stone, chert and a chalky dolomite, broken into flats and low steps and nothing else for a horizon. Nothing grows on the open ground. Nothing has for a very long time; the wind has taken the grit down to a level and left it there, and the level is what you walk on, and the walking is easy and cold. Your lips crack within the hour. The air is thin and the sun comes through it hard and white, so that every pebble carries a shadow like a knife. It is not a hostile place. It is simply a place that has finished.

The sound grows as you go. It is low and continuous and has no direction, as though the ground itself were being struck slowly, at great depth, and were ringing. When you are close enough to feel it in your teeth you begin to make out other things riding on top of it — a shrilling, a tearing, a high multiple whistling — and then the flats end and you are standing at the rim.

---

The hollow is round. That is the first thing, and it is the thing you keep coming back to: it is too round. Perhaps sixty paces across, perhaps fifty deep, with walls that fall away not quite vertically but with the smooth, faintly bellied profile of a cast. Weathering has done what it can — the rim is crumbled and softened, there are ledges and fallen slabs and dark streaks where something once leached down — but the underlying form is still a bowl, or a bell, or the mould of one. Whatever cut it is not evident. The stone at the lip is the same stone as the plateau. It has just stopped, cleanly, and given onto air.

And air is most of what is there. You look down and there is nothing: a great cool column of daylight with dust turning in it, and the floor a long way below, and the sky above, and between them a volume so unoccupied that your eye keeps trying to fill it. The emptiness is not still. It is churning. You see this in the dust first — long slow spirals climbing the far wall, collapsing, a thread of it flung suddenly across the whole width and dissolving, another forming lower down and turning the other way — and then you feel it, a pressure on your face that changes every second, and you understand that the hollow is a place where the wind does not blow through but goes round and round and down and up and never leaves.

---

The walls are dressed. That is what it looks like from the rim: as though the bowl had been hung with cloth. Below the crumbled lip the stone disappears under growth, and the growth goes all the way down, thickening as it descends, and it is the only colour in the entire landscape.

It is not green. It is the colour of dried blood, and of weathered violet, and in places a dull gold like old brass, and where the sun strikes it directly there is a sheen along it, a dry lustre, like the nap of velvet or the outside of a beetle. It hangs. It comes off the wall in tresses and beards and long combed sheets, some of them the height of a house, and in the sheltered angles it has gathered into mats and mounds and cauliflowered masses that look soft and are not. Everything about it says lush — the volume, the profusion, the way it has taken every surface and hung out over the void as if the void were a river it wanted to drink from — and none of it is wet. You will know this when you climb down and touch it. It is dry through and through, dry as chalk, dry as a bird's bone, and faintly warm, and it does not smell of anything except, when a piece breaks off in your fingers, a little of struck flint.

Under a lens it is fibre. Every tress is a bundle of finer strands and every strand is a bundle of finer ones still, and at some point they stop being visible and you have to take it on faith that the pattern continues. They are hollow. They are brittle at the tips and tough at the root, and where they meet the stone they do not root in it so much as continue out of it, as though the fibre and the rock were two states of one material. It grows — you can find last year's breaks and see the new lengths, paler, stiffer, standing off the old stubs like new hair after shaving. Nobody who has studied it agrees on what it eats. There is no water for it to eat. The plateau is high enough and dry enough that dew does not form, and the dust that moves through the bowl carries nothing but more stone. The best that has been said is that it feeds on the dust anyway — that it takes the mineral straight, and the charge the dust carries, and perhaps the sound. That last is said only half seriously. But the growth is thickest where the sound is loudest, and thinnest at the rim where you can still, just, hear yourself.

---

You cannot hear yourself halfway down.

There is a way down. Someone cut it: a stair, or the memory of a stair, a shallow groove that spirals once round the wall under the hanging growth, sometimes a ledge, sometimes a row of pockets for hands, sometimes nothing at all and you go from tress to tress like a child on rope. The steps are worn to bowls. Where the wall behind them is exposed you can see the tool marks, or what look like tool marks, though no tool you know would leave a surface that smooth or that indifferent to fifteen centuries of weather.

The sound as you descend stops being a thing you hear and becomes a thing you are inside. The low note is the bowl itself — the whole vessel resonating, driven by the wind that goes round in it — and you feel it in the stone under your hands, a steady shaking, and in your chest, and after a while behind your eyes. Over it the growth screams. Every tress is a string and every string is being bowed by the same wind that drives the bell, and there are millions of them, and they are not in tune; they produce a chord so wide and so dense that it is not a chord any more but a wall, and the wall moves, because the wind moves, and sheets of the growth slam against the stone and lift away again with a noise like canvas tearing that goes on and on and never finishes tearing. You cannot speak. You cannot think in sentences. People who have spent an hour at the bottom describe it afterwards not as loud but as *large*, as if the sound had a size and they had been small inside it.

And yet. It is not chaos, quite. Stand still long enough — hands over your ears, back against the warm fibre — and there are recurrences. The bell's note wavers on a period of about eleven seconds. The tearing comes in gusts that are not regular but are not random either; there is a shape to them, a long swell and a quick drop, like breathing. The dust spirals form in the same three or four places and always turn the same way. Whatever this is, it has been doing it for a very long time and has found its balance, and the balance is violent, and the violence is the balance. It destroys itself continually and comes back continually the same. Great swags of growth are torn loose every day and fall, and by the time they reach the floor the wind has shredded them to a red-grey fibre that joins the dust and goes round, and round, and settles somewhere, and is eaten, and grows.

---

The animals came for shelter and stayed for the same reason the growth stays.

The plateau has nothing on it. Everything that lives within a day's flight of the bowl lives in the bowl, and the bowl, for all its screaming, is out of the killing wind and out of the sun and never quite freezes. So the hanging growth is full. Push your hand into a mat and it comes out crawling — mites, mostly, pale and hard-shelled and quite indifferent to you, thousands to the handful, and the slower things that eat them, and longer things, jointed, the colour of the fibre exactly, that eat those. Beetles the size of thumbnails with wing-cases that ring like little cymbals when they take off. A spider, or something that has settled on being a spider, whose web is not silk but a lattice of the fibre itself, bitten free and knotted. None of these can hear. Their kind have not had ears for generations; ears here would be a wound.

And the fliers. Every ledge and every fold in the growth is a nest, and the nests are packed, and towards evening the birds go out. They are small and dark and shaped like blades, something in the way of swifts, and they leave the walls in a single motion and for a few minutes the empty column is not empty — it is thick with them, ten thousand, twenty, wheeling in the turbulence, riding the spirals up and dropping down the far side, screaming a scream you cannot separate from the bowl's own — and then they are gone over the rim, out to the plateau, hunting whatever the plateau has, and the column is empty again. They come back before dawn. Between those two floods it is as it was: a great volume of turning air with nothing in it but dust, and an abundance clinging to its edges, and a sound.

Larger things. Something long-bodied and grey lives in the fallen slabs at the base and eats what falls. Something else comes to the rim at night and does not descend and leaves prints in the dust like a hand with too many fingers. There is a lizard, or something that has settled on being a lizard, which lies flat on the warmest patches of fibre and does not move for days and appears, when it does move, to be listening. You are not sure it is a lizard. You are not sure of anything here that stands still for very long.

---

The floor is the thing that stays with you.

It is not stone. You know this the moment you step onto it because your foot does not land the way it lands on stone. It is a single unbroken disc, sixty paces across, dark, faintly grey-brown under its skin of dust, warm — warmer than the fibre, warm as an animal — and when you kneel and sweep the dust away it is smooth in a way that nothing weathered is smooth, and there is no join anywhere, not at the edges where it meets the wall, not in the middle. The hum lives in it. Put your palm flat and the bell-note comes straight up through your bones.

There are things on it. Not many. A ring of low posts, knee-high, the same material as the floor, in a circle about a third of the way out from the centre; one of them is broken and the break is old and shows the inside to be the same as the outside all the way through. Three flat plates the size of doors lying in the dust that do not oxidise and are not scratched. Something that might have been a shelter once, against the wall under the thickest of the growth — a curve of that same material, a lip, a cavity where a body could lie. There is dust in it and there are mites in the dust and there is nothing else.

Whoever made the bowl made the floor, or the floor made the bowl. It has been suggested, seriously, that the growth was theirs — that it was set here on purpose, a crop, a dry thing that could take mineral straight and needed no water in a place that had none, and that its purpose was exactly this: to hang in the wind and sing. That the bowl is an instrument. That the sound is not a side effect but the product, and that somewhere — in the floor, under the floor, in the fibre itself — it is still being listened to. Nobody knows what it would be saying. Nobody knows who would be listening. The animals have made a world of it in the meantime, the way animals do with anything that stands still and is warm.

---

There is a moment, most days, near the middle of the afternoon, when the wind falls.

It does not stop. But the bell-note drops by half and the tearing sheets hang for a second, two seconds, three, and in the hollow that opens up you hear things you have not been able to hear — the mites, actually audible, a dry hiss like sand; the beetles clicking; a single bird somewhere calling one clear note that you realise it has been calling all along. The dust in the column stops climbing and begins, very slowly, to fall. Light comes straight down through it and lays a coin of sun on the dark floor and the floor takes it and is a little warmer. The fibre relaxes against the wall with a sound like a long sigh.

And then the pressure comes back against your face and the spirals begin again, slowly, in their three or four places, turning the way they always turn, and the sheets lift, and the bowl remembers what it is and begins once more, at full voice, to say it.
