You hear it before you see anything, and you go on hearing it after there is nothing left to see. From the lowlands it is a rumor in the bones of the feet, then a pressure in the chest, then a thing that fills the skull so completely that speech stops being possible and, after a while, stops being thought of. It does not rise and fall. It does not have a rhythm you can find the edges of. It is one sound, held.

The ground goes up for a long time. Past the tree line there is no tree line — the forest simply keeps going, thickening as the air thins, which is the first wrong thing and not the last. At an elevation where there should be lichen and wind and nothing else, there are trunks the width of houses, leaves like tarpaulins, ferns bowing under the weight of their own wet. The air is hot in the way the inside of a mouth is hot. Every surface sweats. The rock sweats. Water beads on the underside of leaves and falls and beads again, and the falling never seems to empty anything.

Then the streets.

They come up out of the forest gradually, so that you can't say where the slope ended and the city began. Terraces, first, stepping up the mountainside in long even courses. Then the terraces have walls, and the walls have openings at regular intervals, and the openings give onto corridors that run level around the contour of the peak, and the corridors cross other corridors, and there are steps between the levels, and there are courtyards, and there are squares. It is a city. It has the density of a city — the sense of things stacked on things, of no volume left unclaimed. Roots the thickness of arms cross what would be avenues. Moss fills what would be doorways.

Nothing here was built. Look at any wall closely enough and it is bedrock — the grain runs through it uninterrupted from the mountain into the masonry, because there is no masonry, only rock that has weathered into the exact shape of masonry. The joints between the blocks are natural fractures. The steps are strata, tilted and cut back by water over a duration you don't have a word for. There is no chisel mark anywhere on the mountain. There is no mark of any tool, or any hand, or any intention. The city is the shape the mountain settled into. It is what erosion does when it has finished.

And it has finished. This is the second wrong thing, and it takes longer to feel. The water falls from every ledge in white sheets and the sheets are loud — that is part of the sound, though not the whole of it — but the pools beneath the falls do not overflow, and the channels that run down the middle of every corridor are full to the exact same line on the stone in every corridor, and the line is not wet above and it is not wet below. The moss is neither growing over anything nor dying back from anything. The trees have no saplings at their feet and no dead among them. The leaves fall and there are no fallen leaves. The light is the light of mid-morning, bright through a high haze, and it is that light on the near face of the peak and it is that light on the far face, and there are no shadows that could be said to lean in any direction. You can sleep here and wake and it is mid-morning. You can wait and it is mid-morning.

Nothing lives here except what is rooted. No birds, at any height, in any of the thousand trees. No insects in the standing water, which is clear all the way down. No frogs, though every ledge weeps and every hollow is a pool. No tracks in the moss. Once, in a courtyard where a fig has forced its way through the paving-that-is-not-paving, there is something that might be a shell, small and white; it is a piece of the rock. The forest is complete in itself, saturated, and it has never needed to be pollinated or eaten or carried anywhere, and it never will be.

The sound is the thing you keep returning to, because you have no choice. Stand at the lip of one of the falls and it is the sound of the fall, roaring in a way that goes past hearing into a kind of vibration of the whole body. Walk a hundred paces away, around a corner of rock into a corridor where no water is falling at all, and the roar is exactly the same — not quieter, not different in pitch, not coming from behind you. Put your hands over your ears and it does not lessen. Put your head under the surface of a pool and it is there in the water. It is not a sound that is happening in a place. It is a property of the place, the way the heat is, the way the wet is. If you could ask the mountain when the sound began, the mountain would not understand the question. It is not old the way the walls are old; the walls were once something else. The sound was never anything else.

There are other wrongnesses, smaller, that accumulate. You go up a stair and count the steps, and go down the same stair, and the count is different, and neither count is the same twice. You follow a corridor around the contour of the peak, keeping the drop always on your left, and arrive back at the courtyard with the fig from the direction you left it. There is a place where a fall of water comes down through a gap in an overhang and you can see, on the far side of the sheet, a terrace; and when you go around to reach that terrace it is not there, and when you return to the fall it is. In the haze above the highest ledges — and there are always higher ledges — the horizon is not a line but a fold, and the fold is the peak again, seen from below, forested, streaming, mid-morning.

It is not a ruin. Ruins are what happens to things that used to be otherwise. This is not becoming a ruin and it did not come from being whole. It is a city that was never built, with a noise that never started, in a rain that has not gone on falling because it has not ever stopped, at a height where the air should be too thin for the leaves that shade you and is not. It is hot and green and drenched and empty and it is exactly as far along as it will ever get.

You stand in the courtyard by the fig with the roar filling your head, water running off your hands into a channel that does not rise, and you understand that the only thing on this mountain that is still moving toward something is you. Everything else has arrived.
