You go down, and the first thing that changes is not the light but the temperature. The stairs — there are stairs, at least at the beginning, cut into something that was once concrete and has been recoated so many times it now has the slick, faintly organic finish of a tooth — get warmer under your hand as you descend. Not hot. Warm the way a body is warm, the way a room is warm when many people have recently left it. By the time the last of the surface glow has been swallowed behind you, the air has thickened into something you wear rather than breathe. It smells of wet mineral and hot copper and, underneath, something sweetish and vegetal that you can't place yet.

Then the stairs stop and the floor levels out and the space opens, and you know it has opened not because you can see it but because the sound changes.

It's loud down here. That's the second thing. A steady, immense, layered roar — not a single machine but a chord of them, or of something. There is a low component you feel in your sternum, a pulse too slow to be a rhythm, maybe one beat every eleven or twelve seconds, that seems to come from below the floor and from the walls at once. Over that, a constant hiss that could be steam or could be moving water, very far away or very near, impossible to tell. And over that, intermittently, a sound like a sheet of metal being drawn across another sheet of metal somewhere up in the dark — long, unhurried, with an almost conversational cadence, as if the ceiling were clearing its throat.

The dark is not total. Your eyes, given time, find things. There is a faint grey-green cast to the air in some directions, the kind of not-quite-light that a photograph of a cave gives you rather than the cave itself. It doesn't come from any fixture you can identify. It seems to be a property of the surfaces themselves — the walls emit a dimness, the way a warm stone emits heat. Where water has run down them for a long time, the dimness is slightly stronger, so that the chamber is mapped for you in its own drainage: pale vertical seams, pale fans at the bottom of those seams, pale rings around the places where the floor has sunk and collected pools.

And it's a chamber. Room-sized isn't right; cathedral is closer, but only in the sense of proportion, not decoration. It is high — you can't see the ceiling, only infer it from where the metal-sliding sound is coming from — and it is longer than it is wide, and it has walls you could walk to in a few minutes, and it is almost entirely empty. That is the strangest thing about it, stranger than the sound. Whatever this space was built to hold, it isn't holding it. The floor stretches away in the grey-green dark with nothing on it but water and the occasional low shape, and the emptiness has a pressure to it, the way a very large hangar has a pressure, the way you lower your voice in one without deciding to.

The built quality is unmistakable, though. This is not a cavern. The walls meet the floor at angles somebody chose. There are ribs, or buttresses, or ducts — long vertical members set into the walls at regular intervals, thick as old trees, and when you put your hand on one it is cool. Cooler than the air, cooler than the floor, noticeably. It is the only cool thing in the room, and it is dry where everything else sweats. You can't tell what it's made of. It isn't metal; there's no ring to it when you knock. It isn't stone. It has the faint give of something that would rather not be touched, and after a moment you take your hand away without being entirely sure why.

The ribs are the newest thing here. That's a guess, but it feels right — everything else has the look of long weathering. The floor between the pools is a mosaic of old surfaces, one poured over another, each cracked and patched and eventually abandoned to the next. Mineral crust has built up in fringes around the drains, thick as a wrist in places, ochre and white and a bruised violet. Water has been dripping onto the same spots for long enough to hollow them into cups, and the cups have overflowed and cut channels, and the channels have their own crusts. There are stalactites forming on the undersides of things that were never meant to have stalactites — a walkway bracket, the lip of a housing — thin translucent fangs of redeposited whatever-this-is. The room has been down here long enough to have a geology.

But the ribs are clean. And every so often, without warning, one of them makes the metal-sliding sound, and you realise the noise isn't coming from the ceiling after all. It's coming from inside them, or moving along them, from the floor up into the dark, a sound with a direction. Something is passing through. Something is being carried or conducted or removed. The pulse under the floor comes a half-beat after each pass, as if in response.

You don't know what this place does. That's the honest position. It's plainly infrastructure — the city is up there, tons of it, and you can hear the city in the roar, faintly, if you listen for the difference between a mechanical sound and a civic one: a texture of very distant traffic, a hum that has voices dissolved in it — and this room is doing something for that city, has been doing it for a long time, and the doing is loud and warm and produces water. Perhaps it's a heat sink. Perhaps the city puts its waste warmth here and the ribs draw it off to somewhere else, and the dampness is condensation, the sweat of a cooling mechanism the size of a nave. Perhaps it's the other thing, the opposite, and the warmth is what's being made and the city above is heated from it, and the roar is the sound of a fire that has no flame. Perhaps the ribs don't move heat at all and the coolness is incidental and the sound is something else being conducted — data, mass, time, a thing there isn't yet a word for. The room does not explain itself. It has been here long enough that it no longer expects visitors who would need explaining to.

What lives here has adapted to not understanding, too.

Near the base of the third rib from the stairs, there's a low mound of something that at first you took for more mineral crust and that, when you crouch to it, is growing. A moss — or a moss-like thing — in a dense, felted pad, no higher than your knuckle, a dark brownish-red in the grey-green light. It is dry. That's what makes you look twice. Everything around it beads and drips, and the pad is dry as flannel, and warm, and when you hold your palm just above it you can feel that it's warmer than the floor. It's clustered on the side of the rib that faces the nearest pool, and there are others — small colonies, none bigger than a dinner plate, placed at the foot of each rib with an almost cultivated regularity, always on the pool-facing side. Whatever they eat, it isn't light. There isn't any. They're eating the gradient. They live in the seam between the cool rib and the warm room and make a slow living from the difference, and their spacing tells you they've been at it long enough to have found the exact best places and stopped.

There's nothing else green — or red, or growing — for a long way. The sparseness is part of the place's grammar. Then, at the far end of a long pool, a stand of something pale and jointed, ankle-high, like the etiolated shoots of a plant that has given up on ever finding the sun and has decided to be interesting in other ways. Their tips flicker very faintly. Not glow — flicker, in time with the sub-floor pulse. Maybe they're piezoelectric, wrung by the vibration into a little light. Maybe it's the other direction and they're listening. You stand near them for the length of several pulses and each time the tips brighten a fraction and the room, for that fraction, is a slightly different shape.

The fauna, when it shows itself, does so once and briefly. Something crosses the far shore of the pool — low, long, unhurried, the size of a cat or a large rat, moving with the flat side-to-side gait of an animal that has never needed to run. It stops at the flickering shoots. It stays there through two pulses. Then it goes on and the dark takes it. You hear, a while later, a faint plash from a part of the room you'd thought was dry floor, and realise you were wrong about where the pools end.

There are birds, or something that flies. You never see them. But twice something passes overhead, up in the region of the ceiling, with a leathery flap and a short falling cry that the room takes and multiplies and returns to you from six directions, and you understand that the loudness isn't only the machinery. The chamber is an instrument. It has been shaped, or has shaped itself, so that everything in it is heard everywhere in it. The drips are in it. Your footsteps are in it, coming back to you delayed and warped, so that after a while you can't tell whether the second set is echo or company.

The water cycles. You notice this only because you stay. A pool you stepped around going out is deeper coming back; a channel that was carrying a steady trickle has gone dry and its crust is drying pale. Somewhere above, in the ceiling you can't see, there's a period — an hour, less — when the dripping rises to a general patter and the roar acquires a rainy surface, and the warm air gets wetter and closer and the moss-pads at the rib-feet visibly darken. Then it passes. Then the ribs draw whatever they draw, and the pulse comes, and the sheet-metal sound goes up into the dark, and the room begins, slowly, to give its water back to itself.

It is not a hostile place. That surprises you, given the dark and the noise and the unexplained warmth of the walls. It has the temperament of something very old and very busy that has no particular opinion about you. The stairs are behind you and the grey-green will show you the way to them. The heat will not rise. The floor will not open. The ribs will go on being cool and dry and slightly reluctant under your hand, and the thing that crossed the far shore will go on crossing it after you've gone, and the moss will go on making its careful, warm, dry living out of a difference it can't see and doesn't need to.

You go back up. The stairs cool under your hand as you climb. The roar thins into a hum into a pressure into nothing. When you come out into the surface light, it's loud there too, and you find you'd forgotten that — that the city has a sound of its own — and you stand for a moment trying to hear, underneath it, the eleven-second pulse of the room that keeps it warm, or keeps it cool, or keeps it.
