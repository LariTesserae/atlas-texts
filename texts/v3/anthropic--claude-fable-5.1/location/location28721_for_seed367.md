The plateau has been worn so long that it has stopped being a shape. From a distance it is only a line: a table of dark stone lifted high into thin warm air, so old that every ridge has been planed down to nothing and every canyon filled with its own rubble. Nothing on top of it stands higher than a knee. Wind goes across it without touching anything. You could walk for a day up there and believe the world was finished.

Then the ground opens.

It is not a large opening. A seam, perhaps the length of a fallen tree, where two slabs of the plateau's skin have pulled apart by a hand's width and, underneath, the rock has quietly given out. The seam is dark. Air comes up out of it. The air is warmer than the air above, and it smells of something — not water, exactly, but the memory of water: dung, resin, feather, the sweetish rot of dry leaves that have never been wet enough to properly decay.

Below the seam is a room.

---

Call it a pocket. A sinkhole the plateau made for itself a geologic age ago, when there was still something down there to dissolve, and then forgot about. It is roughly round, forty paces across at the floor, and its walls rise and lean inward until they nearly meet overhead. The stone lid that remains is the same planed-flat plateau you were walking on; the seam is the only place it failed. Light comes through the seam as a single blade, and the blade moves across the room through the day like the hand of a clock, and everything in the room arranges itself around where it falls.

It is dim. Not dark — the walls are pale enough, a chalky ochre, that the one blade of light gets bounced and softened and spread into a kind of permanent late-evening glow — but dim in the way of a house with the shutters drawn. Your eyes adjust and then keep adjusting. There is always something further back you didn't see.

And the room is full.

---

Start with the walls, because that is where the city is.

Every surface of stone from the floor to the lid is built on. The lowest tier is mud — or what passes for mud here, where there is no standing water and never has been: dust worked with spit and dung and the juice of chewed stems, pressed into pellets, the pellets stacked into cups, the cups fused into terraces. Swallows made the first of these. Thousands of nests, generation on generation, each new one cemented to the ruin of an old one, until the wall is a honeycomb of mouths, some occupied and hissing, some fallen in and colonized by something else, some so old they have gone the same color as the stone and can only be told apart by a rim.

Above the swallows, the wasps. Paper cities, grey and layered, hanging like lanterns from every overhang, some as big as a torso. Above the wasps, where the wall begins to curve inward and the angle is wrong for anything that has to land, the bats have the ceiling. They are the color of the ceiling. In the daytime you know them by sound and by the dry dark streaks their leavings make down the stone.

And in the middle band, between swallow and wasp, where the wall is most nearly vertical and the light falls warmest in the afternoon — there are the other constructions. Rooms. Small, the doorways not much above waist height, the walls made of the same spit-and-dust pellets the swallows use, but pressed with palms instead of beaks. You can see the palm marks. Dozens of these rooms, clustered, stacked two and three high, connected by ledges chipped into the stone and by ladders of knotted fiber. Smoke-blackening over some of the doorways. Rows of gourd-hard fruit rinds set along the sills. Bundles of dry grass tied under the eaves. A drying rack of thin bones lashed together with sinew.

The people who live here build the way the swallows build, out of the same materials, in the same tiers, and after a few centuries it is not always obvious which is which.

---

The vegetation should not be here and is everywhere.

There is no rain on the plateau; there is no spring in the pit; there is no seep, no pool, no damp corner. Dig in the floor and you find dust, then packed dust, then stone. Yet the walls between the nests are furred green, and from every crack above the human tier hang long grey-green beards of a plant that has no roots, only a rasp of threads to hold the rock, and that drinks nothing but air. In the bottom of the pit, where the guano of ten thousand years has built up into a spongy black soil a full arm deep, grow things with thick waxy leaves and swollen stems — plants that have given up on water arriving and have decided instead to be reservoirs themselves. Their leaves are pale and bloom-dusted. Break one and it bleeds a clear gel, and something will come for the gel within a minute.

Once a day, in the hour before the light blade appears, the whole room breathes in. The high thin air outside is cold at night, and when it slides down through the seam into the warmth held by the stone, it lets go of what little it carries. The beard-plants go dark and heavy. The moss swells. Every leaf and web and mud-cup and hair and feather in the room beads with something that is not quite dew — it never forms a drop, never runs, never gathers anywhere you could cup. It is simply there for an hour, on everything, and then everything drinks it. Lizards lick it off the walls. Swallows wipe their bills along the moss. The people go along the ledges with scraps of woven fiber, pressing them to the rock and then wringing them into their mouths, into bowls, over the gourds where they keep the sour fermented pulp that is what they mostly drink. By the time the blade of sun touches the far wall, the room is dry again. It has been dry, in this sense, for longer than anything alive can measure. Water here is not a thing in the landscape. It is a thing that passes through bodies.

---

The noise starts before the light.

It starts with the bats coming home — a soft pattering rush overhead, like handfuls of dry beans thrown against the ceiling, growing until it is a continuous rustling roar. Then the swallows wake, and the swallows do not so much sing as argue, ten thousand short hard notes overlapping into a texture like tearing cloth. Under that, lower and steadier, the wasps: a hum you feel in the bone behind the ear, rising and falling as the sun warms one lantern and then the next. Under that, the click and skitter of the floor — beetles in the guano soil, the dry rattle of the lizards, something larger turning over in the leaves. Somewhere someone is grinding seed on a flat stone, and the grinding has been going on so long that the stone has a bowl in it deeper than a fist.

It is not deafening. You can talk. But you cannot hear silence anywhere in the room, at any hour, and after a while you stop expecting to.

---

The room is never still.

The blade of light comes through the seam and lays itself on the eastern wall, and everything that wants heat goes to it. Lizards — there are three or four kinds, from a fat blunt one the size of a forearm to a flicker of copper no longer than a finger — line up along the lit ledges nose to tail, close enough to touch, and turn their heads to follow it as it slides. Wasps pour out of the lanterns the moment the sun touches them and begin working the succulents for gel and the guano for grubs. Swallows stream up through the seam in a rope of bodies, out onto the plateau where there is nothing, and come back with their throats full of the small hard things that live in the nothing, and the nests scream to be fed.

Around midday the blade is on the floor, and that is when the snakes move — slow, thick, patient things, marked like the ochre wall, that live in the deepest cracks of the swallow tier and come down along the ledges when the sun is on the floor and everything else is drowsy with heat. The swallows know the snakes and rise in a shrieking sheet from whichever section of wall is being worked. The people know the snakes too. They do not kill them. A child on a ledge, thin, dusty, with a string of lizard teeth around her neck, sits with her heels drawn up and watches one pass an arm's length below her with the same flat, interested patience the snake has.

The blade climbs the western wall in the afternoon, and the people are busiest then, in the warm hour, on the ledges, doing the dozen small things that make up a day: pressing new pellets onto a wall where a room has slumped; splitting stems for fiber; scraping a hide with a chip of the black glassy stone they find in the plateau's rubble and knap into edges; picking grubs out of an old wasp lantern that has been knocked down and gone quiet. A man on the highest ledge is taking eggs from a swallow nest — one egg from each of six nests, never two from one — and the swallows are hitting him about the head and shoulders and he does not seem to notice.

Then the blade goes thin and slides up the wall and out through the seam, and the light in the room goes from evening to something deeper, and the bats wake.

The bats are the largest single event of the day. They leave through the seam in a column that lasts most of an hour — a solid pouring of bodies, chattering, the smell of them coming down on everything — and while they leave, the swallows come in, and for a while both are in the air at once and the room is a thick moving soup of wings. Something waits at the seam every evening for this. From below, in the last of the glow, you see it only as a dark shape hunched at the lip of the crack, and now and then a snap and a scatter of the column. The people watch it and say nothing. There is no one to say anything to.

---

The floor is the oldest part.

The soil there is not soil. It is the accumulated leavings of every animal that has ever slept in this room, compacted, worked, turned over by beetles, rooted through by the succulents, and it is deep enough that a spear pushed into it disappears. Dig in it and you find layers — a band of paler dust where the bats must have been fewer for a few centuries, a band of shell where something laid eggs here in great numbers and does no longer, a flat of packed floor with a hearth-black spot in it a full arm below the present floor, and the same palm-pressed pellet walls below that. The people say their grandmothers' grandmothers lived on that floor. They say it in the same tone you would say the sun rises in the east.

Nothing in the room is new. The stone stopped changing before there were swallows. The swallows have been building since before there were people. The lowest human rooms have been rebuilt so often that they are more patch than original and yet the doorways are exactly where they were, because the ledges beneath them are exactly where the swallows' first cups made a foothold, which is exactly where a fault in the ancient stone gave them purchase. The city has the shape of the room's oldest weakness.

---

At night the blade is gone and the seam is a strip of stars.

The room does not go quiet — the floor gets louder, if anything, as the beetles and the things that eat beetles come out — but the pitch of it drops. The wasp-hum stops. The swallows settle into a grumbling. On the ledges, small fires of dried stem and bat dung, no bigger than a cupped hand, glow in a few of the doorways and then go out. The warmth of the stone holds. Up on the plateau the air is thin and bitterly cold and there is nothing, in any direction, for a day's walk; down here it is close and warm and crowded and smells of a hundred kinds of breath.

Before dawn the cold air slides in through the seam and meets the warm air rising, and the room breathes in, and everything goes damp for the one hour it is ever going to, and the licking and wiping and wringing begins.

Then the bats come home. Then the blade.
