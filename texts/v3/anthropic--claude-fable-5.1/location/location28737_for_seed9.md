The first thing you notice is that nothing here is old.

You come up onto the yard through a door in a wall, and the wall is poured concrete with the grain of the formwork still crisp in it, and the sill of the door has no dip worn into it, and the bolts holding the frame have no rust-run beneath them, only the faint bloom of first oxidation, as though the iron were still deciding whether to corrode. Everything has this quality. Edges are true. Corners are corners. The world has not yet started rounding anything off.

It is high. You know this from the air more than from any view, because there is no view. The yard is walled on three sides by more of the concrete and on the fourth by a cloud that stands against the parapet like a fourth wall, opaque and slow and white-grey, moving in a single body. Cloud comes over the parapet in a continuous shallow spill, breaks up into strands among the racks and planters, and disappears. It is warm. The warmth comes up through the floor plates — a bodily, breathing warmth, like standing on the flank of an animal — and it is this warmth that eats the cloud. You breathe in and the air is thick and green and tastes faintly of hot metal and wet lime.

The floor is grating. Beneath the grating, a metre down, there is another floor, and that one is solid: a dark, glassy, pocked material with a dull sheen, laid in slabs that have not quite merged with each other. Slag. Between the slabs there are seams where something paler has welled up and set. Down there, in the gap between the two floors, it is warmer still, and you can hear water dripping onto hot surfaces and flashing into steam with a sound like a match being struck, over and over, unhurried, never quite in rhythm.

This is what the place is for, or was for, or is still for; it is hard to tell. It is a yard where ground is being made.

---

The racks run in rows. They are steel, tall, three tiers each, and every tier holds long troughs, and every trough is full. Not of soil — there is no soil here, nothing you could honestly call soil — but of a coarse dark crumb, like coffee grounds mixed with broken glass, which is slag again, crushed and washed and sitting in the warmth and wet until something can grow in it. Something has. Out of every trough, over the lips, down the uprights, along the cross-braces, the growth comes: moss first, in fat pillowy mounds so saturated they release water when you look at them; then liverworts, flat and scaled and glistening; then ferns, unrolling; then things with true leaves, broad and dark, that have no business at this altitude and are here anyway because the floor is warm and the cloud never stops.

The plants are lush the way plants are lush in a greenhouse that nobody has thinned. They lean into one another. Fronds lie across fronds. A climbing thing with heart-shaped leaves has gone up one rack, across a run of pipe, and is coming down the next rack head-first, and where it touches the trough below it has put out roots into the slag-crumb without waiting to reach the ground. You cannot walk between the rows without pushing through, and when you push through, the leaves leave water on you and the water is warm.

There is a rule here you work out slowly. The plants grow only where there is *made* ground. On the bare grating, where cloud-water pools in the mesh and drains, nothing grows, not even algae. On the concrete walls, nothing. On the pipes, nothing — the vines use them but do not root in them. But wherever the slag-crumb has been laid, or has spilled, or has been tracked in a boot-print from one place to another, there is a green so intense it looks poured. As if the crushed slag were the only thing in the world the plants recognised as earth.

---

There is a channel. It runs along the base of the eastern wall, cast into the concrete, and cloud-water runs in it, and the water is faintly milky and warm to the wrist. It follows the channel exactly. It has not undercut a single edge. It goes where it was told to go, turns the corner it was given, drops the step it was given, and vanishes into a grated sump at the yard's low point with a soft, continuous, throaty sound. A stream a hundred years old would have found its own line by now. This one is still obedient.

Above the sump, a pipe rises, elbows, and runs up the wall into a machine.

The machine is the largest thing in the yard and the quietest. It is a squat cylinder of riveted plate two storeys high, with flanged ports, a pressure gauge whose glass has fogged over from the inside, and a single flywheel the size of a cartwheel that is turning. Slowly. One revolution in perhaps half a minute. There is no sound from it except, when you put your hand on the casing, a hum you feel in your teeth rather than hear. The casing is warm. Every so often — you will wait a long time for it — a valve somewhere on its shoulder lifts with a sigh, releases a slow pale plume of steam into the general cloud, and settles again.

Moss has started on the machine's north side. Only there. Only a hand's breadth. Where the slag dust has settled.

---

Behind the racks, against the western wall, is where the work is. Or where the work has paused.

There are moulds. Long rectangular troughs of iron, open-topped, in a row on rails, and some of them are full of the dark glassy slab-material and some are half full and one is empty and pooled with rainwater. Above them, on a gantry, is a ladle — a great rusted-looking bucket, though it is not rust, it is scale, it is the colour a thing goes when it has held liquid rock — tipped forward at an angle, as if it had been about to pour and had been told to wait. Beneath the spout, a stalactite of the slab-material has formed and set, a long dark drip frozen mid-fall, and at its tip, impossibly, a tuft of moss.

The rails run to a doorway in the western wall. The doorway is dark. There is warmer air coming out of it, and a smell like a struck flint, and very faintly, far down, a sound you cannot place: rhythmic, deep, slow, like something breathing in its sleep. That is the furnace. That is what warms the floor. That is where the ground comes from before it is ground.

You understand, then, roughly, the sequence. Rock is melted below. Slag is poured into moulds here and set into slabs, and the slabs become floor. Other slag is crushed to crumb and racked, and cloud is drawn up the mountain and fed to it, and in the warmth and the wet the crumb becomes — is becoming — something a root can hold. When the troughs are ready they will be emptied out onto the slabs. Then there will be a landscape here where there is now only a yard. Then grass, presumably. Then something else. The plants have not waited for permission. They have started early, on the racks, in the moulds, on the stalactite.

Whether anyone is coming back to finish is the question the place does not answer.

---

There is not much that moves under its own power.

Once, a long-legged bird — grey, heron-shaped, but with a strange crest that lies flat in the wet — stands in the channel with the milk-warm water running over its feet, motionless for so long you take it for an ornament. Then it takes one step and stabs at the water and comes up with nothing, and stands again. There cannot be fish. It stays anyway, perhaps for the warmth on its feet. Later it is gone, and you did not see it leave, and you are not certain it was ever there.

There are the moths. Small, pale, a great many of them, and they do not fly; they sit on the undersides of the leaves in rows, wings folded into little tents, and when the valve on the machine sighs, all of them shiver at once. That is all they do. You will not see them do anything else.

And there is something in the racks — a sound of moving leaves where no wind is — that stays ahead of you and that you never catch up to.

---

By afternoon (if it is afternoon; the light does not much change, only brightens and dims as the cloud thins and thickens, a flat luminous grey that casts no shadows and makes every leaf the same distance away) the cloud has come further over the parapet and is sitting in the yard to shoulder height. The tops of the racks show above it like the tops of trees above a flooded field. The flywheel turns. The valve sighs. The channel-water runs in the line it was given. Under the floor, the dripping continues, strike after strike, each one a small breath of steam that comes up through the grating and joins the rest.

There is no wind, and then there is a wind, all at once, warm and downward, and the cloud in the yard is pressed flat and pushed out through the racks in long streamers and for perhaps a minute you can see the whole of it: the walls, the moulds, the paused ladle, the wall of green on its steel bones, the concrete floor beyond the grating where nothing grows, the cast letters over the western doorway that say a number and a word you don't know. Then the wind stops and the cloud stands up again and comes back over the parapet in the same place, at the same rate, as though it had been waiting.

You put your hand down on the slag-crumb in a trough. It is warm, and wet, and not yet earth, and full of roots.
