The basin lies low enough that you feel the air thicken as you come down into it. The hills around its rim are old and rounded, grey-brown, worn into the shapes that rock takes when nothing has bothered it for a very long time. From up there the floor looks white, and level, and steaming in perhaps two dozen places, each plume a good walk from the next.

Down on the crust the whiteness resolves. It is sinter — silica laid down grain by grain out of hot water until it has built a floor a hand's breadth thick over the whole valley, cracked into plates that ring faintly underfoot. In places the white shades to buff, to rust, to a hard glossy black where the water carries iron. Around some vents there are crusts of sulphur the yellow of a chemical, growing in needles that break if you look at them. The ground is warm through your soles. Not hot — warm the way a wall is warm on the shaded side of a building that has stood all day in the sun.

Nothing grows. Nothing has grown here in any span you could name. There is no soil for it, and the crust bleeds acid, and what falls from the sky as rain comes back up out of the ground as something else. What there is instead lives in the pools: mats of colour laid down in bands where the water cools as it spreads from each source — orange nearest, then green, then a brown that is nearly black at the outer edge. They are the oldest kind of life and the simplest, and they are content. Brine flies stand on them in the thousands, and a small dark bird comes down at intervals to walk along the margins eating flies, and that is the whole of what moves here on legs. Once a day, roughly, a hawk crosses high over the basin without descending. It does not seem to be looking for anything.

Then there is the sound.

It begins before you reach the floor — a broad low tone that seems to come from the ground itself, felt in the chest more than heard. As you walk out onto the crust it resolves into parts. A steady roaring hiss from the nearest vent, exactly like a boiler venting past a relief valve. Behind it, from farther off, a rhythmic thud, thud, thud where a pool the size of a room lifts and slaps its own surface at three-second intervals and has done so, the sinter around it suggests, since before there were people anywhere. From the east side a whistle — a real whistle, high and clean, a steam whistle without a factory — where a fissure narrows to a slot and the escaping vapour finds its pitch. Somewhere underneath everything a knocking, irregular but unhurried, like a heavy piston working at the bottom of a shaft. No one has ever found the source of the knocking. There is nothing to find. It is water and stone and gas doing what they do under pressure, and there is a great deal of pressure.

It is loud enough that you speak up without deciding to, and after an hour you stop speaking, because the basin does not seem to want conversation.

The air is wet. Not with rain — it rains here as elsewhere, occasionally, and the rain vanishes into the crust before it has properly landed — but with the steam, which rises from every vent and cools and falls back and never entirely leaves. It beads on rock. It beads on you. Anything you set down acquires within a few hours a fine grey film, and by the second day the film has a texture, and by the end of a week a metal blade left on the ground has begun to be part of the ground. The basin does not tolerate objects. Nobody has ever put up so much as a marker post here, and the reason is not prohibition but futility: the crust grows over and around whatever it is given. The place remains unbuilt because it will not stay built.

And yet. Walk long enough between the plumes and you start to notice the way the sinter has shaped itself around the vents. Rounded collars. Smooth ridged cylinders where a jet has laid down its own casing over centuries. Flanges. Something like a manifold, where three fissures join. A surface at the northern pool that is broad and flat and faintly grooved, as though milled. None of it is made. All of it looks made — has the finish of tooled parts — and the impression, standing among it while the vents roar and the pool slaps and the whistle holds its one note, is not of a wild place but of a works. An installation. Something engineered on a scale that makes engineering beside the point, running unattended, producing sulphur and silica and heat at a fixed rate for a market that does not exist.

The smell supports this. Sulphur, yes, and hot mineral water, but also, near the black-stained vents on the western edge, a distinct oily note where bitumen seeps up with the water and lies in tarry lenses on the crust. It smells like a machine shop. It smells like something has just been running and has not yet cooled down. Nothing has just been running. Everything has been running for ten thousand years.

What holds you, finally, is the steadiness. Each vent has its interval and keeps it. The whistle has not changed pitch in the memory of anyone who has been asked. The pool lifts and falls, lifts and falls, and if you sit on the warm crust with your back to a sinter collar and watch it for an afternoon you find that your own breathing has been taken over by it, slowed and regularised, so that the basin's rhythm is now yours. The steam goes up and comes down. The light stays the same grey-white diffusion, bright enough, shadowless, the sun a paler patch in the moving vapour. The bird eats flies. The hills do not move. Weather comes over the rim occasionally and cannot get purchase; the basin makes its own air and keeps it. There is no gradient to ride. Whatever this place is doing, it finished becoming it long ago and has simply continued.

You could stay a long time. It would not notice. The knocking would go on under your feet, and the crust would begin, gently and without malice, to grow over your boots.
