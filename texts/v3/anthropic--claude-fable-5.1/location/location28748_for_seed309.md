You come to it by going down, but not far — a slope, not a shaft. The soil gives out after the first few body-lengths and after that there is no soil at all, only root, and the root has been walked on so long that it has gone hard and dark and faintly glossy, like the treads of an old stair. The passage narrows, then bells outward, and you are inside.

The first thing is the noise. It arrives before the shape of the place does, and for a moment it's all there is: a low engine-note that seems to come up through the floor into the long bones of your legs, and above that a wet mechanical percussion, irregular but not random, like a great valve opening and closing somewhere behind the walls. Under both of those, a hiss — a fine continuous seething, the sound of a very large number of small things moving at once. It takes a while to sort them out from each other. It takes longer to stop expecting it to quiet down.

Then the eyes catch up.

The chamber is not large. Call it the size of a village chapel, or a wide bay in an old boathouse — long enough that the far end is soft in the dimness, not so long that you couldn't shout across it if shouting could be heard. It is entirely root. Not roots, plural, woven — one root, or what has become one, a mass so old and so fused that the seams between individual limbs have healed over and only the grain remembers them. The ceiling is a shallow vault of knuckled wood. The walls are wood, swelling and receding. The floor is wood, worn down in a broad shallow trough along the chamber's length, like a riverbed, and polished there almost to glass. Nothing here was quarried or set. Everything was grown, and then used, and the use made the shape.

The light is the color of the inside of an eyelid: a dull warm amber, sourceless at first. Then you see where it lives. Set into the walls and running along the vault, half-buried in the wood like veins under skin, are conduits — long vessels, some no thicker than a wrist, some you could crawl through — and they are faintly translucent, and inside them the wood is bright. Not bright like a lamp. Bright like a coal seen through ash. The glow comes in slow pulses that don't match the valve-percussion and don't match each other, so the chamber breathes light at three or four different rates, and the shadows on the floor swim.

It is warm. The warmth has a smell, resinous and animal both, a stable-and-sawmill smell, and it is damp without being wet. The vault sweats. Condensation beads on the underside of every bulge and rib, and gathers, and runs down the grain in slow threads to collect in the hollows where the floor dips — a scatter of dark pools no bigger than basins, most of them, each one skinned with a film of something living. Every so often a drop lets go from the ceiling and falls the full height with a sound you can't hear over everything else, and you only know because you see the ring spread.

---

What is strange takes a while to come into focus, because at first you read it as a ruin, and it isn't one.

Look at the conduits again. Where two of them meet, there is a swelling of the wood — a node, a gland — and the gland *works*. It clenches. You can watch it: a slow contraction that travels along its length and squeezes whatever is inside further down the line. The percussion you heard on the way in is these, dozens of them, all through the walls, each one opening and shutting on its own count. Some of what they move is sap, viscous and lit from within. Some of it is water. And some of it — you see this when a conduit passes close enough to the surface to show its contents — is animals. Small ones, packed, moving in a dense sliding column like grain in a chute, many-legged and pale and entirely unbothered.

The plant is plumbing. The plant is also, in some sense you don't have the words for yet, an engine. Here and there along the walls the wood has been drawn out into fibers, cords of it running taut from one anchor to another, and the fibers *hum* — that is the engine-note, that is what comes up through the floor — and when you get close to one you feel the fine hair on your arms lift. There is charge in them. Somewhere the plant is making a voltage, a great slow living battery, and the fibers carry it, and where a fiber enters a conduit the conduit's glow flares and its gland clenches harder. It is a nervous system doing the work of a mill. Or a mill that has been alive so long it has grown nerves.

Nobody built this in the sense of standing and planning it. But it was built. That is the rule of the place, and you can read it in every surface: *whatever is used, thickens*. A path walked becomes a groove; a groove becomes a trough; a trough, if the traffic keeps up long enough, closes over and becomes a tube. The conduits were once open channels. You can see one that hasn't finished — a half-pipe along the north wall, its lips curling inward but not yet met, and things still running along its floor in the open. Somewhere in the depth of the root, the plant registers pressure and load and responds the only way it knows, by adding material where the strain is and letting it wither where the strain has gone. The animals wore the roads. The root made them permanent. Neither party ever agreed to anything. The whole system is very old and very settled, and the settlement is not stillness — it is a kind of equilibrium that hums.

---

The animals.

It is not that there are many. It is that there are more than the space has room to hold, and it is only the sparseness of the chamber — its long empty floor, its high ceiling, all that dim air between one presence and the next — that keeps this from being obvious at every moment. The chamber is a junction. Things pass through it. They pass through in *tides*.

Between tides it is almost quiet, or what passes for quiet here, and you can count the residents. In the wall-pockets — cavities where a limb rotted out long ago and the surrounding wood scarred smooth around the absence — there are colonies of something small and gray and soft-furred that hang in packed rows and turn their heads all together when the light pulses. In the floor-pools, blind pale salamander-shapes lie with only their gill-fringes moving, the fringes a deep pink in the amber, feeding on the film. Along the taut humming fibers there are beetles — flat, iridescent, the length of a thumb — that cling motionless with their undersides pressed to the cord, and they are not resting; they are *drawing*. When one lets go and drops, its shell throws off a faint blue crackle before it hits the floor. Up in the vault, where the wood is dry and the conduits pass closest to the ceiling, there is a permanent slow-moving crust of something — mites, or the larvae of the beetles, or both — that shifts across the glow like weather on a lit window.

And there are the gaskets. This is the strangest thing in a strange place. Where a gland meets a conduit there is a seam, and the seam does not seal itself. What seals it is an animal — a pale, boneless, ring-shaped thing that has grown into the gap, that lives its whole life pressed between the two surfaces, opening its body when the gland opens and clamping when it shuts. You can see them at every join. Some are as small as a fingernail. Some, on the great conduits, are as long as your forearm laid in a circle, their skin gone the same amber as the wood around them, so that only the wet shine and a faint independent twitch tell you they are not part of the plant. They eat what leaks. Nothing leaks much. It is not clear which came first, the seal or the seam, or whether the root grew the gap to the animal's size or the animal grew to fit the gap; but the fit is perfect, and the fit is ancient, and the whole system would fail in an hour without them.

Then a tide comes.

You hear it first as a change in the seething — a rising, a thickening — and one of the great conduits along the east wall begins to bulge along its length, a swelling that travels toward you, and the gland at its mouth clamps and the gasket-creature there stretches and goes translucent with strain and then the gland *lets go*, and the animals come out.

Not one kind. Never one kind. A rush of the pale many-legged things, spilling across the floor in a sheet; and among them, taller, a loping wet-furred form with a long snout and too many toes, dozens of them, moving in a shoving pack and paying the smaller animals under their feet no attention whatsoever; and above the pack, released from somewhere in the ceiling by the same pulse, a fall of the gray soft things dropping from their pockets and taking to the air, so that the whole space is full of them for the length of a breath, a storm of small bodies, wings and nothing-wings, all following the trough in the floor toward the western wall where another gland has already begun to open to receive them. The sound is enormous. The floor drums. Drops shake loose from the vault and come down like rain and the floor-pools shiver and the salamander-shapes vanish into their depths.

And with the tide come the ones who hunt it. You don't see them arrive. You see a place in the flood where the flood *parts*, a wedge of empty floor, and something moving in the wedge that is the same color as the wood, low and fast and jointed the wrong way, and then a gray body goes down and does not get up and the flood closes over the place. Three of them, maybe four. They take what they take and they are gone before the gland on the west wall has finished closing. What they leave, the floor-pool things surface for. Nothing is wasted. Nothing stays on the floor long enough to be waste.

The tide passes in what might be a minute. The western gland shuts. Its gasket relaxes and dims. The last stragglers — a few of the many-legged, a single long-snouted animal limping — find their own way into the smaller conduits and are gone. The seething drops back to its baseline. The chamber is nearly empty again, high and dim and warm, the humming fibers, the slow pulse of light, the drip.

It happens again before you have caught your breath. From a different conduit. A different mix.

---

Sit in it a while. Let it show you its timing.

The tides come in a rhythm that isn't regular but isn't random either. Something upstream is making decisions — or something upstream is a great valve that opens when a pressure is reached, and the pressure builds at the rate the animals arrive, and the animals arrive at the rate the plant elsewhere lets them, and so on back through the root in every direction until the question of *who decides* stops meaning anything. The plant keeps the schedule. The animals keep to it. The gaskets tell you when a gland is about to go: they tighten a moment before, and their color changes. The beetles on the fibers come off in a scatter just before the big east conduit opens, every time, and you realize they are not falling — they are dropping to clear the charge, because the surge that drives the gland runs down that fiber and would cook them where they cling.

The residents know all of this. The gray things in the wall-pockets go quiet and still a few seconds ahead of each tide and then come alive all at once. The floor-pool things surface at the right moment to catch what falls. Even the hunters know it; that is how they are always there in time. The whole chamber is a set of animals that have made themselves into the schedule's instruments, and the schedule is a plant's slow heart, and the plant's heart runs on the animals' passage. You cannot pull one thread out. That is what *established* means here. Not stone-still. Wound so tight for so long that nothing can be moved without everything moving.

The water keeps its own slow time under all of it. The sweat on the vault gathers and falls and pools and is drunk and evaporates in the warmth and rises and condenses and gathers again. Sometimes a gland somewhere upstream vents pure water instead of sap or animals — you can tell by the sound, a hollow gush instead of a scramble — and the eastern conduit glows clear for a moment and the floor-trough runs an inch deep from end to end and drains away through cracks you hadn't seen, and for a little while afterward the whole chamber drips at once, and the smell changes, and the salamander-things rise to the surface of their basins and lie there in the false rain with their pink fringes spread.

---

Where it goes, in either direction, you can't see. The conduits leave the chamber and enter the root and the root is everything. Somewhere above there is a surface and presumably the enormous thing this is the root *of* — a tree, if it is a tree, so old and so wide that its underground half has become a country, and this chamber a single crossing in it, one node among who knows how many, each one humming, each one with its own gaskets and residents and hunters and its own dim pulse. You will not see the tree. Nothing down here has seen it. The gray things have eyes the size of pinheads and the salamander-shapes have none and the many-legged tide does not look up.

But it is easy, sitting on the warm wood with the noise in your bones, to think that the tree does not much matter — that whatever it does up in the light, in the wind, is a kind of idle exhalation, and this is the working part. The part where the pumps are. The part where things are moved, and eaten, and moved again, in the amber dark, on a schedule older than any single life that has ever kept it, in a chamber whose shape is nothing but the sum of every passage that has ever crossed it and every join that has ever needed a body to hold it shut.

A gasket on the north wall tightens and goes the color of weak tea. The beetles let go of the fiber. The seething rises.

Here it comes again.
