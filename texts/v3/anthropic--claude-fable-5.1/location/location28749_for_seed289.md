There is a room under the field. It is not large — you could lie down in it, if there were anywhere to lie down — and it has never been finished.

Start with the walls, because the walls are the first strange thing. They are not stone yet. They are the moment before stone: a pale wet mineral in the act of setting, calcite and clay in the posture of hardening, and they have held that posture for longer than anyone has counted. Press a thumb into the wall and it would give, slightly, the way cheese gives. But no one presses. The surface is dimpled everywhere with small hemispheres, each one the beginning of a crystal that decided to begin and then did nothing further. Under a hand lens you would see edges trying to form, planes trying to meet at their proper angles, and not quite. The geology of this room is a sentence with no verb. It is all subject.

Now the light. It should be dark here — it is two arm-lengths below the roots of the grass, with no shaft, no crack, no way in — and instead it is bright. Not the gold-through-the-eyelids bright of sun, but a white, level, sourceless brightness, the brightness of the inside of a shell. There are no shadows because the light does not come from anywhere. It comes from everything equally: from the unset walls, from the water in the low places, from the skin of the animals. If you could stand inside, you would cast nothing on the floor. You would look at your own hand and see it lit from below and above and behind at once, flattened, like a specimen.

And the room is full.

That is the thing to understand: the room has no room in it. From the floor to the low ceiling it is packed, body to body, with the living. Newts, mostly — the small mud-colored kind with flanks the color of old butter — pressed flank to flank, their throats against each other's tails, their toes spread on each other's backs. Between the newts, filling every gap that a newt's shape leaves, isopods, hundreds of them, gray and segmented and still. Where there is water — and there is water, standing in three or four depressions in the floor, and beading on every surface of the ceiling in droplets that never quite gather the weight to fall — there are snails, and something like a leech, and a fish. One fish. Blind, the color of a fingernail, wedged in a pocket of water no larger than a cupped pair of hands, its gills moving. It has been in that pocket long enough that the pocket has its shape.

Nothing moves. Nothing can. But nothing is dead, either. Eyes are open. The newts' eyes are open, gold with a black horizontal slit, and they are looking — at the wall, at each other's undersides, at the one blind fish. The air, or what stands in for air here, is exactly as wet as it can be without becoming water. What condenses on the ceiling is precisely what lifts off the floor. Every droplet is in balance with every other droplet. If one were to fall, the whole arrangement would have to adjust, and so none does.

Here is the sound. It is loud. It is loud the way a struck bell is loud at the exact instant after it is struck — a full, round, metallic note that fills the ear to the edges — except that the instant does not end. The room rings. It has one pitch, somewhere in the low middle of what a voice could reach, and the pitch has no wobble, no decay, no swell. It is not clear what makes it. The walls do not vibrate. The animals do not breathe in unison, or rather they do, but not in a way that could produce this. The best guess is that the sound is the sound of the light, or the light is the sight of the sound, that in a place this full and this still the senses have run together like paints in a jar of water that nobody stirs.

Vegetation: almost none. From the ceiling, in two or three places, root hairs of the grass above have come through the unset mineral — white threads, finer than sewing thread, hanging into the packed mass of animals and ending in nothing, in a newt's shoulder, in the air. They have not grown since they arrived. There is a smear of something green on the eastern wall, at what would be knee height, that is either a moss or the memory of one.

And there are marks. This is the part that the newts, if they could report, would report first. On the wall opposite the green smear is a handprint — an adult hand, fingers splayed, pushed into the mineral when the mineral was as soft as it still is. The impression is perfect. The lines of the palm are in it. Below the handprint, on the floor, mostly buried under isopods and one large newt lying belly-down, is a ring of eight fist-sized stones set in a circle, the kind of circle a person makes to hold a fire, though no fire could have burned here and nothing in the ring is blackened. And in the northeast corner, upright, is a small pot. Clay, unglazed, the height of a forearm, its mouth open. It is full of newts. They are packed into it exactly as densely as they are packed into the room, and they look out of the pot's mouth as if from a doorway.

Whoever made these things made them and left, or made them and did not leave. There is no way to tell. Nothing of them is here except the shape of one hand and the choice of eight stones over seven.

The rule of the place, as far as a rule can be inferred, is this: the room reached its condition and then declined to alter it. It is not frozen — frozen implies cold, and this is only cool, the cool of a cellar, the cool of a cheek pressed to a stone floor in summer. It is not stopped — the fish's gills are moving; the light is being emitted; the note is being sounded. It is simply *balanced*, and balanced so completely, on so many axes at once, that there is no direction left in which anything could tip. The walls cannot finish becoming rock because becoming would require a change, and every change would have to be paid for out of some other change, and the account is closed. The droplets cannot fall. The newts cannot leave, and do not, so far as anyone knows, wish to. Whatever enters — a root hair, a pot, a hand — is absorbed into the arrangement and given a place, and then the arrangement closes over it and rings.

There is a field above. Grass, ordinary grass, and the wind moving through it in the usual way, and rain that comes and goes, and cattle sometimes. The cattle stand on the field over the room and do not know it is there. Their weight does nothing to it. Rain does not get in. In winter the ground freezes to a depth of a hand and the room, below that, stays cool and damp and white and loud and full. In summer the grass roots reach downward and two or three of them, every century or so, break through the ceiling into the brightness and stop, and hang there, and are counted, and become part of the total.

Nobody has been inside. Nobody could be. There is no room in the room. But if you put your ear to the ground in the right place — a place marked by nothing, a place the grass does not distinguish — you might, in the right silence, hear the one note coming up through the soil, and feel under your cheek a coolness a little cooler than the earth around it, and understand that beneath you a hand is pressed into a wall, and a fish is looking at nothing with its eyes that do not work, and a thousand small gold eyes are looking at the fish, and all of it is lit, and none of it will ever, ever move.
