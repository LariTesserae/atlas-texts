The first thing is the sound, and it arrives before you understand where you are: a low, continuous roar, pitched somewhere between a furnace and a crowd, with a rhythm under it that is too slow to be mechanical. Then the dim comes into focus around it, and you see that you are inside a room, and that the room is full of the sea.

It was a pumping hall. That much is still legible. A long rectangular chamber, perhaps forty paces end to end and fifteen across, with a ceiling that vaults into ribbed concrete and iron six or seven meters overhead. There is nothing here that was not made. The floor is poured concrete scored into a grid of shallow drainage channels. The walls are brick behind a skin of cement render, and where the render has gone the brick shows in courses so regular they look drawn. Steel beams cross the ceiling on riveted brackets. There is no rock, no sand, no mud that came from anywhere but here: the silt on the floor is rust that flaked from the machinery, paint that lifted in sheets and settled, lime that leached out of the mortar, and shell, generation on generation of shell, ground into a pale grey flour by the tide.

The room lies below the waterline of a coast that no longer has any use for it. Above the ceiling there is more built mass—earth-fill, concrete, the footings of something that may have been a road. The sea enters through the intake: a pair of tunnels, each large enough to stand in, that open through the far wall behind the skeletons of bar screens. It exits through a rank of outflow culverts in the near wall, lower down, half-buried in the shell-flour. Between the two the water moves. That is the roar.

---

The machines stand in a line down the center of the hall on a raised concrete plinth, four of them, spaced evenly, with wide gaps between. They were centrifugal pumps driven by something—steam once, later electric motors bolted to the same beds. What remains of them is difficult to name as machinery. The casings are whole but no longer have edges; they have been rounded by rust until they look poured rather than cast. The rust itself has stopped. It reached the state where oxide protects iron and then went no further, and over it grew the first barnacles, and over those the first mussels, and the mussels died and their shells cemented, and the colony grew on the cement, and so on for long enough that each pump now wears a rind a hand-span thick, hard as slag, colored a dull violet-brown in this light. The rind follows the original shape loosely, the way a snowdrift follows a fence.

Only one thing still moves as it was designed to. The third pump carries a flywheel nearly two meters across, and the flywheel turns. Not under power—there has been no power here for a span of time you would measure in the thickness of shell—but because the tide pushes water through the hall and the wheel is a wheel. It is very slow. One revolution takes most of a minute. The bearing has worn to something that is no longer a bearing so much as a shape water has agreed to pass through, and with each revolution there is a sound: a deep wooden knock, then a shriek that trails off into the general noise, then nothing for a while. Knock. Shriek. Roar. It has been doing this so long that the rim of the wheel is polished where the water passes fastest and the barnacles could not hold.

---

The light comes from one place. Near the intake end, a section of the ceiling failed—not recently; the edges of the break are as encrusted as everything else—and above it, through ten or twelve meters of green water, there is daylight. It arrives as a soft column, not a beam, greyish-green, without enough definition to cast shadows. It falls on the two pumps nearest the far wall and on the floor between them, and everything else recedes into a brown that is not quite darkness. You can see the whole room the way you can see a room at dusk with your eyes adjusted: shapes, distances, the suggestion of texture, no color that is not a tone of the same color.

Under the light-fall, and nowhere else, there is growth of the vegetable kind. A patch of the floor and the flanks of the second pump carry a low turf of red algae, coarse and dark, with a few strands of something longer that streams in the current and lies down when the current stops. On the highest ledge of the fallen ceiling slab, where the light is strongest, a single stalk of kelp has taken hold, thin, perhaps a meter long, tattered at the tip. That is the extent of it. Move ten paces into the shade and the surfaces are bare of anything green: only the encrusting animals, only the shell-rind, only rust.

The water is cool—not the cold of the deep, but the cold of the shade in a north-facing place, a cold that comes from never having been reached by the sun. It is also very clear, which is unexpected for a room so full of sediment. The tide keeps it swept. What silt is disturbed in the flood settles out in the slack, and by the time the ebb begins the water has cleared again to the point where you can see the far wall from the near one, dim as it is.

---

The animals are everywhere the light is not, and this is the second thing you notice after the sound: that the room is populated far beyond what the emptiness of the space suggests. The emptiness is a matter of arrangement. The hall is largely open water, and the open water is largely unoccupied, and the four pumps stand at wide intervals with nothing between them. But every surface—floor, wall, ceiling, plinth, machine—is a pavement of living or once-living things, and the population moves.

Mussels first, because they are the ground everything else stands on: a continuous bed in the current-swept places, packed, oriented, their shells gaping to the flood and closing to the ebb in a wave you can see pass down the hall as the tide turns. Barnacles on the mussels. Anemones in the crevices of the machinery where the rind has cracked, dozens of them, small, dull orange and dull white, all stretched out in the current like an audience facing the same way. Sponges in the deep shade under the plinth, slumped and grey. Tube worms with their fans out, the fans withdrawing all at once when a shadow crosses.

Then the mobile ones. Shore crabs in numbers you would not believe in a natural place—the drainage channels in the floor are a grid of runs and they are used as runs, crabs moving along them purposefully from one machine to another, sidling under the shell-rind where it overhangs. Larger crabs in the outflow culverts, backed into the dark with only their claws showing. Blennies and gobies on every ledge, in every rivet-hole, flicking from one hold to the next. A shoal of small silver fish, pollack or something like them, occupying the middle of the hall in a loose cloud that contracts when the flywheel knocks and expands again in the interval. Under the intake screens, where the current is strongest, a single large wrasse holds position without visible effort, facing into the flow, taking whatever the tide brings through the bars.

And there is predation, and it is ordinary. A crab has a goby. A larger crab has the smaller crab. Something long and low—an eel, or a fish with the habits of one—comes out of the outflow culvert at the turn of the tide and moves along the base of the plinth and goes back in. Starfish are working the mussel bed at the slow pace of starfish, and where they have been the bed shows bare patches of rust the color of dried blood, and the bare patches are already dusted with the white specks of new barnacle spat. The room eats itself and grows itself at the same rate, and has done so for long enough that the rate is stable.

---

The sound resolves, if you stay. The roar is the intake: two tunnels of moving water compressing through the gaps in the bar screens, a turbulence that never fully stops because even at slack the swell above pushes and pulls. On top of that, a crackle like frying, which is the snapping shrimp in the mussel bed—thousands of them, each one a click, together a static that fills the high range. Under it, when you listen for it, a series of grunts and croaks in no rhythm: fish, calling in the dim, the kind of thing that only makes sense in a place where sight is nearly useless and the water carries sound so well. The mussels themselves, closing, make a sound like a handful of gravel dropped on a plate, but only when the shoal spooks and they all close at once. The building groans—not often, and not alarmingly, a settling of iron against concrete when the tide load shifts. And the flywheel, which is the metronome: knock, shriek, roar. Knock, shriek, roar.

It is loud in the way a factory is loud, which is to say continuously and structurally, so that after a while you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as the shape of the room. The far end sounds different from the near end. The space under the plinth sounds different from the space above it. Silence, when it comes—at the exact moment of slack water, for a minute or two, twice a day—is so complete that the shrimp become deafening and the fish stop calling and the flywheel, released, drifts to a stop with a last long grinding sigh, and the whole room waits. Then the ebb begins, and the roar comes back from the other direction, and the wheel catches and turns the other way.

---

The tide is the only weather here. It comes in through the intake and goes out through the culverts on the flood, and reverses on the ebb, and everything in the hall is arranged around those two directions. The anemones face one way, then the other. The algae streams one way, then the other. The crabs work the upstream side of each machine for what the water brings, and switch sides when the water switches. The silt migrates in a slow figure-eight across the floor, thicker at one end at high water and thicker at the other at low. The polished band on the flywheel rim is polished on both faces.

There is a spring-tide version of the room and a neap-tide version. On the springs the roar is enough to feel in the chest, the shoal presses itself against the lee of the pumps, the light-fall through the ceiling break is troubled by the swell above until it flickers, and things come loose—a sheet of rind, a mussel clump too heavy for its anchor—and are carried down the hall and into the culverts and gone. On the neaps the hall is almost calm, the current a slow drift you could swim against, the shrimp-crackle dominant, the crabs out in the open on the plinth as if it were a square in a town. Between these two states everything cycles, and nothing that cycles has changed the room in longer than any inhabitant's lifetime.

That is the fact of the place. It has arrived. The iron will not rust further because the rind seals it. The concrete will not spall further because the surface it presents to the sea is no longer concrete but shell. The ceiling break will not widen because its edges are armored. The flywheel will turn until the bearing is worn to nothing, but the bearing wears at a rate the rind repairs, and so it may simply turn. Somebody built this to move water from one side of a wall to the other, and the water moves from one side of the wall to the other, and the machine that was meant to do it participates, in its way, turning because it is turned. The purpose has been kept and the purpose has been reversed, and the room does not seem to mind which.

At the intake end, in the light-fall, the wrasse holds its place against the flow. The kelp stalk lies over and stands up. The knock comes, and the shriek, and the shoal contracts and opens. In the drainage channel nearest the outflow a crab is carrying half of another crab toward the dark, and the dark is full of small bright eyes, and the tide is coming in.
