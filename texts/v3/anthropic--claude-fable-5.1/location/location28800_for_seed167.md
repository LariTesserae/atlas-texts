Start with the dark, because that's what there is most of.

It isn't the dark of a room with the lamp out. It's the dark of a place where light has never been a factor — where the word for the sea's surface would be meaningless to anything living here, because the surface is a mile and a half of water away and no photon has ever made the trip. Eyes here are vestigial or absent or repurposed. The dark is not an absence in this place. It is the medium, the way air is a medium for a forest.

The second thing is the warmth. Come down through the cold, black column and at some point the temperature stops falling and begins, strangely, to rise. Not hot — warm, the warmth of a hand held near but not on. The water tastes of eggs and metal. It is coming up out of the seabed itself, through cracks in a low, broad rise of old basalt that the ocean has been leaning on for a very long time.

And on that rise, something has been built.

---

Not by anyone. There are no tools here, no hands, no intention that would recognize itself as such. But the word *built* is still the right word, because the structure has the properties of a city: it is organized, it is layered, it has districts and thoroughfares and a founding date lost to its own accumulation. It covers the rise from edge to edge, a roughly oval sprawl maybe six kilometers across, and it is made almost entirely of the bodies of the things that live in it and the things that lived in it before them.

The oldest part — call it the foundation, though nothing laid it — is a thick cemented crust of tube. Worm tube, calcareous and pale (though pale means nothing here), each one the diameter of a finger and packed against its neighbors like the cells of a honeycomb seen from the side. These are dead. They have been dead for centuries. The animals that made them grew, fed, spawned, and died, and their neighbors grew over them, and the whole crust thickened outward and upward at roughly the pace of a fingernail. In places it is three or four meters deep. If you could cut it you would see growth bands, dark years and light years, the record of the vent flow rising and falling like a slow tide over a scale of decades.

On top of the dead crust stands the live city.

---

The towers first. Where the warm water comes up strongest, in a dozen or so main seeps scattered across the rise, the tubeworms grow in dense columns — not the flat crust of their ancestors but reaching upward into the flow, a meter, two meters, bundled so tight that a hand couldn't be pushed between them. Each animal is a soft red plume at the mouth of a white tube, and the plumes lean toward the current together, so the whole tower has a grain to it, a lean, like a stand of grass under wind. They are not feeding in any sense a surface creature would recognize. They have no mouths. They are farming bacteria inside their own bodies, and the bacteria are eating the sulfur in the water, and everything else here is eating them.

Between the towers, the water is calmer and slightly cooler, and here the beds begin. Mussels, dark-shelled and long, packed so densely they lock together into a pavement you could stand on. Their beds spread outward from the base of each tower in irregular lobes, following the diffuse warmth through cracks in the crust, and where two lobes from different towers meet they simply merge and go on. The mussel pavement is the city's floor. It is uneven — humped, ridged, pocked — and it is alive, all of it, and if you laid your palm on it you would feel the faint continuous shift of thousands of shells opening a millimeter and closing again.

Then the streets. Because there are streets, or at least channels: gaps in the mussel pavement, a hand's width to a body's width, where the crust below is fissured and the warm water wells up too hot or too sulfurous for the mussels to tolerate. These channels run more or less radially out from each tower and branch as they go. Nothing moves along them the way traffic moves along a road, but they are the corridors through which the current moves, and so they are the corridors along which everything drifts — larvae, detritus, chemical signal, the plume of an animal's death — and the inhabitants have arranged themselves accordingly, faces to the channels, backs to the walls.

---

The inhabitants.

Everywhere. That is the first thing. There is no bare surface in the city that stays bare. Every tube, every shell, every fragment of dead crust carries a crowd on its back. Limpets the size of a thumbnail grazing the bacterial film off the worm tubes. Snails with shells thick as armor, moving with the imperceptible patience of things that have never had to hurry. Barnacles cemented to barnacles. Small pale anemones filling the gaps between mussel shells like grout. The density is such that the city has no real surfaces at all, only a continuous coat of small mouths.

Above the coat, the walkers. Long-legged shrimp, white, blind, in numbers that beggar the term *abundant* — they are the city's population in the sense that pigeons are a plaza's. They swarm over the mussel beds and up the worm towers, picking at the film, at each other's leavings, at the dead. Squat lobsters with oversized claws working the channel edges. Crabs, hairy-limbed, that move in slow deliberate steps across the pavement and stop, and stop, and stop, and then take a shrimp in one motion and go back to standing still. Brittle stars in the mussel gaps with their arms threaded up through the pavement into the water, a field of slow-waving filaments. And in the channels themselves, where the water is hottest, a few things that live nowhere else: worms with bristled backs that sit in the seep flow like animals warming at a fire, and endure water that would cook the shrimp.

Then the ones that pass through. Eels, long and ribbon-thin, the color of old paper, threading between the towers with their heads low, tasting. A pale octopus that has taken up in a hollow in the crust at the city's western edge and comes out to work the mussel beds at intervals no one has measured. Fish shaped like drops of oil, mouths pointing upward, holding position over the channels and letting the current bring them things.

None of them has seen another. Everyone here knows everyone by chemistry and by touch.

---

The city is not silent, but it is close. The water is so thick and the dark so complete that sound is what a visitor would notice most, after the warmth. Under everything there is a low, irregular ticking — the vents themselves, the crust flexing minutely as hot water finds and loses its way through the cracks. The shrimp make a dry, faint sound in aggregate, a crowd-rustle, thousands of small legs on shell. Occasionally a mussel bed shifts and there is a soft clatter, like a handful of gravel let go. Occasionally, from far off through the rock, something deeper — the rise settling, or a rumble from whatever is heating it. Mostly it is quiet the way a library is quiet: full, but hushed.

---

The processes.

The vents breathe. Not on a schedule anything here could predict, but over years the flow through each seep strengthens and weakens, shifts a few meters east or west as the cracks below rearrange. When a seep strengthens, its tower thickens and its mussel beds spread and the shrimp gather there in greater numbers and the crabs follow the shrimp. When a seep weakens — and this is the city's essential fact — the tower begins to die from the base, the plumes withdraw, the mussels gape and are eaten and their shells are grouted over by anemones and then by bacterial film and then by the next generation of tube, and within a decade or two the district is dead crust, part of the foundation, and something else is growing on it. The city's map is always being redrawn. The city's mass only ever increases.

Below that cycle, faster ones. The shrimp molt in pulses, and for a few days afterward the channels are full of their cast shells, drifting and settling, and the crabs and the eels grow fat on soft bodies. The tubeworms spawn all at once in response to some chemical cue and the water goes briefly cloudy with it, and the filter-feeders gorge, and the vast majority of the larvae are eaten before they have settled, and the few that survive settle wherever the current happens to have dropped them, which is why the towers stand where they stand and nowhere else.

And the crabs. Always, at any hour of the perpetual night, in every part of the city, a crab is standing still on the mussel pavement, and a shrimp is walking toward it.

---

There is one strange thing here — only one, and it isn't very strange.

At the eastern edge of the rise, where the crust runs out and drops away toward the abyssal plain, something has fallen from above. It did so a long time ago. It was wood once, a tree or part of one, carried out to sea and waterlogged and sunk, and it landed at the city's edge and has been there ever since, half-buried in shell debris and dead tube. It is nearly gone — eaten hollow by boring clams, sheathed in bacterial mat, colonized like everything else. But it is the only thing in the whole warm dark expanse of the place that came from a world where there is light, and the rules of the city seem to bend around it slightly. The mussels grow differently along its length. The shrimp avoid it. The pale octopus, when it emerges, will sometimes go and sit against it for a while, arms spread over the soft rotten grain, for no reason that has anything to do with food.

Then it goes back to the mussel beds, and the crabs stand, and the towers lean into the warmth coming up out of the rock, and the city adds another layer to itself, in the dark, in the quiet, as it has been doing since before anything alive here had ancestors.
