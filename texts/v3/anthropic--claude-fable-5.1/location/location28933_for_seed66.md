Begin with the altitude, because everything else follows from it.

The plateau sits high enough that the air is thin and the sun, when it gets through, is hard and white. But it does not often get through cleanly. The plateau holds water — shallow water, warm water, water that came up hot from deep in the rock and has been cooling for the entire length of its slow crossing toward the low country it never reaches. It evaporates before it gets there. It rises off the flats as steam in the mornings and as a general thick brightness in the afternoons, and it condenses on every surface that is even slightly cooler than the air, which at this height is most of them. So the place is wet from above and wet from below at once, and there is no such thing here as a dry stone.

Nothing grows. That is the first thing a person notices, after the smell. The water is alkaline enough to strip skin, saturated with soda and salt and dissolved minerals from the deep rock, and it kills anything with roots within a season. The old shorelines are ringed with white. The rock itself — a dark grey-brown volcanic stone, pocked and heavy — is crusted with pale evaporite wherever the water has touched it and retreated, and it has touched almost everywhere at some time in the last few thousand years. Crusts on crusts. Some of the terraces of mineral deposit are as tall as a person and have the layered, curtained look of things built one thin film at a time over a duration nobody was present to measure.

And yet the water is not dead. The opposite. Look down into any of the channels and it is red — not bloody, more the red of brick dust or old rust, and it moves. The redness is bacteria, uncountable, forming mats and skins and flocculent clouds, and it is the bottom of everything. The mats are fed on by flies. Not a few flies: the shallows are furred with them, a living pelt a hand's width deep along every waterline, and when a person walks along an edge the flies lift in a wave that rolls ahead and settles behind, a sound like heavy cloth being shaken. They do not bite. They do not need to. Above the flies are the birds.

There are, at a conservative estimate, more birds on this plateau than there are hours in a human life. Long-legged wading things, pink and white and rust from the food they eat, standing in the shallows in numbers that make the water look solid and feathered from a distance. They stand almost touching. They feed with their heads down and the noise of them feeding — a wet, rhythmic, grumbling filter-sound, multiplied past any individual — is the baseline of the place, the thing under all other sounds, the way a river is under a town. Above the waders are the gulls and the raptors and the scavengers, and among the rocks are the small dark mammals that live off dropped eggs and dead chicks and the beetles that live off the mammals. In the warm fissures where the steam comes up there are bats, thousands to a crack, and at dusk they come out in a column that leans in the wind. In the channels there are fish, small and fast and pale, that have somehow made the alkaline water their own and are in it as thickly as the flies are on it. Everything is at its maximum. Every niche is full to the brim and slightly over.

The city was built into this, not beside it.

It is hard to say where the city begins, because the city is made mostly of the same material as the shore. The first builders quarried the dark stone and mortared it with lime they burned from the middens of shell and bone that the birds had already been leaving for ten thousand years before anyone arrived. So the walls went up grey and were white within a generation, and the white kept accumulating — condensation carrying mineral, drying, condensing again — until the oldest districts have the softened, poured look of things that were never quite built so much as deposited. Corners are round. Lintels sag into their openings without cracking. Windows have been reduced to slits by the accretion around their frames and people simply cut them open again every few decades, and the cut faces show the layers like a felled tree.

The newer construction is iron and brick, and the iron is a problem here, and the problem is part of the character of the place. Iron does not last. It rusts into the same red as the water, scales, is replaced, rusts again. So the industrial quarters are in a permanent state of patient repair, and the sound of them is not the roar of a factory at full stretch but the steady, unhurried thump and hiss of engines that have been running at the same pace for so long that stopping them would be a stranger event than anything they do while running.

What the engines do: they boil. This is a soda-works, at bottom — the whole city is. The water is pumped up out of the flats through pipes furred with white on the inside, into pans the size of courtyards, and the heat comes free from the ground so the pans are set directly over the vents, and the water goes off as steam and leaves behind soda ash and salt in ratios the pan-workers can read by colour. The ash goes down the mountain in carts, for glass and soap and everything else the low country cannot make without it. The salt goes down too. The pans breathe steam constantly and the steam joins the cloud that the plateau already generates, so the city sits inside its own weather; the pan-quarters are a warm fog that smells of soda and cooked mineral, and the fog is what the light comes through, so that the light in the city is always a diffuse bright grey-gold with no shadows to speak of, and things at a hundred paces are silhouettes, and things at three hundred are gone.

Second industry: the birds. Everything the birds are and make is used. Their droppings are scraped from the roosting cliffs in the dry season — a dry season here means a few weeks when the condensation is merely heavy — and processed for fertilizer that goes down the mountain to soils that can still grow something. Their dead, of which there are always a great many in a population this size, are rendered for oil and fat and feathers in long low sheds whose roofs are so thick with living birds that the roofs have to be reinforced, and are, and the birds nest in the reinforcements. Their eggshells and bones are burned for lime. The red bacterial mats are dredged and dried for pigment; the city's own doors and shutters are painted with it, so the whole place under its white crust is streaked and blotched with rust-pink, the same colour as the flamingoes, the same colour as the water, the same colour as the iron dying.

Nobody here regards the animals as a nuisance or a wonder. They are the substrate. Children grow up with the sound of a million birds the way children elsewhere grow up with the sound of the sea, and the flies are simply a condition of air, and the bats at dusk are a clock. The birds nest on the cornices and in the drainpipes and in the cold pans and on the roofs of the workers' terraces, and a pan-worker going up to his door at the end of a shift will put his hand on a rail and the rail will be a bird and the bird will move over. Nothing here is more than an arm's length from something else alive. That is the density of the place — not the density of a crowd, which comes and goes, but of a coral, where every surface has been claimed and the claims are old.

It is loud, therefore, but it is a steady loudness. The feeding grumble of the waders, the shaken-cloth roll of the flies, the engines, the steam, the crying of the gulls, the click and drip of condensation off every edge onto every ledge. A traveller coming up the switchback road from the low country will hear it an hour before the cloud-line, a broad grey roar with no direction to it, and will mistake it for wind, and then will realize there is no wind.

The odd thing about the place — and there is really only one, and the people who live there do not think it odd at all — is how little it changes. The water rises and falls, but across centuries, a hand's width in a lifetime, and the old shorelines are mapped and dated and the maps are boring. The birds do not migrate; where would they go, and why would they leave a lake that is warm all year and floored with food? The bacteria bloom and the flies hatch and the chicks fledge in overlapping waves so that there is never a season without them. The pans boil. The iron rusts and is replaced. Nobody builds anything new, exactly; they maintain and extend and cut the windows open again. A person who left as a child and came back old would find their door in the same place with a slightly deeper crust around it and the same birds, or the descendants of the same birds, on the same rail.

There are stories, low down the mountain, about the plateau city — that it is a place where time has stopped, where the white stone is a spell, where the birds are the souls of the workers. The people of the plateau find these stories mildly embarrassing. Time has not stopped. It is simply that everything here that could happen has already happened, and reached its level, and stayed there; the birds and the bacteria and the boiling pans and the salt-poured walls have all found the same slow equilibrium, and the equilibrium is the place. What looks like stillness from below is, from inside the cloud, an enormous and steady turning-over — the flies lifting and settling, the steam going up, the mineral coming down, the red water grumbling with a million heads in it — that has been going on so long, and is so complete, that it has become indistinguishable from rest.
