The first thing is the edge. You are walking on a plain of pale, chalky ground, dry as biscuit, and then the ground simply stops and there is warm air coming up at you — a whole moving mass of it, wet and heavy, smelling of hot lime and something else, something faintly like the inside of a shell. The plain has broken open. What you are standing on turns out to be a roof, and the roof is only partly there.

Below, the country continues. That is the second thing. It is not a pit or a cave but a region — chambers running away in every direction, each one the size of a village square or a valley floor, connected by low arches and buried passages and simple gaps where a wall has not finished forming. In places the roof is intact and the light under it is dim and amber. In more places the roof has fallen, and the slabs lie tilted on the floor like dropped plates, and daylight comes straight down through the openings. The light does not reach the ground as light. It reaches it as a wash, because between the sky and the floor there is a permanent film of vapour, and the vapour holds the brightness and spreads it around so that there are no real shadows anywhere. Everything is lit the way the inside of a cloud is lit. You can see perfectly well. You cannot see far.

---

The rock is white and cream and the yellow of old teeth, and all of it is wet. Not flowing — beaded. Every surface carries a skin of condensation that gathers and runs and gathers again. Where the drip is steady it has made things: columns, lips, terraces stepping down in shallow basins, each basin holding a finger's depth of warm milky water that trembles constantly with what lives in it. The stone is soft in the way that new stone is soft. You can press a thumbnail into a fresh ledge and leave a mark, and the mark will be gone by tomorrow, filled and glazed over.

Because this is the thing about the place: it is not being worn down. It is being put up. The whole region is under construction, and the builders are the animals.

---

They are on everything.

At first you take them for a texture — a graininess on the walls, a bristling on the terraces, a boil in the pools. Then a piece of the wall moves and you understand that the wall is coated, is in fact largely composed of, small armoured bodies, each about the length of your thumb, plated in translucent shell the same colour as the rock, packed so close that they overlap like scales. They graze the mineral film off the stone with mouths you cannot see and lay it back down behind them in ridged lines of cement. Where a thousand of them have passed the surface is smooth and glossy. Where ten thousand are gathered the surface is growing outward while you watch — not fast, but visibly, a lip extending, a column thickening at the base, a new terrace tipping into being at the edge of an old one.

That is one kind. There are more kinds than you can count. Long pale things in the basins, jointed, with feathered limbs that comb the water. Flattened things that move under the surface of the mud and leave the mud heaving. Clouds of soft-bodied fliers in the upper darkness of the roofed chambers, silent individually and thunderous together, coming down in sheets when the light shifts. Small hard climbers that live only on the columns and whose shed casings, pressed into the growing stone, give the columns their strange pebbled skin. Something the size of a dog, low and many-legged, that pushes through the crowds of small ones and eats them in mouthfuls and is followed by a wake of them closing up again behind it. Eggs in gelatinous ropes hung from every overhang. Eggs in the pools. Eggs laid in the fresh cement and cemented over, so that the stone has a faint pattern of ovals in it when you look at a broken face.

There is no bare floor. When you step down you step on bodies, and they slide out from under your foot, and the foot lands on stone that was under them, and by the time you lift it the stone is covered again. You cannot walk quietly. You cannot walk cleanly. The pools splash up warm and thick with life against your shins.

---

The sound is the sound of all of them at once. It is not any single noise. It is a scraping, a clicking, a wet susurrus, a rustle of thousands of plates against thousands of plates, and beneath that the constant tick and hiss of water, and above that at intervals the roar of the fliers moving from one chamber to the next. It fills the whole space. It has a pitch. If you stand still long enough you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as a kind of pressure, and then when a chamber goes briefly quiet — as happens, for reasons that are never clear — the silence is so sudden and total that it feels like a change in the air.

---

Nothing here holds still long enough to become a fact.

A terrace that took a season to grow will be crowded out and abandoned in a day when a new seep opens in the roof and the builders swarm toward the fresher water. The abandoned terrace dries, cracks, and its edges are eaten back by the things that eat abandoned stone. A column thickens until it touches the ceiling and then the ceiling shifts on it — you hear this, a deep grinding somewhere out of sight, followed by a fall of powder and a rush of fliers — and a slab comes down, and the slab is colonised within hours, and in a year it will be a low white hill with a new column starting on top of it. The pools migrate. The mud-heaves move like slow waves across the floor and carry whole populations with them. When it rains above — it does rain, not often, hard — the water finds every crack in the roof and comes through in bright strands and the entire system accelerates: the basins overflow, the builders go frantic, cement goes down in ridges thick as rope, and the floor of a chamber can rise a hand's breadth overnight. Then the water thins to drip again and the thing is left fixed in whatever shape it reached.

So the stone remembers the crowds. Look at any column and you can read the surges in it: bands where the building was fast and the surface is rough and full of trapped shell, bands where it was slow and the stone is smooth. Some of the older columns have grown in the shape of the swarms that made them — bulged where the animals massed, pinched where they thinned — and in the wash of light they look like frozen crowds themselves, stopped mid-press.

---

People come here. Not many, and not to stay.

You find their marks in the driest places, which is to say the least dry. A hearth ring of slabs under an overhang that the drip has not reached yet — though a fringe of new stone is already forming above it, and in another year or two the hearth will be under a curtain. Three stakes of bone driven into a ledge, with cord of twisted fibre strung between them, marking a path across a basin: the path itself, a line of laid stones, is already half buried under fresh cement and in places the stones have been lifted and carried off by the heave of the mud. A net, weighted with pierced pebbles, hung to dry and never collected, now stiff with lime and part of the wall. Hand-shapes and lines and a repeated hooked figure daubed in red ochre on a surface that was once a wall and is now, because the wall has grown out over it, a ceiling: you have to lie down to see them, and the drip has glazed them so that they look like something set under glass.

There is one built thing that could be called a shelter — a low hut of stacked slabs against the back of a chamber, roofed with a single fallen piece, its door a gap you have to crawl through. Inside, dry-ish, a litter of shell plates sorted by size, a hammerstone, a scraper of chipped grey flint that must have been carried in from far off because there is no flint here. The plates are the reason. Cleaned and dried they are hard and iridescent and you can see why someone would want them. The people come in with baskets when the swarms are high, and they take plates and eggs and the soft fliers, and they leave before the next rain, and they leave their paths behind them to be buried, and the next time they come the chambers are not where they were.

---

Deeper — you can go deeper, the region does not stop, it only gets darker and warmer and the roof gets more complete — the light thins to amber and then to brown and the fauna changes without lessening. The fliers give way to crawlers. The builders are paler. The pools are hotter and the mineral in them is thicker and the columns they make are fatter and closer together, so that in places you walk between them like walking through a crowd standing shoulder to shoulder, all of it moving, all of it wet, the sound closing over your head. Somewhere further down is presumably the source — the hot seep from which all this water and all this dissolved stone comes up — but no one who goes in that far comes back with a clear description of it, only of the walls getting closer and the animals getting denser until the two seem to be the same thing.

---

Come back up to the edge. Stand on the plain again, where the ground is dry and still and nothing moves.

Behind you the whole country breathes out its warm breath through the holes in its roof, and you can hear it — a low continuous roar, like a distant shore, like a city heard from a hill. The vapour rises and disperses. Somewhere under your feet a column has just reached the ceiling, and a slab is about to come down, and a hundred thousand small bodies are already moving toward the place where it will land.
