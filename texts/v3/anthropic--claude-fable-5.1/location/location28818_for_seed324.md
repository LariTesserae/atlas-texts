**The gallery**

You reach it by descending through the body of the dam itself, so that although you are more than two thousand meters above the sea, you are also underground — or under-concrete, which is a different thing. Rock has been here for a long time and has made its peace with water. Concrete has not. Concrete is eleven months old here, and the water only arrived in the spring.

The corridor is two meters wide, two and a half high, cast in place, its walls still bearing the ghost grain of the plywood forms: the seams between panels, the round dimples where the form ties were cut and plugged with mortar a slightly different grey. Nothing has softened these edges. A finger run along the corner where wall meets ceiling comes away with a fine white dust and a thin line of blood if you are careless. Everything is exact and everything is recent.

And everything weeps.

The dam is taking its first load. On the other side of the upstream wall — twelve, fifteen meters of concrete away, depending on where you stand — is a reservoir that did not exist a year ago, and the water in it is doing what water does when it is pressed against something: finding the way through. It comes as sweat along the cold-joints where one pour ended and the next began, beading first, then gathering into a skin, then into threads that run down the wall and darken it in vertical stripes. It comes through hairline cracks that opened when the mass cooled, cracks nobody has mapped yet because nobody has been down here long enough with a torch. It condenses on the ceiling from the air, which is saturated and a few degrees warmer than the walls. Drops fall from the light fixtures. Drops fall from the conduit. The floor is never dry and was never meant to be: a shallow V-channel runs down its center, cast into the slab, carrying a clear film of water that speaks — you can see it speak, if not hear it — toward the collection sump at the low end.

Where the water has run longest, the concrete is beginning to answer. Faint white threads follow the leak paths: calcium, leached from the cement and re-deposited in air, the first stitches of what in fifty years will be stalactites. They are almost nothing now. A fingernail flakes them off. But they are there, and they are new, and they are the first thing in this place that could be called a landform.

The corridor bends, following the curve of the dam, and the bend is not a door but it functions like one. On the far side is the chamber.

---

It is not large. Five paces across, perhaps eight long, the ceiling stepped higher toward the downstream side where a steel ladder climbs to a hatch that is bolted from above. The purpose of the room is written in its fittings: a bank of piezometer standpipes along one wall, each a capped tube coming up through the floor with a stenciled number; a V-notch weir set into the drainage channel where it widens, so that seepage can be measured by the height of water passing the notch; a pendulum well — a vertical shaft the diameter of a dinner plate, dropping into darkness, in which a wire hangs plumb from somewhere far above so that the engineers can read whether the dam is bowing. Grey-painted electrical boxes. An emergency telephone in a yellow housing, its handset beaded with condensation. Two fluorescent tubes behind wire cages, one of which works.

The light is the color of dishwater. It reaches the walls and gives up. Beyond the reach of the tube there is a second, smaller light — a portable halogen work lamp on a stand, left plugged in by whoever was last here, aimed at nothing in particular, at a patch of wall near the floor. Someone was inspecting a crack and stopped and did not come back for the lamp.

And in the small warm circle of that lamp, something is growing.

Not much. A green stain the size of a hand where the wall stays wet, a green that is really a grey-green, algae or the beginnings of moss, arrived as spores in the air or on a boot or in the aggregate itself. In the corner where the channel turns, one true plant: a fern, or the first fronds of one, no taller than a thumb, pale from lack of light, rooted in a crust of the leached white lime and a smear of silt the water brought in. That is the whole of it. Two living things in a room built to keep the mountain from moving, and both of them accidents, and both of them looking for the lamp.

There is nothing else alive. No insects — the shaft down was sealed, the air is too clean and too cold, there is nothing here to eat. No bats, no spiders, none of the small tenants that arrive in any human cavity within a season. The seepage carries no fish, no larvae, nothing but dissolved rock. The place has a body's dampness and a body's temperature-gradient and none of a body's inhabitants. It is still sterile in the way a new wound is sterile.

---

The sound arrives before you know it is a sound. You feel it in the ladder rungs, in the standpipe caps, in your teeth.

Below the chamber — directly below, through the floor slab and a few meters of mass concrete — is the outlet works. The reservoir is being drawn down for its first-fill test, and a hollow-jet valve is open, and through it the entire pressure of the young lake is discharging as a ring of white water into a stilling basin the size of a cathedral. Nothing of that water is visible from here. All of it is audible. It comes up through the concrete as a single sustained roar that has no beginning and no rhythm, a roar so total that the ear stops registering it as noise and begins to register it as *place* — as though the room were not built of concrete at all but of the sound, and the walls were simply where the sound happened to become solid.

It is loud enough that you cannot hear your own footfall, your own breathing, the drip of the water from the conduit to the floor. A shout dies in the mouth. The fluorescent tube's hum is imaginary; you know it is there and cannot find it. You can feel the drops striking your shoulders but you cannot hear them, and this severs something — the drip becomes silent film, the whole room becomes silent film, everything moving and nothing making a sound, while underneath it the one sound goes on and on and on.

The water in the V-channel trembles with it. Standing waves ripple in the puddle at the base of the ladder, small perfect rings that never spread because they are being made and remade a hundred times a second. The wire in the pendulum well is not still; it hangs true but it vibrates with a blur you can see. The fern's fronds shiver.

---

The place is not static, though it looks it. The dam is moving.

Not much — a few millimeters at the crest as the reservoir level changes, a slow lean downstream under the load and a slow recovery as it is drawn down. But the structure is a single piece of stone that men made, and it is finding its shape under weight for the first time, and this chamber is where that finding is recorded. Over an afternoon the water at the weir rises a centimeter and falls again. A crack in the upstream wall that was dry at noon is dark by evening. The standpipe caps sweat more or less. The whole chamber breathes in the slow tide of the reservoir, and the roar beneath it rises and falls in pitch as the valve is opened and throttled from a control room far above, where people who will never come down here watch a needle.

Sometimes the valve is shut. Then there is a silence so sudden it is a kind of blow, and in the silence — for a few minutes, for an hour — the drips are audible again, the tube hums, the seepage over the weir makes a small clean domestic sound like a tap left running in another room. The concrete ticks as the vibration leaves it. And the place, deprived of the roar, becomes suddenly, plainly what it is: a small wet grey room in the belly of a wall, high in the mountains, where water is finding its way through something that was built to stop it, and is being measured while it does.

Then the valve is opened again, and the room fills with sound to the ceiling, and the fern trembles, and the white threads on the wall lengthen by a width too small to see.
