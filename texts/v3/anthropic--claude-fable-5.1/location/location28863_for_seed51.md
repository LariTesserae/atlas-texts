The basin is low enough that the water has nowhere to go, and has stopped trying. It sits at the surface everywhere, or just under it, so that the ground is less a floor than a decision you make each time you put your foot down. Sedge, then a hand's depth of dark water, then peat that takes the weight and gives some of it back. Beyond the sedge, alder. Beyond the alder, more sedge, and grey willow, and tussocks with hollow water between them, and then the alders again — and it goes on like that in every direction for what is probably a day's walk, though walking here does not measure distance the way it does elsewhere.

There is no high ground. There are only places where the water is a little slower.

The light is the light of about an hour before dusk, on an overcast day, and it stays that way. It is not that there is no daytime; there is a brightening and a dimming, a long grey pulse, but even at the brightest the sky is a lid of felt and the colour of everything is pulled toward slate, moss-green, the brown of steeped tea. What is strange, and takes a while to notice, is where the light seems to come *from*. It should fall from the sky onto the water. Instead the water gives back more than the sky offers. The pools are a shade lighter than the clouds above them. Stand at the edge of one and your shadow lies not on the water but faintly *on the underside of the cloud*, as though the fen were the source and the sky the receiver.

---

It is loud. That is the second thing, after the wet.

There is a roar underneath everything, the sound a large river makes going over a weir, except there is no weir and the water here barely moves. It comes up through the peat. Put your palm flat on a tussock and you feel it as a tremor in the fibres, not rhythmic, not quite random either — like an enormous crowd heard through a wall. Over this the reeds rattle. They rattle when there is no wind, and are still when there is. Alder leaves clatter against each other with a hard sound, harder than leaves should make, as if each were a small plate of slate. Somewhere, constantly, water is falling from height, though nowhere in the basin is there any height for water to fall from. And there is thunder — long, low, walking thunder — without lightning, without cloud change, arriving from beneath the horizon at intervals that never quite settle into a period.

Two people ten paces apart must shout. Beyond twenty paces, they are alone.

---

The rocks weep. There are not many of them: knuckles of a dark, fine-grained stone that break the surface among the alder roots, weathered to a softness like old soap, greened with liverwort where they stay damp, which is everywhere. Water runs down them in threads, continuously, though nothing is above them to feed it. When you cup a hand under one of these threads it fills, and the water is very cold, and very slightly too heavy. Not enough to be sure. Enough to notice the second time.

If you set the cupped hand down and let the water go, it moves across the peat toward the nearest pool. It does this uphill if the pool is uphill. It does not hurry.

The rocks are the closest thing here to a made object, and they are not made. Or rather: if they were made, it was so long ago and by so complete a method that making and weathering have become the same thing. There is no seam on them. There is no face that is flatter than another. Whatever they were, they have been rained on for a period long enough that the question no longer has an answer, and the fen has grown up around them and through them and treats them as it treats any other stone — as something to sit on, drip from, root against.

This is true of the whole basin. Nothing here is built, and nothing here is *natural* in the sense that word carries anywhere else. The peat is peat — dark, fibrous, sour-smelling when you pull a handful apart — and it also holds its shape a moment too long after you release it, and settles back not to where it was but to where it should have been if you had never touched it. The water is water and it routes itself. Not visibly, not by any channel you can point to, but a pool that is knee-deep in the morning will be ankle-deep by the grey noon and knee-deep again by the darker grey of evening, and the water has gone *somewhere*, and come back, and there is no inflow and no outflow. The moss grows in patterns. You will notice this at the edge of your vision — a regularity, something like script, something like circuitry — and when you look directly it is moss, tufted, ordinary, growing where moss grows. Look away and the pattern reassembles.

Whatever engineering was done here was done at the scale of matter itself, and then left. It has weathered into the world so thoroughly that it *is* the world, the way a river that was once dug becomes, after enough centuries, simply a river.

---

The vegetation is dense enough that you cannot see far at ground level, and layered enough that there are several ground levels.

Sedge and cottongrass and bog-bean at the water. Above them, horsetail, bottle-brush green, in stands so thick they hiss when you push through. Then the shrub willow, grey-leaved, growing sideways as much as up, so that walking through it is a matter of ducking and climbing at once. Then the alder canopy, low, twenty feet at most, black-barked, dripping. The alders are close enough that their branches have grown into each other. Whole stands share a single interlaced crown and you cannot say where one tree stops. In the crotches of the alders, more moss; in the moss, small ferns; on the ferns, a film of something green and wet that may be algae and may be a finer moss still. Every surface has something growing on it. Every something has something growing on *that*.

It grows fast. Not so fast that you see it move, but fast enough that a willow shoot you broke off in passing will be a hand-length longer when you pass back, and you will pass back, because the paths here return you.

That is one of the rules. Walk toward a stand of alders you have picked out. You will arrive at a stand of alders. It will be very like the one you picked out, and you will be nearly certain it is not the same one, and there will be no way to check, because the one you picked out is now behind you, at about the distance you have walked, and looks exactly as it did.

Another rule: rain falls, but not from where the clouds are. It falls as a fine cold drizzle from a part of the sky that is brighter than the rest, and the darkest overhang of cloud will sit directly overhead, dry as chalk. You can stand under it and stay dry while rain hisses onto the sedge fifteen feet to your left.

Another: some of the pools reflect. Most do not — the water is too dark, too still, and what you see in it is peat and the shapes of drowned stems. But some pools reflect a sky, and it is not the sky that is over the basin. It is a sky with stars in it, or a sky in full sun, or a sky the colour of copper. The reflected sky is in the correct position for a reflection. Nothing is above the pool that could account for it. If you put your hand into such a pool, the hand is cold and wet and comes back with nothing on it, and the reflection closes over the place where your hand was as water does.

Another, and this one is the hardest: the same fallen alder trunk lies across the same channel in at least four places in the basin. It is the same trunk. It has the same broken stub of branch, the same bracket fungus in the same place, the same shred of green cloth — no, of green *something*, fibrous, weathered — caught in a split in the bark. Each of the four is the original. The fen does not seem to find this a problem. Moss grows on all four. The channel runs under all four.

---

The cold is the cold of a wet place, not a high one. It never freezes. Breath just barely shows. Everything you touch is the temperature of something that has been in water all night, and after an hour your hands are that temperature too.

Fauna is scarce, and what there is repeats.

There is a heron. You see it standing at the edge of a pool, grey on grey, motionless, and then in the next clearing, and the next, in the same posture, facing the same direction. It may be many herons. It has the same broken barb on the same primary feather each time. It never flies. Once, from behind, you see it stab at the water and come up with something dark and thrashing that it swallows whole, and the thing does not so much go down its throat as *cease to be outside it*.

There are snails — small, brown, wet, ordinary, in numbers, on the horsetail. There is an eel, or the impression of an eel, in one of the slower channels: a long shape under the surface that moves against the drift, and is gone when you look properly. Once, distantly, something large shifts under the sedge — a long swell of the ground itself, thirty feet of it, rising and subsiding as though a back had rolled over beneath the peat. The roar does not change. The reeds keep rattling. Nothing surfaces.

No insects. This is the thing that is wrong in a way the rest is only strange. A fen this wet should be alive with midge and dragonfly and water-boatman, and there is nothing over the water at all, and the air is clean, and the loudness of the place is entirely the loudness of water and stone and leaf and thunder with no lightning, with no living voice in it except, sometimes, the heron's single dry croak, which arrives a half-second before the heron opens its beak.

---

It has been like this for a long time.

You can tell by the balance of it. Nothing here is in the process of *becoming* — no raw edges, no fresh collapse, no bank being cut. The alders die and fall and rot and are grown through, and the falling and the rotting and the growing are all happening at the same time in the same stand, so that the stand as a whole does not change. The water rises and drops on its unaccountable rhythm and the sedge is exactly as tall as the highest water and no taller. The weeping rocks have wept for long enough that the peat beneath each has a smooth shallow bowl in it, and the bowl is exactly as deep as it needs to be to hold the water that runs from the rock before the water sets off, uphill or down, toward wherever it goes. Whatever was done to this place — whatever the water was taught, whatever the moss is spelling, whatever the fourfold trunk is for — has settled. It has weathered into a state that maintains itself. It is not a ruin. A ruin is something that was once whole and is now less. This is something that has finished becoming what it is.

Toward what you take to be evening, though evening is only a slightly deeper grey, the thunder comes more often and from more directions, until it is nearly continuous, a rolling floor of sound under the roar, and the rain starts falling from a bright place low in the west and drifts slantwise across the alders, and the pools rise, and the water in the weeping rocks runs faster, and the heron is standing at the edge of the nearest pool, facing away from you, and the pool it stands beside reflects a sky full of very clear, very close, unfamiliar stars.

You could stay. The place is not hostile. It is not anything. It is wet and cold and loud and dim and green, and it is holding a shape it has held for longer than there has been anyone to notice, and it will go on holding it, uphill and down, in four places at once, whether or not you find your way back to the alders you started from.
