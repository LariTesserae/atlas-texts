**The Roof Where the Wind Falls**

Start with the sound, because that is what you would start with. Not because it is loud in the way of a noise that happens — a rockfall, thunder, a crowd — but because it does not happen. It is already there when you arrive and it is there when you leave, and it never once, in any measurable way, changes.

The plateau sits at the top of the range, higher than anything else within four hundred kilometers, and it sits in the path of the air. The whole weight of the atmosphere on the windward side of the continent piles up against the mountains and then pours over this one tableland the way a river pours over the lowest point in a weir. The wind here is not weather. Weather implies variation. This is a permanent condition, a structural property of the mountain, like its mass. The air comes over the western lip at a speed that has not shifted in the lifetime of any living thing, and it makes a sound as it comes that fills the entire plateau to the brim, edge to edge, top to bottom, and leaves no room for anything else.

You cannot hear your own voice here. You cannot hear a stone dropped at your feet, or the click of it landing. You feel the sound more than hear it — in the sternum, in the roots of the teeth, in the water of the inner ear. After an hour it stops being a sound and becomes a medium you are standing inside, like cold, like light.

---

The light is the second thing. The plateau is above the cloud deck almost always; the cloud is a floor two thousand meters below, white, wrinkled, going all the way to the horizon in every direction, and the sun on it and on the pale rock is a hard flat unshadowed brightness that has nowhere to hide from. There is very little air overhead to soften it. The sky is a dark blue that is nearly the blue of deep water, and the sun in it is small and white and violent, and everything it touches is lit from every side at once because the cloud floor throws it back up from below.

The rock is a pale grey quartzite, weathered to something like the color of bone. It has been in the wind for so long that the wind has finished with it. There is nothing left to knock loose. Every surface has been polished to the shape the air wants — long low fluted ridges lying west to east like the ribs of something, each one tapering downwind to a point, each one with a smooth rounded prow facing the wind and a slightly hollowed back. Between them are troughs where the sand that used to be rock moves through without stopping. There is no loose stone anywhere except where a rock is too heavy to move, and those the wind has sculpted into standing forms — elongated, keeled, leaning very slightly east, each one identical in its logic to every other. This is a landscape that reached its final state a very long time ago and has simply been maintaining it since.

---

It is damp here, which surprises people who expect a high dry place. The wind comes over the western lip having crossed a great deal of ocean and then having been forced upward by the range, and it is close to saturated. On the plateau's western half it is too fast to condense. But in the lee of every large rock, every fluted ridge, every hollow, the air slows for a moment and drops what it carries. The east faces of the standing rocks are always wet. They run, in the bright light, with a thin sheet of water that never dries and never freezes, cool as the air, and where the water reaches the ground it collects in shallow basins — dozens of them, hundreds, scattered across the plateau, each a few meters wide and a hand deep, black and clear, their surfaces stippled flat by the wind so that they never reflect anything, not even the sun.

And in the lee of the largest formation — a long spine of rock near the eastern edge, forty meters high, half a kilometer long — a thin fog forms and hangs and streams eastward, a permanent veil, always the same length, tearing off into the general air at the same point it has torn off at for ten thousand years.

Where there is water there is a little life that is rooted. Cushion plants, dense and grey-green and hard as wood, sit in the wet lee of the rocks like moss on a headstone. Lichen in orange and black, so thin it looks painted. A low woody thing with leaves the size of grains of rice that grows only on the east faces and never above knee height. None of it grows in the open. Nothing grows in the open. The open is for the wind.

---

But the plateau is not empty of life. It is one of the most populated places you will ever stand in, and it is populated almost entirely by things that are flying.

The wind that pours over the western lip goes over the eastern lip as well, and there it falls — into the enormous lee of the mountain, a downdraft the size of a city — and just before it falls, along the eastern edge, there is a band of air perhaps two hundred meters wide where the up-pressure off the plateau and the down-pull off the edge hold each other in a standing balance. A bird can sit in it with its wings open and not move. It can sit there for hours. It can sit there, it seems, indefinitely.

The birds have found this. They are a kind of swift — long dark scythe-winged things, narrow-bodied, with eyes ringed in white — and there are so many of them that at first you take them for a fault in your vision, a smear, a static across the sky. They hang above the eastern edge in a layer that reaches from a few meters off the rock to a height you cannot estimate. Thousands. Tens of thousands. They do not flap. They lie on the wind, adjusting, and eat whatever the wind brings them — and it brings a constant traffic of insects lifted from the lowlands three thousand meters below and carried up and over the plateau, small pale moths and flies and things too small to see, so many that at the right angle to the sun the whole airstream glitters with them.

The swifts are silent, or if they are not you would never know. They speak with their bodies. A tilt of the wings, a dip, a roll — you see a gesture pass through a whole flank of them like a wave through grass. They nest, when they nest, in cracks on the sheer eastern face where the wind cannot reach, and they come and go from those cracks without once touching anything with their feet.

There are others. A large white gull-like bird with a black head that patrols the basins for whatever falls in. A small brown falcon that takes the swifts, and that the swifts ignore completely except for the one that dies. Small mammals like fat marmots live in burrows dug into the sand-filled troughs, and come out into the wet lees to eat the cushion plants, and sit up, and look around at a world they cannot possibly hear anything in, and go back down. Their eyes are large. Everything here has large eyes.

Yet you can walk for an hour across the western half and see nothing living at all — the swifts are a band along the edge, the mammals are in the leeward hollows, and the plateau between is forty kilometers of bone-colored ridges and black basins and wind. The fauna is abundant and the space is nearly void, and both things are true because the life has all gathered where the life can be, and the rest is left.

---

There is one place you can hear.

The long spine of rock near the eastern edge has, at its foot on the lee side, a cavity — not a cave, just a fold where the rock leans over on itself and the fog streams past the mouth of it. If you go in as far as you can go, four or five meters, the roar drops away as though a door had shut. You can hear your breathing. You can hear the water running down the rock. You can hear, for the first time, the swifts — a high thin twittering carried in on the fog from the edge, a sound like something being sharpened very far away.

Someone has been here before. On a dry ledge at the back there is a scatter of flaked stone — chert, not from the plateau, carried up from somewhere below — perhaps a hundred pieces, the debris of somebody sitting for some hours and making a few edges. Three finished points lie together at one end, blade-thin, one of them broken. There is nothing else. No fire, no bone, no mark on the wall. Somebody climbed to the top of the world and found the one place on it where they could hear themselves think, and sat there and worked, and left. It could have been last year. It could have been a very long time ago. The plateau does not keep that kind of record.

---

Stand back out in the open at the end of the day. The sun goes into the cloud floor in the west, and for a few minutes the whole white sea is orange and the swifts are lit from below like coals scattered on a dark hearth. The wind does not slacken. It never has. The fog streams off the spine of rock at the same length. The basins do not reflect the sunset. The standing rocks lean their few degrees eastward, as they have since before there was anyone to see them lean. Somewhere in the band above the edge a falcon takes a swift, and a ripple goes through the flock, and closes.

Tomorrow it will be exactly the same. That is what the place is for.
