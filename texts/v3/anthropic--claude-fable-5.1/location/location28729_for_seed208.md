The first thing to understand is that the ground here is finished. It has been eroded past the point where erosion has anything left to work on. There are no hills, because the hills were carried off long before anything lived to see them. What remains is a plain of bedrock so old and so level that water lying on it cannot decide which way to go, and so mostly it doesn't go; it stands. It sheets across the stone a finger deep and reflects a sky the color of tarnished pewter, and the stone underneath it is the color of the sky, and from a distance you cannot say where the wet ends.

The rock is a dark grey gneiss folded so many times that its banding looks like the grain of driftwood. It is polished. Not by glaciers—there is no memory of ice here—but by the slow passage of water and of bodies, an unbroken traffic of soft things over hard, for longer than seems reasonable. In places it has worn into shallow bowls a few paces across, and these hold water even when the sheet drains, and in the bowls the water is a little warmer and smells of iron.

Water comes up as much as it comes down. The rock is porous in a way rock should not be, riddled with old fractures healed with quartz and then re-opened, and groundwater under some faint pressure seeps out along every seam. Where the plain has any relief at all—a low bench, a rounded knob no higher than a kneeling person—the faces weep. Threads of water run down them constantly, bead on the underside of every overhang, drip. The air is saturated. Breath fogs. Anything left in the open is wet within an hour, without rain, simply from the air coming to rest on it.

There are no plants. Not moss, not lichen, not the green smear you would expect on stone this wet. Whatever it is in the water—and there is something in the water, a mineral taste at the back of the throat, a faint slickness—it did not permit them. What grows instead is a film. On the undersides of ledges, in the bowls, spreading across the seep-faces in fans, there is a dark, faintly iridescent skin, purple-black going bronze at the edges, thin as paint. It is something simpler than a plant and it feeds on the water and the dim light and whatever is dissolved in the rock. In the bowls it thickens into a gelatinous mat that bulges when you press it and slowly recovers. This is the whole floor of the world's economy. Everything that lives here eats the film, or eats what eats the film.

And nearly everything here is alive.

You would not think it from the emptiness. The plain is open in every direction, and the first impression is of enormous vacancy—a few low benches, a scatter of dark knobs, the pewter water. But look at the water's edge and it is moving. Look at the seep-face and it is furred with bodies. The animals are the landscape's only texture, and they are everywhere the film is, in numbers that stop being numbers.

The commonest are the lows: flat, ribbed, shelled things the size of a palm, the color of the rock, that creep across the film and scrape it away. They move in fronts, thousands abreast, grazing a seep-face bare and leaving it pale grey behind them, and the film regrows in the wake before the front has gone a hundred paces. They have no eyes. They have a hard rasping mouth and a shell of laminated mineral that they build out of what they eat, and when they die the shells stay, and the shells are a large part of what has polished the stone.

Above them, in the sense of eating them, are the striders: long-legged, long-necked, spindle-bodied creatures with the gait of wading birds and the skin of something amphibian, pale and translucent so the dark of their gut shows through. They pick lows off the rock and crack them. They gather in flocks of several hundred on the benches at the water's edge. Their heads are all beak; they have no visible ears, and this is the first clue.

Above the striders are a few things that eat striders, mostly seen at a distance—a low, heavy, wide-mouthed shape that moves through the water with only its back showing, and once in a great while a shadow that passes overhead under the cloud.

None of it is what you would notice first.

What you notice first is the sound. Long before you can pick out an animal you can hear the plain, and by the time you are standing on it you can hear nothing else. It is not one sound. It is the rasp of a few million lows grinding shell against stone, which alone is a noise like surf that never breaks. Under that, and around it, are the striders, which are never silent: they call to each other in a flat, tearing bray that they make continuously, all of them, so that the flocks are not so much loud as solid, walls of noise you could lean against. And under both of those is the ground.

The ground rings.

This is the thing that takes a while to understand and that the people here have known for as long as there have been people. The fractured, water-filled gneiss is a resonator. Every drip, every shell-scrape, every footfall goes into it and comes back out changed, deepened, delayed, from somewhere else. The whole plain hums at a pitch below hearing and you feel it in your sternum and in the water in your ears. When the striders bray in unison—and they fall into unison, in waves that pass through a flock like wind through grain—the ground answers a half-second later from every direction at once, and the effect is of the world itself being struck. Cups of water on stone shiver into rings. Your teeth buzz. Nothing living here has external ears, because external ears would be a liability; the striders hear through their legs, and the lows, if they hear at all, hear through the shell.

The sound is why the light seems dimmer than it is. It is only an overcast afternoon, cool, the sun a paler smear in a grey sky—but the roar occupies so much of your attention that the eyes seem to give up their share, and the plain looks darker, greyer, further away than it can be.

The people who live here are few. Their marks are everywhere and they themselves are seldom seen, and both facts follow from the noise.

The marks: lines of cairns, waist-high, stacked from lows' shells cemented with the mat itself, which hardens when it dries, marking routes across the sheet-water where the rock is sound. Shallow pits hacked into the film-beds with hand-picks of chipped quartz, where the mat has been harvested and pressed and left in slabs to cure—it is eaten, it is burned for a smoky heat, it is caulk. Middens of strider bones, split for marrow, the long leg-bones saved and made into tools. A single upright stone, chest-high, out on the open plain with nothing near it, its top worn smooth and grooved by rope.

And the houses. There are only a few in any stretch of the plain, and they are all the same: low, round, drystone domes of fitted gneiss slabs a full arm's length thick, sunk half into the rock where a bowl offered a start, with one small door facing away from the nearest strider-bench and hung with a curtain of pressed mat. They are not built for warmth, though it is cool enough, and not for rain, though it is always wet. They are built for quiet. Inside one, with the curtain down, the roar drops to a thick hum in the walls and you can hear your own breathing for the first time in hours, and the shock of it is close to grief.

The people wear plugs of cured mat in their ears, packed deep. They speak with their hands, a fast sign of fingers and the flat of the palm, and when they need to say something at distance they strike the ground with a bone or a stone in patterns, and the ground carries it. A single blow travels farther here than a shout ever could. They know which stone under their feet leads to which stone under a neighbor's. They can tell from the ring of a footfall where a flock is moving. The children learn the sound-map of the plain before they learn to hunt, and an old person who has gone deaf—they all go deaf—still reads the plain through the soles of the feet.

They hunt striders at the close of the wave. When the bray passes through a flock and the ground answers, there is a moment in the return where the striders stand rigid, listening down through their legs, and in that moment a person who has crept up along a cairn-line can take one with a thrust. They do not kill many. There is no need. The plain makes more of everything than can be eaten; the difficulty here is never food. The difficulty is the sound, and the wet, and the way the two together get into a body and never entirely leave.

The plain cycles. The sheet-water rises and falls with something that is not the tide and not the rain, some slow breathing of the ground itself, and when it rises the lows move onto the benches and the striders follow and the noise concentrates and climbs, and when it falls everything spreads and the roar spreads with it into a wider, lower thing. The film dies back grey where the fronts have grazed it and floods back purple within the day. Shells accumulate; the plain very slowly rises, a fingernail's width in a long life, on its own dead. Somewhere, always, a flock is braying and the ground is answering, and somewhere a strider is stooping to a shell, and somewhere a seep-face is weeping the same water it has wept since before the hills were gone.

It is the oldest place. It has nothing left to become. And it has never, for one instant, been quiet.
