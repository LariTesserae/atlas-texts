You come to it from below, if that word means anything here, because there is no higher ground to come from. The whole world has been brought down to one level. What was once mountain and what was once seafloor have both been resolved into the same surface, a plain that runs in every direction until the curve of the planet takes it away from you, and the plain is made.

Not made in the sense of a city, where made things sit on a natural floor. There is no natural floor. The surface you stand on is the outermost layer of something that goes down for a distance nobody standing here can measure, layer under layer of the same pale, fitted material, each one older than the last, each one still at work.

---

The light is the first thing, and for a long time it is the only thing.

It comes from everywhere. There is a sun, probably — a brighter region drifts across the white sky over the course of a day — but it is not responsible for most of what you see by. The plain itself is luminous. Not glowing the way a screen glows, with a source behind it, but the way a heated thing glows, from within its own substance. The color is close to the color of the inside of a shell. Bone, but softer. Your shadow is a faint gray rumor pooled under your feet, and it does not move when the bright region in the sky moves, because the ground is lighting you from beneath as much as the sky is lighting you from above.

After an hour your eyes have narrowed as far as they will go and you understand that this is simply what the world is now. It is a radiator. Something deep in the stacked layers is still hot, and the plain exists to carry that heat outward and let it go as light. Every tile of it is a mouth exhaling brightness into the sky. It has been doing this for a length of time that has worn the edges off everything.

---

The tiles.

Once you have stopped squinting at the whole and started looking at the ground, you see that the surface is not continuous. It is an assembly of enormous fitted pieces — some the size of a room, some the size of a field — and the seams between them are the only lines in the landscape. From a standing height the pieces look flat and polished. Lie down and put your eye to the surface and you see that they are not flat at all: each one is very slightly domed, a rise of a few centimeters across many meters, and the surface is minutely pitted, dimpled, softened. It has the texture of something that has been rained on for an unthinkable time, except that it has never rained here. Whatever wore it was not water. It was the light itself, leaving. Or the wind. Or just duration.

The seams are dark. They are the only dark thing. They run in patterns that are almost regular and never quite, the way the cracking on very old glaze is almost regular, and they are wide enough to put your fingers in. Inside them the material of the tiles goes down and becomes another tile's edge, and below that another, and the darkness between them is not shadow but distance. Warm air rises out of the seams. It smells faintly of something mineral and something metallic and something that is neither — a clean, hot, empty smell, the smell of a place where nothing has rotted for a very long time because there has been nothing to rot.

---

You would expect a world this bright to be loud. It is the quietest place you have ever stood.

The tiles make no sound in their work. The heat leaves as light and light is silent. What you hear, when you stand still long enough for your own body to stop being the loudest thing, is the wind, which is not so much heard as felt in the ear — a pressure that comes and goes. And under the wind, very rarely, a tick. A single small dry sound, like a stone settling in a wall. It comes from no particular direction. You wait a long time for another and when it comes it is somewhere else, kilometers away perhaps, carried to you across the smooth surface the way sound carries across ice.

The ticks are the tiles adjusting. This whole plain is alive in the mechanical sense; it holds its own level. Beneath it the planet still has a geology — it still has convection, still has continents that want to drift — and the plain does not permit any of that to reach the surface. Somewhere down in the layers, pieces are lifting and sinking by fractions of a millimeter to keep the top perfectly, planetarily flat. Each adjustment is a tick. A thousand ticks a second, across a hemisphere, and from where you stand you hear one every few minutes.

---

The plain has weather, because anything this large has weather. The heat coming off it rises, and cooler air from somewhere — from the night side, presumably, from wherever the great bright region has not yet reached — slides in low to replace it. So there is wind, and the wind is nearly constant and nearly gentle, and it is warm the way the inside of a hand is warm. It never gusts. There are no obstructions for it to gust around. It passes over the domed tiles and the dark seams and it makes, where it crosses a seam at the right angle, a faint whistled tone that is more like the memory of a tone than a tone. You hear it once, walking, and stop, and it stops.

The wind carries dust. Not much. The tiles shed a fine pale powder as they age and the wind moves it around and drops it in the seams, where it accumulates, and where something happens to it.

---

Here is the first living thing.

In a seam wide enough for two fingers, at about the depth of your wrist, on the shaded face of the lower tile — shaded is the wrong word; less bright — there is a crust. It is gray-green, the color of old copper roofing, and it is textured like felt. It is a lichen, or the thing that lichen has become here. It grows only in the seams, only on the faces that receive light from the sky but not from the ground, and it grows very slowly, and it lives on the dust and the warm exhaled air and on whatever trace of moisture that air carries up from the deep layers, where something down there still has a chemistry that requires a little water and lets a little water go.

There is no other water. You will not find a pool, a drip, a bead of condensation. If you walked for a year you would not find a place where the ground was damp. But there is this: the air rising out of the seams is fractionally more humid than the air above, and the felt-crust knows how to take the fraction. It is the whole of the vegetation of this world. It is sparse in the way stars are sparse — a great deal of nothing between each patch — and because the seams run everywhere there is, in the aggregate, an enormous amount of it, a planetary lichen, a green-gray hairline map of the whole surface that you would never see from any height, because from any height the plain is simply white.

---

You walk for a day. Nothing changes and everything moves.

This is the strangeness of the place, the thing that takes you longest to accept: the plain is entirely still and constantly in motion. Sit in one spot for an hour and watch a seam. It will widen. Not dramatically — a millimeter, two — as the tile beside it lifts on its slow tide. Then it will narrow again. The tiles breathe. Over the course of a day a single tile will rise and fall perhaps a centimeter, and its neighbors will do the same but not quite in time with it, so that the whole surface is very slightly, very slowly undulating, a solid swell with a period of hours and an amplitude you could cover with your thumb. You cannot see it. You can feel it if you lie flat and stop thinking. The ground is rocking you.

And the brightness pulses. This takes longer to notice than the breathing, because your eyes adjust and hide it from you, but the tiles are not all at the same temperature at the same time. A wave of slightly greater warmth moves across the plain, and the tiles brighten as it passes and dim behind it. The waves are hundreds of kilometers long. Standing still you feel it as a slow tide of warmth against your face — warmer, over the course of several minutes, then less warm — and it has nothing to do with the sky.

Once, near the end of the day, you see a tile replaced. There is no warning. A seam about fifty meters from you widens and does not stop widening, and the tile within it sinks, level, into the darkness, and the seam is for a moment a square hole full of warm rising air, and the tile that comes up in its place is the same color and the same size and its surface is smooth — polished, unpitted, not yet worn. It rises and settles with a single soft tick and the seam closes to its finger's-width and it is over. The new tile is brighter than its neighbors. It will take, you understand, a very long time to become as old as they are, and there is a very long time available.

---

The fauna, when it comes, comes at that new tile.

You almost miss it. Something the size of your hand, pale, low, moving along the seam beside the new tile with the deliberate speed of something that has never had to hurry. It has too many legs and no obvious front. It is the color of the plain and it is eating — not the lichen; there is no lichen on the new tile's edge yet. It is eating the tile. Or cleaning it. It moves along the fresh edge and where it has passed the edge is fractionally less sharp.

It is the only animal you see all day, and then, farther along, near dusk, another, and then nothing for three days. They are rare in the way the lichen is sparse: there is room for them everywhere and they are almost nowhere. They live in the seams and they come up to attend to new edges and to the lichen, which they also eat, and something eats them — you never see it, but twice you find, in a seam, the emptied shell of one, pale as the plain, clean as everything here is clean, with a hole in it. Whatever made the hole is also part of this. A world this large and this old does not have a single species. It has a few, very spread out, going on with each other in near-total silence while the ground they live on radiates the heat of a planet into a white sky.

---

There is a horizon and it is wrong.

It is too far. The plain is so flat and the air so clear that you see farther than a horizon should let you, and the far edge of the world has a faint upward hook to it, as though the plain were the inside of a shallow bowl. It is not. It is the light: the ground is bright and the sky is bright and in the distance they merge, and your eye, looking for the line, puts it higher than it is. Distant things — the darker suggestion of a wide seam, a pale hump where a tile has risen a little more than the others — seem to float. There are no objects here that are not the ground. No towers, no ruins, no wreckage. Whatever was here before the plain was either taken down into it or became it. The built thing is total; it has finished. There is nothing left over.

---

At night the great bright region leaves the sky and the sky goes from white to gray to a deep, luminous violet, and the plain does not dim at all. It goes on giving up its light. You lie on the warm slow-breathing ground under a sky that is dark for the first time and the ground beneath you is as bright as noon, and the dust the wind carries is lit from underneath as it passes, so that the whole air is full of very faint rising sparks, and the seams are black lines through a white world, and far off, a tick. Then, far off in another direction, another.

You realize that you have not, in all this time, seen anything break. Nothing has fallen, cracked, failed. The plain is not ruined. It is not even old in the way ruins are old. It is old the way a riverbed is old — worn into the shape that it will stay in, having found that shape, having finished changing. It has the settledness of a thing that has reached its final form and now simply persists in it, adjusting by fractions, replacing one piece in a million, exhaling, breathing, holding itself flat against a planet that would rather not be flat, and lit, and quiet, and going on.

Whoever made it is not here. That is the last thing you understand, near dawn, when the bright region begins to come back into the violet and the sky starts its climb toward white. There is no sign of them and no sign that they left in any hurry. They are not in the seams. They are not under the tiles. They built a world that would not need them and it does not, and it does what it was built to do, and the small pale things go along the edges and the gray-green felt takes its fraction of water from the warm rising air, and the wind moves the dust, and the dust falls into the dark and is turned into something else, and a tile somewhere lifts by a hair, and settles, and the sound of it settling arrives at you across the whole flat bright breadth of the world, a single dry click, and then it is quiet again for a long time.
