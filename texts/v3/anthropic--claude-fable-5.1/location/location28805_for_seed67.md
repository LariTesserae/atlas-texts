Start with the floor, because everything here is floor.

It is a single poured surface, or wants to be taken for one. Up close it resolves into plates: hexagonal at some places, long rectangles at others, no consistent module, as if different foundries cast different provinces and the seams between them were simply accepted. The material is a pale grey ceramic with a faint green cast, dense enough that a knuckle against it returns almost nothing — no ring, only a dull tock that the air swallows. Mold lines are still on it. Along many of the seams there is a low ridge of flash, a millimeter or two of overspill that no one ever ground off, sharp enough to catch a fingernail. The corners of the plates are crisp. Nothing has rounded them. The floor is warm to the touch, always, the temperature of a hand, and it is very slightly wet everywhere, a film that does not bead and does not evaporate but simply stays.

It is low. There is nothing here that could be called a rise except the machinery, and even the machinery keeps its head down. A person standing here can see, in the direction the gradient of the seams suggests is downhill, some fifteen or twenty kilometers before the haze closes over the view, and in that whole distance the surface drops perhaps a meter. It is not a plain. A plain has been leveled by something. This was made level, and there is a difference you can feel in the feet — the way the drainage seams run exactly parallel, the way water on the surface moves in one direction only and at one speed only, a slow shear toward the collecting channels, with no puddles and no dry spots, because nothing has ever been given a reason to pool.

---

The water comes from above and from below.

From below: at intervals of a few hundred meters the floor is interrupted by low housings, waist-high, hexagonal or round or square depending on the province, with a grille on top and a shallow apron around the base. Warm water rises through the grilles. Not fast. It wells, rather, and the surface of it trembles, and steam leaves it in a sheet. There is a note under the welling — not a sound exactly but a pressure in the sternum, the presence of a pump that is very large and very far down and turning at a fixed rate. It has been turning at that rate for as long as the floor has been here. The water is clean, slightly mineral, slightly alkaline, the temperature of a bath that has been left a while. It carries almost no dissolved gas. It smells of nothing, or of warm ceramic.

From above: the sky is the same grey as the floor, without a seam in it. Whether there is a true sky above this one is a question the place does not answer. The light is a full, even daylight — no shadows fall, or every shadow falls straight down and is swallowed, and nothing here is high enough to cast much — and it does not brighten or dim. The vapor rising from the wells and from the animals and from the floor itself goes up into that grey and condenses and comes back down as a drizzle so fine it is closer to a heavier kind of air. It does not fall in drops. It arrives on the skin as a temperature. The steam sky and the wet floor are the two halves of one system, and the system is in balance: exactly as much water goes up as comes down. There is weather here, in the sense that the air moves in enormous slow cells you could map if you had a year and a continent to map them on, but the weather never changes, so it is hard to say it is weather at all. It is more a climate that has been left running.

---

Along the seams, where the mineral in the water has had time to work, there is the first and only erosion: a pale scale, like the deposit in a kettle, a hairline of white crust following every drainage channel. In the older provinces — one guesses they are older; the flash is a fraction more rounded, the scale a fraction thicker — the crust has built to the height of a coin. That is the full extent of what time has done to this place. Elsewhere there are scuff marks, a dull polish on the plates in the lanes where the animals crowd, and that is all. The place is new the way a coin is new: it has been handled, but it has not aged.

---

Now the animals, because they have been in the picture the whole time and it has been an act of attention to describe the floor first.

They are everywhere. They are, in terms of what a visitor actually sees, the landscape. The floor is visible only in the lanes between them and in the aprons around the wells, and in those places it is visible only because they are shoulder to shoulder just beyond. They stand. Mostly they stand. In the near distance, where the drizzle has not yet greyed them out, they can be counted in the low thousands from a single vantage; further off they become a texture, then a tone, a rumpled brown-grey that fills the whole width of the view and goes under the haze and does not stop.

They are roughly the size of a large hog, or a small cow, long-bodied and short-legged, with skin rather than fur — a thick, slightly translucent skin with a bloom of moisture on it that catches the flat light. Their heads are low and blunt. Their eyes are small and mild and set forward, and they do not blink often. They breathe steam. In the warm damp air each exhalation is briefly visible, and above a crowd of them the vapor rises in one continuous column and joins the sky, and it is clear, once you have watched this for a while, that they are a large part of the weather.

They are quiet. This is the thing that takes longest to understand, and the thing the place most insists on. Several million animals in view and no calls, no lowing, no snorting, no hoof-clatter; they do not move enough to clatter, and when they do move it is a slow shift of weight, a step, a settling, and the ceramic under their pads gives only that same swallowed tock. What is audible, if one stands still and stops trying to hear the machinery, is breathing. A sound like surf a long way off, but with no rhythm in it, because there are too many of them for their breaths to fall into phase. That, and the drip. And under both, the pump.

---

There is nothing green here. Nothing that photosynthesizes, nothing that could. In the collecting channels, where the warm water runs a little slower, there is a slick — a brownish, gelatinous film that thickens in the eddies behind the well-housings, that smells faintly of iron, that is neither plant nor exactly anything else. It is the closest thing the place has to a wild ecology, and the animals do not seem to eat it.

What they eat comes from the floor.

At intervals — a different interval from the wells, set on a different grid, so that the two lattices interfere across the whole surface in a slow moiré — there are troughs. Long shallow channels, cast as part of the plates, with a slot along the bottom. Something is extruded from the slot: a pale paste, warm, faintly sweet-smelling, the consistency of set custard. It comes out at a rate that exactly matches the rate at which it is eaten, so that the trough is always a hand's-depth full and never more, and there is a steady quiet slow motion of animals toward the troughs and away, no faster than the paste appears. They do not compete for it. There has never, it seems, been a reason to.

Where the paste comes from is not shown. But the place is a closed loop and everything in it goes back to the floor eventually, and there are other slots — in the aprons of the wells, in the lanes — that are not troughs. Low grilles, wider than a body, over which the warm air moves a little faster than elsewhere and from which a faint smell of rendering sometimes rises before the drizzle takes it. An animal that lies down and does not get up is, within a period that is not long, gone from the lane. Nothing is seen to take it. The lane is scuffed there, and warm, and then it is only the lane.

So the fauna has no plants under it. It has a factory under it. The animals are warm because the water is warm, and the water is warm because the pump is turning, and the pump is turning — one assumes — on the animals: on their heat, or their weight, or their slow conversion of paste into more animal. It is a ranch with no rancher and a power station with no grid, or it is neither of those and the loop has no purpose but the loop. The place does not say.

---

Some things that hold here, that a visitor learns not by being told but by waiting for them to fail and finding they do not:

The population is constant. Not roughly constant — constant. There is birth, low and quiet and unremarkable, a wet small animal standing within the hour, folded in among the others so that in a minute it cannot be found. There is death, as above. The two rates are the same, or are made the same, and the count of animals on any given province of floor does not change from one day to the next by more than a rounding error. This is not natural. Populations do not do this. It is the kind of stability a thermostat produces, and there is no reason to think the analogy is only an analogy.

The light does not change. The temperature does not change. The animals do not sleep in any way that can be distinguished from their waking. There is no dawn to gather at, no night to huddle against, no season to move with, and so the entire behavioral repertoire of a large warm-blooded herd animal has been reduced to standing, stepping, eating, breathing, and occasionally, in a slow spreading shrug that passes through a crowd like a slow wave through a field of grain, all of them shifting their weight to the other side at once. Whether that wave is communication or simply a fluid effect of pressure among bodies packed too closely to move independently is not clear. It travels perhaps a kilometer and dissipates. Nothing comes of it.

Nothing wears down. That is the strangest rule and the one that takes longest to feel as strange. In any other place, this many bodies moving over a surface this long would have cut lanes, worn troughs, broken plates, silted channels, colonized every crack with something alive. Here, the flash is still on the seams. The mold marks are sharp. The kettle-scale along the channels is the one concession, and it is as if the place allowed exactly one clock and set it to run very slowly, so that a visitor could confirm that time was passing at all.

---

Walk, and it does not end. The provinces change — plate shapes, trough profiles, the pitch of the pump-note dropping or lifting slightly as one crosses onto a different set of foundations — but the animals do not thin, and the drizzle does not lift, and the haze stays fixed at its twenty kilometers like a wall that comes with you. There is no edge that can be reached. The scale of the place is not something that presents itself as a vista, because there are no vistas, only the near crowd and the grey. It presents itself instead as a kind of pressure: the knowledge, confirmed by every hour of walking, that the standing steaming millions in view are a sample, and that the sample is representative, and that the whole is large enough to have its own weather and to keep that weather, forever, exactly the same.

It is warm. It is close. The animals are near enough to touch and mostly do not mind being touched; the skin is warm and damp and gives slightly, and under it there is the long slow pulse of something that has never been asked to run. They look at nothing. They breathe out. The breath goes up into the grey and comes back down on everything as a fine warm rain, and the floor takes the rain to the channels, and the channels take it to the wells, and the wells lift it back, warm, trembling, into the light that does not change.
