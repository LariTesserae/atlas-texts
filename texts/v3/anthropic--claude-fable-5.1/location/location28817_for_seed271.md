The plateau above is dry. That is the first thing to understand about the hollow: it is a wet room set into a dry country, and the dryness presses on it from every side like the walls of a house press on the air inside.

You come to the edge before you see it. The ground is pale limestone scabbed with lichen the color of ash, and it runs flat and cracked for miles in the heat, and then the cracks widen and the ground simply is not there. The hole is perhaps forty paces across, roughly oval, deeper than it is wide. The rim is rounded, not sharp — softened by ten thousand years of small rains dissolving the stone grain by grain. Nothing here is recent. The walls that drop away are fluted and dimpled, gray shading to yellow shading to a dark rust-brown where water has worked longest.

There is a way down. Someone found it or made it, and the difference no longer matters: a diagonal ledge, wide enough for a foot placed sideways, with three steps cut into the steepest part. The cuts are shallow now, the edges blunted, but they are cuts. A chisel did that. Nothing else in the hollow suggests the chisel ever came back.

---

At the bottom the light changes.

It is not dark. Dark would be simpler. It is the light of a room with one high window on an overcast afternoon — enough to see the color of things but not the detail, enough that the walls seem closer than they are. Direct sun reaches the floor for less than an hour, around midday, as a warped rectangle that slides across the stone and climbs the far wall and is gone. The rest of the day the hollow is lit by sky reflected off the pale upper rock, a light that comes from everywhere and casts no shadow.

The air is warm. It is always warm. The stone holds the day's heat and gives it back at night, and the walls block wind, and so the temperature down here hardly moves between noon and midnight, summer and winter. It is the warmth of an animal's flank, of bread an hour out of the oven. It sits on the skin.

And it is damp. The walls sweat. Not everywhere — the dryness of the plateau above extends a few feet down the rock, and there the surface is powdery and warm — but below a certain line, a line that runs level around the whole hollow like the mark of an old flood, the stone darkens and glistens. Water comes through it. Not in streams. It emerges as a film, a sheen, gathering into beads that hold themselves against the rock for a long time before their weight wins and they run. The drops fall from overhangs at intervals you could set a clock by. Some of them have been falling onto the same spot for so long that the floor beneath holds a shallow cup, smooth as the inside of a shell, always full.

That is the sound of the place. Drops. Irregular, spaced, each one distinct — a soft *tk* onto stone, a rounder *plip* into water. Between them, silence. Not the silence of absence but of enclosure: the hollow swallows the wind that scours the plateau, and what remains is the sound of your own breathing and the drops and, if you stand very still, a faint continuous hiss that may be the seep itself, water moving through the porous rock behind the walls, or may be the blood in your ears. It is hard to tell. After a while you stop trying.

---

The floor is uneven — a slope of fallen blocks and rubble at one end where the wall came down long ago, its angles all worn to curves, and a flatter apron of packed silt and stone at the other. Between the blocks, in the cracks where water collects, the plants live.

There are not many. A fern, dark green, glossy, no taller than a hand, growing in a horizontal seam where a drop lands every eleven seconds. A mat of moss in the shape of a spilled drink, following the exact outline of the wet zone on one boulder and stopping cleanly at its edge, brown and crisp beyond the line. A single shrub with small leathery leaves, rooted in the rubble, leaning toward the noon rectangle of sun, its trunk twisted from decades of reaching the same direction. On the ledges of the walls, where seep water lingers, thin green films and small liverworts, flat and lobed, pressed to the stone like wet paper. That is the vegetation, more or less. Each plant has found its one square of workable conditions and stayed there, and there is bare stone between every one of them.

Nothing moves among them. No insects hum; no lizard slides across the warm upper rock. Whatever eats here has already eaten and left, or never came. The absence is not sinister — it is simply that this room is small and the food is smaller, and the way down is steep, and the plateau above offers nothing to encourage a crossing. The hollow is a place life has visited rather than colonized. The ferns are the settlers. Everything else is passing through, and almost nothing passes.

---

The built things are easy to miss, and then impossible to stop seeing.

The cut steps, first. Then, in the flat end, a low arc of stones — six or seven, set on edge and fitted together, knee-high, forming a partial curve that once must have been a ring. It encloses nothing now. Behind it, in the wall, a niche has been squared off: a rectangular recess about the size of a loaf, its back wall smooth, its floor slightly hollowed. Empty. Water has run down over its lip and left a pale mineral beard beneath it.

And the basin. This is the most deliberate thing in the hollow. Where the largest overhang drips steadily, someone deepened the natural cup into a proper bowl, perhaps an arm's length across, and cut a small notch in its downhill rim so that overflow runs in a controlled trickle across the floor and into the rubble rather than pooling. The basin is full. It is always full. The trickle from the notch is a thread the width of a finger, and it makes the second sound of the place — a whisper under the drops, so constant that you register it only as a change in the texture of the quiet.

The water in the basin is clear. You can see the chisel marks on its floor, rounded now, under a skin of brown mineral. The rim has been worn smooth on one side, at exactly the height where a hand or a jar would rest.

That is all. No door, no roof, no tool left behind, no mark on the wall except the mark of water. Whoever came here came for the water, and shaped the room slightly to make the water easier to take, and stopped. They did not build a house. They did not need one. The hollow was already a house.

---

What holds the place together is its refusal to change.

The seep does not flood and does not fail. It is fed by rock that stores rain from the whole plateau and releases it at the same slow rate regardless of season, so that the wet line on the walls has not moved in living memory or in any memory before that. The basin overflows at the same speed on the driest August day as in the wettest winter. The moss keeps its shape. The fern does not spread, because there is no other seam like its seam. The shrub does not grow, or grows so slowly it comes to the same thing. The blocks at the rubble end fell once, long ago, and have been settling into each other ever since, and now they are done settling.

Stand at the bottom long enough and you feel it: the hollow is not waiting for anything. Places above are waiting — for rain, for wind, for the year to turn. This place has arrived. The drops fall. The stone is warm. The light is the gray light of every hour. The water rises to the notch and runs out and the basin is exactly as full as it was.

If you drink, you drink from a bowl that has held the same water for as long as there has been a bowl. It tastes of stone.
