**The Basin at Its First Hour**

The land is low and it is new. You come down into it over a lip of pale rock that has not yet learned to crumble, and the descent is almost nothing—a few meters, a gentle slump of the world—and then you are in the basin and it goes on for a very long way.

The ground is stone that broke recently and has not been touched since. It lies in plates, and the plates have edges. Not the rounded edges of anything that has known weather; these are the edges of something snapped an hour ago, thin enough at the rim to be translucent, sharp enough to open skin. Between the plates there are gaps a finger wide, and the gaps go down into a darkness that has no smell coming out of it. No grit has settled into them. No dust lies on the surfaces. Nothing here has been walked on or rained on or blown across, and the stone keeps the faint mineral sheen of a thing that has just cooled.

It is warm. The warmth comes up from the plates and down from the sky at the same time, an even bath of it, the temperature of an afternoon that has decided not to end. The air is drier than any air you have breathed. It takes the moisture off your lips within minutes and then, having taken it, seems satisfied. There is no wind. There is no dew. There is, as far as you can tell in any direction, not one molecule of water that is not inside your own body, and the basin seems faintly curious about that.

And it is green.

---

The growth begins a few strides from the rock lip and does not stop. It comes up out of the fissures between the plates in dense standing masses, chest-high, head-high, in places twice a person's height, and where the fissures run close together the masses merge into a solid wall of foliage that you could not push through without breaking it. It is lush in the way of a rainforest understory—layered, crowded, leaf over leaf over leaf so that no space is wasted—and it is the color of deep chlorophyll shading to a bluish black in the shadows, with the surface gloss of something waxed. From the lip it looks like a jungle that has been poured into a dry riverbed and left to set.

Up close the leaves are the width of a hand, long, tapering, ribbed lengthwise with fine parallel veins. They are stiff. When you bend one it resists, and when you bend it further it does not tear; it breaks, cleanly, with the sound of a thin tile snapping, and the broken face is dry. Not dry like a dead leaf—dry like a slate, like a wafer of graphite. There is no sap. There is no green smell. The veins, when you look into them with your eye close, are hollow, and they are empty, and they are very precise.

The leaf in your hand weighs almost nothing. Let it go and it falls, and lands, and lies on the stone, and you have the sense—impossible to verify yet—that it will lie there in exactly that attitude for a length of time that has no useful name.

The stalks are dark and columnar and cool to the touch despite the warmth of everything else, as though they were conducting heat somewhere. Where they emerge from the fissures there is no soil, no root mat, no crumbled leaf litter. The stalk simply enters the stone, and the stone is unbroken around it, and you understand that the plant did not push up through the rock. The plant and the rock happened at the same time.

---

The basin is the size of a valley, though nothing has ever flowed through it. You can see across it from the lip: a shallow bowl kilometers across, floored with fractured pale stone and filled nearly to the brim with dark green growth, the far rim a low line under a sky that is simply bright. Normal daylight. Cloudless. The light falls straight and even, and the leaves cast crisp shadows onto the plates below.

You look for the sun and it is there, high, a little to your left. You look at the shadows and they say the sun is a little to your right.

You look up again. The sun has not moved. The shadows have not moved. Both are correct in themselves, and they disagree, and the disagreement is as still as everything else.

This is the first of the rules, and the others come slowly, the way you notice that a room is not quite square.

Walking into the growth toward a stand that seemed fifty paces off takes many more than fifty paces. Turning back, the lip of rock where you entered is close enough to touch. The basin is a fixed size when seen and a variable size when crossed, and it does not seem to consider this a contradiction; it is simply that distance, here, is a property of looking and not of being.

The leaves are the same leaf. You notice this on the third or fourth stalk: not similar—the same. The same slight torsion a third of the way along the blade, the same faint notch in the left margin, the same eleven ribs. You go to another stand and find it again. The vegetation of the whole basin, however much of it there is, is one leaf instanced without limit, one stalk repeated with small permitted variations in height and angle. It is not that the world here is poor in detail. It is that detail, past a certain depth, was not needed, and someone or something knew exactly where that depth was.

You put your hand against a stalk and feel, under the coolness, a faint continuous tremor, far too fine to be called vibration. Not a pulse. Nothing so periodic. It feels like a sound that has been held at the beginning of its first cycle and not allowed to proceed.

---

Nothing here was built. There are no stones set on stones, no paths, no marks, no discarded thing of any kind. And yet nothing here grew either, not in the way growth is usually meant. There was no seed, no seedling, no season. The basin came into being as a completed statement: this rock, fractured just so; this canopy, at exactly this fullness; this light, at this hour. Whatever process wrote it worked below the scale of matter and left no tool behind, because it never had a tool. The engineering is in the way the leaf's hollow veins are laid out to carry a substance that was never going to exist. It is in the way the stone cooled without a heat source. It is in the fact that the plants are the color of chlorophyll in a place where chlorophyll would have nothing to do.

You understand, after a while, that this is what makes the basin *young*. Not that it was made recently by any calendar—there is no calendar that reaches into it—but that it was made at a particular instant and the instant has been kept. The edges are sharp because there has been no second moment in which to dull them. The fallen leaf will lie where it fell because the basin does not contain the time in which it would move. Erosion is a thing that needs a before and an after, and here there is only a during.

---

It is quiet. Not silent: your own footfalls sound, your breath sounds, the tile-snap of a broken leaf sounds. But these sounds behave the way distance behaves. A step taken in the shadow of a stand returns from the far side of the basin a half second later, from a direction that has nothing to do with the step. Break a leaf and the snap comes back to you twice, once very close and once from somewhere above. Between your own noises there is the hum—if it is a hum—that comes up through your feet and through your palm on the stalk. It is at the lower edge of hearing and it has no rhythm. It is the sound of a very large quantity of something being held exactly as it is.

You wait for a long time and nothing rustles.

Then, once, you see an animal.

It is small, the length of a thumb, a segmented thing with a domed back the color of the stone, resting on a leaf a little above eye level. It does not move. You watch it and it does not move, and you walk around the stand to see it from another side, and it is there too, on the leaf on that side, at the same height, in the same posture. You back away and there it is on the leaf of a farther stalk. It is not following you. There is one of it, and it is on every leaf you happen to look at, in the same way that the sun is wherever you look up and the shadows are wherever you look down. It is not hiding and not watching. It is the basin's single instance of fauna, placed once, and rendered wherever the placement needs to be visible.

You do not touch it. You feel, obscurely, that this would be a request the basin could not fulfill.

---

By the time you have walked far enough—by whatever measure walking has here—that the lip of rock is a thin line behind you and the far rim is a thin line ahead and the green stands crowd close on every side, near enough to touch, layered so densely that the light comes down through them in slots and blades, the place has become clear to you in the way a picture becomes clear when you stop trying to find the object in it.

It is a garden with the water taken out. Not dried: taken out, at the level of the description, so that everything which in an ordinary place would be built around water—leaf, stem, channel, the whole architecture of thirst—is present here in perfect fullness and simply does not need it. The lushness is real. The dryness is real. There is no seam between them because there was never any moment at which they were negotiated.

It is a landscape in the tense of a photograph. Warm, bright, low, dense, green, silent, sharp-edged, and finished. Someone, or some process past the reach of any physics you were taught, wrote a valley at the moment of its first afternoon and then declined to write the second. What remains is that afternoon, extending regionally in all directions, at equilibrium, with a sun that is in two places and a leaf that is in every place and a small stone-colored animal that is in every place too, and a stone floor whose fractures will never be walked smooth because there is no weather in the basin and there never will be, because weather is a kind of change and change is the one thing the basin was not given.

You break one more leaf, to hear the sound. It comes back from behind you, and from above, and then the quiet closes over it like the surface of a very still, very dry pool.
