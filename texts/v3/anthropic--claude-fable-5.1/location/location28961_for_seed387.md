The place is a room, and the room is full of water, and the water is never still.

Start from above, because that is where the city is. A district of the city sits on top of it: tramlines, a laundry, a school with a tar roof, a power station with two brick chimneys, a canal basin where barges once unloaded coal and now unload nothing. Under all of that, beneath the basements and the sewers and the deeper sewers, there is a vertical shaft about forty feet across and something over a hundred feet deep, lined with engineering brick laid in the 1890s, and it is full to within a few yards of its cap with warm, dark, moving water.

It was built as a surge chamber. When the turbines up in the power station take load or drop it, the water in the great penstock tunnels has nowhere to put its momentum, and it comes here, into this shaft, and rises, and hangs, and falls. That is the whole function of the place. It is a lung. It exists so that somewhere else does not burst.

---

Come down into it.

The cap is a ring of iron plate with a manhole in it that no one has opened in a long time, and under the cap there is a foot or two of air that smells of hot metal and old wet brick, and then there is the surface, and the surface is a thing you would not want to be near. It heaves. It does not wave — there is no wind, no fetch, no shore — it heaves as one body, rising two or three yards in the time it takes to draw a breath, hanging, sinking with a sound like a ship's hull settling. When it drops, a rim of brick is exposed for a moment, black with a century of algae and mineral crust, streaming, and then it is covered again.

Below the surface the water is warm. Not bath-warm; warm the way a stone step is warm at dusk in summer, because the water in these tunnels has been through the station's condensers and has taken heat from them, and has been round and round this system for years, decades, and every circuit adds a little. The temperature is even from top to bottom. There is no thermocline, no cold layer near the floor, because nothing is allowed to settle here long enough to stratify.

There is very little light. A single lamp in a cage — the fitting is brass, the glass is thick and pale green and has been scoured almost opaque — is fixed to the wall about a third of the way down, and it is still, against all reason, lit. The light it gives is the light of a candle behind a wet sheet. It reaches perhaps ten feet before the water takes it. Beyond that ring the shaft is not dark, exactly. It is brown. It is the colour of tea seen from inside the cup.

---

The room is empty. This is the first thing the body understands about it, once the eyes have adjusted and the shock of the sound has become merely the condition of being here. It is a huge volume and there is almost nothing in it. There is the water. There is the brick wall, a continuous curve, so that turning in place you meet it on every side and it never presents an edge or a corner. There is the lamp. About two-thirds of the way down there is a single iron ladder-rung, the sole survivor of a ladder that must once have run the whole height; the rest have corroded away, and this one remains for no reason anyone could give, standing out from the wall like a letter of an alphabet. There is, on the floor, a low mound of something — silt, scale, the black paste that industrial water leaves — and half-buried in it, a spanner the length of a man's forearm, its jaws crusted shut.

That is the inventory. Four things in a chamber you could stand a church in.

---

And the sound.

Nothing about this place is more itself than the sound. Water carries sound four or five times as fast as air and refuses to let it go, and this shaft is a resonator, a closed brick pipe coupled at its foot to miles of pressure tunnel and at that tunnel's far end to turbines the size of houses spinning at a few hundred revolutions a minute. Everything the machines do, the water reports. Not as noise, or not only as noise. As pressure. It arrives in the chest before the ear. There is a fundamental — a deep, unbroken groan that seems to come from the wall itself, from the brick, and is really the turbine runners passing their blades through the flow, blade after blade, each one a small hammer — and above that a shriek of bearings, thinned by distance to something almost like a whistle, and above that the screams and knocks of the water tearing at itself in the elbow where the tunnel turns up into the shaft.

And then the surges. When a gate opens somewhere upstream the whole column jerks. There is a report like a gun fired in a cellar, a compression you feel in your teeth, and the water begins to climb, and as it climbs the sound climbs with it, a rising roar that has weight, and then it peaks and hangs, and in that hanging there is a half-second of something almost like quiet — never quiet; the machines never stop — and then the fall, and the fall makes a hollow booming that goes on much longer than the fall itself, echoing round the curve of the wall, meeting itself, interfering.

If you stayed long enough — and no one has — you would begin to hear that it has structure. The shaft is an organ pipe, and it has notes. Certain frequencies the brick likes; certain frequencies the water likes; at certain heights of the column the two agree and the whole chamber rings at a single tone so pure and so loud that the small debris in the silt lifts and hovers. The wall knows this. Where the sound has stood for a century at its nodes, the mineral crust has grown thick, in bands, so that the shaft is ringed at intervals with pale ridges of calcite like the marks left inside a kettle — a written record of the notes this room can hold.

---

Erosion here is not weathering. Nothing here is exposed to weather. But everything here has been worked on, continuously, for a hundred and thirty years, by warm moving water, and it shows. The brick is not rough. Every edge has been rounded, every mortar joint recessed into a shallow groove, so that the wall has the texture of a thing that has been handled — a stair rail, a coin. The iron of the lamp cage has swollen with rust into something rounded and lumpish and almost organic, a black cauliflower with a green light in it. Whatever the original floor of the shaft was, it is gone, replaced by that mound of dark scale, which the surges continually rearrange without ever removing.

The place has settled into itself. Whatever it was when it was new — raw brick, bright iron, clean grey water — it is not that any more, and it has not been for a long time. It is in a state. The turbulence is a permanent condition, not an event. A storm that has been blowing since the reign of a dead queen.

---

Life.

Almost none. This is not a dead place, but it is very nearly a place without anything living in it.

There is no plant. There has never been enough light for a plant and the water is too hot and too fast and too full of the metals it has picked up in the condensers. There is, on the brick in the upper half of the shaft, above the lamp, a film. It is dark red-brown, and it is slick, and it is thickest in the band that is exposed when the water drops. It is bacteria, a mat of them, iron-eaters, sulphur-tolerant, things that do not need light and do not mind heat and have found in this violent room something they can live on. When the surface falls and they are uncovered they gleam for a moment like wet leather, and then the water covers them again.

And there is one animal. Or there is, at least, something that moves against the flow rather than with it.

It is an eel. It came in, presumably, as a young thing through some screen that had rusted open, years ago, and it did not find its way out again. It is long now — longer than anything the intake screens should have admitted — and pale, having lived so long without light, and it lives in the seam between the bottom of the ladder-rung and the wall, wedged there, and it comes out at the peaks of the surges when the water is briefly slack, and moves through the brown dark with a slow fluent motion, and then returns before the fall. It eats the bacterial film, perhaps, or things carried in from the tunnels. No one knows. It has never been seen by anyone who could have reported it. It may be the only thing in the city that has heard the turbines every second of its life and does not know that they are a sound.

---

Some rules.

Nothing in this room is ever quite where you left it. The surges move everything that can be moved. The spanner has travelled, over its long life on the floor, perhaps thirty feet in a slow circuit around the shaft, following a current that no one has mapped.

Nothing here is ever still, and nothing here is ever quiet, and neither of these is an emergency. Above, in the station, men watch dials and pull levers and the shaft is a number on one of the dials — a level, a pressure — and when the number moves violently they nod, because that is what the number is for.

The water is warm everywhere and equally. The warmth is the city's warmth, the heat left over from lighting its streets. Whoever entered this shaft would be bathing in the residue of a million evenings.

The light does not go out. It should have. The circuit it is on was decommissioned before the war; the bulb should have failed within a year of that. It has not. Nobody has been down to check, so nobody has had to explain it, and the room keeps its one small unaccountable fact to itself, glowing green in the brown.

---

Stand — hang, rather, since there is nothing to stand on — in the middle of the shaft at the height of the lamp. The wall curves away on every side into tea-coloured dark. The water leans on you from all directions with the same warm weight, and then it leans harder, and you rise, and the roar rises with you, and the pale ridges of calcite go by — one, two — and you hang, and the eel is somewhere below you moving through the slack, and for a half-second you can hear the room's own note under all the other noise, a single tone the whole chamber agrees on, brick and water and iron and the two chimneys far above and the tramlines and the laundry and the whole weight of the city humming down through its foundations into this one hidden hollow —

and then the fall.
