You come down to it by a ladder of galvanized steel that has gone the color of tea, and the heat reaches you before the light does. It rises up the shaft in a column, wet and mineral, so that by the time your boots find the last rung your shirt is already dark at the collar and your glasses have fogged twice. There is no season down here. There is only the temperature the machines make and the temperature the rock makes, and the two have agreed on something around body heat plus a fever.

The chamber is not large. Call it the size of a parish church that has been laid down on its side — long, rounded at the ends, its ceiling perhaps twelve meters up where the excavation crown was shot and then sprayed. Because that is what this is: a room blasted out of living rock and given a concrete skin, a junction vault where three district heating mains meet a fourth that comes from somewhere deeper and hotter than anyone above ground thinks about. The walls were never meant to be looked at. You can see the drill holes still, a stubble of them, each with its little collar of grout. In places the shotcrete has slumped before setting and hardened mid-slump, so the wall carries the frozen shape of wet material giving up on gravity. It has the look of a thing not finished. The rock beneath, where it shows through, is dark and greasy and unweathered, edges still sharp from the fracturing — geology that has not yet learned it is exposed.

And yet the room is already making new rock. Every seam in the concrete weeps. The water comes through warm, saturated with lime pulled from the cement, and where it drips it leaves a little more of itself behind than it takes. Straw stalactites hang from the crown in ranks, most of them no longer than a finger, some of them long enough to have started curving under the breath of the ventilation. Beneath each one a nub is rising from the floor. Between the pipes, where the drip has been steady for years, there is a rill of flowstone the color of old teeth, pooled in tiny terraces, and in each terrace the water stands for a while before it overflows and moves on. This is the pace of the place. The pumps cycle on a schedule measured in hours. The bat colony moves on a schedule measured in the length of a night. The stone moves on a schedule that will outlast the pipes it is slowly wrapping, but you can see it moving if you know how to look: the rills are wet, and the wet parts are the newest parts, and the newest parts are always the same width as a single drop.

The mains themselves are the room's furniture, and there is little else. Four insulated pipes, each thick as a mature tree, come in through steel collars in the end walls and meet at a manifold in the center — a cast housing with hand-wheels the size of cart wheels, painted a red that has survived only on the undersides. The cladding is scarred and stained with rust bleeding down from the strap buckles. Two pumps sit on plinths off to one side, their motors housed in grey drums, and beside them a transformer cabinet with a caged lamp on it. That is the inventory. Between these things there is floor: a wide raw floor of gravel and cured slurry and shallow standing water, with much space between one presence and the next, so that the manifold stands in the middle like an altar nobody goes near.

The light is poor and comes from four sodium fixtures on stanchions, two of them out. What's left is the color of a match seen through amber. It does not reach the crown; the ceiling is an inference. Around each working lamp there is a halo of condensation drifting in the heat, and on the wall behind each lamp, in a circle no wider than a dinner table, the only green in the place: a film of algae, a crust of moss, and in the largest patch a single fern that has found enough grit in a crack to hold itself up. It is the size of a hand. It has not grown in a long time. It is not dying either. Beyond the lamp circles the walls are bare and black and slick.

What is not bare is the ceiling, and you understand this by sound before you understand it by sight.

The room is loud in a layered way. The lowest layer is the pumps, a rolling mechanical roar that you feel in the sternum, with the transformer's fifty-cycle hum threaded through it. Above that is water — drip everywhere, but also a steady pour from a failed seal somewhere at the far end, falling into a sump and hissing where it hits the hot pipe on the way down. And above that, laid over everything, is a texture you take at first for static: a granular, shifting, ceaseless chitter, a sound made of ten thousand small sounds, and when your eyes adjust and you tip your head back you see that the crown of the vault is not concrete at all but a moving skin. Bats. Free-tailed bats, packed shoulder to shoulder across the whole arched ceiling and down the upper walls to the height where the lamp glow begins, a living upholstery that ripples when a disturbance passes through it and then settles. Their bodies are warm and they add to the heat. Their breath adds to the wet. The sharp, sweet, ammoniac note under the mineral smell — you had taken it for the machinery — is theirs.

Below them the floor belongs to what they feed. The gravel is not gravel in places; it is guano, decades deep in the dry lee of the manifold, and it seethes. Cockroaches in numbers you would call a carpet if carpets moved. Cave crickets with legs too long for their bodies. Beetles that live nowhere else and have never seen the sun and will not miss it. In the shallow standing water there are amphipods and worms, and in the sump under the failed seal a slow population of pale fish nobody stocked, which came in through a culvert years ago and have found the warm outflow entirely adequate. Geckos hold the concrete around the lamps. A rat crosses the gravel with the confidence of a landowner. Above the bats, in the ventilation duct itself, swifts have wedged nests into the louvers, and their chatter comes down the shaft at dawn as the bats come up it — two traffics passing in a tube, the one going out to the dark and the other coming in from it.

There is so much life here that it has stopped being remarkable, the way a crowd stops being individuals. It fills every surface that will hold it. But it does not fill the space. The middle of the room stays open, gravel and puddles and the amber cones of the lamps, with a single beetle crossing it like a thought.

Nothing happens quickly. The pumps come on and something shifts in the manifold, a long groan of expanding metal, and the bat skin shivers and re-settles. A drip lands. A stalactite is one drop longer. At some point in the evening, without any signal you can detect, the colony begins to peel from the ceiling in sheets and funnel up the shaft you came down, and for twenty minutes the chitter becomes a roar that competes with the machines, and then the ceiling is bare grey concrete for the first time, sweating, studded with straws, and you can see how much of it there is. The pumps keep going. The water keeps coming through. The room, uninhabited for a night, goes on quietly turning itself into stone.
