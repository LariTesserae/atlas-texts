The chamber is smaller than a room and you enter it from above, through the water.

There is no door. There is a square hole in a floor, and under the floor there is water, and the water fills a box of made stone right up to the underside of the floor so that the surface trembles against the lip of the hole like something held in a cup. When you drop through, the sound changes before anything else does. Above, it had been a dull rumble. Below, it is a roar folded in on itself — a single hard voice coming from every wall at once.

The box is perhaps four strides across and a little more than a man's height deep. Its walls are brick, but not brick you would see anywhere else: they were fired hard for this, laid tight and true, then coated in a lime plaster that has been floated smooth with a wooden trowel until it is the colour of the inside of a shell. The plaster is not finished curing. Where the light strikes it you can see the trowel's passes, long crescents overlapping, and in one corner, low, a set of finger-marks where someone pressed a patch into a joint and left it. The corners are crisp. Nothing has softened yet. No lime has run, no salt has bloomed, no green has taken hold except in the one place it has, which we will come to.

The light is the second thing.

The floor above is not solid. It is a grid of beams, and between the beams there is only sky, and the sun stands very nearly overhead. It comes down through the openings in stiff square shafts, and where each shaft enters the water it breaks and multiplies. The whole chamber is full of moving white. The plaster walls throw it back. The floor of the tank — also plaster, laid a finger thick over a bed of crushed brick — throws it back. There is no shadow to rest your eyes in. Even the bricks that make the underside of the beams, seen from below through the water, are lit from beneath by their own reflection. Every ripple prints itself on every surface. When the surface is briefly still, the shafts of light become columns you could almost put your arms around; when it is disturbed, the columns shatter into a net that slides across the walls faster than you can follow.

The water is warm. Warmer than blood, not so warm that you would flinch. It comes in through a clay pipe in the north wall, low down, a pipe with a mouth you could fit your fist into, and it comes hard. This is the roar. Somewhere upstream — beyond the wall, beyond the reach of this place, in a channel you cannot see and are not meant to — someone has built a fire under a cistern, or led a hot spring down a stepped channel, and this is where it arrives. The jet from the pipe pushes a fat column of turbulence out into the middle of the tank, and the whole body of water turns over slowly around it, a wheel a room wide, rising on the north side and sinking on the south. Loose bits of lime that flaked from the trowel work spin in this wheel. Nothing living rides it.

On the south wall, opposite the inlet and higher up, a second pipe takes the water away. Its mouth is cut square, not round, and it has a stone sill worn — no, not worn; the sill is sharp, it was cut sharp and it has stayed sharp — a stone sill set exactly at the height the water is meant to reach. The surface stands a thumb's width below the beams because of this sill and no other reason. The exit pipe sings a higher note than the inlet, a sucking whistle under the roar, and when the inflow surges it gulps.

You can stand on the floor with the water over your head, and the soles of your feet register the grid of the sun through the water, alternately warmed and less warm as the ripples pass. You can push off and hang in the middle of the wheel and let it carry you once around. It takes about the length of a held breath. You will see, on the way round:

The inlet's white bloom of bubbles. Bubbles climbing the north wall in a sheet, each one carrying a bead of sun. The east wall, blank, blinding, its trowel-crescents. The exit pipe's dark square mouth and the fringe of very fine, very new mineral crust just beginning to form along the top edge of its sill, a fur no thicker than breath. The west wall, and low in it, the thing that is growing.

It is not much. Where two bricks meet under the plaster there is a hairline the plasterer missed, or the plaster shrank from as it dried, and in that line something has caught — a green so faint it reads as a discolouration until you are close enough to see the threads. Warm water, day-long light, mineral to hold onto: it will not stay this small. In a season the whole west wall will be soft with it and the roar will have a body to muffle against. For now it is a single line no longer than your forearm, and it is the only thing in this chamber that anyone did not decide.

Otherwise nothing lives here. No fish, because there is nowhere for a fish to come from — the inlet is too hot upstream and the outlet falls through a grate. No insects on the surface, because the surface is inside a house. No snails on the plaster, because the plaster is three weeks old. The water is clean in the way only made water is clean: it tastes very slightly of lime and very slightly of clay pipe and of nothing else.

Left on the floor of the tank, in the southeast corner where the wheel's current is weakest, are the things that made it: a wooden float, its face grey with dried lime; a mallet with a fired-clay head bound to a stick; a length of cord with knots at even intervals, coiled and sunk with a stone. Someone set the level with that cord. Someone left the tools and climbed out through the hole in the floor and did not come back for them, or came back and looked down through the beams at the water standing exactly where it should and decided the tools could stay.

The sun moves. Over an hour the shafts of light lean, the squares on the floor pull long, the bright side of the room swaps with the less bright side — though there is no dim side; there is only the side the columns strike and the side that receives their scatter. The inflow does not change with the sun but it changes with whatever feeds it: for a while it pulses, a stronger push every few breaths, and the wheel of water speeds and the exit pipe gulps in time; then it settles to its steady shove and the whistle steadies with it. Steam does not rise here, because the surface is pressed against the beams and the air above is the air of a lit house. But when you surface into the gap beneath the floor, your face in the finger of air, the beams are wet on their undersides and every drop that lets go strikes the water with a sound you can hear separately from the roar, small and exact, the way a single voice can be heard under a crowd.

Look up through the grid, from underneath, at the sky.

Then go under again, where it is louder and brighter and warmer and the walls are new and the one green line on the west wall is a little longer than it was when you arrived, and the wheel takes you round.
