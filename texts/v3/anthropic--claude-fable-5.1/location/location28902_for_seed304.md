The ground gives before you reach it. For a long way there is only marsh — sedge, standing water the colour of weak tea, hummocks that take your weight and then decide not to. The reeds are the last real vegetation. They thin as the ground firms, and where the ground finally becomes something you would call ground, they stop altogether, because nothing roots in what comes next.

What comes next is a mound. It is not high — a house-height above the marsh, perhaps two — and not wide, a few hundred paces across, and it is pale, the pale of a wet tooth. From the reed line it looks like a heap of shells somebody stacked and then rained on. Closer, the shells resolve into roofs, and the roofs resolve into a town.

---

There is a spring under it. That is the whole explanation and also not enough of one. Cold water comes up through the middle of the mound, comes up hard and steady from somewhere below the marsh, and it comes up carrying lime — carrying so much dissolved stone that when it touches air it cannot hold it. It lets go. Everything the water runs across, it stones over. The mound is the spring's own deposit, built up over centuries by nothing but this, a finger's width a year, and the town is what people built on top of it because the mound was the only firm thing for a day's walk in any direction and they needed to be near the water for the same reason they needed to be away from the marsh.

So the town is packed. It has no choice. Off the mound is not an option; the edge of the deposit is the edge of the buildable world, and you can see the line exactly, a pale lip where the crust ends and black mud begins. Everything that was going to be built has been built inward and upward. Walls share walls. Roofs do not meet at gutters; they overlap like scales, each one stepped a hand's width below the one uphill of it, so that water sheeting off the top of the town passes over every roof on the way down and leaves a little of itself on each. There are no streets. There are gaps — shoulder-wide, some of them narrower, some that you have to turn sideways for, some that have closed entirely and are now just seams in the stone where two houses have grown together.

---

The material is the water's own. Where the spring comes up, in a wide shallow basin at the crown of the mound, the deposit is still soft — a white paste with the give of hard cheese, laid down in the last few seasons. This is where the stone is cut. It cuts with wood. It cuts with a sharpened antler, with a flat bone, with the edge of a shell. Blocks are lifted out wet and dripping and carried down through the gaps on people's shoulders, leaving a trail of white slurry, and they are set into walls while still soft enough to mark with a thumbnail. Two weeks in the air and they are stone. A season and they are joined to the blocks beside them by the same process that made them, because the water is still running over the wall, still letting go.

There is no mortar. There has never been any need for mortar.

Nor is there much metal. A few copper things, traded in, kept dry in lidded boxes and used carefully — a small blade, a needle. The copper goes green in a month here and then goes white, because the crust does not care what it covers. Most of the town's tools are wood and horn and its own stone, ground and reground. The scrapers are wood, always. A hard scraper would dig into the walls; a wooden one takes the skin off and leaves the block.

---

Scraping is the labour of the place. It is what you hear, when you hear anything.

The air on the mound is cool and never dry. The spring water comes up cold, and the sheet of it moving over every surface takes the warmth out of the air above, and the marsh sends up its own damp from below, and where these meet the town sits in a soft persistent condensation. Every surface is beaded. Lintels drip. The insides of the walls sweat, and the sweat, being spring water that has seeped through the block, leaves its faint whiteness on the inside too, so the rooms crust as well as the outsides, more slowly, from the walls inward. A house left alone shrinks. The doorway narrows a little every year. The floor rises. The ceiling comes down. Eventually the room is a cavity the size of a fist in a solid white block, and eventually not even that.

So nothing is left still. This is the first rule and the one the children learn before they learn anything else. A basket set down by the outflow channel for a night has a skin on it by morning that will not brush off. A shoe left on a step is a shoe-shaped stone in a month. Doors are not hinged — a hinge would seize — but lifted, whole, out of the frame every morning and set down somewhere else, and the frame scraped, and the door scraped, and the door set back. Sleeping mats are rolled and hung. Cooking is done on stone that is itself replaced each season, the old hearth-slab pried up an inch thicker than it was laid.

The people scrape their houses the way people elsewhere sweep them. Morning, evening, a pass over the walls with a flat wooden blade, and a little white powder falling, and the powder swept into the channels and carried off to become part of the mound again lower down. It is quiet work. The sound is a dry hush, repeated, from many directions at once and never loud, like a town full of people brushing crumbs off a table.

---

The channels are the second rule. The water has to be told where to go.

From the crown basin, the overflow runs down in cut runnels — hand-deep, palm-wide, ancient in the sense that they have been cut and recut in the same places for as long as anyone knows, because if a runnel is neglected it fills itself, and the water climbs out of it and goes somewhere else, and the somewhere else is always a wall or a floor or a threshold. A family that lets its channel go for a season finds the house downhill of theirs sealed shut. There have been quarrels about this that lasted generations and left their evidence in the stone: a gap where a house was abandoned and let fill, so that now it is a solid white mass with the ghosts of a window and a lintel still visible in its face, and the houses on either side leaning into it.

Where the runnels leave the mound they fan out over the pale lip into the marsh and the water goes brown and ordinary and disappears among the reeds. At the lip the crust grows fastest — thin terraces, rimmed pools, a scalloped edge that advances into the mud a little each year, so the buildable world does get bigger, but slower than the town needs it to.

---

Very little else lives here.

Moss does. Moss is the only green thing on the mound and it is everywhere the water moves slowly — a dark velvet on the shaded sides of walls, in the runnels, along the lower roofs. It is also the thing the crust loves most. Moss holds water, and held water lets go of its stone, and so the moss stones over from the roots up even as it grows from the tips, and the result is a stone that is green all the way through, faintly, like a bruise. The people cut it deliberately. It is the finest material they have — it takes a polish, it shows the lace of the moss inside — and the lintels of the oldest houses are made of it, and it is a thing you can trade, the only thing.

At the edge, in the marsh, a few willows, stunted, their roots cased in white where the outflow reaches them so that they look as though they are wading in it. Reeds. Nothing with a trunk on the mound itself.

Fauna is rarer still. A dipper works the outflow channels — a small dark bird that walks into moving water and comes up with something. Snails, small ones, pale, on the wet walls in the mornings, and these too are scraped off, with regret, because a snail that dies in a crevice becomes a stone snail in a season and stone snails are unlucky. Eels, in the brown water beyond the lip. Once or twice a year a heron stands on the pale edge for a morning and then goes. There are no dogs. There were dogs once, the stories say, and they would sleep against the warm hearth-walls and wake with their fur crusted white and shed it in patches all summer, and they were unhappy, and they were taken away. There is a cat that belongs to no house and sleeps nowhere twice.

---

The light is grey-white, most days. Cloud sits low over the marsh and the mound throws it back. When the sun does come through, the whole town glitters wetly and briefly and every drip becomes visible, thousands at once, and then it clouds again. There are no shadows sharp enough to be called shadows. Things are lit evenly, from all sides, by the sky and by the pale ground.

Under the sound of scraping there is the sound of the water, which you stop hearing after a day. Under that, very rarely, voices, kept low — the wet stone eats sound, and people who live among it learn not to raise theirs. A child sometimes. A block being set down. The bird.

---

The town is old in a way that is hard to see, because the old parts are inside the new ones. Dig down — people do, sometimes, cutting a new cistern or reopening a filled channel — and you go through floors. Below the present floor, a hand-span of white, and then an older floor. Below that, the upper part of a room that someone lived in, its ceiling cut through, its walls still standing, its interior half-full of the same stone as the walls, so that a bench along one side is a bench-shaped swelling and the hearth is a lump. Objects are in there. They are recognisable and unrecoverable — a bowl, a loom-weight, once a whole ladder, encased where it leaned. The people do not dig them out. They cut what they need and lay a new floor over the opening and that room becomes part of the mound.

So the town is always doing two things. It is being built — blocks cut soft, walls raised, roofs restepped — and it is being buried, at the same rate, by the same water, from below. It rises on its own history like a thing standing on its own shoulders. Nobody knows how many towns are underneath. Nobody has reached the bottom of the crust.

---

In the evening, when the light thins, the scraping stops house by house, and the doors are lifted back into their frames, and the mound is quiet except for the water. Somewhere a last wooden blade goes over a wall and a little powder falls and is carried away. And overnight, over every roof and every step and every threshold that was cleaned that day, the spring lays down its finger's width of the year to come, cold and patient and white, and in the morning they begin again.
