From a distance it reads as a peak. That is the first mistake everyone makes, and the mountain has had a very long time to make it easy: the flanks are the color of rock, the summit is capped in white, cloud pools in its saddles and slides off its shoulders the way cloud does on any high thing. It is only when you are most of the way up, above the last of the weather, above the altitude where anything with lungs finds the air adequate, that the regularity becomes impossible to ignore. The slopes meet at angles that are almost the same on every face. The ledges recur at intervals. The white on the summit is not snow but scale, mineral crust, laid down and smoothed over until it looks like something the sky did rather than something that was poured.

It is a tower. It was a tower. What it was for has been eroded off it along with its edges, its ornament, its stairs, whatever ran along its outside. Wind and water have worked it back toward a shape that wants nothing, a broad low-shouldered cone, the way old teeth go. It is not falling down. It has finished falling down. What remains is the part that could not fall any further, and that part has settled into itself with a kind of patience that reads as contentment if you are inclined to read it that way.

The room is inside the cap.

---

There is one way in, and it is a way water uses more than anything else does: a grille near the top, iron once, now a rounded lattice of rust and lime that looks grown rather than cast, its openings wide enough to put a body through if the body is careful. Water comes out of it. Not much. A steady spill, not a torrent, that slides down the outer face and freezes a little way down and thaws a little way after that and eventually, far below, joins the cloud. From inside the grille you can hear it leaving.

Inside is full. That is the thing that has to be said plainly. The room is a single chamber perhaps the size of a large kitchen or a small chapel, its floor and walls and low domed ceiling all of one material, a brick that has stopped being brick, and it is filled to the ceiling with water. There is no air pocket. There is no surface. The light comes in through the grille and through a second, smaller opening nearly opposite it, and it comes in already wet, greened and softened, and it falls across the chamber in two long slow bars that move when the water moves.

The water is warm. Warmer than the air outside by a margin that seems, at this altitude, like a violation of some agreement. It smells of iron and, faintly, of the sea, though the sea is a week's travel in any direction and several miles down.

---

Everything in the room is built. There is no stone here that was not placed. But the placing was so long ago and the water has been so constant that the made things have gone soft-edged and organic: the corners of the room are not corners anymore but curves, the mortar lines have filled and vanished under a brown-gold deposit, the floor has a shallow bowl worn into it where a current has turned in the same place for longer than the current's makers have existed. If you press your palm against the wall it is slick and slightly warm and there is nothing under the slickness that a hand can identify as a joint.

There is one object. It stands a little off the center of the floor, roughly the height of a person, and it is the pipe.

The pipe rises out of the floor and stops at about chest height with a flared mouth like a bell or a trumpet. It is iron and lime like everything else, but it is rounder and thicker and the crust on it is layered in visible rings, decades or centuries each, and it is the loudest thing in the room.

Something is under the pipe. Something very far under it. Every few seconds—not regularly, not quite, there is a slight drift in it, a slowing and hurrying that suggests a mechanism working against a load that changes—the floor of the room shudders and the pipe gives a low, hard, single stroke of sound that you feel in the sternum before you hear it. A cough. A lift. And with each stroke a bolus of warm iron-smelling water surges up out of the bell and rolls across the ceiling and pushes an equal volume out through the grille, and the whole room breathes once.

The engine has never been seen. It is somewhere in the body of the tower, in the miles of it that are now indistinguishable from mountain. It may be one engine or many. It may have been designed to do this or may be doing it because whatever it was designed to do has long since been worn away and this is the motion that remains when nothing else can. What is certain is that it has not stopped. The water in this room has never gone still. The overflow line on the outer face is a groove nearly a meter deep.

Between the strokes there are other sounds, and this is where the room gets loud in the way a factory floor is loud rather than the way a drum is: the pipe hums, a continuous, slightly sour tone that has to be the column of water in it resonating with whatever drives it; the grille rings faintly as the outflow passes through it; and the walls themselves tick and click, constantly, everywhere, at a frequency just above hearing and a volume just below discomfort.

The ticking is alive.

---

The vegetation is the second thing you notice after the pipe, and then you cannot stop noticing it. It is not lush the way a jungle is lush, crowded and competitive. It is lush the way a very old garden is lush: a few things, each of them enormous and established and unhurried. Curtains of a dark green weed hang from the ceiling in the two bars of light, some of them longer than a person, their tips brushing the floor, moving in a slow rhythmic sway that has the pump's tempo in it. A red-brown alga carpets the north wall in a single continuous sheet, thick as felt, that has been growing there in the warm current so long that it has developed a topography, ridges and gullies of itself. On the pipe's bell, and only on the bell, something grows in white nodules like frozen cauliflower that turns out on closer inspection to be partly plant and partly mineral, an alga that precipitates lime as it lives and is slowly building the pipe taller.

There is a great deal of empty water between these presences. The room is not full of things. The room is full of warmth and sound and light, and things stand in it at intervals, like furniture in a room whose owners have died.

The animals came up the pipe. That is the only route in and it is the route they took, however many generations ago, arriving blind and pale from whatever hot dark reservoir the engine draws on and finding at the top of their ascent a lit room full of food. They have been here long enough to change. There are shrimp, small and glassy, in the weed curtains, in the alga felt, in the crevices of the wall where the brick has receded—thousands of them, clicking, and they are the ticking. There are small fish with eyes too large for their bodies, eyes that are new, evolutionary afterthoughts, that hang in the light bars and pick at the shrimp. There is a thing like a flatworm the length of a forearm that grazes the red alga in slow arcs, leaving pale trails that the alga closes over within a day.

And there is the eel.

It lives in the throat of the pipe, below the bell, in the dark where the surge comes from. It is thick as a thigh and mottled the exact color of the lime crust and when the pump strokes it rides the surge up into the bell mouth and hangs there for a moment with its head out in the light, and then the surge passes and it settles back down. It has learned the tempo. Every so often it comes all the way out, on a stroke, and takes a fish, and returns on the next.

It is possible that the eel is why the tempo drifts. It is possible that it partially blocks the pipe, and that whatever is far below labors against it, and that the room's breathing has, for as long as the eel has lived there, had an animal in it.

---

The rules of the place are few and the place has stopped arguing with them.

Water comes up. Water goes out. The volume is constant and has been constant so long that the room's shape is the shape that volume makes. The current turns once around the chamber between the pipe and the grille, always the same direction, and everything that lives here lives in relation to that turning: the weed hangs downstream, the alga is thickest where the current slows, the shrimp cluster where it eddies.

The iron in the water comes out of it slowly and settles on everything. The walls are eroding under the current and being rebuilt by the precipitate at almost the same rate. The room is, in effect, remaking itself continuously in its own image, and the image is very slightly rounder each century. This is the ancientness of the place: not that it is falling apart, but that it has found a shape in which decay and construction are the same motion, and is holding it.

Cold air comes through the grille sometimes, in the small hours, when the outside temperature drops sharply enough, and where it touches the warm water a skin of fog forms on the outside of the lattice and hangs there and burns off with the sun. From inside you can see it as a dimming of one light bar. That is the weather.

Once in a long while something new comes up the pipe. A different shrimp. A pale crab. Once, judging by a shell wedged in the crust of the far wall, something with a spiral to it. The room accommodates or does not. The eel takes some of them.

---

It would be possible to stay a long time. The water is warm enough. The light is enough. The sound, after a while, stops being sound and becomes the floor, the given, the thing everything else stands on—the stroke and the hum and the ten thousand clicks, a made room at the top of the world announcing to nothing that it is still running.

What is strange is not that a machine still pumps water to the summit of a mountain that used to be a tower. Machines do what they were set to do. What is strange is how much the room seems to want it. How the weed leans toward the bell. How the eel keeps time. How the walls tick with appetite. Whoever built this cannot have intended a warm green loud full room at the end of it, a pocket of ocean above the clouds with its own small hungers. But the water has been arriving for so long that the arriving has become the point, and the room has arranged itself around that, and it is difficult, hanging in the light with the pipe's stroke going through you, not to feel that this was what the tower was for after all—that it wore itself down to exactly this, and stopped.
