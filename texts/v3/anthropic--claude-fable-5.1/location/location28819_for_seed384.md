Start with the cold, because the cold arrives before anything else. It is not the cold of air, which touches you; it is the cold of water a fraction above its freezing point, which holds you. It presses evenly from every side. It slows the blood. It makes the dark seem thicker than dark should be.

The dark is not total. Down here, twenty-odd fathoms under a surface you cannot remember, there is a light that is less than light — a rust-colored suggestion, low and to one side, the way a stove's door glows in an unlit kitchen. It comes from the works.

---

The works sit on the floor of a drowned valley, at the lowest point, where the old river once slowed enough to be useful. It is a small place. You could cross it in the time it takes to say a long prayer. There is a boundary wall of dressed stone on three sides, sagging but unbroken, and on the fourth the ground simply stops and goes down into a blackness even the rust-light does not enter. Inside the wall, everything is packed close: brick sheds shouldered against brick sheds, a boiler house, a tall square chimney, a second chimney that has snapped and leans against the first as if tired, cooling tanks, a gantry crane, a pump house with a round window like an eye. Nothing has room. Pipes run overhead between every building, doubled and tripled, so that the sky of the place — if the place has a sky — is a lattice of iron.

All of it is furred. Every surface wears a coat of something: the fine cold-water growth that is not quite algae and not quite silt, grey-brown, an inch thick on the flat places and paler on the edges where the current thins it. It softens the corners. It has rounded the bricks. It has been growing so long that the buildings look less built than accreted, like the town was secreted by something slow.

---

Vegetation is scarce and comes in only two kinds. The first is a ribbon-weed the color of old paper, anchored in the cracks between paving stones, that lies flat when the current runs and lifts, all at once, in the slack. The second is a single stand of kelp, tall as the chimney, growing not from the ground but from the top of the boiler house, where a hatch was left open and some seed found the warmth inside. Its fronds reach up into the dark and are lost there. It is the only thing in the town that seems to be trying to leave.

---

Animals are rarer still. A fish lives in the pump house. You see it once in a long while, a pale unhurried shape passing behind the round window like a face at a door, and then not for hours. Something with too many legs works its way along the base of the wall, never crossing open ground. Once in a season a shape comes in from the drop-off — large, without a clear edge, more a thickening of the water than a body — and moves through the streets, and the fish is not seen for a while, and then it is.

That is all. There is no chorus of small life. The water is too cold and the floor too poor. Whatever feeds here feeds on the snow.

---

The snow falls constantly. It falls from above, from the surface world, whatever is left of it: flakes of dead matter, grey, slow, so slow you can watch one for a minute and lose it only because you blinked. It settles on the pipes and on the tops of the sheds and on the rim of the cooling tanks. It is the weather of this place. In some hours it thickens until the far wall vanishes; in others it clears, and the rust-light reaches almost to the drop-off.

And it goes the other way, too. This is the first strange thing you notice, after you have stopped noticing the cold. From the mouth of the standing chimney a column rises — not bubbles, not smoke, but a shaft of water clearer than the water around it, faintly shimmering, faintly warm. It goes straight up until the dark takes it. The snow falling into it is lifted, turned, carried back the way it came. So the town has its own upward weather, a thin exhalation, and the snow around the chimney falls thinner than elsewhere, as if the works were shielding itself.

---

The chimney is warm because the works are running.

Not all of it. Most of the sheds are dead — you can tell by the thickness of the fur on them, by the way the snow lies undisturbed. But the boiler house hums. You feel it before you hear it; the hum comes through the water and into the bones of the hand you put on the wall, a vibration so deep and steady it is almost a temperature. Inside, through a gap where a window was, there is the rust-light: banks of gauges, their faces glowing that dull ember color, and behind them something turning. A flywheel, or a turbine, moving at a pace that seems too slow to do anything and never stops. The needles on the gauges move. They rise a little, fall a little, rise. Whatever they measure is being measured.

Where the power comes from is not obvious. There is no fire. There cannot be. Perhaps the drop-off breathes — the tidal push into the valley and out again driving the wheel like a millrace — and the works were built for exactly this, long ago, by people who understood that a river becomes something else when the valley floods and did not see why the machinery should stop. Perhaps. The gauges give no answer. They are labeled in a script the fur has half-eaten.

---

Sound: almost none. The hum is felt, not heard. The snow makes none. The weed lifting makes none. Occasionally — perhaps twice in a day, if days are still counted — a sound does come: a single metallic knock, somewhere in the pipe-lattice overhead, as some valve the works still uses opens or shuts. It travels through the water sharp and clean and is gone, and the silence after it is larger than before.

---

The cold does its own work here, and this is the second strange thing. Around the dead sheds, where the water is coldest and stillest, ice grows downward. Not sheets — fingers. Brine, denser and colder than the sea, leaks from somewhere above (a fractured tank, a surface floe, a wound in the world) and sinks in threads, and where the threads fall they freeze the water around them into hollow tubes, white, delicate, a hand's width across. They descend from the pipe-lattice like stalactites. They grow while you watch, an inch in the time it takes a snowflake to cross a doorway. Where they touch the ground they spread, and the paving stones ice over, and the ribbon-weed there goes stiff and stops lifting.

The brinicles never reach the boiler house. Its warmth stops them a yard from its walls, where they hang unfinished, dripping cold upward. The town is divided by this: a warm quarter, small, alive, humming, where the fur is thin and the fish keeps house; and a dead quarter, white-fingered, silent, where the snow lies deep and undisturbed on everything. The border between them shifts. In a bad season the ice takes another shed. In a good one the warmth pushes back and the tubes fall from the pipes and shatter on the stones without a sound.

---

You come to understand, slowly, that the place is not a ruin. A ruin is something that has stopped. This has not stopped. It has settled into a form that includes the water, the way a tree settles around a nail. The snow falls; the chimney lifts it. The ice grows; the works hold it off. The kelp reaches; the fish waits; the large shape comes and goes. The needles move. The valve knocks. Everything is very close to everything else and nothing is touching, and it has been this way for so long that it no longer looks like an accident.

At the edge of the wall, before the drop-off, there is a bollard where boats were once tied. The rope is still on it. It stands straight up in the water, held by a float that has long since ceased to be anything but a lump of growth, and its far end disappears into the dark above, toward a surface that may or may not exist, tethered to nothing anyone here remembers.
