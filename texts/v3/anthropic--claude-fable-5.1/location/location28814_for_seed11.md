Begin with the rock, because the rock is the only thing here that has finished.

It is a tableland so old that it has no edges you would call edges. It rises out of the world in a long grey shrug, an immense slope so gradual that a person climbing it would not know they were climbing, and it does not stop rising until it is higher than most things have any business being. Then it stops. Not at a cliff — there is nothing so dramatic left — but at a place where the slope simply forgets to continue, and the surface goes level, and stays level for a distance that takes days to cross. This is the top. Whatever was up here once — peaks, ridges, the architecture of a young mountain — has been rubbed away by so much time that even the memory of it is gone from the stone. What remains is close to a plane. A slight undulation. Shallow basins you could mistake for the impression a body leaves in a bed.

The stone is dark and dense and warm. That is the first strangeness. At this height the air should be thin and cold, and the air *is* thin — visitors breathe quickly, and their blood goes wrong for a while — but it is not cold. The ground gives off heat the way a hearthstone gives it off long after the fire has been banked. Whether the heat comes from far below, from something the plateau is still sitting on, or whether the rock has simply held the sun for so many ages that it has forgotten how to let go of it, nobody has established. Put your palm flat on bare stone and it is like putting your palm on a sleeping animal.

The plateau has finished. The air above it has not even begun.

---

The wind is the second thing, and after a short time it is the only thing.

It does not come from a direction. Lower down in the world, wind has an origin and a destination; here it has neither. It arrives from the east and the west in the same minute, rolls over itself, stalls, drops out of the sky as a downdraft that presses the vegetation flat in a widening ring, then lifts off again in a spiral that carries loose stalks and grit and water droplets a hundred feet up before letting them fall. It changes ten times in an hour. It changes in the middle of a single gust. There is no lee side of anything for longer than a few breaths, because the wind will come round and find you.

And it is loud beyond anything that can be described in words about loudness. The reason is in the rock. Over the ages the erosion that flattened the plateau did not stop at the surface; it found weaknesses, and it drilled. The tableland is riddled from top to some unknown depth with vertical shafts — some the width of a wrist, some wide enough to drop a house into — worn smooth as the inside of a shell. There are thousands. Tens of thousands. In the flat light they are just dark spots in the green, easy to miss until you are standing at the lip of one. When the wind crosses a shaft mouth it sets the column of air inside it ringing, and the shafts are all different lengths and widths, so every one has its own note, and the wind is never steady, so the notes never resolve into a chord. It is a landscape-sized organ played by something with no sense of music. The sound is a roaring, moaning, whistling, pulsing pressure that lives in the sternum more than in the ears. People who live here go deaf young and do not consider it a loss. They speak with their hands and with a kind of shouting-into-the-throat that is felt, not heard.

Occasionally a shaft will breathe outward instead of inward — a warm exhalation from below, smelling of wet stone and something faintly sulphurous — and for a moment its note drops an octave and the ground trembles a little under the feet. Then the wind takes the top of the breath and shreds it and the note goes back to what it was.

---

The vegetation should not be here and is here abundantly.

It is low. Nothing on the plateau stands higher than a person's knee, and most of it is lower than that, because anything that stood up would be torn out within a day. Instead it spreads. The surface of the tableland is covered almost without gap by a thick, resilient, deeply green mat of growth — not grass, not moss, something between the two and older than both, with long tough runners that knot across each other and a dense fine leaf that holds water like felt. It is lush in the way a wet sponge is lush. Walking on it is like walking on a mattress laid over stone. In the shallow basins it grows thicker and softer and the colour deepens almost to black-green; on the slight rises it thins to a bright yellow-green, wind-bitten, with the dark stone showing through in patches.

The mat is what the damp is for. Rain, when it comes, comes sideways and does not last — a grey wall shoving across the plateau, soaking everything in a minute, gone in five. But the clouds are constant. This high, the plateau is more often inside a cloud than under one, and the mat drinks condensation all day and all night, beading with it, dripping into the shafts. The runners are always wet to the touch. There is no standing water anywhere, no pool, no stream, because anything that gathers drains at once into the stone, and yet every surface is damp, and the air, when it is briefly still, is heavy enough to taste.

The wind tears the mat constantly. Great sheets of it lift at the edge, peel back, and go rolling across the plateau like a thrown carpet, exposing bare warm stone that steams faintly. The torn sheet lands somewhere else, upside down or in a heap, and within days it has rooted again wherever it fell. Nothing here is where it was last season. The green is permanent; the arrangement of the green is not. There are small hard fruits in the mat that ripen and are stripped off and carried by the wind and germinate wherever they fetch up, so that the whole surface is being continuously unmade and remade, a slow turbulent boil of vegetation over a rock that has not changed in ten thousand years.

---

There are almost no things.

This is the emptiness of the place, and it is difficult to convey to anyone who has not stood in it. You can see for a very long way — when the cloud lifts, the flat green goes out in every direction to a horizon that is slightly convex, the curve of the world showing — and in all that distance there might be three things. A boulder, left over from something, smoothed to an egg. A shaft mouth larger than the rest, with a lip of pale worn stone. Very far off, a low dark hump that might be a shelter. Between them, nothing but the mat and the wind and the sound. The eye slides across the space looking for something to rest on and finds only distance. People new here describe a sensation of being enormous and of being nothing; both, at the same time, and the wind pushing them from side to side.

The shelters are the built things, and they are built downward. Nobody raises a wall on the plateau; the wind would have it before the second course was laid. Instead the people who live here — and there are people, a few hundred perhaps across the whole vastness, in groups of a dozen or two — find one of the wider shafts and go into it. A few feet down, the wind loses interest. Ten feet down it is a heavy roar overhead, like being under a waterfall. They wedge slabs of stone across the shaft at that depth, pack the gaps with wet mat, and live on the platform they have made, in the warm dark, with the note of their own shaft humming through everything they touch. Above ground the only sign is a ring of stacked stones around the mouth, to keep the mat from growing over and to mark it as taken, and a wind-shredded thing on a pole — a rag of woven runner-fibre — that has no purpose anybody remembers.

Their tools are stone and bone and fibre. The plateau gives them dense dark rock that flakes well, and the mat gives them cordage, and the fruits give them a sour, filling food, and the shafts give them heat and water — a bowl left at the bottom of a shelter fills overnight from the drip. They have no metal because nothing here has ever been mined. They have very little of anything. An old woman sitting in the mouth of her shaft, out of the worst of the wind, twisting fibre between her fingers and feeling the note of the rock come up through her spine — this is most of what the built world amounts to. Sometimes, walking a day from one group to another, one passes an abandoned shaft with its ring of stones half swallowed by mat, and does not know whether the people left, or died, or simply went further down.

---

The animals are rare enough that each one is an occasion.

Something flies here. It is broad and grey and almost never seen, because it lives in the cloud and comes down only when the cloud does. When the plateau is inside weather and the visibility is an arm's length, you may hear — feel — a change in the wind, a downbeat, and then a shape passes over and is gone. Feathers turn up in the mat sometimes, long and stiff and the colour of the sky. Nobody has found a nest. The assumption is that they roost in the shafts, far down, past where anybody goes.

There are things in the mat — small, blind, many-legged, eating the runners from beneath — and things that eat those, quick and dark, flattening themselves into the green when the shadow of a person crosses them. Every few years a large slow animal is reported, a grazer as low and broad as a table, moving across the plateau with the wind pressing its hide, and then it is not reported again for a long time. Whether it is one animal or several, or whether it exists, is not clear. In this much emptiness the mind is generous with what it sees.

---

There is a moment, perhaps once in a day, when the wind falls.

It is not silence. The shafts go on humming from whatever pressure remains, and the ears — for those who still have them — ring with the absence. But for a few seconds the mat lies still, the beads of water on it stop shivering, and the cloud overhead stops tearing and simply hangs. The plateau is suddenly what it actually is: a flat warm stone, grown over, very old, at the top of the world, with nothing on it. Then somewhere far off a downdraft hits and the green goes flat in a ring and the sound comes up again and everything is moving.

The people say the plateau is asleep and the wind is its dreaming. This is not a belief so much as a description. The rock has done all it is ever going to do; it has arrived at the shape it will keep until the world ends. The air above it has never in its existence held a single form for longer than a breath. They lie against each other, the finished and the unfinishable, and between them the green is torn up and set down again, and the shafts ring, and a few hundred deaf people twist fibre in the warm dark, and something grey passes over in the cloud, and the sound goes on and on and on.
