The way to it is not a path. You come over the last rim of the cone on your hands, because the rock there is too new to have any give — it broke where it cooled and it cooled last month, or last week, and the edges have not yet learned to be edges anyone could walk on. The air at this height is thin enough that your ears feel wrong, and the sky is the dark, saturated blue that only exists above most of the atmosphere. And then the ground drops a body-length in front of you and you are looking down into it.

It is small. That is the first thing. A hollow in the black rock perhaps four strides across, roofed on one side by a lip of overhanging lava that froze mid-pour, so the space underneath has the feel of a room whose ceiling is dripping. You could lie down in it. You could not lie down in it comfortably, because it is very hot, and because of everything else that is in it.

---

The rock is the young kind: glassy, iridescent where the skin cooled fastest, ropy where it slumped. Along the fractures it is a deep oxblood red going to rust. Nothing has smoothed it. There is no soil, no lichen crust, not a single stem of anything — you are far above where plants agree to live, and this rock is far too new for them anyway. It is a mineral place. It shines.

And it weeps. Water comes out of the stone everywhere, in beads, in threads, in a slow uniform sweat, warmed by whatever is still cooling underneath. Where the overhang shadows the hollow the whole underside is fogged with condensation that gathers and falls in fat drops onto the floor, where it collects in a pool the size of a spread cloak. The pool is hot enough that you would not put your hand in it. It steams faintly. The steam rises to the overhang and condenses and falls again, a rain cycle two arm-lengths tall, running continuously, without weather, in perfect quiet.

Here is the first thing that is wrong about the water. It does not always go down. On the back wall a bead will form, tremble, and then travel upward along a fracture, unhurried, joining others that are doing the same, until a small stream is running vertically up the rock and dispersing into the fog under the roof. Farther along the same wall, the water behaves normally. The two behaviours are separated by a line you cannot find no matter how carefully you look.

---

The second thing is the animals.

There are more of them than there should be in a space this size. There are more of them than there could be. The floor of the hollow is not rock at the moment you first look at it — it is a shifting, dense pelt of small bodies, a layer several deep, moving in slow circulation toward and away from the hot pool the way a crowd moves toward and away from a fire. Some are segmented worms, blood-red and translucent, the kind that live in vent chimneys at the bottom of oceans and have no business at the top of a mountain. Some are things like flattened woodlice, pale, with too many legs, that graze the mineral film off the weeping rock. Some are mites so numerous that the red walls appear to shimmer. There are things with shells the colour of the rock itself that only reveal themselves when they move. There are small blind fish in the pool, in water that would cook a fish. There is a bird, or the shape of a bird, which is folded into the far corner under the overhang with its head tucked and its feathers beaded with condensation, and it is the same bird you will see later in the opposite corner, and again on the lip above you, and it is not three birds.

None of them make any sound. That is the thing you notice once you have stopped counting. Thousands of legs, mandibles, bodies pressed together and sliding over each other, and the hollow is nearly silent — only the drip of the two-arm-length rain, and once in a while a faint tick as a shell touches glass. The air is so thin up here that sound does not carry well, but that is not the whole of it. Some of the animals are making sounds. You can see the woodlouse-things stridulating, the small thoraxes working. You just cannot hear them. Their noise arrives as a pressure in your jaw.

---

The third thing takes longer.

The pool's surface is still, apart from the steam, and it reflects the overhang. In the reflection, the animals crowding the underside of the overhang are not the animals crowding it above your head. The reflected ones are larger, fewer, sleeker, and they are watching the water from their side as intently as you are watching it from yours. Occasionally one of the small fish rises, and in the reflection something rises to meet it, and the fish is gone, and there is no ripple.

The hollow is also, you begin to suspect, deeper than it is. When you lean under the overhang the far wall is a long way off — a room's length, a hall's length — though from outside the whole pocket was four strides. The worms recede into a reddish distance, packed thick all the way, and where the distance should end there is instead a kind of doubling: the same pool, the same overhang, the same you, leaning in from the same rim, at a slight angle, like an image printed twice by a shaking hand. When you pull back the hollow is small again. Your knees are hot. The rock has left its print on your palms.

---

There are no made things here. Nothing has been built. But on a shelf of the fresh glass at the pool's edge there are flakes — obsidian chips, thumbnail-sized, with the unmistakable curved fracture and fine edge of stone that has been struck deliberately. You watch long enough to see how they are made. One of the shelled things carries a small nodule of glass in its front limbs, braces it against the shelf, and brings a second stone down on it with a motion that takes it four tries. A flake leaps off. The animal turns it, tests the edge on its own carapace, and then carries it down into the pelt of worms, where the flake is passed from body to body and eventually used — you see this, or the reflection shows it to you — to scrape the film of red minerals off the weeping wall more efficiently than a mouth can. There are hundreds of these flakes scattered through the hollow, and all of them are being used, and none of them has been kept. There is no pile, no cache. The tools exist for a moment in someone's grip and then go back to being rock.

You realise the flakes are also forming on their own. The glass at the hot margin fractures as it cools and produces the same curved chips, and the animals do not distinguish between the ones they made and the ones the rock made, and after a while neither do you.

---

The hollow works. That is what keeps you at the rim longer than the heat should allow. It is a system in motion, a small closed engine — heat from below, water sweating from the rock, minerals dissolved and carried and deposited as red film on the walls, grazed off by the pale things, which are eaten by the shelled things, which are eaten, you assume, by whatever hunts in the reflection. The worms cluster where the water is hottest and thin out where it cools, a living thermometer. The fog under the roof thickens through the day as the rock warms in the thin sun, and the two-arm-length rain quickens, and the pool rises a finger's width and the crowd on the floor rearranges. Toward evening, when the air outside will drop below freezing in minutes, the overhang keeps a bubble of heat in place and the animals draw in under it, layer on layer, nearly still. The bird — the one bird, in its several places — unfolds and lifts its head and looks up at you with an eye like a bead of the same water that is running the wrong way behind it.

You could stay. The hollow seems, in some sense, to have room. But you have been sitting at the rim for what feels like the afternoon and the sun has not moved, and your shadow falls into the pool and does not appear in it, and your hands smell of hot iron and steam, and under your palms the glass has begun — very faintly, and without any sound at all — to sweat.
