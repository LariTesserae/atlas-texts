High on the shoulder of a volcano that has not finished being made, there is a room in the rock.

It is not a cave in the old sense. Nothing has dissolved here, nothing has been patiently carved by water over the ages, because there is no water; this high up, on this side of the mountain, rain is a rumor and the air arrives already wrung dry. The room is a bubble. A river of lava ran under a cooling skin, drained away, and left behind the shape of its own passage: a tube, a swelling in the tube, a chamber roughly the size of a village hall, with a floor that was once a liquid and a ceiling that was once the underside of a crust. The walls still carry the glaze of that moment. Run a hand along them — you would not, the heat and the dark would stop you, but if you did — and you would feel the lava's last gesture set in glass: drips that froze mid-drip, ropes, a shelf where the flow paused and skinned over before dropping again. Nothing has yet softened any of it. The edges are as sharp as the day they hardened. Geologically this place is an infant, a few centuries old at most, and the mountain it sits inside is still growing somewhere below, adding to itself in the dark.

The way in is a single throat, a place where the roof thinned and slumped, and it admits light only in the sense that the blackness near it is a slightly browner black. Past the first bend there is nothing. Not dimness — nothing. The eye, given time, does not adjust, because there is nothing to adjust to.

But the room is not empty. The room is full to the point of bursting.

Every square hand's-breadth of the ceiling is occupied. The bats hang so closely that they do not hang as individuals but as a fabric, a living upholstery that lines the vault and runs down the upper walls and crowds the glassy stalactites until the stone shapes are lost inside fur. Tens of thousands. Perhaps more; nobody has counted, nobody can. They shift constantly, a slow shuffling reallocation of space, a wing folded here so a neighbor can settle there, and so the ceiling has the appearance — if there were light to see it by, which there is not — of a surface that breathes.

They are the reason the room is hot. The mountain contributes; the rock underfoot is warm from below in the way a stove is warm the morning after, and there are fissures at the far end that exhale a faint, steady breath smelling of sulfur. But that is background. The real furnace is the colony itself. Each small body runs hot, and there are so many of them, so tightly packed under a roof that holds heat like a bell, that the chamber sits at a temperature the human body finds frankly hostile. The air is not humid — the mountain steals moisture too quickly for that — so it is a dry heat, a kiln heat, and it presses on the skin like a hand.

And it is loud. This is the thing that would meet you first, before the heat, before the smell. The roost produces a sound that has no gaps in it: a roar assembled from a hundred thousand small vocalizations, squabbles over space, the ultrasonic layers you cannot hear but can feel as a kind of pressure behind the ears, the constant papery percussion of wings being folded and refolded, the wet click of teeth. It is the sound of a plant running at capacity. Standing in it, you would find yourself thinking of turbine halls, of the interior of some enormous machine that has been left switched on. The volume rises and falls, but slowly, over the course of hours, tidal rather than rhythmic, and it never approaches silence. Underneath it, if you could subtract the colony, you would hear the mountain: a low, near-inaudible hum that comes through the soles of the feet more than the ears, the sound of a very large object doing something very slow.

There is a rain in this room. It falls continuously from the ceiling to the floor, a soft, steady patter under the roar, and it is not water. It is the colony's output, and over the centuries it has built the only sediment this young place has. The floor of the chamber, which was once a plain of black glass, is buried under a deposit that in places is deeper than a person is tall. Its surface is not still. It is crusted, and it moves.

Because the floor has its own population, and this one may outnumber the ceiling's. The deposit is a city of beetles — dermestids in numbers that defy the ordinary sense of the word, the surface layer a granular shifting mass of them, working the fresh fall as it lands. Under and among them, cockroaches the color of the substrate, pale and quick. Mites in uncountable drifts. Small predatory things — pseudoscorpions, a few centipedes — patrolling the edges of the industry, taking their percentage. Occasionally a bat, old or sick or simply unlucky, loses its grip on the ceiling and falls, and the floor receives it, and within a day there is nothing left but a shape in the crust and then not even that. The sound of the floor is the sound of dry grain being poured, endlessly, in the dark: a whispering, seething rustle that fills the frequencies the bats leave empty.

Everything here is fed by what comes in through the throat at dawn. Nothing photosynthesizes; nothing could. The bats leave at dusk in a stream that takes the better part of an hour to empty the room, spend the night hunting over the desert scrub far below on the mountain's lower slopes, and return before light with the night's insects converted to fuel. In that sense the chamber is a refinery. Raw material is gathered across a hundred square kilometers of dark country, concentrated into this one hidden pocket of rock, processed by the colony, and its byproduct is handed down to the floor, where a second tier of workers processes it again. The heat is a byproduct. The noise is a byproduct. The ammonia — thick, eye-stinging, a smell so dense it is nearly a texture — is a byproduct, and it crystallizes on the cooler glass near the fissures in pale, delicate crusts, the room's only flowers.

For all its violence of sound and smell, almost nothing here changes quickly. The colony has occupied this chamber for as long as the chamber has been cool enough to occupy, and the deposit on the floor has been growing at the pace of a fingernail — a millimeter, two millimeters a year, so that the floor is a slow tide rising toward a ceiling that will, in a few thousand years, meet it. The lava glass on the walls is not weathering; there is no weather to do it. The mountain beneath is not erupting; it is only warm, and thinking about it. The one fast thing, the dusk exodus and the dawn return, is a cycle so regular that it reads less as an event than as a heartbeat, and a slow heartbeat at that. Come back in a century and the room would sound exactly as it does now, and be a hand's width shallower.

It is, in the end, a small place. A pocket in a mountain's raw flank, no bigger than a barn, that you could walk past on the surface and never suspect, marked only by a stain of pale dust around a hole in the black rock and a smell that reaches you when the wind is wrong. Everything inside it is pressed against everything else; there is no unused space, no vacancy, no quiet corner. It runs hot and dark and deafening and full, a factory nobody built, in a chamber the mountain made by accident and has not yet gotten around to unmaking.
