Begin with the sky, because there is almost nothing else to begin with. It is not black. It is the dark of a room with the curtains drawn in the hour before dawn, a dark that has some brown in it, and it goes all the way down to the ground without a horizon line. You would think, at this height, that the stars would be close and hard. They are not there. Something very fine is suspended in the upper air — not cloud, nothing so wet — a dust so light it will take a season to settle, and it has erased everything above.

So the ground has to make its own light. It does, a little.

You are standing on the top of a thing that is still being built. Call it a plateau, though the word implies a finished surface, and this is not finished. It is the crown of an uplift so recent that the rock has not yet decided what shape it will hold. Under your feet the stone is warm — not hot, not the heat of a forge, but the heat of a body, of a wall with a chimney behind it. The warmth comes up through the soles and into the calves. It is the only comfort here, and it is constant, which is more than can be said for anything else.

The rock is dark and glassy in places, dull and pocked in others, and it has not been touched by anything since it formed. No rain has found it. No wind has had time to grind an edge. Every fracture is sharp enough to open a hand. Where the surface split as it cooled, the splits are clean as broken crockery, and in the deep ones, a few meters down, there is a color — a low red-orange, the ember shade, so dim you only see it by not looking at it directly. That is the light. There are perhaps a dozen such seams within walking distance and they are the only things that let you know where the ground is. Between them, the dark is entire.

Now the part that is hard to describe, because it is a violence with almost no sound.

The ground moves. Not shaking — the shaking is too slow and too large to feel as shaking. It is more that the ground is not the same ground from one hour to the next. A slab the size of a market square tilts a hand's width over the course of a night, and you notice only because the ember-seam you were using as a landmark is now uphill of you instead of level. A ridge that was a ridge at dusk is a step by midnight, with a new face of raw stone exposed along its foot, the face still faintly warmer than the rest. Fissures open. Not with a crack — the air is too thin up here to carry a crack any distance, and most of what would be sound is swallowed before it reaches you — but with a long, felt shudder and then a sigh, an exhalation of warm dry gas that smells of struck flint and something like scorched hair. Elsewhere a fissure closes, its two lips meeting and grinding, and where they meet a low ridge of crushed glass builds itself in the dark like frost forming in reverse.

The whole crown is doing this, all of it, all the time, in every direction, for as far as anyone could walk in a week. It is a turbulence without weather. Nothing falls from the sky and nothing runs across the ground. It is the earth itself that is in storm-state, rearranging its floor with the restless energy of a sleeper who cannot get comfortable, and the only sound it makes is that occasional long sigh, and a dry, high, intermittent ticking from every side — the sound of new rock contracting, stone cooling by single degrees and complaining of it. That ticking is everywhere and nowhere. Stand still for a minute and you will hear a hundred of them, none of them close.

Nothing lives here. Nothing has had the chance. There is no soil because there has been no time to make it; there is no water because the warmth drives off anything the thin air might have carried; there are no seeds because nothing brought them. The one exception — and it is barely an exception — is a bloom you find on the older slabs, the ones that have held still for a few weeks. A crust, pale grey against the dark stone, thin as breath on glass. Mineral, probably. Salt drawn out of the rock and left behind. But it grows in rings, and the rings grow outward, and where two rings meet they do not overlap but stop and form a seam, the way territories do. Whether that is chemistry or the very first, least ambitious thing that could be called a life, no one is here to say. When the slab beneath it tilts or splits, the crust goes with it, and the new face is bare again.

And yet someone has been here.

You come upon the first sign without warning: a stack of stones, seven of them, each chosen and set, the flattest at the bottom. A cairn. The kind of marker a person builds to say *this way* or *I passed*. It leans, badly, because the slab beneath it has tilted since it was built, and one of the seven has fallen and rolled and lies a few paces off. There is no path leading to it and none leading from it. Whatever it once pointed to has moved.

Then, much later — you cannot say how much later, the dark has no hours in it — a low wall. Two courses of stacked stone, fitted dry, running for the length of a room and then stopping where the ground has dropped away beneath it, the wall's end hanging in air over a fresh fissure that breathes its warm flint-smelling breath up over the stones. Behind the wall, a rectangle of ground very slightly smoother than the rest, as though someone had cleared it, or slept on it. A hollow the size of a body. Nothing else. No tool, no vessel, no burnt place. Whoever made these things made them with their hands and left with nothing.

The cairns recur. You find perhaps one an hour, if you are moving. Some are toppled entirely, their stones scattered across a slab that has since bucked and cracked so that you can no longer tell which stones were the cairn and which are only stones. Some are freshly built — you think — the stacking still neat, the balance still true, though the ground under them is already beginning its slow tilt. Some seem to have been rebuilt on the ruins of an older one, the new stack incorporating the old fallen stones. Whoever is doing this is not far ahead of you, or is not far behind. They are marking a route across a country that will not hold a route. Each cairn is a small, patient argument against the ground, and the ground is winning every one of them, and still the cairns keep appearing.

You begin to suspect the purpose is not to find a way across. There may be no across. The uplift extends past every ember-seam you can see, and the seams, when you look at them all together, do not describe an edge — they describe an interior. The purpose may be simply this: to put something still into a place that will not be still, and to see how long it lasts, and to put another.

The warmth comes up through your feet. Somewhere to your left a fissure sighs. Somewhere behind you the ticking rises for a moment — a slab, deciding something — and then settles back. In the ember-light of the nearest seam you can see the pale rings on the stone, and the stacked stones leaning, and beyond them nothing, and you understand that in the morning, if there is a morning under this sky, none of it will be where it is now, and all of it will still be here.
