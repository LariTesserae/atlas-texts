The way in is not a door. It is a place where the packing thins for a moment—where two of the great pale ribs lean apart by the width of a shoulder—and if you turn sideways and let your arms go slack you can pass between them, and then the packing closes behind you and you are inside.

Everything here is made. There is no ground under the made things; the floor is made, a paste that set long ago and has since been walked on, or leaned on, or simply pressed by its own weight until it took a slight dish shape, like a very old step. The walls are made. The ribs are made. The struts that cross between the ribs at every height, the shelves, the cells, the frills that hang from the underside of the cells, the pillars that hold up nothing in particular—all of it is the same substance, and the substance is a dry white mineral paper. It has the give of chalk and the grain of very fine cardboard. Where it has weathered it has not cracked but softened; every edge in the room has been rounded by a long, patient, uneventful time, so that nothing is sharp and nothing is new. Put your palm against a wall and it takes a faint impression, then holds it. The room is full of these palm-marks, at every height, made by no one you will meet.

It is a room, roughly. A pocket. If you stand in the center and stretch your arms out you will touch made things on both sides. If you look up, the made things go on above you, tier on tier, until they blur into a general brightness. The room is not large, but it is so full that it feels larger than it is, in the way a cupboard packed to the door feels larger than an empty hall. Every place your eye lands, there is something: a rib, a cell-mouth, a pale ridge, a shelf holding a smaller shelf. There is no empty air. Even the gaps you move through are not empty; they are simply the places where the made things happen to have left room for a body.

It is bright. That is the first thing, or the first thing after the sound. The brightness has no direction. It does not come from above more than from below. It comes through the walls—the mineral paper is thin enough, in most places, that light passes it, and so every surface glows faintly from inside, and the light bounces from glowing wall to glowing rib to glowing cell until the whole packed chamber is filled with an even white shine and there are no shadows anywhere. You can see under the shelves as well as on top of them. You can see into the cells. There is nothing in the cells. The light is cool. It has no warmth in it at all, and the room holds a steady chill, the chill of a pantry or a limestone cellar, and your breath does not show because there is no moisture in you that the air wants.

And the sound.

The sound was there before you came in and it will be there after and it does not change. It is a chord, or a single note so thick it sounds like a chord. It is loud enough that you feel it in the roof of your mouth. It seems to come from the walls the same way the light does—from everywhere, evenly—and perhaps it does: the whole enormous mass, of which this room is one hidden pocket among uncountable others, may be a resonator, and somewhere far above or far outside something moves against it without ever pausing, and the note is the note the mass makes in reply. Or the note is simply the sound the made material makes, being what it is. There is no way to test this. There is no lull in which to listen for the source. The sound has no beginning that you could stand at the edge of.

After a while it stops registering as loud and starts registering as the shape of the room. You would notice it only if it stopped, and it will not.

There is life here, a little. In a few places the mineral paper has been split from inside by a stalk—pale, bloodless, the color of the wall it grew out of, so that at first you mistake it for another rib and only understand it is a plant when you see the way it tapers, and the way its leaves, which are few and thick and folded like the pages of an unopened book, tilt toward the brightest part of the wall. There are perhaps six of these in the room. None of them touches another. Their roots go into the made substance, not through it; the wall is their soil. Whether they eat it, or the light, or the sound, is not clear. They do not move. There is no draft to move them. Press a leaf and it is cool and slightly damp, and that dampness—a film on the inside of a folded leaf—is the only water in the room, and the plant does not seem inclined to share it.

Nothing else lives here. Nothing has crawled across the floor; the dish of the floor holds a fine dust that lies exactly as it fell and shows no tracks. Nothing has nested in the cells. Nothing has chewed the frills. If something made this—if the paper was spat or secreted or laid down by a body—that body is gone, and has been gone for long enough that the marks of its making have been worn smooth with everything else. What remains is the made thing, holding its own shape, glowing and ringing and cool, packed to the brim with itself.

The rules are simple and they are absolute. It does not get darker. It does not get warmer or cooler. The note does not rise or fall. The plants, if they grow, grow at a rate that cannot be observed. The dust does not settle further because it has already settled. There is no wind to bring anything in or carry anything out. Whatever balance was struck here was struck long ago, and the room has since done nothing but be in balance. You could stand in the center with your arms out, touching a rib with each hand, for as long as you liked, and the only thing that would change would be you.

When you leave—sideways, through the same slack gap between the same two leaning ribs—the brightness stays behind you and the note stays behind you, and for some time afterward your ears will keep making it, quietly, the way a room keeps the shape of a chord after the hand has lifted from the keys.
