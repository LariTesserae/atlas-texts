Start below the bluff, where the ground is barely higher than the river it drains toward. This is flat, warm country, a limestone lowland under a thin skin of red soil, and the forest on it is not tall but it is thick — wild fig, strangler, a canopy that stays close to the ground as if it knows there is no reason to reach. The heat sits in it all day. The air is wet enough to feel but not wet enough to be rain, most of the time.

Somewhere in the lowest fold of that ground, a spring comes out.

It does not come out with any force. It seeps from a crack at the foot of a low grey face, no higher than a house, and the water is so saturated with dissolved stone that it cannot hold all of it. Wherever the water slows, wherever it touches air, wherever it runs over a leaf or a strand of moss, it lets go of a little of what it carries. It has been doing this long enough to have built something — but not long. This is a young place. It is still being laid down.

What it has built is a lip. The spring's overflow, sliding down the face, has thickened the face with its own deposit, and the deposit has grown forward faster at the top than at the bottom, the way a wave curls, so that now the water leaves the rock a little above head height and falls free for the last stretch of its drop. Not a waterfall. A veil, a few fingers thick, sliding more than falling, breaking into strings and rejoining. Behind it, where the stone has curled forward and left the space beneath untouched, there is a room.

The room was not carved. Nothing here has been carved. It is the shape the stone has not yet grown into — a pocket of air that the rock is slowly closing around, its ceiling extending outward by less than a fingernail's width in a year. If you stood in it long enough you would be enclosed. You would not live that long. Nothing does, except the stone.

Inside, it is dim in a specific way. Light comes through the water and through the fig leaves that hang over the lip above the water, so it arrives already green, and it moves. The whole back wall trembles faintly with the shadows of falling strings. The floor is uneven, a series of shallow rims and basins that the seepage has built — each basin a little terrace, each rim a low wall of new lime, some of them still soft enough to mark with a thumb. Water stands in them, warm, absolutely clear. Between the basins the stone is dry, or nearly, and pale. The room is perhaps ten paces across, and most of it is empty. That is the first thing you notice after the light. How little is in it. A few things, spaced far apart, each one very distinct.

Against the back wall, where the seepage comes through, the moss grows. It grows toward the light in a green fringe, and behind the fringe, where the older growth is, it is grey and hard, because the water has stoned it in place. The moss is alive at its tips and mineral at its roots, and there is no clear line where one becomes the other. Leaves that fell from the figs and landed in the wrong place are already half-cased, their veins picked out in white crust, lying where they landed. A twig. A snail shell, empty, being slowly cemented into the floor of a basin by the same water that fed the snail.

A fig root has come down through a crack in the new ceiling. It is thick as a wrist, pale, and it goes straight down into one of the basins without touching the floor around it. Where it enters the water it is beginning to whiten. The tree above is feeding on the spring and the spring is feeding on the tree; in a few decades the root will be a column.

And there is the city.

Under the deepest part of the overhang, where the ceiling is lowest and the light is least, swallows have built. Dozens of nests, more — it is hard to count in that light — each one a closed gourd of mud pellets with a short neck, pressed against the stone and against each other, so that they run together into a single rough mass with many mouths. The mud is the red soil from above, carried down pellet by pellet in the beaks of birds that never saw the value of a tool and never needed one. The new nests are red-brown and rough. The older ones, higher and further back, have been catching the mist off the veil for years, and they are going white. The lime has found them. Their mouths are narrowing. The oldest, at the very back, are sealed entirely, smooth pale bulbs fused to the wall, no longer distinguishable from the stone they are becoming. The colony builds forward and downward, and the rock follows behind it, closing the abandoned rooms as it goes. It is a city in which the old quarter is being turned to stone and the new quarter is being built of mud, at about the same speed.

The birds are quiet in the heat. Now and then one drops out of a nest mouth, cuts once through the veil — a soft tear in the water, healed instantly — and is gone into the bright outside. Now and then one comes back in the same way, a wet flick, and vanishes into a hole. There is no song. There is hardly a call. In the middle of the day the whole colony seems asleep with its eyes open.

Other things use the city. A wasp works a rim of new mud onto one of the lower nests, or onto something of its own, a small pot fastened to the underside of the mass — it is hard to tell, in the gloom, where the swallows' construction ends and the wasps' begins. A gecko lies flat on the pale stone above the nests, patient, the colour of the stone. Where the fig root enters the water, a freshwater crab has its place, and it comes out sideways to take something off the surface of the basin and goes back. Frogs sit at the seep line in the moss, so still and so green that they register only when they move, which is rarely. Snails work the wet stone, leaving trails that will still be there when they are gone, filled in white. In the veil itself, if you look for a while, small fish hang in the pool where it lands, just outside the room, in the last of the light.

Sometimes a snake comes. It comes over the lip from above, following the fig root, or it comes along the wet edge of the floor from the outside, and it goes up the wall toward the nests without hurry. When it reaches them, nothing calls out. The swallows leave, or they do not. The snake puts its head into a mouth and stays there. Then it withdraws and goes to the next. The colony does not defend itself; it has never done so; it builds more nests than the snake can empty and it lets the stone close the ones that are lost. Afterwards, the snake lies along the base of the wall in the warm dim for a long time, and the birds come back, and the wasp goes on working, and the water goes on sliding down.

In the afternoon the rain comes. You hear it before anything — a change in the pressure of the air, then a hiss in the canopy above, then a thickening in the veil as the run-off finds the lip and the strings of water join into a sheet. For an hour the room is a little louder, and darker, and the basins fill and overflow into each other and the rims take on a new skin. The mist reaches further back into the overhang than usual and settles on the white nests and the moss and the fig root, and everything that is turning to stone turns a little more. Then it stops. The sheet thins back to strings. Somewhere outside a heron walks at the edge of the pool and is not heard.

That is the rhythm of it. Rain and seep, mud and lime, the birds building out, the stone building over. The gecko changes position. The crab comes and goes. Water falls in silence, which should not be possible, but the room is small and soft-walled and the water does not fall so much as arrive, and the sound of it arriving is closer to breathing than to any noise. You would have to stand very still to hear the drip from the ceiling into the nearest basin. You would have to stand very still for a long time to notice that the ceiling is not where it was.
