The plateau is the color of the dark, which is to say it has no color at all until you have been in it long enough for the eye to give up and the ear to take over. What the eye does eventually make out, somewhere around the third hour, is that there is no ground.

There is a surface, certainly. You are standing on it. It gives slightly under the boot, dry on top and cool beneath, and it is not soil. It is what the termites have made of soil — carried up grain by grain from bedrock that has not seen air for longer than the mounds have existed, mixed with saliva and dung and pressed into walls, and then the walls collapsed and were rebuilt, and collapsed again, and the rubble of the old was folded into the new, until the whole high basin from ridge to ridge, forty kilometers of it, is nothing but mound. Mound standing, mound fallen, mound half-swallowed by the next mound. Nowhere in it, if you dug, would you reach anything that had not passed through a mandible.

The mounds themselves are two, three, four meters tall, and they lean toward one another like people in a crowd trying to hear. Between them the gaps are seldom wider than an arm. They are not the sun-baked cathedrals of the lowlands. Up here, at this altitude, with the cloud coming through most nights and the rain arriving in short warm handfuls a few times a week, everything they built has been colonized. The mound walls carry a fur of moss so thick that a hand sinks in to the wrist. Filmy ferns hang from the overhangs. Small orchids the size of a thumbnail have found the vents and grow in them, their roots gripping termite plaster. Bromeliads have lodged in the old chimney throats and filled with rainwater, and each of these is a pond, and each pond has frogs in it.

That is what the sound is. Mostly.

It arrived before anything else. Long before you could see the mounds you could hear the plateau — a wall of noise that seemed at first like weather, like a river in flood, and then, closer, resolved into something worse than weather because weather does not have parts. This has parts. There is a low continuous drone that is probably the katydids and crickets, tens of millions of them in the moss, a sound so even it stops being a sound and becomes a pressure. Over that, the frogs: dozens of species, each with a pitch it has fought for over a hundred thousand years so as not to be drowned by the others, so that the chorus is not a chord but a filled spectrum, every frequency from a bass thump to a glass-tap click occupied by some small wet throat. And over that, cutting through it because they must, the night birds — nightjars with their churring, the boom of something owl-sized that lives in the fallen mounds, and from the higher terraces a colony of swifts that never seems to sleep, whose screaming comes in waves as birds leave and return through the dark. Bats too, which you cannot hear, except that sometimes the frog chorus dips in a wave passing across the plain, a Mexican wave of silence a hundred meters wide, and you understand that something has just flown over, and that everything below it knows.

It is not possible to speak here. You could try. You would see the other person's mouth move.

The animals are not hiding. That is the second thing the eye eventually learns, once it has finished learning that there is no ground: everything is in reach. Put a hand out into the dark and there is a frog on the moss, cold and unbothered. Another on the fern. A gecko on the mound wall above it, pale, eating a moth. The moths themselves, thousands, pouring through the gaps in a slow current, some large as a hand, all of them silent. Snails the size of apples moving up the wet faces of the mounds, so many of them that you can hear — if you press your ear to the moss and somehow filter out the rest — a faint scraping, which is their rasping the algae off the plaster. Millipedes. Whip-scorpions in the vents. The termites themselves, invisible, working under it all, the actual builders, whose presence is only detectable as a faint warmth radiating from the wall of the mound when you lay your palm flat: the heat of a few million bodies breathing.

And birds nesting. That is where the smell comes from — an ammoniac tang under the vegetable dampness. The higher, older mounds, the ones that reach four and five meters, are riddled with cavities, and the cavities have been taken by parrots and by small owls and by the swifts, and their droppings have run down the mound faces for centuries and fed the moss, which is why the moss is thickest on the oldest towers. In places between the mounds the guano has built up into its own soft grey terrace, and things grow in that too. Made upon made upon made.

Nothing here happened quickly. The mounds are thought to be the oldest continually inhabited structures on the continent — the deep ones have been dated at close to four thousand years, and the rate at which they grow is a few centimeters a century, so that the whole basin has the settled, finished quality of an old city that has stopped expanding and simply persists. Mounds do fall. When they fall it is over decades: a crack, a lean, a slump, moss knitting the ruin to its neighbor, new orchids in the exposed vents, new frogs in the new pools formed where the collapse cupped the rain. The colony inside simply moves. In the time it takes a mound to fail and be rebuilt, the swifts that nest on it will have gone through forty generations and remembered nothing.

The water is never enough to be called wet. It is condensation, mostly. The cloud comes up over the western rim around midnight and moves through the mounds at walking pace, and everything beads, and the frog chorus, if that were possible, gets louder. Twice, three times in a night, a brief rain falls — warm, heavy drops, over in ten minutes — and runs down the mound faces into the moss and is gone. In the bromeliad ponds and the catchments of the fallen towers, water stands. Everywhere else it is damp to the touch and dry beneath, and the termites keep the interiors of the mounds at whatever humidity they have decided upon, and the frogs live on the surfaces of things.

Light comes from nothing here. There is no moon behind the cloud. There is the phosphorescence of certain fungi on the north faces of some mounds, faint enough that you doubt it, and the eye-shine of the frogs when there is nothing to shine. Dawn, when it comes, comes late and grey and from below, the cloud lightening rather than the sky, and in that light the plateau is finally visible for what it is — thousands upon thousands of moss-cushioned towers, close as teeth, running unbroken to the ridges, green and grey and rust where the plaster shows through — and the chorus does not stop for the day. It only shifts. The frogs hand off to the cicadas. The swifts leave in a screaming mass and the parrots begin.

You can walk it. That is the strangest thing. The gaps are narrow but they connect, and if you keep moving, brushing moss from both shoulders, stepping over snails, it is possible to cross the whole basin in a day and a night and arrive at the far rim where the true rock resumes and the mounds stop as if at a wall. Standing there on stone, in the relative quiet of ordinary forest, the noise behind you is still loud enough to feel in the chest. You will be a long time getting used to silence. And for a while afterward the ground under your feet — real ground, mineral, unbuilt — will seem the improbable thing.
