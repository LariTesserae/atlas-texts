**The Settling Grounds**

At first it reads as night. That is the mistake everyone makes, arriving: they wait for their eyes to adjust. They don't. There is nothing for them to adjust to. This is not a dark that thins toward the horizon or grays at the edges of things. It is dark the way stone is heavy — a property rather than a condition. You can hold your hand a finger's width from your face and there is no difference between having it there and not.

So the place has to be built another way, and it builds itself through the soles of your feet first.

The ground is new. That is the second thing you learn, after the dark. Not soft-new like silt but sharp-new, the way a casting is sharp when it comes out of the mold. Underfoot it is mostly a granular crust, warm through the shoe, and where it has cracked the edges of the cracks are keen enough to open skin. Nothing has rounded them. Nothing has had time. If you kneel and put your palm flat to it, you feel a slight give, not the give of mud but the give of something that has not entirely finished becoming solid — press for a minute and your hand sinks a little, press for five and it sinks a little more, patiently, as though the ground is thinking it over. Lift your hand away and the impression stays. Come back in an hour and it is still there, slightly shallower. The ground is closing it at a pace you'd have to measure with instruments.

It is warm. The whole basin is warm, blood-warm, and the warmth comes from below and from certain fixed points that you learn to locate by walking toward the heat on your face. The air is saturated. It has no way to cool and dry, so it condenses on anything a degree colder than itself — your skin, your hair, the iron of the structures, the backs of the animals. Everything is beaded. If you stand still long enough you drip.

---

Water is everywhere and nowhere in particular. There are channels — you find them by the change in the sound of your own step, a hollow before the edge — and the channels carry warm water slowly in a direction that is, as far as anyone has established, downhill, though *down* here is less reliable than it should be. The channels have raw sides, cut cleanly through the young crust, with none of the undercutting and slumping of an old stream. They are shallow. Most are thigh-deep at the center. The water has a mineral taste and a faint sweetness, like the water that runs off a hot machine.

Between the channels the ground weeps. Water comes up through the crust in sheets and beads, the way sweat comes through skin, and gathers in the low places into standing pools that are exactly the temperature of the air and therefore make no sound when you enter them, no shock, only a sensation of the boundary of your body becoming approximate. In some pools you can feel a slight upward current. Something below is pushing water out.

The whole basin is, as best anyone can tell from walking it, the size of a modest valley. Hours across. You know this because the fixed heat sources are far enough apart that you can lose one entirely before you find the next, and because the character of the crust changes — finer here, coarser there, ridged in places like a poured floor that was raked — and because the water moves in a consistent direction for a long, long time before it gathers into anything you'd call a pond. There is a rim. The warmth thins toward it. Nobody has reached the rim in the dark and come back to say what it is.

---

The machines are the fixed points.

There is a pumphouse — you know it is a pumphouse by the shape of the casing when you find it with your hands, by the flywheel you can feel turning slowly behind a grille, by the pipe as thick as your thigh that leaves it and runs off into the dark on low trestles you can follow by touch for what feels like a mile before it goes into the ground. The pumphouse is hot. Its walls are corrugated iron and it hums, and the hum has a period to it, a slow rise and fall over the course of what might be a minute, like breathing.

Inside, if you find the door, there is a bulb. You know it is a bulb because you burn your fingers on it. It is on. It is burning. You can feel the heat of it on your face from a yard away, that particular dry heat of an incandescent filament, and you can hold your hand under it and feel your palm warming and see nothing at all. Light is being made here. It is simply not going anywhere. It comes out of the filament and stops, or is used up in the making, or arrives at your eye as heat and nothing else. Every structure in the basin is lit this way. You can find them by the warmth of their windows.

There is a transformer standing alone in a stretch of open weeping ground, on a concrete plinth so freshly cast the aggregate hasn't weathered out of the surface. It hums at a different pitch than the pumphouse. The air around it prickles, and if you bring your hand near the casing the hair on your arm lifts, and the water beading on the insulators crackles faintly as it drips. It is connected to nothing you can find. The cables leave it and go into the crust and the crust has closed over them.

There is a rail spur. Two rails on ties, the ballast sharp and unsettled, running straight for a great distance and then simply ending, descending gently into one of the wider channels and continuing under the water — you can follow them with your feet — until they are too deep to follow.

There is a shed with a diesel engine running inside it. It has been running for as long as anyone has been coming here. Its exhaust warms a patch of ground behind it where the crust is softer and the frogs gather thickest. No one has found where the fuel comes from.

None of the structures are near each other. Each is alone in its stretch of dark with its own hum and its own heat and its own useless lit bulbs, and between them is the ground and the water and the animals.

---

The animals are the density of the place.

They are not loud. That is the strange thing, given how many of them there are. You'd expect a marsh at night to be a wall of sound, and this is not that. There is the occasional frog, a single low note that is not answered. There is a splash. There is a soft tick from the trestles where the pipe is cooling or warming. Mostly there is the drip and the hum and your own breathing, and underneath all of it the sense — you can't call it sound — of a great many small bodies moving carefully around you.

You are never not touching something. That's the plainest way to put it. Put your hand down anywhere and it lands on a shell, a back, a length of something cool and muscular that slides away without hurry. The crust is a carpet of snails, each one beaded with condensation, and they part around your feet and close behind them. The channels are thick with eels. You feel them against your calves like a slow current made of muscle. In the standing pools there are things the size of a hand that walk rather than swim, many-legged, and they climb your boots and sit there. The frogs are everywhere near the heat sources, so dense on the warm ground behind the diesel shed that you cannot step without moving them, and they do not jump away, they *shift*, all together, a slow rearrangement, and settle again against your ankles.

There are moths. This is the thing that gives people trouble, that there should be moths with nothing to see by. They gather on the transformer casing and the pumphouse walls and the hot bulbs — you feel them on the glass when you reach to touch it, a soft crust of wings — and they gather on you, on your face and the backs of your hands, wherever you are warmest, and they cling there. They are seeking heat. Whatever the light was for, they've found the thing it made instead.

You'll feel things you can't name. Something with fur, low and long, that presses along your leg the way a cat does and is gone. Something with a hard edge that could be a shell or a tool. Something you'd swear was an eel until it took hold of your finger with a mouth that was, briefly, a beak. The animals here have not entirely committed to being what they are. Or you have not entirely committed to knowing. In the dark, the difference is hard to establish.

---

What grows here grows toward the hum.

There isn't much. Reeds along some of the channel edges, stiff and hollow, taller than a person, that you find by walking into them. They lean. When you follow the lean it points you at the nearest machine. The stalks are cool and slick and when you break one — they break cleanly, young growth — the inside is wet and smells like nothing, like water. There are mats of something on the warm crust, fibrous, slightly rubbery, that you can pull up in sheets. There are stalks in the pools that are neither plant nor animal to the touch, that flinch.

Nothing here has been colored, is the sense you get handling it. Not bleached — never colored in the first place. You are touching things that were made to be felt.

---

The rules, such as they are, come slowly, over many visits.

The lit bulbs are the first. Light is here and does not travel. Whatever governs its passage has been suspended locally, or exhausted, and what remains is the heat and the hum of its manufacture. You can find a lamp by warmth. You cannot see by it. This seems to be the basic condition from which the rest follows.

Distance holds, mostly, but not always. The pipe from the pumphouse takes about a mile to follow to where it enters the ground. Some visits it takes noticeably less. The rails are straight — you can check this by sighting along them with your hands — and still, following them, people have arrived back at the pumphouse from the wrong side.

Counting fails. You put your hand on a rail and it is one rail. You slide your hand along it and for a stretch it is two rails, side by side, then one again. The snails under your palm are seven and then, without any of them having moved, nine. Nothing you set down is where you set it, exactly. It is near there. It is approximately there. It is where it would be if something had thought about where it should be and then not quite decided.

Sound does what light does, sometimes. You hear your step land a half-second before your foot comes down. You hear a splash from the channel and then feel the water hit your legs. The hum of the pumphouse occasionally stops entirely, for a period that could be a minute or an hour, and in the silence you can feel the flywheel still turning under your hand. The sound is being made. It is simply not going anywhere.

The warmth is reliable. That is the one thing. Heat travels. Heat is the last thing that still crosses the space between one body and another honestly, and everything in the basin has organized itself around that — the moths, the frogs, the reeds, the people who come here, all of them orienting by what is warm, moving toward what is warm, touching.

---

Change is happening. It is happening at the pace of geology.

The ground is rising. Old measurements — a nail driven into a trestle post at ankle height — are found, years later, at the sole of the boot. The crust is thickening from below, pushed up or laid down by whatever is pushing up the water. The channels have moved. Not far. A pace or two. Cutting new raw sides through the young ground and leaving the old channels as shallow troughs that fill with standing water and snails. The machines do not move. The ground rises around them and they sit lower in it each year. The concrete plinth of the transformer, which used to come to your knee, comes now to mid-calf. In a hundred years the pumphouse will be a warm patch on level ground with a hum underneath, and after that a hum, and after that a warmth.

The water level cycles on a period of days. Not tides. Something slower and less regular, a filling and a draining, and at the low end of the cycle the channels drop to ankle-deep and the eels crowd into the deep places and you can walk on the channel beds and feel the sharp new ridges the water has cut. At the high end the standing pools join and the basin is a single warm shallow sheet with the crust just under the surface and the frogs sitting on it, on the skin of the water, thousands of them, silent, and the machines humming out of the dark at their separate distances, and the whole valley beaded and dripping and lit, by every account, to no purpose.

It is quiet. You would think, standing in it, that it was empty. And then you move your hand and everything moves with it.
