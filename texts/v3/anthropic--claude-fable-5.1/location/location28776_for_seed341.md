The levee is what you cross first, and it is the only thing here that tries to keep anything out. A ring of packed earth faced with concrete slabs, shoulder-high, its top worn into a footpath by nothing you can identify. Inside the ring the ground drops a meter and a half and does not rise again. You are standing at the rim of a bowl a few hundred paces across, and the bowl is full.

Not full of water, exactly. Full of a condition. The air inside the levee is thicker than the air outside, warmer by a degree you can feel on the back of your hands, and visibly grey with moisture that has not decided whether to be mist or rain. It falls in patches. Twenty meters to your left a curtain of drops is coming straight down out of a low cloud that sits on the compound like a lid; where you stand it is merely damp; to your right the concrete of the walkway shows a dry patch the size of a table, steaming. The cloud is not the sky. Above it, through it, there is a brighter smear that must be daylight, but the light that reaches the ground has been through so much water that it arrives without direction. Nothing here casts a shadow. Everything is lit the way the underside of a bridge is lit.

The sound arrives before you understand it. It is enormous and it is constant, and your first instinct is to look for the waterfall. There is no waterfall. There are pumps.

Six of them, seven — you will not be sure of the count until you have walked the full ring, because the mist closes behind you and the buildings do not stand in a pattern you can read from any single point. Each is a house: concrete, two storeys, a flat roof, tall windows with the glass long gone and the steel mullions still in place, rust flowering along every weld. They are far apart. That is the first thing that feels wrong about a place with this much machinery in it. A hundred meters of open water and reed between one pump-house and the next, and the raised walkways that connect them run straight and thin across the emptiness on concrete piers, a single handrail on one side, the other side open to the drop. The compound has the density of a village that has lost most of itself, except nothing has been lost. The gaps are the design. Each house needed its own intake, its own reach of water to draw from, and so they were set out like wells in a field, and the field has grown up between them.

Grown up is not strong enough. The bowl is planted the way a lung is planted. Reeds taller than a person, their stems as thick as a thumb, stand in ranks so close you could not push a hand between them, and then stop, sharply, at a line where the water deepens, and from there out the surface is skinned with a floating leaf so dense and dark it reads as ground until a drop from the cloud lands on it and it rocks. Trees have come up through the walkways where the concrete has cracked — not many, a few, one every fifty paces perhaps — and they are the wrong shape for trees, drawn upward into the mist, pale-barked, their crowns lost in the cloud so that they seem to hold the lid up. Vines have gone into the pump-houses through the empty windows and come out through the roofs. The whole surface of the place, every rail, every pier, every horizontal edge, wears a fur of moss so saturated that when you put your palm to it water runs out over your wrist, hot as blood.

Nothing eats any of it. You notice this slowly, and then all at once. There is no browse-line on the reeds, no leaf chewed to lace, no track in the mud at the base of the piers, no ripple on the dark water that is not a raindrop. The sound that you took at the edge of hearing for frogs is a relief valve on the third house, venting on a cycle of about eleven seconds. The sound that you took for a bird is a bearing. You walk the walkways for an hour and the only animals you find are the cross-sections of shells in the aggregate of the concrete itself, tiny spirals in the grey, quarried from some limestone that had its own animals once, a very long way from here.

The pumps run. That is the second wrong thing, or perhaps the first, depending on what you expected. Inside each house — and you can go inside, the doors are steel and swung open on hinges that have been greased, recently, the grease still amber and clean on the pin — a single vertical shaft rises through the floor from the intake below and turns. The motor sits on a plinth at the top. It is old in the way that makes a thing permanent rather than fragile: cast housing, a nameplate whose letters have been worn away by hands, cables as thick as your forearm running out through the wall in conduit that has become a trellis. There is electricity. You can hear it in the motor, under the mechanical roar, a hum with a pitch. You will not find where it comes from. The cables go out into the reeds and under the water and do not come back.

Each shaft drives an impeller you cannot see, and each impeller lifts water — you can hear it lifting, the deep slapping rush of it inside the casing — up a riser pipe that leaves the house through the roof. Outside, the risers are the tallest things in the compound. They go up into the cloud and stop being visible. Follow one with your eyes and you lose it at about the height of the lost tree-crowns, and above that there is only the grey.

And this is the shape of the place, once you have stood under enough risers and been rained on enough: the water goes up, and the water comes down. The pumps lift the bowl into the air. The air, hot and already carrying everything it can hold, cannot keep it. The cloud sits on the compound because the compound is making the cloud, and the rain falls in patches because the risers deliver in pulses, and the pulses are not synchronized, so that the weather in this hamlet-sized world is a beat pattern of seven engines running slightly out of phase. When two pulses coincide the rain over that part of the bowl becomes a downpour for half a minute and the reeds bow under it and the roar doubles. When they interfere the mist thins and you get one of those dry patches, steaming, and for a moment you can see almost to the far levee and count the houses and get it wrong.

Where the water came from originally, and where it was meant to go, is not recoverable. There are outfall pipes on the outer face of the levee, big ones, their mouths dry and full of roots. There are sluice gates at the base of the ring, rusted into their frames at half-open, the water on both sides of them standing at the same level. Whatever this was built to drain or to feed has come into balance with it. The compound draws from itself and returns to itself and the levee holds the loop, and the only thing that leaves is heat and sound.

The heat is real. You have been walking for an hour and your clothes have not so much gotten wet as changed state. The concrete underfoot is warm through your soles, warm from the water that runs over it, warm from the machines beneath it whose casings you can feel, when you rest a hand on a pump-house wall, transmitting a low steady temperature into the structure like a body. This is not a place that has weather. It is a place that is weather, at a small enough scale to walk around.

There are rules, and you learn them by breaking none of them, because they are the kind that enforce themselves. You do not leave the walkways, because the water is deeper than it looks and the floating leaf will take your weight for exactly one step. You keep to the inside of the rail, because the outside rail has gone into moss and the moss has gone into the rail and neither would hold you. You go into the houses if you want, and you can put your hands on anything except the shaft, and nothing will happen, and nothing will ask you why you are there. You listen for the pulses, and after a while you can predict the rain by the pitch of the nearest riser, a rising note as the column climbs, a drop in the motor's hum as the load falls off, and then, a few seconds later, the drumming.

Someone greased the hinge. Someone chalked a number on the plinth in the fourth house — a reading, a date, a pressure, you cannot say — and the chalk is not yet run. There is a rain jacket hung on a nail by the door of the sixth, and it is dry on the inside. You do not see anyone. You do not, if you are honest, expect to, and the expectation has nothing to do with hostility or abandonment. It is more that the place reads as fully occupied already, by the process, and a person would be a redundancy. Whoever tends the machines tends them the way you would tend a river, from the bank, without any illusion about who is in charge.

Toward what would be evening, if the light had a direction to lose, the cloud lowers. You feel it more than see it: the risers vanish sooner, the far houses go, the roar takes on the closed quality of sound in a room. The rain becomes general. The dry patches stop appearing. And the reeds, which have been standing all day in a stillness you assumed was their nature, begin to move — not in wind, there is no wind inside the levee, but in a slow rolling shiver that runs across the bowl from house to house and back, and you understand, standing on the walkway with the water coming off your hair, that it is the pulses, that the pumps are drawing hard enough now to move the whole body of standing water through the root-mat, that the plants are being breathed.

You climb the levee to leave and the sound falls away with each step like a thing being lowered on a rope, and at the top the air is merely hot and merely damp and the sky has an edge to it, a place where the light comes from. Behind you the cloud sits in its bowl, and from outside it is just a low grey dome the size of a village, faintly luminous, rumbling, dropping rain on itself with the regularity of an engine that has been running for a very long time and has found, somewhere in all those uncounted cycles, the exact speed at which it can run forever.
