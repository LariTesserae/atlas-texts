You come to it from above, which is the only way anyone comes to it: a shaft in the limestone, dry at the top, then damp, then streaming. The walls of the shaft have been cut square by someone and then uncut again by water, so the corners are gone and the ledges are gone and what remains is a slot that has been convinced to be round. Somewhere in the descent you stop hearing yourself. The sound below comes up the shaft like heat from an oven door.

Then the shaft opens and you are standing on a shelf of rock at the top of the room.

It is a room the size of a nave, roughly, or a large barn — a boundary you could walk around in a quarter hour if there were anywhere to walk. The roof is a dome of grey stone slick with condensation. Water runs down it in sheets, gathers along seams, and falls. The floor is not a floor. The floor is water, and the water is in a state of violence.

The light is the first thing that is wrong. There is no aperture in the roof, no lamp, no fire. The light comes from the water. Not from within it, the way a lit pool looks from above, but from its motion — every place where it breaks, or folds over itself, or strikes rock, gives off a white glare that fades as the water calms, which it never does. So the whole basin is a shifting field of brightness, and the brightness climbs the walls as spray and hangs in the air as fog, and the fog is lit from inside, and the roof is lit from below by a light that will not hold still. There are no fixed shadows here. Your own shadow swings and doubles and disappears. The mist beads on your arms and the beads themselves shine faintly, like something you have caught rather than something that has fallen on you.

The water is warm — bath-warm, breath-warm. The air is thick with it. You breathe in and it tastes faintly of iron and of the particular sweetness of stone that has been wet for a very long time.

And it roars. Not the steady white roar of a waterfall but something with articulation in it: a low continuous boom that is the basin itself, and over that the slap of columns of water hitting the walls, the hiss of sheets sliding back, and now and then a sound like a struck bell when a surge finds one of the iron posts. You feel it in the shelf under your feet. You feel it in your teeth. Speech is not possible. If someone stood beside you, you would communicate by pointing.

---

Stand a while and the chaos begins to have a shape.

The water throws itself against the far wall and climbs it, a green-white sheet three or four times your height, and hangs, and comes down. Then it does this again. Not identically — it is turbulence, it is never the same twice — but it does it in the same place, and the wall there is worn into a broad shallow trough, polished, exactly where the water climbs. Further along, the surface bucks and a column stands up out of it, thick as a tree, and holds for a moment and topples toward the left, always toward the left, and where it falls there is a pit in the bedrock you can see when the water briefly clears it. Near your shelf the water turns in a wide slow gyre that is the calmest part of the basin, though it would still take you under and keep you, and the rock at the gyre's edge has been worn into a smooth lip, curved to fit the eddy the way a riverbed is curved to fit the river.

This is what makes the place feel old rather than merely wild. The storm has been going on for so long that the rock has learned it. Every violent motion in the basin has its groove, its polished patch, its undercut. The chaos is complete and the chaos is settled. It has been in this state for longer than anything has been in any state nearby, and it has eroded the chamber to its own liking, and now the chamber and the storm fit each other like a hand and a glove that has been worn for a lifetime.

You wonder what would happen if it stopped. You suspect the rock would not know what to do with itself.

---

The built things are few and easy to miss.

A walkway runs along the right-hand wall about a body-length above the mean surge — a ledge cut into the rock and then, where the rock failed, bridged with a metal that is black and slightly matte and shows no rust, no scale, nothing at all after what must be a great length of time in warm spray. It is wet like everything is wet but the water does not seem to sit on it; it beads and runs off immediately, as though the surface would rather not be touched. Posts of the same material stand at intervals along the walkway's edge, and the water strikes them — you see it strike them — and this is the bell-sound, and the posts do not move.

The walkway goes around the room and ends at a small chamber cut into the wall, roofless now, or rather its roof has become one of the seams that drips. Inside there is a stone bench and a channel in the floor for water to run out of. Someone was meant to sit here. Someone was meant to watch.

Above the bench, incised in the wall, are marks. They are not deep — the spray has softened them — but they are regular, a grid of short strokes in rows, and they are the kind of marks a person makes when they are keeping count of something over a long time. There are a great many of them. Some of the lower rows have been erased almost completely by the water, which climbs into this cell when the surge is high, and the higher rows are sharper, and you can see the moment, halfway up the wall, where the marks stop.

Set into the floor of the cell, flush with the stone, is a disc of the black metal about the width of your two hands. It is warmer than the rock around it. It hums — you can feel it through your palm, a pitch beneath the roar — and it does nothing else that you can detect. It is the only thing in the room that looks like it might be the reason for the room.

There are rings in the rock at the waterline in several places, worn thin, some of them broken. Near the shelf where you came in there is a coil of something that might once have been rope and is now a shape of mineral. Further along the walkway there is a shoe. That is all.

---

Nothing much grows here. The light is bright but it is not the right light, or the water is the wrong water, or the constant scouring makes it impossible for anything to hold on. What does grow grows in the lee of things.

Behind the iron posts, where the spray is broken, there are cushions of a moss so pale it is nearly white, and in the wettest ones, threads of something red. On the highest shelf, just under the dome, where the water only reaches as fog, three plants have found a crack and put down roots into whatever is there: long strap-leaves, translucent, the colour of celery, streaming permanently with condensation. They lean toward the basin as though toward a sun. In the corner of the roofless cell, sheltered by the bench, there is a fern, a real green fern, small and improbable, and you think it must be very tired.

There is one animal. You see it after a long time, because it is the colour of the wet rock and it does not move quickly. It clings to the dome high up, where the sheets of water part around a knob of stone, and it is something like a salamander and something like a bird, long-bodied and blunt-headed, with feet that must grip the stone by some means you cannot see. It faces downward. Every so often, when a column of water topples and throws its glare across the roof, the animal opens its mouth and the mouth is full of the same light for a moment, and then it closes it. It is drinking the spray, or drinking whatever is in the spray that makes it shine. There is one of them. You look for a long time and there is only one, and it does not look at you, and you understand that it has never needed to.

---

Stay longer and the rules of the place begin to assert themselves, quietly, the way they do.

The water never leaves. You can see this: the basin has no outflow you can find, and though water pours into it constantly from the dome and the shaft you came down, the level does not rise. The surge climbs the far wall and does not come back down the far wall — it comes down somewhere else, or it comes down thinner than it went up, and the difference has to go somewhere. It goes into the light. That is your best guess. The water is being spent as brightness at some rate that exactly balances what falls in, and this has been the arrangement for as long as the marks on the wall were kept, and long before.

And the turbulence is not entirely honest. Watch the column that stands up and topples left. A real column of water, standing that height, would need something under it, and there is nothing under it. It stands because it is stood. The sheet that climbs the far wall hangs at the top a fraction longer than falling things hang. The gyre near your shelf turns against the direction the inflow should push it. None of this is loud about itself. It does not look like a miracle. It looks like water in a violent room — but it is water that has been told what to do, a very long time ago, by something that spoke a language water does not ordinarily understand, and the water is still doing it, and the disc in the floor of the cell is still warm.

Whatever it is for, it is not for you. Perhaps the point was never the basin. Perhaps the point was the light, and the basin is only the way the light is made — turbulence as a kind of combustion, water as fuel. Perhaps the point was the sound, and somewhere far above, through rock, someone listens to a room that roars and knows from the roar that all is well. Perhaps the person who sat on the bench and kept count was counting the days until it would stop, and it did not stop, and they stopped instead.

You are wet through now. You have been wet through since the shaft. The warmth means you do not mind. The mist has made the front of you glow so faintly that you can only see it by looking at your hands against the dark of the roofless cell, and even then you are not sure.

The room goes on being what it is. A sheet climbs the far wall and hangs and falls. A column stands and leans left and drops into its pit. The bell-note rings from the third post along. The one animal on the roof opens its mouth to the glare. Somewhere under the noise, the disc hums its single pitch. Nothing has changed here in longer than anyone has been counting, and nothing is still for an instant, and both of these things are true, and the rock has been worn smooth by the effort of holding them together.
