You come down into it. That is the first thing to understand: the land does not rise anywhere here, it only lowers, in broad steps of grey stone that were once the floor of something and are now the floor of something else. The steps are not sharp. Whatever cut them finished cutting a long time ago, and since then the rain has been rounding every edge, so that the whole country has the softened look of a thing worn in a pocket.

The light goes before the slope does. There is a sky, technically. It is a lid of cloud that never lifts, sitting so low and so thick that midday is a brownish dusk and everything else is night. The cloud is not weather in the usual sense; it does not arrive and leave. It is generated here, exhaled here, condensed out of the basin's own breathing, and it drifts only within the rim of the low country like water sloshing in a dish. Under it the stone is wet without ever having been rained on properly. Moisture simply appears. It beads on every surface, gathers in the pits and rills of the limestone, runs in threads down the faces of the steps, pools in the flat places until the pool finds a crack and goes underground, where a great deal of this place goes.

Nothing green grows here. Nothing grows here at all in the way that word usually means. There are no stems, no leaves, no mat of moss, not even the black crust of lichen that colonizes the most hostile rock elsewhere. The stone is bare in the way bone is bare. Where it is not bare, it is covered by something that is not a plant.

---

The sound reaches you before you can see anything clearly, and once it has reached you it does not stop.

It is difficult to describe a noise that has no gaps. Waterfalls have a roar you can stand near and eventually stop hearing. This is not like that. This is a roar made out of individual voices, millions of them, each one pitched and timed and aimed, so that the ear keeps trying to resolve it into language and never can. Underneath it is a low continuous pressure you feel in the sternum, a tone so deep it seems to come from the ground. Above it, a shrilling that lives at the very top of hearing and then goes past it; you know it is there because your teeth know. In the middle band, everything: clicks, whistles, croaks like stones knocking, a sound like a wet cloth being torn very slowly, a chorus that rises and falls in slow waves across the whole width of the basin as if the country itself were taking breaths.

You cannot speak here and be heard. You cannot hear yourself. After some time you stop trying, and something shifts, and you begin to understand that the noise is not chaos. It is the place seeing itself.

---

The basin is a collapsed country. Once there was a plateau of limestone here, riddled with caves the way old wood is riddled with worm-holes, and at some point the roofs began to fail. They failed in a thousand places over ten thousand years, and now the low country is a maze of sinks and troughs and half-roofed galleries, some open to the cloud, some still arched over by stone that hangs a few hundred feet up in the dark, streaming with condensation. The floor of one sink is the roof of another. Passages connect them at every level. Rivers run through the lowest galleries, black and quick and audible only as another layer in the roar.

It is a region, not a site. You could walk for days along the floor of it and never come to the far rim. From any raised shoulder of rock you can make out, dimly, the shapes of the next three or four sinks: their walls pale in the near-dark, their floors lost. Everything is grey and slightly luminous with wet, and everything is moving.

---

The moving is the fauna, and the fauna is the second thing to understand.

There is no space here that is not occupied. That is not an exaggeration made for effect; it is close to a literal description. The walls are furred with bodies. The ceilings are hung with bodies. The floor is a moving carpet, and beneath the carpet is a second carpet, and under the stone, in the cracks and the flooded galleries, more. The air itself is thick with things flying, so that walking through the open sinks means walking into a soft continuous collision, small warm impacts against the face and hands, leathery, chitinous, feathered, wet.

It works like this. The basin is a roost. Not for one kind of animal but for hundreds, converging from an entire surrounding continent of drier, brighter country where they feed by night and cannot, for reasons of heat or predators or ancestral habit, remain by day. They come back here. They have been coming back here for longer than the roofs have been falling. Every hanging surface in the low country is claimed by fliers of one lineage or another: things like bats but larger, with faces built entirely for sound; things like swifts that never land except here; things with no counterpart anywhere, membrane-winged, six-limbed, packed together so tightly in their crevices that the wall appears to breathe.

And what falls from them feeds everything else.

The floor of every sink is deep in the leavings of the ceiling: droppings, shed skin, the dead and the fallen young. Elsewhere this would rot and be gone. Here, in the cool and the wet and the dark, it is the base of a food web that has had time to grow as elaborate as any forest's. There are things that eat the fall directly, pale segmented things the length of an arm that move through the drifts like fish through water, and smaller things by the uncountable million, and things that eat those, and things that eat those. Beetles as broad as plates, blind, armored, that raise their shells and sing by rubbing them. Crabs of some kind, or things descended from crabs, that walk the walls and pick the weak fliers off their perches. Long soft predators with no eyes and a hundred feet that lie along the ledges and taste the air for warmth. Amphibians the color of candle wax, gathered in pools in tens of thousands, whose voices make up much of the middle of the roar. Something large that moves in the lowest galleries and is known only by the way the sound changes when it passes, a hole in the noise moving slowly through the dark.

There are also the ones that stand in for plants. In the driest sinks, where a little light leaks through gaps in the cloud, the stone is covered with colonies of a sessile animal, something like a coral that has learned to live in air. They are ranked in shelves and fans and slow branching towers, and they filter the air the way their sea-ancestors filtered water, straining out the fine rain of dust and skin and microscopic life that drifts constantly down from the ceilings. They are cold and slightly slick to the touch and they click, each polyp, when disturbed, so that brushing a hand along a shelf of them produces a ripple of sound like a run of small stones. In the deeper, darker sinks their place is taken by fungi and by things stranger than fungi, mats and pillars of pale tissue that grow on the drifts and are grazed down and grow again.

---

The darkness is not total, and the not-total is important.

Some of the animals glow. Not many, and not brightly, but enough: a blue-green smear along the belly of one of the wall-walkers, a pulsing ring around the mouth of a pool-dweller, a faint constellation of points on the ceiling where something that lures by light hangs among the fliers. Water dripping from the roofs picks up the glow and carries it down in threads. The effect, once your eyes have given up looking for anything else, is of a country lit from within by its own inhabitants, very dimly, in a color like the last of a sunset seen through deep water.

But sight is the least of it. The sound is the light here. Every flier in the roost is echolocating, at every frequency, every second, and every crawler is drumming or stridulating or calling, and the stone, which is riddled and hollow and full of water, resonates with all of it. The walls of the sinks are shaped, in places, in ways that seem to have less to do with erosion than with acoustics: smooth-scooped bowls that focus the noise, ridged galleries that carry it. Whether the sound shaped the stone or the stone shaped the sound is not a question the place has any interest in answering. The animals map this country by hearing it. The country, in a sense, is constantly announcing itself to itself, and the roar you cannot stand inside is the sound of that announcement.

---

Things happen here in cycles, and the cycles are the third thing.

There is the daily one. Toward the hours that count as evening, the roar changes pitch. Something in the upper bands drops out and something in the lower comes in, and the ceilings begin to shed their occupants. It goes on for hours. Millions of fliers pour up out of the sinks and through the cloud, out to the bright country to feed, and for the length of the emptying the noise in the basin is not less but different, thinner, more spatial, so that you can suddenly hear distances in it. The floor takes advantage. This is when the crawlers move most, when the pools boil with mating, when the long soft predators come down from the ledges. Then, toward what counts as dawn, the fliers come back, and the return is worse than the leaving: the whole sky of the sink fills with descending bodies, and the roar climbs and thickens and closes over again like water over a dropped stone.

There is the longer one. The cloud lid is not perfectly stable. In some seasons it thins, and a grey light reaches the floors of the shallowest sinks for a few hours a day, and the coral-things spread, and certain of the fliers stop returning, and the balance tips a little toward the pools and the crawlers. Then it thickens again and everything tips back. In other seasons the underground rivers rise, and the lowest galleries flood, and whatever lives in them either climbs or drowns, and the higher floors receive a season of drowned things to eat. The place has done all of this many times. Nothing about it is in crisis. It is a system in the full maturity of its form, worn to fit itself, every niche occupied by something that has been occupying it since before the stone above it fell.

There is also, perhaps, a longer cycle still, the one measured by roofs. Somewhere in the basin, on any given day, stone is failing. Water has been working at a ceiling for ten thousand years and finally the last thread of it gives, and a gallery that was black becomes a sink that is merely dark, and a few million fliers lose their perches and a few billion crawlers gain a floor. When it happens there is a sound even this place notices: a deep single note, felt rather than heard, and then, for a few seconds, an almost-silence as everything in the country stops to listen. Then the roar resumes, slightly rearranged.

---

You will not stay. That is not a rule anyone made; it is just what the place is like. The cold gets into you and the wet gets into you and the noise gets into you last and deepest, until you find that you are no longer thinking in words because there is no room left for them. The animals do not mind you. Very few of them can see you and none of them have any use for you, except the ones that would eat you, and they are patient.

But before you climb back up the soft grey steps into the light and the quiet, stand once in the middle of a sink with your hand on the wall, and feel the wall move under your hand, and hear the country hear itself. There is no green in it anywhere. There is nothing in it that anyone built. It has been like this for as long as anything has been anywhere, and it is not waiting for anything, and it is not empty for a single instant in any direction, and it is loud.
