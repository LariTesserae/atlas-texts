The plateau begins where the last trees gave up, so long ago that nothing remembers them but the soil, and the soil here is only a rind. Above the rind is rock, and the rock has been rock for a very long time. Nothing on it is sharp. The ridges have been rounded down into the shapes that stone takes when it has nothing left to prove — long backs, low domes, shallow basins that hold cold air the way a bowl holds water. From any rise you can see the next rise, and the next, and then the grey folds into itself and becomes distance.

The light is not night and not day. It is the light of a ceiling. The sky is one uninterrupted overcast, thick enough that the sun is only a paler region moving slowly from one side to the other, and the ground receives that light without shadow, so that everything is lit from everywhere and nothing stands out. Colours here are the colours of a long soak: slate, ash, the bruise-brown of old lichen, the pale green-white of frost on lichen. The eye adjusts. After a while you find you can see very far, very dimly, and that this is enough.

The cold is steady rather than bitter. It is the cold of a place that never warms, and so has never had to relearn how to be cold. Frost forms on the north faces of the domes and stays there for the season, not thick, just a crust, a fine crystalline fur. On the south faces, where there is no south to speak of under this sky but a slight lean toward the pale region, the frost gives way to wet. The stone sweats. Beads gather in the pores of the rock and run together and slide down into the basins, and in the bottom of each basin there is a skin of water a hand's breadth deep, perfectly still, reflecting nothing but grey.

It is not clear, at first, where the water comes from. It does not rain often; when it does it is a fine drift that seems to hang in the air rather than fall. The basins fill from below the sky, not from it. You notice that the wet faces are always the faces that lean the same way, and that this has nothing to do with light.

Then you notice the animals.

They are not hidden. They were there the whole time, and the eye slid over them because they are the colour of the stone and shaped, roughly, like the stone — long, low, rounded, without edges. Each is the size of a fallen boulder. Each is alone in its own patch of lichen, a few body-lengths from the next, and the next, and the next, and when you begin to count them you find that you cannot see a place on the plateau where they stop. They thin, they scatter, they never end. Fifty from this rise. Fifty from the next. The plateau is a continent, and the continent is populated at this density everywhere, and this is what it means for a place to belong to its animals rather than to its ground.

They make no sound. This is the thing you feel before you understand it — the pressure of silence over a landscape that should not be silent. Their mouths work at the lichen with a motion so slow you have to watch for a full minute to be sure it is motion. Their breath comes at intervals of many minutes, and when it comes it does not sound; it appears, a long pale exhalation that hangs in the still air and drifts, always, in the same direction — the direction the whole herd faces — and settles onto the stone in front of them, and condenses.

That is the water. The plateau is wet with the breathing of its animals. The basins fill from a million lungs. The lichen they eat drinks what they exhale, and grows back, slowly, behind them.

Up close they are warm. This is the second surprise. The air around each one is a few degrees kinder than the air a stride away, and if you rest a hand on the flank — they permit this; they have no reason not to — the warmth is deep and even, a warmth that has been generated at a steady rate for the whole life of the animal and will be generated at the same rate until it stops. Under the hide, which is thick and slightly rough and the grey-brown of iron left out in weather, something is working. Not a heartbeat exactly. A rhythm too slow for a heart, a shifting of masses, a grinding. They eat the lichen and, with it, the mineral rind the lichen grows in, and they carry in their guts a cargo of stones they have swallowed over decades, and the stones turn and the rind is milled down and the metal in it is drawn out into the blood. Their blood smells of it. If you stand downwind of a large one long enough you catch, under the wet-wool smell of hide, something sharper — hot iron, a foundry smell, the smell of a thing that processes rock.

They are electrified. It is faint, and you would not notice it in daylight, but the light here is never daylight. Along the ridge of the back, where the hair is longest and driest, there is sometimes — not always, only when the air is coldest and stillest — a thread of pale blue, a corona, no brighter than the frost. When two of them come near enough to touch, which happens rarely, since they keep their distance by some rule of their own, a small light passes between them and is gone. There is no sound of it. There is a smell afterward, briefly, of ozone, and then the wet-wool and the iron again. Whether the charge is a byproduct of the grinding inside them, or something they use, or something they simply are, no one is present to ask.

This is what the plateau has instead of industry: no chimney, no wheel, no cut stone laid on stone, nothing made — and yet the sense, everywhere, of production. Of material being fed in at one end and drawn out at the other at a scale that has nothing to do with any individual animal. Each one is a furnace working at a temperature just above the frost. Each one is a mill running at a speed just above stillness. Multiply by the number you cannot count, spread over a country you cannot cross, run for a duration you cannot measure, and the plateau becomes a single slow engine, with no operator and no output but itself.

What they leave behind is not made either. Their dung is hard and dark and glassy, sintered, and it weathers slowly into black grit that the lichen will not grow on for a generation. Their tracks — for they do move, though you will not see it happen — are worn into the stone in long shallow grooves that run the length of the plateau in a great slow curve, the paths of a migration that takes longer than a lifetime to complete one circuit. The grooves are the only lines here. Everything else is curve. The grooves are polished, and hold water, and from a rise they look like the marks of a huge comb dragged once across the world.

And they die. Not often, for their lives are long, but at this density the plateau holds a great many dead, and the dead are as unmoving as the living and much the same colour, so that only the absence of breath tells them apart. There is nothing large to eat them. The cold does the work instead, and the small things do: tiny hard-shelled grazers, flat as coins, that live in the hide of the living and move to the dead when the warmth goes, and pale threadlike worms in the wet beneath, and in the end the lichen itself, which finds the bones and covers them. The bones weather into something like unglazed pottery — the iron gone out of them, back into the rind, back into the lichen, back into another mouth. A skeleton twenty years dead is a low grey dome. A skeleton two hundred years dead is a rise you might stand on to look for the edge of the herd and fail to find it.

There is a wind, sometimes. It is not strong. It comes from behind the herd and moves over them and is warmed, very slightly, and carries their breath before it, and where it slows in the lee of a dome the breath falls out as fog. The fog is the only thing here that moves at a pace the eye can follow. It pours over the rounded backs of the animals and the rounded backs of the stone and does not distinguish between them, and it collects in the basins and becomes water, and the water becomes frost on the cold side, and the frost becomes the fine fur on the north faces, and the fur melts a little each season and runs down, and the lichen drinks it, and the mouths work at the lichen, and the breath goes out.

The circuit is closed. The plateau has been in this state for so long that the state has become the place. Nothing here is becoming anything. The rock is already worn, the animals are already spread, the water is already circulating, the light is already dim. Change occurs, but it occurs as the herd occurs — in every direction at once, at a rate below the threshold of attention, so that you could stand at the same spot for a year and see nothing happen and return in a thousand and find the grooves an inch deeper and the herd, still, in every direction, breathing.

The silence is the part you carry away. Not the absence of sound but the presence of a sound-shaped nothing, laid over a country full to its horizons with living, working, warm bodies, each one turning stone into iron into breath into water into stone, and none of them saying anything about it at all.
