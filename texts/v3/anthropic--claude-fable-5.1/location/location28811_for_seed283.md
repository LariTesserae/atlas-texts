Night on the low ground, and the heat has not gone anywhere. It sits in the hollows the way water would if there were water enough. The land here barely rises; it is a floor, a shelf of old limestone a few meters above whatever sea used to sit on it, and the rock has been dissolving for so long that it has stopped looking like rock. It is pitted, fluted, honeycombed, rounded off at every edge. Rain, when it comes, does not stay. It goes straight down through the stone and is gone within the hour, and so the forest grows out of a ground that is always drinking and never full.

And yet the forest is enormous. Not tall—the trees are stocky, wide-crowned, buttressed low against a soil that is mostly leaf-rot packed into cracks—but total. There is no gap. Where a trunk ends, a strangler has taken up the space beside it; where the canopy would thin, a fig has wandered across on aerial roots and closed it. Lianas hang so thick that in the dark they read as a second set of trunks. Every leaf is broad, waxy, angled to shed. Everything drips. The air is so loaded with moisture that by the middle of the night the leaves have condensed a rain of their own, and this is the only rain the place reliably gets: a slow, uneven ticking of drops, warm as blood, falling from the upper canopy onto the lower, from the lower onto the ferns, from the ferns onto the stone, and into the stone.

It is dark in the way that only a closed canopy on a moonless night is dark. Not a black that the eye adjusts to but a black that the eye gives up on. There is a faint difference between up and down—up has a brownish stain in it, a color like the inside of a copper pot—and that stain is the only light. It does not come from the sky. It comes from beyond the forest, past the edge of the plateau where the limestone drops to the coastal flat, where something industrial has been burning for years: a flare stack, or a row of them, or the sodium wash of a refinery, or a smelter's furnaces. The forest never sees the source. It sees the glow rubbed off the underside of the haze, and the haze is always there, because the works put it there and the heat keeps it low.

The works are also in the ground. Not as a sound exactly. Lie against the stone and there is a periodic weight in it, a thud every four or five seconds, so low it arrives through the sternum rather than the ear. Pumps, probably. Something reciprocating and heavy, kilometers off, coupled to the same bedrock. The animals have never known a night without it. It is part of what quiet means here.

Because the forest is quiet, and the forest is full.

In the places where the limestone has collapsed—dolines, some the size of a room, some the size of a village—the water that fell through the rock has pooled against a clay floor, and these are the only open water for a long way. Every one of them is occupied. Around the largest, at the bottom of a funnel of ferns and dripping root-curtains, the mud is trodden into a paste by an accumulation of bodies that in daylight would be unbelievable and in this dark is only felt: the shifting of hooves, the exhale of something large lowering itself to drink, the small hard clicks of shells knocking together where the crabs, hundreds of them, have come up out of the crevices to sit in the wet. Bats work the surface—the pool is dimpled continuously, not by rain but by the tips of wings taking water on the pass. Frogs sit shoulder to shoulder on every wet ledge, and they are not calling. It is too hot to call. They breathe, and their breathing, multiplied by thousands, is a texture in the air rather than a noise.

That is the register of the place. Not silence—there is no silence with this many lungs—but a hush of enormous mass. Insects are so thick that a hand moved through the dark meets them as resistance. Moths settle on any surface that holds a trace of warmth or moisture, which is every surface, so that the trunks are furred with them, sleeved. Ants run in columns wide as a wrist, both directions at once, up the buttresses and down, and the column never breaks, never thins, has not thinned in living memory. Snails the size of fists graze the algae on the limestone. Between the dolines, along the old drainage lines that no longer drain, the ground is moving with millipedes, with land leeches waiting on leaf-tips for the next warm passing thing, with the slow reorganization of a termite population that has hollowed the deadwood into paper and then hollowed the paper.

In the crowns, invisible, the roosting birds sit packed along branches like fruit, so many that the branches carry them with a slight permanent bow. Fruit bats hang in the figs in such numbers that the trees look, if you could see them, wrong—too heavy, too lumpish. Now and then one of these colonies stirs and the whole tree shivers and drips, and a smell comes down that is sweet and ammoniac at once, and then it settles again.

Nothing here is built, and nothing here is new. The forest has been at this a long time. There is no bare rock left that has not been rounded, no fallen trunk that has not been furred over, no edge. The trees that fall are absorbed by the trees that stand within a handful of years. The dolines have collapsed slowly, over centuries, and the forest simply moved down into them and carried on. The paths to the water are not made by anything; they are worn, hoof-deep grooves in the stone from animals that have walked the same line since there was a line to walk. It is a landscape in a settled argument with itself, where the rock keeps dissolving and the forest keeps filling in behind, and the two rates have long since matched.

The industry beyond the ridge does not disturb this so much as tint it. The glow in the haze is the color the night has always been, as far as any animal alive can tell. The soot that arrives with certain winds settles on the leaves and is washed off by the condensation and goes down into the rock like everything else. The thud in the ground is the pulse the frogs sit inside. The forest has taken the works the way it takes a fallen fig: as a fact, and slowly, and without comment.

Toward the end of the night the heat does not break. It never breaks here; the ground gives back all day what it took and the canopy holds it in. But the dripping picks up as the leaves reach their wettest, and the pool in the largest doline rises by perhaps the width of a finger, and a few more bodies come down the worn grooves in the dark to lean into it, and the crabs shift over to make room, and the bats keep dimpling the surface, and far off under the coppery haze something reciprocates, and reciprocates, and reciprocates.
