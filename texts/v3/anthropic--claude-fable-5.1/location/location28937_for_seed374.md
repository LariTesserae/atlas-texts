You hear it before the fog lets you see anything, and by the time you can see anything you have stopped being able to hear yourself.

The basin sits at the bottom of a wide shallow fold in the land, lower than everything around it, and the sound rises out of it the way heat rises out of a fire. Not a roar exactly — a roar has a shape, a beginning. This is more like a pressure. Water falling over ten thousand lips of stone at once, none of the falls large, all of them continuous, so that the individual note of each is lost and what remains is a single flat wall of white sound that fills the whole valley to the brim and stands there. Under it, woven through it, are other sounds you learn to pick out only after hours: the ticking whir of wings in the millions, the clack of stone on stone, voices pitched high and short because nothing longer can survive.

The fog is not weather. It is the basin breathing. Warm water comes up out of the ground at the head of the valley from a line of springs no one has counted, comes up already hot and already saturated, and where it meets the air it gives up its heat as steam. The steam never clears, because the water never stops. It hangs thick from the ground to a height of perhaps forty or fifty feet and then thins into a ceiling that lets through a pearl-grey light with no direction to it. There are no shadows in the basin. You cannot tell where the sun is. Everything is lit the same dull luminous way, as if from inside, and the light does not change from what you would call morning to what you would call evening, because the ceiling of steam is the same thickness at all hours and the springs do not know what an hour is.

---

The stone is white. Not the white of chalk or of bleached bone but a wet, almost translucent white, the color of a fingernail, and in places where the water runs thinnest it takes on the colors of what lives in it — rust, saffron, a bruised olive-green — laid down in bands finer than hair. It is travertine, or tufa, or whatever name you like for what happens when hot water carrying dissolved lime meets air and lets go of its burden. The whole floor of the valley is made of it and the whole floor of the valley is still being made.

This is the first thing to understand about the place: nothing here has been worn. There are no sharp edges anywhere, but not because they have been rounded off by time. They were never sharp. Every surface is a surface of deposit, a surface that is thickening as you look at it, and so everything has the softened, half-formed look of a thing pulled from a mold before the mold was finished. Ledges swell like lips. The rims of pools bulge outward and downward in curtains and fringes that look as though they should drip and in fact do drip, each drip leaving behind a little more of itself. Rock weeps here, and the weeping is what the rock is made of. Underfoot the ground is faintly warm and faintly yielding at the very top, a skin of fresh mineral a day or two old, and beneath that it is hard as porcelain.

The terraces step down the valley in their hundreds. Each is a shallow basin with a raised lip, each basin brimming, each lip pouring over into the basin below in a thin sheet or in strings of separate falls, and the water is a pale milky blue-green that goes opaque a hand's depth down. The terraces are not arranged in rows. They pile, they overlap, they branch; a large pool feeds four small ones which feed one large one again. They fill the valley from wall to wall so that there is no dry ground anywhere except the ground people have made dry by directing the water elsewhere, and even that ground sweats.

---

People have lived here for as long as anyone's memory reaches back, and the way they live is built on a single trick, which is that the water builds for them.

A wall here is not quarried and stacked. A wall is grown. You take a screen of woven reed — the reed does not grow in the basin; nothing does; it is carried in from the dry country over the rim by people who spend their lives walking in with bundles on their backs — and you stand it where you want the wall, and you cut a channel with a hand adze of hardened tufa so that a sheet of water runs down the face of the screen, and you wait. Within a month the reed is crusted. Within a year the crust is a hand thick and the reed inside has long since rotted to nothing and been replaced by stone, and the wall stands. Two years and it will hold a roof. The roof is the same: a lattice of reed, a lip of stone above it that spills a thin curtain of water over the lattice, and the water leaves its lime behind and the lattice becomes a vault. When it is thick enough you cut a groove to divert the water away and the vault stops growing, though it goes on sweating for the rest of its existence, and every room in the city has a ceiling that drips.

So the city is white and rounded and continuous with the ground it stands on, because it is the ground it stands on, only guided. Houses press against houses. There is no space between them because there is no reason to leave space; a wall grown between two houses is one wall shared. The whole settlement climbs the terraces in tiers, room over room, the floor of one house the ceiling of the next, stairs that are simply small terraces with the water led off them, doorways that are nothing more than places where a screen was never stood. From a distance — if there were a distance from which you could see it, which the steam does not allow — it would look like a single enormous formation, a reef, with the people living in its pores.

They own almost nothing that the basin did not give them. Their tools are tufa, which cuts tufa well enough when it is fresh. Their bowls are the little cupped forms that water makes on its own when it drips into the same spot long enough; they are lifted off the rock and used. Their cords and screens and mats are reed from outside. Their fuel is bird dung, dried on the warm stone, which burns hot and stinks and is inexhaustible. They have no metal and want none. A blade of black glass, traded in over the rim from very far away, is a thing a family might own one of and pass down for six generations.

---

The fauna is the second half of the sound.

Every overhang in the basin, every grown vault and natural cave and undercut lip of terrace, is a roost. Small dark birds — swifts of some kind, with sickle wings and no visible feet — nest in the tens of thousands in the hollows, and they are never all still. There is always a portion of them in the air, a smoke of birds in the smoke of steam, shrieking a shriek that is one long continuous thread laid over the water's roar. Where the swifts are not, there are bats, hanging so densely from the ceilings that the ceilings appear furred and the fur breathes. Below both, on every surface the water keeps wet, are the flies: a dark, shifting crust of them, feeding on the mineral films, rising in a body when a foot comes near and settling again a stride behind you. Their wings are a drone you feel in the roof of your mouth. The flies feed the birds and the bats. The birds and the bats feed the pools with their droppings, and the pools grow thick mats of orange and green — not plant, nothing so complex, only the simplest living films — and the mats feed the flies. It is a very short circle and it runs very hot.

In the deeper pools there are fish, small, colorless, blind-looking, in shoals so dense the water seems to thicken when they turn. On the terrace lips are snails whose shells are the same white as the stone and are being slowly cemented to it while their owners are still alive inside. The people take the swifts' nests, which are made of spit and harden like the rock; they take the eggs; they take the fish in reed baskets; they take the bats only when they have to.

Nothing green. Nothing that would make a shadow, if there were light enough for shadows. The rim of the valley has a fringe of scrub and beyond it there is presumably country of the ordinary kind, but inside the basin the water is too hot and too heavy with lime for anything with roots, and the only vegetable matter is what has been carried in on backs and is already on its way to being stone.

---

The third thing to understand, and the last, is that the basin does not change.

The springs come up at the same heat and the same volume. They have done so for longer than there have been people to notice. The rate of deposit is fixed by the heat and the volume and the air, and the air is always the same air, so the rate is fixed. A screen stood on a given day will be a wall on a given day. Everyone knows how long. Children are taught it the way children elsewhere are taught the months.

And because the stone never stops growing, the city cannot stand still either — but it does not, for all that, go anywhere. The lowest, oldest tiers are always being swallowed. A house in the bottom of the valley is a house whose door has narrowed to a slot and whose floor has risen to meet its ceiling, and its people have long since moved up. Above, new screens are always standing in new channels. The settlement migrates upward at exactly the pace the stone climbs after it, and so, seen across any length of time you could actually live, it has not moved at all. It is a standing wave. The same number of houses, the same number of people, the same number of birds, the same number of flies. Everything is replaced and nothing is different. There is no season. There is no weather but the fog. There is no year. There is no direction the light comes from and no hour it fails.

There is only the sound, which was the first thing, and which is the whole of the place if you stand in it long enough: water letting go of stone, a valley wide, forever, under a lid of steam, so loud and so unvarying that after a day you stop hearing it as sound at all and begin to hear it as the shape of silence, the particular silence this place has instead of the ordinary kind, and you speak to people by touching them, and you sleep with your head against a wall that has been growing since before you were born and will still be growing, a hand's breadth thicker and otherwise exactly the same, when your grandchildren lay their heads against it and hear the water in it and think nothing of it at all.
