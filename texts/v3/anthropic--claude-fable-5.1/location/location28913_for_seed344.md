Start with the sound, because that is what you would meet first: a roar with no apparent source. It comes up over a low earthen berm before anything else does, before the smell of warm minerals, before the change in the air. It is not one sound but two laid over each other — a broad hiss like a held breath that never has to be released, and under it a hum you feel in the sternum more than hear, the note that large transformers make when they are working and have been working for years. Together they fill the hollow so completely that when you climb the berm and look down, the space seems smaller than the noise suggests. You expected a river. What you find would fit inside a barn.

It is a shallow bowl in the ground, perhaps forty paces across, sunk below the surrounding flats so that its rim cuts off everything beyond except the sky and the top of a corrugated wall some distance off, and a stack, and a run of insulated pipe on trestles going somewhere else. The bowl is the low point of a low country. Water finds it from every direction and does not leave, or leaves only by rising.

At the far side, the berm has been cut and faced with concrete, and out of the concrete comes a pipe. It is the size of a person's chest. Its mouth has been running for so long that the lip has worn smooth and bright, and the water that leaves it is not falling so much as being pushed — a solid grey-white bar that crosses a yard of air before it breaks on the ground below. This is the source of the hiss. It is also the source of the heat. The air above the pipe shivers. The water is hot enough that you would not put your hand in it, hot enough that its steam does not drift so much as pour, sliding down along the surface of the pool it feeds and then lifting in slow rags only when it reaches the cooler edge.

Where the water lands it has built something. The ground below the pipe is not mud. It is stone — but a new stone, pale as unfired porcelain, laid down in shelves and lips and shallow terraces that step down toward the middle of the bowl. Each shelf holds a skin of water that spills over its edge in a thin uniform sheet onto the shelf below. The edges are sharp. Nothing has had time to round them. Run a finger along one (the lower ones are cool enough) and it feels like the edge of a broken plate. Some of the shelves are still wet-glossy and translucent; the older ones toward the back have gone chalky and opaque. You can read the whole history of the outfall in that gradient, from the pipe outward — the years in the stone as the stone in the water — except that the history is not long. The concrete around the pipe still shows the grain of its formwork. A steel number stencilled on it has not yet fully rusted through.

This is a young place. It was a drainage sump before it was anything else, a nothing, a low spot on a site plan. Then the plant needed somewhere to send its cooling water, and someone ran a pipe over the rim and left it. The water has been arriving ever since, day and night, at the same temperature and the same pressure, and everything you see here it has made.

The pool in the middle is no deeper than your knee. It is absolutely clear and faintly blue, the blue that comes from dissolved silica rather than from depth, and its floor is the same pale new stone, dimpled where the constant flow has worked it. Around the pool the ground is wet in rings. Closest in, it is bare — wet stone, wet crust, the mineral sheen that steam leaves when it condenses and evaporates over and over on the same surface until the surface itself becomes a kind of glaze. Everything within reach of the vapour is glazed: the concrete face, the lower rungs of a maintenance ladder bolted beside the pipe, a gauge on a stalk whose glass face has been frosted white from the inside and no longer reads anything. Even the rim of the bowl above the pipe, where the steam lifts and rolls over, is coated. Rain would rinse it. But when rain falls here, it falls into water that is already hot, and lifts again as more steam, and lays down more glaze.

Further out, where the ground cools enough to be only warm, there is vegetation — not much. A stand of reeds, hollow and yellow-green, growing in a single clump against the north wall of the bowl where a seep of ordinary groundwater comes in cold from the flats and gives them a margin they can live in. Their lower stems are crusted white where the mist has reached them. A few stalks have died standing and gone entirely to stone, still upright, brittle, hollow, ringing faintly if you tap them. Across the bowl, three grey-leaved plants, low and leathery, have taken hold in cracks of the concrete apron. Nothing else. The soil of the rim is scorched bare in a band a yard wide where the steam pours over, and beyond that the flats are their own thin scrub and are not part of this.

There are no animals. This is not a place where something might turn up if you waited; it is a place where the absence is structural. Nothing flies over it — the rising air is hot and wet and the sound is enormous. Nothing has crossed the wet crust, because the crust records everything that touches it and it records nothing. There are no insects on the reeds. There is no skin of algae on the pool; the water is too hot and too pure, and it leaves the stone floor bare. Whatever lives around here has decided, collectively and without discussion, that the hollow does not exist.

What the hollow does have is objects. They are few and far apart, and each one sits alone in enough space that you notice it as a separate event. A wooden pallet, half-submerged at the pool's edge, going to stone from the bottom up — the lower slats already encased, the upper ones still grey wood. A canvas glove, left on a terrace lip so long ago that it has become a glove-shaped stone, fingers slightly curled, weighing far more than a glove should. A length of chain hanging from the ladder, each link fused to the next by a white collar, so that it no longer moves; it hangs like a cast of a chain. A steel plate stamped with a flow rate and a date, bolted to the concrete, unreadable under its coating. These are not ruins. They are furniture that stayed too long. The place preserves by burial, gently, a fraction of a millimetre at a time, and the things it preserves are all the ordinary things of the work that made it — as if the outfall were quietly making a museum of the plant behind the berm, one dropped item at a time.

The light is plain. The sky is high and white-grey, the sun a brighter smear behind it, and the bowl takes that light and softens it further through the standing vapour so that there are no hard shadows anywhere, only a general luminance that seems to come from the stone itself. The pale terraces glow. The pool glows blue. The pipe's water, where it leaves the lip, is the brightest thing in view, brighter than the sky, a bar of light that does not move even though everything in it is moving.

Stand at the bottom of the bowl for a while and the sound does something strange. Because the walls are close and curved and wet, the roar comes at you from every direction at once, and the pipe — which you can see, which is right there — no longer seems to be where the sound is coming from. The hiss seems to originate from the air itself. The hum from below, from the ground, from the stone under your feet, which is in fact carrying it: the transformer note travels through the concrete and the crust and the water, and the hollow acts as a shallow dish that gathers it and holds it. Sometimes, standing very still, you can feel the surface of the pool responding — a fine tremor on the water, a patterning too small to see and too regular to be wind. When the plant shifts load, the note shifts with it, and for a moment the whole bowl retunes.

This is one of the few kinds of change the hollow admits. The others are slow past watching. The terraces advance toward the centre of the pool at a rate you could measure only by returning years apart. The pallet sinks a little further into stone. The stencilled number on the concrete fades under its coating. In the plant, someone occasionally adjusts a valve, and the bar of water from the pipe thickens or thins a finger's width, and the steam pours a little faster or slower over the rim, and the stone records the adjustment as a slightly thicker or thinner band in its cross-section, the way a tree records a wet year. The outfall has never stopped. If it stopped, the hollow would cool in a night, and its clear pool would go cloudy, and the first rain would begin to eat the terraces from their sharp edges inward, and in a decade the place would be a grey scar with a pipe in it. But it has not stopped, and there is no sign it will. The heat is a byproduct. Something else is the point. The hollow is what the point leaves behind.

Near evening the light lowers and the vapour catches it, and the bowl fills up with a pale gold haze that makes the far wall difficult to see and the ladder look as though it climbs out of the mist into nothing. The reeds against the north seep stand black against it. The hum continues, the hiss continues, the water lands and spreads and pours over lip after lip toward the blue pool, laying down its imperceptible layer. If you leave anything behind — a tool, a cup, a footprint pressed into the softer crust at the margin — the place will begin at once, patiently, to keep it.
