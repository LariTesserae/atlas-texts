Down a stair that turns twice and then gives up on being a stair, the air changes before anything else does. It thickens. It goes warm and then warmer, and it takes on a taste — iron, wet lime, the sweetish rot of old cloth — and by the time your hand finds the last rail, the rail is warm too, warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and it does not cool while you hold it.

This is a gallery under something. What is above is not important anymore; it may have been a foundry, a laundry district, a bathhouse quarter with its dozens of boilers all feeding one trunk. The gallery is where the trunk ran. It is perhaps forty paces long and twelve wide, though pacing it is impossible, because the room is full. Pipe fills it. Pipe at the ankle, pipe at the hip, pipe overhead so low you walk with your shoulders drawn in. Cast iron mains as thick as a torso run the length of the room on cradles of riveted angle; smaller lines branch from them and cross and recross, doubling back through flanged elbows, each junction wrapped in a collar of bolts gone soft-edged with rust. Between the pipes there is barely a body's width of dark. You move through the room the way water moves through gravel: by finding the gaps.

The dark is not total. It only feels total. After some minutes the eye recovers two things. One is a seam of dull red along the far wall, low, at the level of the floor — not a light so much as a place where the blackness is warm-colored, where something on the other side of the brick is still burning or still cooling and has been doing one or the other for a very long time. The other is a scattering of faint greenish points overhead, the phosphor of old gauge faces that soaked up light decades ago and have been giving it back a photon at a time ever since. Their needles cannot be read. Some of them, if you put your face against the glass, are not resting at zero.

Everything here was made, and everything here has been made again by what the making did. The pipes were lagged once with wound cloth and plaster; the plaster has crazed into a hide of small tiles and the cloth hangs from underneath in grey fringes that move slightly in air you cannot feel moving. Every joint that ever leaked has built itself a beard. Where steam or hot water wept from a flange for fifty years, the minerals it carried have stayed behind as the water went, and so the iron wears formations of pale, lumpy stone — soda straws, curtains, blunt little cones — a limestone cave grown on plumbing, geology in the wrong order. The largest of these has reached the floor. It stands like a pillar between the second main and the wall, and if you rest a palm against it, it is warm, and faintly damp, and tremendously heavy in the way of things that have been added to grain by grain and never once subtracted from.

The wet is intermittent. It arrives from above as a slow condensation on every cold enough surface, which in this room means nearly nothing, so it gathers only on the highest pipes near the ceiling vault and then, when a bead grows past its own patience, it lets go. The drop falls through two or three layers of pipe, sometimes striking iron on the way with a small dull tock, and lands in the channel that runs down the center of the floor. The channel was cut for exactly this. It is lined in glazed brick the color of liver and still holds a thread of water an inch deep, moving toward a drain grate at the red end of the room, moving so slowly that a fallen thread of lagging cloth lying across it will not shift for the length of an afternoon. Sometimes — perhaps after rain in the world above, perhaps for reasons that have nothing to do with rain — the thread widens to a finger's depth, and the room fills with the smell of wet mortar, and then over a day or two it withdraws again. That is the fastest anything happens here.

Except for the pipes themselves, which breathe. Not steadily. Over hours the iron takes on more heat from the red wall and lengthens, a fraction of a fraction, and somewhere in the run a pipe shifts on its cradle with a noise like a knuckle cracking in another room. Later, cooling, it cracks back. Between these sounds there is the drip, and behind the drip there is a very low hum that might be the building above, or might be the thing behind the wall, or might be the blood in your own ears; it never resolves. The room is mostly quiet, and the quiet has a texture, and the texture is warmth.

Things grow here, in the way that things grow in the bottom of a stopped drain. Where the channel water slows against the grate, a mat of something brown and gelatinous has cushioned the bars. On the shaded undersides of the lowest pipes, where drip has wetted the same spot for years, there are fungal shelves the color of old teeth, layered like the rings of a tree, each new layer paler than the last. And once or twice a year, when the drain backs up and a little silt comes with it, seeds arrive from somewhere — grass, a weed of the streets — and in the total dark they sprout anyway, because a seed does not know. They come up in threads, white and tall and thin as hair, tipped with two leaves folded closed, reaching for a sun that is not down here. They grow perhaps a hand's height on stored strength alone. Then they fall over and lie in the channel and dissolve, and the water carries the last of them to the grate, and the brown mat takes them in. This has been happening for so long that there is a soft grey tidemark of their remains along the brick, and if you touched it, it would feel like felt.

Something eats the fungus. You will not see it — it hears you before you know it is there — but it leaves a dust of rasped-off white along the shelf edges, and once in a while there is a click, a small hard click from behind the pipes, like a pebble dropped on iron. Cricket, maybe. Pale, long-legged, blind or nearly. There cannot be many. The room does not make enough to feed many. Whatever they are, they were not brought here; they came down the same way the seeds came, and unlike the seeds, they stayed, and they have made their arrangements with the heat.

And that is the thing the room wants you to notice, eventually, once your eyes have given up trying to see and your skin has started listening instead: the heat is not going away. Boilers cool. Furnaces, once you stop feeding them, go cold in a day. Whatever is behind the red seam in the wall has been at this temperature since before the lime pillar reached the floor, since before the lagging rotted, since before anyone alive remembers the stair. Nothing runs. The gauges are dead. But put your ear to the largest main, the one closest to the wall, and under the tock of the drips and the hum that may be your blood there is, very faintly, something moving inside the iron — not flow, exactly, more like the sound a house makes when someone is walking on the floor above and you cannot tell in which direction. Then it stops. Then the pipe, patient, lengthens by a hair, and cracks its knuckle, and the drop that has been growing on the ceiling all this while lets go, and falls, and the room is where it was.
