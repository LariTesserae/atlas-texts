The approach is a saddle between two peaks, high enough that the air has thinned to a kind of clarity that would be useful if there were anything to see. There isn't. Cloud sits in the saddle and does not move. It is not fog exactly — fog implies it might lift — but a permanent condensing atmosphere, cold enough that breath is barely distinguishable from the ambient air. The rock, where rock still shows, is grey and sharp-cornered, split along fresh planes, the kind of stone that has not yet had time to round. Frost did that, recently, and will do it again tonight.

Then the rock stops, and the settlement begins.

It is not a settlement in any way that a settlement is usually understood. Nobody built it. There are no tools here and there have never been tools here; there are no marks of cutting or joining. But it has streets, and it has rooms, and it has walls of a consistent height, and it has the density — the packed, shoulder-to-shoulder occupation of ground — that belongs to towns and not to forests.

The whole of it is a single plant.

Or a colony of one plant, which at this altitude amounts to the same thing. It is a low, woody thing, related to nothing that grows lower down, with leaves the size of a thumbnail, dark green on top and a felted rust-brown underneath. It grows outward in cells. Each cell is a rough hexagon of stems perhaps two or three body-lengths across, walled by stems that lean inward and interlock overhead, so that each cell is roofed. Where one cell meets the next, the walls are shared. The cells abut without gaps. From above — if there were an above, if the cloud ever thinned — it would look like the cross-section of a wasp nest, or a city seen from a great height.

From within, it is a warren.

The passages between cells are not gaps in the growth but part of it. The plant seems to leave certain seams unroofed — perhaps where two expanding colonies met and neither could overtop the other — and these seams have become the streets. They are narrow, one person wide, and the walls on either side rise to head height or a little above, and the leaves on both sides are so close they brush both shoulders. Underfoot is not soil. It is the plant again, older, the horizontal roots and dead lower stems of earlier generations compressed into a springy, dark, wet floor that gives a little under each step and then returns. It holds water. Every footfall pushes up a bead of it.

The cells themselves are dim. Light comes through the roof in a grey-green filtered wash, and it is never bright enough to read the details of the far wall. Inside a cell, the air is still. The temperature is perhaps a degree warmer than outside — the plant's small metabolic heat, trapped — and this is enough that the mist, drifting in through the doorways, condenses. Every leaf is beaded. Every stem is dark with wet. The undersides of the roof-stems carry a continuous fringe of droplets, and they fall, one at a time, at intervals of a few seconds, onto the floor, onto the leaves below, onto whatever is standing there. This is the principal sound of the place: not rain, but the residue of rain, a slow irregular ticking from every direction, none of it loud, all of it constant.

The cells are not empty, though they are unoccupied. Each has, at its center, a low raised mound where the plant's oldest stem in that cell emerges from the floor — the founding stem, the one the others grew from. The mound is furred with a fine grey moss, the only other plant here, and moss is perhaps too generous a word; it is more a stain of green on the wood, a co-tenant that pays nothing. Some cells have, along one wall, a pale horizontal band where new growth is coming in: stems the color of bone, not yet lignified, soft enough to dent with a fingernail, pushing out into the passage. This is the young part of a young place. In another season these will have darkened and hardened and the passage will be narrower by a hand's breadth.

That is the first rule of the place: it is closing. Not fast, not on a scale that can be watched, but on a scale that can be measured from one visit to the next. The passages that exist now were wider once. The cells that are roofed now were open once. Somewhere near the center — it is hard to say where the center is — there must be cells whose doorways have grown shut entirely, sealed rooms full of drip and dark, and there is no way to know what is in them, and the answer is probably nothing.

The second rule is that it is climbing. The saddle is not flat, and the plant grows up-slope more readily than down. On the uphill side the newest cells are also the highest, built on top of earlier ones — the older layer's roof becoming the newer layer's floor — so that the settlement is two, in places three, stories deep, and the ground floor there is a basement, lightless, the drip louder because there is nowhere for it to go. Water pools. It collects in the hollows of the compressed root-floor and stands, black and cold and perfectly still, in shallow ponds the size of a spread hand, and there are hundreds of these, one in almost every low cell. They do not drain. Nothing here drains. The whole mass is a sponge that has stopped being able to absorb.

On the downhill edge, the plant is at its rawest. Here the cells are half-formed, three or four walls up and no roof, stems still splayed and pale, and beyond them the raw rock resumes — the same frost-broken grey, but with the plant's runners already laid across it, pressed flat, pale as tendons, finding the cracks. The edge is sharp. There is no zone of transition, no scrub, no gradation from town to country. There is plant, then there is stone, and the line between them was drawn this year.

Fauna is nearly absent. There is a snail, or a kind of snail, dark and small and flat, that lives on the wet undersides of the roof-stems, and there are probably a great many of them, but they are so still and so nearly the color of the wood that one sees perhaps one in a hundred. Occasionally something moves in the leaf-litter of the floor — a springtail, a flicker too small to focus on. Once, and only once, from somewhere higher in the uphill stories, a bird: not a call, just the rattle of wings against leaves, a body moving through a space barely wide enough for it, and then silence. Whatever it was, it did not come back, and it was not clear that it had found what it was looking for.

There is no wind in the passages. The cloud outside moves, if it moves at all, at a speed below perception, and the walls break it before it enters. But there is something that behaves like weather. In the late part of the day — it is hard to say when, since the light does not change so much as dim — the drip quickens. The plant, cooling, gives up more of what it holds, and the ticking becomes closer to a patter, and the little black pools rise by a fraction, and the moss on the founding stems darkens. Then, later still, it slows, and the cold comes properly, and at the very top of the highest cells the beaded water on the leaves goes hard and white, and in the morning — if that is what the slight brightening is — the first sound is the small brittle release of frost letting go.

There is a third rule, harder to state. It has to do with the regularity. The cells are too consistent. A wild plant sprawling over rock should produce something ragged, and this is not ragged; the walls are the same height, the cells are the same size, the passages run in the same directions. There is a grammar to it, a kind of municipal logic, and it comes not from any planning intelligence but from the plant's own constraint — the length a stem can grow before it must lean and touch another, the angle at which two stems can interlock and hold. The settlement is the shape of that constraint repeated a thousand times. It looks built because building is also constraint repeated. Nobody meant it. It means itself.

Standing in one of the middle cells — dark, dripping, the founding stem's grey moss at knee height, the pale band of new growth already pressing into the doorway, the small black pool at one's feet holding a grey reflection of the roof — the sense is less of a ruin than of an arrival. Something has come up here, into the cold and the thin wet air, and has made a place for itself at great expense, and the place is not finished, and will not be finished, and does not appear to be for anyone. The doorway, when it closes, will close on no one. And then the cell will simply be a cell, full and sealed and dripping, one of many, and the town will go on growing uphill into the cloud without it.
