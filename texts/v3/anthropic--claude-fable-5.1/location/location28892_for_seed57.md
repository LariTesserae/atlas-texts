The ground opened here not long ago. You can tell by the edges: nothing has softened them yet. The lip of the hollow is a clean fracture, pale on the break like a snapped bone, and below the lip the walls go down in sharp shelves, each one keeping the angle at which it fell. There has been no time for rounding. There has been only time for filling.

And it filled. Water came in from somewhere below and kept coming until it stood a hand's breadth above the fracture, level with the surrounding field, so that from a few paces away the hollow is not visible at all. It is a pond the size of a room, and then it is not a pond, because you would have to know it was there to see it, and there is no light to see it by. It is a room that happens to be made of water, set into the earth like a stone into a ring.

Inside, it is full. That is the first thing and the whole thing. Not full of water — water is only what holds it — but full of growth, packed to the walls, packed to the floor, packed to the underside of the surface. Long-bladed weed rises from silt that has barely had time to become silt. Moss has taken every raw face of rock and thickened until the rock is a rumor beneath it. Root-mats trail down from the turf overhead where the field's grasses have kept growing, unbothered, into the water beneath them, so that the ceiling of this room is a hanging orchard of white and cream and pale green. There is no gap in it. There is no space between one stem and the next in which a hand could be placed flat. If you pressed inward you would not enter the water; you would enter the plants, and the water would be the thing you found between them, cold and close and tasting faintly of iron and of green.

It is cool in the way of cellars. Not the cold that bites, but the cold that waits, a few degrees below comfortable and holding there through every season, because the sun has never reached this and does not intend to.

There is no light. This needs saying twice, because the vegetation will make you doubt it. Nothing filters down through the turf. Nothing glows. And yet the blades are green, the moss is green, and they are growing — you can feel the slow tautness of it, the way a stem holds itself against the current that isn't there. They grow in the dark the way other things grow in the light, having agreed among themselves that the dark is what feeds them. Whether they are green is not something anyone will ever check. They are green in the way a word is green when it is written down and no one reads it.

At the bottom, in the silt, there is a threshold stone. A single block, dressed on one face, with a shallow groove worn across it by feet that walked over it somewhere else, in some other arrangement of the world, long before the ground here decided to open. Nobody built anything in this hollow. The stone was already down there when the hollow found it — either that, or the hollow was made around it, the way the body makes a pearl around a grit. A few paces off, if paces mean anything, there is an iron nail, unrusted, standing point-up in the silt as if driven from beneath. That is all. Two marks. The rest is the plants and the water and the walls, and the walls are already gone under moss.

The rules here are not the rules outside.

The room is larger inside than the opening in the field allows. Pass from one wall to the other and count your strokes, and then count them back, and the two numbers will not agree, and neither will agree with the third time. The weed does not move aside for you so much as rearrange where you are, and the wall you reach is the wall you started from, only from its other face. There is a sense, hard to put down, that the hollow is a single fold in something larger, and that you are inside the crease.

Sound comes wrong. It is mostly quiet — the quiet of a held breath, of a stopped clock — but now and then a stone settles somewhere in the walls, a small dry tick, and you hear the tick before the stone moves. You feel the water shift in answer to the sound, and then the stone lets go. Once, far up in the root-mat, there is a sound like a single drop falling onto a still surface, though there is no surface up there to fall onto and nothing above the water to fall from. The drop lands. The rings spread through the roots. Then the drop falls.

The plants, too. The weed rises from the floor and the roots descend from the ceiling and somewhere in the middle of the room they meet and pass through one another, and it is impossible, feeling along a single stalk, to say at what point it stopped being a thing that grows up and became a thing that grows down. The stalk does not seem to know either. It grows both ways from a middle that is not fixed. Count the stems along one wall and you will count the same stem twice and be right both times.

There is almost nothing living here that moves. Almost. One eel, dark and long and unhurried, lives somewhere in the packed weed, and you will not see it, and you will not feel it pass, but you will know its route by the one channel through the growth where the stems lean a fraction the same way, a path worn by a body that has taken it many times. It has been in this room since before the room existed. That is not a puzzle; it is simply the order of things down here. And on the threshold stone, in the groove worn by vanished feet, there is a single snail no larger than a fingernail, working its way along the groove in the direction the feet went. It has been on the stone since the water came in. It has moved perhaps the width of your thumb. It will arrive.

Nothing here hurries. The water rose and stopped and holds. The weed grows by a hair's breadth in the dark and does not compete, because there is no space left to compete for; it thickens instead, each stem taking on a little more of itself. The edges of the fracture are as sharp as the day they broke and will be sharp for a long time yet, since nothing washes across them. There is a cycle, if you stay long enough to feel it — a slow cooling and a slow warming that is not the seasons, or not the seasons of the field above, but something with a longer period, a breath drawn in over a year and let out over the next. The moss knows it. The eel knows it. At the low point of the cycle, the water near the threshold stone tastes very faintly of dust, as if the stone were remembering the dry place it came from and leaking that memory into the room. At the high point, the roots in the ceiling let go a fine drift of something pale that sinks through the weed and never reaches the floor.

It is a small place. You could walk around it in the field above in fourteen steps and never know. But it is entirely occupied. Every part of it is already taken by something that is growing, or waiting, or settling, or arriving in the wrong order, and there is no room in it for one more thing, and it makes room anyway, the way a full dark room makes room for a person who steps in and closes the door behind them and stands still.
