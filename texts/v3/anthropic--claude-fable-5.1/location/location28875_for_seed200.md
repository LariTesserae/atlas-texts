The basin has no name because nothing in it has ever needed to refer to anything outside itself.

Start with the ground, since the ground is the strangest part and the hardest to see. It is black rock, volcanic, poured out in sheets and coils and cushions and then cooled — and that is the whole of its history. It cooled, and then nothing further happened to it. The ropes of lava still hold the exact twist they took in the last minute they could move. The pressure ridges still have edges that would cut a hand. Where the crust arched and buckled it has stayed arched and buckled, hollow underneath, ringing faintly if anything heavy crosses it. There has never been a frost here to split it. There has never been a river to grind it. There has never been a dry season to shrink it or a wind hard enough to sand it. It is bedrock in the condition of having just been made, and it has been in that condition for as long as anything has been alive on top of it, which is a very long time.

So you have a landscape that is geologically an infant and biologically ancient, and the two states lie directly against each other with no soil between them. The mat of life sits straight on the raw stone. Roots grip the glassy folds. Moss fills the vesicles. In places the vegetation is so deep you'd swear there must be earth under it, and there isn't — dig down and you go through wet fiber, dead leaf, living leaf, the pale bodies of things that eat dead leaf, and then your fingers hit black glass and stop.

The basin is broad — a day's walk across, if walking were possible, which it mostly is not — and it is low, lying well below whatever land surrounds it, though you never see that land. The rim is lost in the ceiling.

The ceiling is cloud. It is always cloud. It hangs perhaps two hundred feet up, uniform, the color of wet slate lit faintly from within, and it never breaks and never thickens and never rises and never comes down. The light that filters through it is the light of the last quarter-hour before nightfall, and that is the only light the basin has ever known. There is no noon here. There is no morning. If there is a sun above the cloud it has never announced itself, and the animals below have no word for it and no rhythm keyed to it. The dimness is total in its evenness: no shadows sharp enough to be shadows, only a deepening of green toward the ground and a deepening of black beneath the leaves.

The heat comes from below. The rock is warm. Not hot to the touch, not dangerous — warm the way a body is warm, warm the way a hearthstone is warm long after the fire. It has been warm forever and it does not fluctuate. Put your palm flat on the bare basalt anywhere in the basin, at any hour, and it is exactly as warm as it was the last time and the time before. The air above it is thick and heavy and sits at the temperature of blood. Nothing cools. There is no night to cool it. There is no wind. There is no season.

And there is water everywhere, but there are no streams.

This is the thing you notice second, after the ground. The whole basin is wet — the leaves drip, the rock weeps, standing water lies in every pit and hollow and in long shallow sheets across the flatter flows, an inch deep, two inches, warm as a bath — and none of it goes anywhere. Water has never carved a channel because the rock has never been soft enough to carve, and because the water has no gradient to follow, and because it simply does not need to leave. It rises out of the warm ground as vapor. It condenses against the underside of the cloud. It falls again, not as rain exactly but as a perpetual heavy settling, a fog so dense it beads and runs. Every surface is a surface of condensation. A leaf held level fills like a cup within a minute. What falls is exactly what rose. The basin breathes its own water in and out and has never once lost a drop or gained one.

So: a bowl of black unweathered glass, a lid of unchanging cloud, warm from beneath, saturated, dim, and airless. Into this, life.

The vegetation is not tall — nothing has ever needed to compete for a sun that isn't there — but it is deep and it is unbroken. It grows in strata. Lowest, filling the cracks and cushions of the lava, a red-brown moss so thick and wet it behaves like flesh. Above that, ferns and things like ferns, broad and dark and coated on both faces with a film of standing water. Above that, a canopy of soft-wooded trees no more than thirty feet high with leaves the size of doors, black-green, drooping, each one a gutter running steadily into the one below. Everything is coated in something else. Every trunk carries moss, and the moss carries liverworts, and the liverworts carry a fine grey fur of fungus, and the fungus carries a frost of tiny white things that move. There is no bare surface in the basin except the rock, and the rock is bare only in places, and only because it is too smooth and too new for anything to have found a purchase.

There is no wind, so nothing sways. The leaves hang exactly where they hang. The only movement in the vegetation is the movement of water down it and the movement of animals through it.

The animals are the basin's real substance. There are so many that "many" stops being a useful word and you have to think in terms of proportion: perhaps a third of the volume of the air, from the ground to the canopy, is occupied by something breathing. They are small, mostly — the largest things here are no bigger than a dog, sleek and short-limbed and dark and damp, moving low through the fern layer in a constant slow traffic — and their smallness is what allows the density. Frogs, or what fill the role of frogs, in every pocket of standing water, sometimes packed shoulder to shoulder until the water is more frog than water. Salamander-things on every wet trunk, rows of them, heads all the same way, pale bellies pressed to the moss. Insects in clouds that do not disperse because there is no wind to disperse them; they hang in the air as a fixed haze and you walk through them as through a curtain. Soft-bodied things the color of the rock, clinging to it. Things with too many legs on the undersides of leaves, so thickly that the undersides are a different color from the tops. Small warm-blooded things in the canopy, round-eyed for the dimness, calling.

Everything is calling. This is what the sound is.

It is loud — genuinely loud, loud enough that a person standing in the basin would raise their voice to be heard at arm's length — and it is absolutely constant. Not a chorus that swells and falls but a single sustained roar built of a hundred thousand small sustained sounds, each one of them steady, none of them ever stopping to listen for an answer. Nothing here has ever had a reason to be quiet. There is no dawn to wake to and no dusk to fall silent for. There has never been a hush. The frogs have called without interruption since there were frogs. The insects have droned since there were insects. If you stood in one place long enough your ears would stop registering it as sound at all and begin to register it as a kind of pressure, like heat, like the damp — a property of the place rather than an event within it.

And the animals do not startle. This is the small strangeness of the basin, the thing that would unsettle a visitor more than the raw rock or the missing sun. Nothing here has ever encountered a change and so nothing knows what one looks like. The salamanders do not move when you approach. The frogs go on calling under your hand. The small dark quadrupeds in the fern layer part around your legs the way water parts and close again behind you and do not look up. They are not tame. Tameness is a relationship with a threat that has been resolved. These animals have simply never developed the machinery of alarm, because alarm is a response to the world being about to become different, and the world here has never once been about to become anything.

They are born and they die, of course. Something must eat something; the dark low-slung things eat the frogs, and the frogs eat the insects, and the insects eat the moss and each other and the dead. But it happens without event. There are no breeding seasons because there are no seasons; the young are always present, in every size, at every moment, in the same fixed proportion. There is no migration because there is nowhere to go and nothing to go for. There is no abundance in one place and scarcity in another; the density is the same in the center of the basin as at whatever edge it may have. Every population is exactly the size it can be and has always been that size. Death here is not a change in the system but a part of its steadiness, like the water falling to replace the water that rose.

You could stand at the center of it — on one of the low tumuli where the crust domed and never fell, up to your knees in warm moss, dripping, deafened, eye-level with the great hanging leaves and their cargo of small wet lives — and look outward through the dim green in every direction, and understand that what you were seeing extended for miles and was the same for miles: the same black glass beneath, the same slate lid above, the same soaked layered life between, the same roar, the same warmth. And then you could stand there for a year, and it would be exactly the same. Nothing would have grown taller. Nothing would have died that was not replaced. The cloud would not have thinned. The rock would not have taken a single grain's worth of wear. The same frogs, or their indistinguishable children, would be in the same pool at your feet, calling the same note.

The basin is not waiting for anything. That is the last thing to understand about it. It is not a landscape paused before its history; it is a landscape whose history was completed in the moment the lava stopped moving, and everything that has happened since — all the moss and fern and calling, all the crowded warm short lives — has been not a continuation of that history but its permanent, humming, waterlogged conclusion.
