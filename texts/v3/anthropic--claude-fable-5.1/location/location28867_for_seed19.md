**The Table**

From below it looks like a cloud that has forgotten to drift. A grey flatness where the mountain should have a summit, hanging above the valleys at a height where the eye expects ice and finds instead a line, drawn straight, as if someone had planed the top of the world off with a single stroke and then never come back to finish.

The climb takes longer than it should. This is the first rule and it is a mild one. The slopes below the table are ordinary — scree, then bare rock, then rock so old it has lost its grain and become a kind of dull, uniform stuff that is neither granite nor sandstone nor anything with a name, just the mineral remainder of a mountain that has been mountain for so long it has stopped bothering to be any particular kind. Nothing grows on the approach. Nothing has fallen recently. The scree does not shift underfoot; each stone has found the place it will occupy forever and has settled into it with the finality of a tooth into a jaw.

The air thins. The cold arrives, but it is a dry cold, a cold without edge, the cold of a cellar rather than a winter. There is no snow. There has never been snow here, or there was snow so long ago that the memory of it has been erased from the rock along with everything else. What looks like frost on the upper ledges is a white efflorescence of salts, a bloom of minerals sweating out of stone over ages, forming crusts that crackle faintly if touched and then, when the hand is withdrawn, are found not to have crackled at all.

Then the last ledge, and the table.

---

It is level. Not level the way a plain is level, with its swells and drainages and the long tilt toward some distant sea. Level the way a surface is level when everything that could be removed from it has been removed. There is no soil. There is no sand. There is only the grey stuff, worn to a matte finish, extending outward under a pale sky to a horizon that is too far away for the eye to fix and too near for the eye to accept — the table is a day across, and it is also a country, and both of these are true in the way that things up here are true, which is to say simultaneously and without apology.

The light is even. The sun stands somewhere above and slightly behind, at an hour that might be mid-morning or mid-afternoon and does not resolve into either. Shadows fall. They fall in a consistent direction. But if one walks a long way and looks back, the shadows fall in the same direction relative to the walker, not to the sun, and the sun has not moved.

And the table is not empty.

At first they look like boulders. That is what the mind offers, because that is what belongs on a high flat waste: erratics, tors, the bones of the mountain surfacing. Grey rounded shapes, thousands of them, tens of thousands, spaced closely, near-touching, receding in ranks across the whole visible surface until they merge with the horizon into a single textured band.

Then one of them is a deer.

Not a carving. Not a stone that resembles a deer. A deer — a doe, coat the same grey as the rock, standing with her weight on three legs and the fourth slightly lifted, ears forward, the wet black of her eye catching the light. She is breathing. The breath is very slow. Between the rise of her flank and the fall of it, a person could count to sixty, then to a hundred, and lose the count.

And beside her a boar. And beside the boar, pressed flank to flank so that their hides make one continuous grey surface, an antelope of a kind that does not exist in the valleys below, and a bear, and a hare sitting upright in the space between the bear's forepaws, and a horse, and something long-necked and heavy-shouldered whose name has been lost with the ages that produced it. Birds stand on the ground among them, wings folded, and birds stand on the backs of the larger animals, and none of them fly. Lizards on the flat stone between hooves. A wolf, mouth slightly open, tongue visible, not panting. Cattle. Foxes. Reptiles that are not lizards. Insects — the ground, where it can be seen at all between the bodies, is patterned with beetles and moths at rest, and they too are grey, and they too do not move.

Every animal that has ever lived, one thinks, and then corrects: every animal that has ever come up here. And then corrects again, because the second thought is no more supportable than the first. They did not come up. There is no track. The scree below is undisturbed. They are simply here, and have always been here, and the grey of their hides is the grey of the rock, and the salt-bloom that whitens the ledges also whitens the ridges of their spines and the crowns of their skulls, and on the broad backs of the largest of them there are lichens.

The lichens are the only vegetation. Rosettes of ochre and rust and bone-white, flat against the hides as though painted there, edges perfectly defined. Lichen is slow — it grows in centuries — but these are not slow. These are finished. They have reached the size they will be and stopped. Peer closely at one and it is possible to see, within its crust, the individual hairs of the animal beneath, each hair grown through the lichen body and exiting on the far side, so that the two are woven, so that it is not clear which is host and which is guest, or whether the distinction has any meaning at a place where nothing is ever going to change.

---

To cross the table one must pass among them, and there is no path, and so one passes through.

This is the second rule. The animals are dense enough that a body cannot fit between them, and yet a body does. One steps toward the gap between a horse and an aurochs, a gap perhaps the width of a hand, and one is on the other side of it. There is no sensation of contact. Looking back, the horse and the aurochs stand where they stood, gap between them, nothing displaced. Either one has passed through their bodies, or one has passed through some fold in the table that the bodies happen to occupy, or one did not pass at all and is simply now elsewhere. All three explanations are available. None is preferred.

Walk far enough among them and the same animals recur. A stag with one antler broken off at the second tine. A lynx with a pale scar across the muzzle. They stand in different places, at different distances, in different company, and they are not similar animals but the same animal, the broken antler broken in exactly the same way, the scar with the same small hook at its end. Whether there is one stag standing in many places, or many places standing around one stag, cannot be determined and does not appear to matter to the stag.

Their eyes are open. They see. That, at least, seems certain — turn suddenly and the eye of the nearest animal is on you, and it was on you before you turned. But they do not follow. When you move, their gaze does not move; it is simply already where you have arrived. There is no menace in this. There is no interest in it either. It is the attention of things that have nothing else to do and infinite time to do it in, and it is, in its way, restful, once the first hour of it has passed.

---

The sound is breathing. That is the whole of it.

Not any one animal — the individual breaths are far too slow and too soft to hear. But tens of thousands of them, spread across a country, breathing at their separate glacial rates, sum to a single low hush that lies over the table like a second sky. It has no rhythm. It does not rise or fall. It is the sound the ocean might make if the ocean were a fixed thing, a sound that has reached its final value. Within it, occasionally, there is a click — a hoof settling against stone, or a beak, or the joint of a wing — and one looks toward it, and nothing has moved, and one understands that the click has already happened, has always already happened, is a sound that exists in the table rather than in time, and is simply heard whenever a listener passes near enough.

Beneath the hush, one's own pulse. The thin air makes it loud. It is the only thing up here that is not in equilibrium, and it feels rude.

---

There are marks.

Here and there, in the narrow floors between animals, a circle of stones, knee-high, laid without mortar. A windbreak, or a hearth, or a boundary drawn around a sleeping place. No ash inside. The stones are the same grey as everything and have not been moved since they were set down, and the animals stand around them, and sometimes through them, and one circle has a small deer standing in its exact centre, as though it had been built around her, or she had been placed within it.

A knapping floor: a scatter of flakes, grey on grey, a few finished points among them, the worked edges so sharp they must have been struck yesterday and so unweathered they must have lain here forever. A core of the mountain-stuff, half-reduced, set carefully on a flat stone beside a bone awl and a scraper of horn. Someone stopped mid-task. Someone set the tools down in order and did not pick them up.

A cairn of horns. Ibex, aurochs, ram, twisted together into a stack as tall as a person, tips outward. It is the only thing on the table that has a direction — it faces the way the shadows fall, and, like the shadows, it faces that way from every side.

A hide, staked out flat to cure, in a dry wind that does not blow.

And once, at a distance that may have been an hour's walk or several days', a figure. Upright, wrapped in skins, standing among the animals as they stand, weight on one hip, a spear-shaft grounded beside the foot. Grey. Salt on the shoulders. A rosette of lichen on the back of one hand. It is not possible to get nearer. Every approach delivers one to the same distance, as though the figure were a point on the horizon that has been placed, out of some courtesy, slightly closer than the horizon itself. Its eyes are open. It is breathing. Between the rise of its chest and the fall, one could count to a hundred, and lose the count, and begin again.

---

Nothing has happened here. This is the last rule and the one that contains the others.

The mountain wore down to the table over a span of time in which everything else in the world was made and unmade several times over, and then it stopped wearing, because there was nothing left in it that could be worn. The salt bloomed and finished blooming. The lichens grew and finished growing. The animals — whether they climbed, or grew from the rock, or are the rock, or are what the rock became when it ran out of ways to be rock — took their positions and finished taking them. The people came, and built small things, and laid down their tools, and either left by a way that left no track or did not leave.

And the whole of it settled, the way water finds level, into this: a grey height in a pale unmoving light, crowded to the horizon with living things at rest, silent but for the sum of their breath, where the distances do not agree with one another and the same stag stands in a hundred places and the shadows fall toward a horn-cairn from every side — and where none of that is strange, because strangeness requires a norm to depart from, and the norm departed long ago, and the table has been exactly this ever since, and will be exactly this, and is.

One can stand among them for as long as one likes. That is what the table is for. It will not notice the difference.
