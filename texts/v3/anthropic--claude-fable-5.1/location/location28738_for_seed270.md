You come to it across a plain that has finished. There is nothing left for the wind to take from this country; it took everything a long time ago and now moves over the surface without friction, and the surface is a pale grey-buff, cracked into plates, dusted with a fine gypsum flour that does not blow so much as settle a little further every year. It is cool. The sun is up and ordinary and gives no warmth to speak of. Nothing grows. There is no horizon to speak of either, only a slow greying where the ground and the sky agree to stop.

You hear it before there is anything to see. A sound like a mill running with a broken bearing — a roar with a grind inside it, and inside the grind a thousand smaller frictions, ticks, squeals, something like the hiss of wet steam though there is no water within a day's walk. It comes from below the plain. You walk toward it for longer than seems reasonable and then the ground simply isn't there in front of you: a lip, and a drop of a few meters, and a dish in the earth perhaps eighty paces across, sunk so cleanly into the flat that you could pass a stone's throw to either side and never know.

The basin is full.

---

Full is the word. The floor of the dish is not visible. What you see instead is a surface of bodies — a heaving grey-white pelt of birds packed so tightly that where one lifts a wing the neighbour's flank shows through the gap and then closes over it again. They are about the size of a gull, narrower, with a black cap and a bill the colour of old brass. They stand shoulder to shoulder on their nests, and their nests are only shallow scrapes in the crust, so near to each other that a bird settling onto its own eggs will rest its tail on the head of the bird behind. Every one of them is calling. The sound in the basin is not a chorus, it is a pressure; it has the flat continuous quality of machinery because nothing in it ever stops long enough to be a single voice.

Look longer and the surface resolves into strata. The birds are the top layer. Beneath them, between their feet and under the eaves of their bodies, there is a second, darker traffic — beetles the size of a thumbnail, hard-backed, iridescent in a dull way, moving in currents through the shade the colony casts on itself. Beneath the beetles there is a third layer of small things you only see by their effect, a general glittering shift in the grain of the ground. And working the edges of the colony, along the walls of the dish where the birds thin out, are lizards, dozens of them, flat and quick and the same colour as the salt, waiting for anything that falls.

Nothing here eats anything that grows. The colony imports everything. Twice a day, at no signal you can detect, a portion of the birds — a third, a half — rises, and the sound changes pitch as the roar of the colony is joined for a moment by the deeper thunder of wings, and they pour out over the lip in a long grey vein and go somewhere. They are gone for hours. Wherever they go there is water, and food; you will not see it. What comes back is the birds, and what they carry inside them, and what they give up in the basin — fish and brine-fly and something else, half-digested, passed to the young or dropped or simply voided — and this is the whole economy of the place. The beetles live on it. The lizards live on the beetles and on chicks that stray. The mites live on the lizards and the birds and the beetles alike. Spiders string webs across the intervals of iron high enough to be out of reach of feet, and take the flies that come to the guano. Everything else is salt.

---

The salt is where the age is.

The basin is not a lakebed, or it stopped being a lakebed so long ago the distinction is moot. What the colony stands on is a rock of its own making: guano and feather and shell and salt, deposited and pressed and cemented by thin brines drawn up from somewhere far below and evaporated in the cool air, season upon season, until the floor of the dish is a laminated stone that rings faintly when a lizard runs across bare patches of it. Where the wall of the basin has been undercut by the birds' scraping you can see the layers — pale and less pale, a millimeter or two each, thousands of them, descending into a bluish crystalline dark. It is the same stone all the way down. There has never been anything else here. The birds have been in this hole, in these numbers, making this noise, since before the plain around them finished flattening; the colony is older than the erosion.

Which is why the machinery is strange, and why it isn't.

There is iron in the basin. Not much. Along the northeast wall, half buried in the pressed guano, stands the frame of an evaporating pan — a low rectangular trough of riveted plates, perhaps six meters long, its corners rounded off by decades of birds landing on them. The iron has not rusted so much as bloomed; it is covered in a crust of white-and-orange salt that has grown outward from the metal in bladed crystals, some as long as a hand, and the birds nest among the blades as if they had been put there for the purpose. Beside the pan is a fly-wheel, taller than you, on a shaft set in a stone plinth. Beyond it, mostly under the colony, the humped shapes of something that was once an engine: a cylinder, a housing, a length of pipe descending into the floor where someone once drove a bore for the brine. A few meters of narrow rail lead off from the pan toward the wall of the dish and stop, buried. A winch drum. A skip, upended. Cables gone to green thread.

Someone came here to make salt, and found the salt was already made, and the making of it was not theirs.

The fly-wheel turns. Not fast — a revolution every twenty or thirty seconds — and not by any force you can find. There is no belt on it. Some part of the engine under the colony is still doing something; you can feel it in the soles of your feet at the lip, a slow knock, one and one and one, much slower than the birds' din and buried under it, and each knock coincides with a hitch in the wheel's turning. Birds stand on the wheel. As it comes round they step from spoke to spoke and stay level, without looking, the way birds on a swaying wire never look. There is a fine white powder on the rim that is worn smooth where their feet fall and crusted where they do not. Whatever drives it does so without heat and without smoke, and has done so for long enough that the colony has stopped noticing.

---

Weather here is the colony. A cloud passes over and the whole dish dims and quiets for a moment, and in that moment you hear the beetles, a dry seething like sand poured slowly. The cloud goes and the noise comes back up as if a valve were opened. In the middle of the morning a hawk-shaped shadow moves along the far wall — you never see the hawk — and a wave goes through the birds, a rising and settling that travels around the basin in a ring, forty thousand throats climbing a third and coming back down. Then the ordinary roar. A lizard takes a chick from the fringe and is instantly under nine birds and does not come out. Dust from the plain sifts over the lip in a continuous thin fall and is trodden into the stone within the hour. The birds preen, and a snow of grey down drifts across the dish and gathers in the corners of the evaporating pan where the beetles work it back into the ground.

There are rules. The birds do not nest on the rail. They nest against it, pressed close, and between the two rails there runs a clear channel the width of a hand where the beetles move in one direction in the morning and the other in the afternoon, and nothing else uses it. The lizards do not enter the colony proper; they hunt the margin, the first two meters of wall, and a lizard that goes further in is not seen again. The birds that return from the flights land first on the fly-wheel and the pan and the winch drum, always, and stand there a while before dropping into the mass — the iron is the antechamber. No bird lands on the plain above the lip. The plain is not part of the place. You can stand on it and be near enough to be spattered and they will treat you as sky.

At the bottom of the basin, if there is a bottom, it is very cold. You can feel it coming up the wall at the undercut, a breath of something that has never been in the sun. The bore the salt-makers drove goes down into that, and the brine that cements the strata comes up from it, and the knock of the engine goes down into it and does not come back.

---

By evening the second flight has returned and the whole colony is present, and the density is almost unbearable to look at: every square hand of the dish a bird, every bird a voice, every voice going. The light does nothing special. It just goes, ordinary and grey, and the birds go on. Their noise comes up out of the ground into the flatness of the plain and moves out across it, and because there is nothing on the plain to stop it or return it, at fifty paces it is a roar and at five hundred it is a hum and at a thousand it is the same silence as everywhere, as if the hole had never been dug and never been found and the fly-wheel had never once come round, slow, with a bird on it, stepping from spoke to spoke and not looking down.
