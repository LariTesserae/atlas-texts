Start with the cold, because that is what reaches you first: not the sharp cold of open winter but a settled, mineral cold, the temperature of iron that has never once been warm. It comes up through the soles of the feet. It comes out of the walls. There is no wind to carry it, so it simply stands in the room like a fourth wall.

Then the dark clears a little, or the eyes give up expecting more, and you see that it is a room. A small one. You could cross it in eleven or twelve steps if the floor were empty, and the floor is not empty. The ceiling is low enough that you keep your head slightly bowed without deciding to. There is no door. There is no obvious reason the room should have a door, since nothing in it appears to have been brought here from anywhere else.

The walls are poured. Not stone—poured, in lifts, each lift about the height of a forearm, and the seams between them are still soft at the edges, the way concrete is soft in its first days. Except it is not concrete. It is greyer, denser, veined with something that catches the little light and holds it a moment too long. Where a lift has not fully cured, the surface is beaded with moisture, and when a bead grows heavy enough it does not run down the wall so much as sink into it, leaving a small dimple that slowly closes. The walls are drinking. That is the first sound you can name: a faint, distributed sipping, very quiet, everywhere.

The light is from the machines, and the machines are the room.

They stand nearly touching—a row of upright cylinders along one wall, a crouched thing of geared wheels in the corner, a bank of shallow trays stacked on rails, ductwork that leaves one housing and enters another without ever going anywhere else. Cast iron, mostly, rough from the mould, with fine ridges where the sand grain printed itself into the metal. Some copper, gone the green-black of copper in wet air. Glass here and there, small panes set into housings, and behind the glass a filament burns at the dull orange of a coal that has decided not to go out but has no interest in flaring. That is all the light there is. It is enough to see the machines by and not quite enough to see the far wall by, so the far wall exists mostly as a place where the light fails.

The machines are working. This is the strange part, and it takes time to understand what they are working on.

The row of cylinders is a press. It takes something grey and wet from a hopper in the ceiling—a slurry that arrives without pipes, simply filling the hopper from above as a tide fills a pool—and it packs the slurry into a mould, and holds it, and the holding is most of the work. A ratchet advances once every few minutes. The click is precise and small, like a clock that has been asked to keep a much slower kind of time. When the press opens, what comes out is not a brick but a slab shaped to fit the wall, and it fits because the wall is made of exactly these. An armature extends from the press—slow, without hurry, brass sliding in an iron sleeve—and sets the new slab against the top of the highest lift, and there is a soft compression, and the seam begins to weep, and the ceiling is now the thickness of one slab further away than it was.

The room is building itself upward. The ceiling is not a ceiling. It is the current top of the wall, and above it is the hopper, and above the hopper is whatever the slurry falls from, and that is not visible and may not be a place. When you look up, past the hopper, there is a darkness that is not the darkness of a shaft but the darkness of a thing not yet decided. The room is being poured into it.

So everything here is made, and everything here is early. The floor has the faint tackiness of a surface still curing. The pipes have never carried anything long enough to scale. There are no worn places, no polished handrails, no path across the floor where feet have gone. There is no history in this room because the room has not finished happening.

The water is the exception, or the water is how the outside gets in. It condenses. The machines are cold and the air is fractionally less cold, and so every iron surface sweats. It gathers on the undersides of the ducts and lets go in slow ticks onto the trays below—the trays are shallow and long and stacked five high on their rails, and each is a little dish of standing water skinned with a film that shows the filament light as a bruise of orange. The trays slide. Every so often a rack of them advances one place along the rails, the way a photograph advances in a machine, and the bottom tray tips and pours into a gutter set in the floor, and the gutter carries the water to the base of the wall, and the wall drinks it. That is the second process. Or it is the same process seen from another end.

And in the trays, in the ones that have stood longest without tipping, there is the vegetation. It is not much. A green so dark it reads as black until the light catches it: a moss, or a thing that has arrived at the shape of a moss by a different road. It grows only on the tray lips where the water touches the iron, a fringe no wider than a fingernail. It grows in a place with no soil, no sun, no seed that could have come from anywhere, and it grows anyway, and the machine has made no accommodation for it. When a tray tips, the moss goes into the gutter with the water and into the wall, and a slab poured after that is faintly, invisibly greener in its veins. Whether this is a mistake or the point, there is no one here to ask.

There are no animals. It is not the absence of a place where animals have died or left. It is the absence of a place that has not got round to them yet—the way a house under construction has no smell of cooking. The room is quiet in the specific way of a place built for a purpose that has not started. Only the ratchet, and the ticks of water, and the sipping walls, and beneath those an engine somewhere in the corner housing turning over at a speed too slow to call a hum. If you put your palm flat on any surface you feel it: a pulse of about one beat per breath, through the iron, through the floor, through the cold itself.

Stand here long enough and the room grows around you. Not fast. A slab an hour, perhaps. The ceiling recedes by degrees and the hopper rises with it and the darkness above the hopper is always the same distance off, and the light from the filaments has to travel a little further each time to fail at the far wall. Eventually—this is the rule, though nothing states it—the room will be tall enough to be a shaft, and then a tower turned inward, and the slurry will thin and the press will slow and the last slab will be set and the machines, having made the room, will find themselves at the bottom of it in the dark, sweating, with a fringe of something green on the lips of the trays, waiting for whatever a finished room is for.

For now it is only this: a pocket of cold in the ground, and in it, patiently, a place being made by the things that will have to live there.
