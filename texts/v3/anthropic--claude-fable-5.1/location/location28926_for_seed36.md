You come to it from above, which is the only way to come to it. The plain is ordinary — dry, pale, a little wind — and then the ground simply stops, and there is the lip.

The lip is rock, but not rock as you know it. It has no cracks. There is no dust on it, no lichen, no line where rain has run. The edge is rounded like something poured and cooled a minute ago, and it is warm against the palm, not sun-warm but warm from underneath. Looking down the face of it, you can see the bands where it settled: faint curves, unweathered, like the growth rings of something that has never been cut. Nothing has happened to this stone yet. It is finished being made and has not begun to be unmade.

Below the lip the basin opens.

It is wide — you cannot see the far wall clearly, only a pale blur where it rises through the heat-shiver — and it is full. Not full of things; full. A single green surface, level as a lake, running from the base of the near cliff to the edge of sight. From above it looks like a meadow, then like a canopy, then like neither, because there is no undergrowth beneath it and no soil beneath the undergrowth, and after a while you understand that the green is not on the floor of the basin. The green is the floor of the basin.

You climb down. There are ways down, and they are one of the first strange things: not paths worn by feet, but shallow scoops in the new stone, evenly spaced, as if someone pressed their thumbs into it while it was still soft. They go down the face in a slight spiral. They are the only marks on the rock anywhere.

---

At the bottom the plant takes you.

It takes your weight, first. What you stand on is a dense mat of stems, each about the thickness of a wrist, lying against one another so closely that your foot never finds a gap. They are not stiff. They give a little, and then they hold, and there is a sense of depth beneath — of stem under stem under stem, the same all the way down, so that the basin is not a hollow with plants in it but a hollow the plants have filled. If there is stone under here, no one has reached it. Push your arm down between the stems and it goes to the shoulder and touches only more stem, warmer the deeper you go.

The stems are a colour you would call green at a distance. Up close they are many colours: a waxy jade on the upper surfaces, going to bronze and then to a deep aubergine on the sides that face away from the sky. They are not leaves, exactly. Each stem flattens along its upper length into a broad, shallow trough, and the troughs are what you see from above — thousands of them, millions, interlocking in a pattern that is not quite regular, each one tilted slightly toward the sun. Where two troughs meet there is a seam, and the seams hold water.

That is where the water is. The air is dry and the sky is empty, but every seam between every pair of stems has in it a bead, a thread, a finger's-depth of clear water that never seems to evaporate, though the heat says it should. Bend down and the surface of it is perfectly still. Bend closer and you can see, magnified through it, the skin of the stem below — fine cells, a tracery — and no sediment, no algae, no drowned insect. New water. Water that has never had anything happen to it either.

Here and there the seams join into something bigger. A channel, knee-deep, of the same clear stillness, running for a hundred paces and then closing again. In one place a pool, wide as a room, which you have to walk around. Its edges are stem, its floor is stem, and it is exactly as warm as your blood.

---

The light is the second strange thing, and it takes you longer to notice because it is so total. Bright here is not the brightness of a clear day. The sun is overhead, yes, but the walls of the basin are pale — that poured, uncracked stone is nearly white — and they throw the light back down and across, so that nothing casts a proper shadow. Look under a tilted stem and the underside is lit. Look into a seam and the water is lit from below. There is no dim corner in the whole basin. You squint, and then your eyes give up squinting and simply accept it, the way they accept snow.

And it is hot. Not the shifting heat of a day that will end, but a steady, held heat, coming as much from the plant beneath you as from above. Sit down and the stems warm you through your clothes. The air over the mat quivers, and through the quiver the far features of the basin swim and steady, swim and steady.

---

Those features. Because the mat is not flat, quite. It rises here and there into long, low swells — the height of a person, the length of a village — where the stems have layered thicker, and from the top of one of these you can see how the basin is arranged: the swells running roughly parallel, like ripples in a pond that stopped; the channels following the troughs between them; the pale walls all around; and, scattered without pattern across the whole of it, the structures.

They are small. The nearest is a ring of stones, waist-high, sitting on the mat as if set down there this morning, though the stems have grown up through its centre and over its lower course. The stones are the same white unmarked rock as the walls. They are not mortared. They are simply placed, fitted, each one shaped with a care that has nothing to do with tools — you can find no chisel marks, no facets, only surfaces that look worn smooth by handling, except that nothing here is worn.

Farther on, a low hut. A dome of the same stone, stacked in tightening courses, with a single opening you could crawl through. Inside: stem. The plant has filled it as it has filled everything, level with the sill of the doorway, the troughs inside holding their beads of water in the dimness that is not dim.

Farther still, a line of upright stones, seven of them, leading nowhere. A single squared block on a swell, warm on top. A curved wall, one course high, that encloses nothing.

There is no sign of who placed them. No hearth, no bone, no midden, no scratch on the rock. The thumb-scoops on the cliff and the stones on the mat are the whole of it. They do not look abandoned. Abandoned things have begun to fall; these have not begun anything. They look as if their makers set the last stone, stood back, and were simply not there anymore, and the plant closed over the place where they had stood without disturbing a single course.

---

There is almost nothing alive here that is not the plant.

Once, a beetle, dark and slow, walking the seam between two stems as if along a road. You watch it for a long time. It does not fly. It does not stop to drink. It goes out of sight along the seam and you do not see another. Once, high up, a shadow crosses the mat — a bird, over the basin and gone — and the shadow is faint because the light comes from everywhere. Once, in one of the larger pools, a shape you thought was a fish and then decided was a stem-tip, curling under the water, though you had not seen the stems curl anywhere else.

And that is all. No hum of insects. No rustle in the mat. When you stop walking the basin is so quiet that you hear your own blood. The only sounds it makes for itself are these: a faint, dry creak when a warmed stem shifts its weight against its neighbour; the tick of a water bead falling from a higher trough into a lower one, though where the bead came from you cannot say; and under everything, so low you feel it more than hear it, a slow tone that might be the heat itself moving in the stone.

---

You stay long enough to learn the rules, or the shape of them.

Nothing here has died. Look for a brown stem, a dropped trough, a wilted tip, a fallen anything, and you will not find it. There is no litter, no rot, no smell of decay; the air smells of warm wax and clean water and nothing else. Every stem is full, mature, at the exact size it will be. You find no seedlings either. Nothing is coming up because nothing has come down.

Nothing here has broken. The stone walls have no fissures. The stones of the huts have no chips. The water has no silt.

Nothing here is on its way to being something else. Put your hand on a stem and come back to it — an hour, a day — and the same bead of water sits in the same seam, the same trough tilts at the same angle to a sun that seems in no hurry to move. The heat does not build toward evening. The light does not shift. You begin to feel that you are the only gradient in the place: the only thing that is warmer than it was, or thirstier, or farther along.

It is a landscape caught at the moment of arrival. The rock had just cooled; the plant had just filled the hollow; the last stone had just been set; the water had just beaded in the seam — and then the moment was kept, and everything that would normally have followed did not follow. Not paused, exactly. A paused thing wants to resume. This wants nothing. It is complete in the way a held breath is complete.

When you climb back out, up the thumb-scooped spiral in the warm white rock, and stand on the ordinary plain with the ordinary wind moving the ordinary dust, you look back down at the wide, bright, breathing green and it looks exactly as it did when you first saw it. You have changed by the length of a day. It has not changed by anything.
