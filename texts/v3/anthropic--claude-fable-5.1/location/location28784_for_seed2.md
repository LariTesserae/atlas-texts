The floor is level. That is the first thing, and it takes a while to understand how strange it is, because nothing that big should be level. It runs away in every direction, pale grey under a hand's depth of clear water that becomes a fathom, then thirty, then a hundred, and it does not roll or dip or gather itself into ridges. It is poured. You can see the pours: slabs the size of city blocks, each one meeting the next at a dark seam of sealant that runs off toward the vanishing point as straight as a surveyor's line. In places the formwork left its grain in the surface, the ghost of plywood sheets, and the tie-holes are still open, small square wells every two metres, each one already home to something with legs.

The concrete is young. It has not had time to go dark. The water in it has not yet finished curing, and along the seams a faint white bloom of lime is still weeping out, so the joints look chalked. Someone's yellow paint stencil — a grid reference, a date — is legible on a slab a long way off, magnified and wobbling through the water. Sediment has barely begun. Draw a finger across the floor and it comes up almost clean: a grey film a few grains thick, the beginnings of the beginning of mud. There is no sand, because there is nowhere for sand to come from. Everything that is here was brought here, mixed, poured, set, and then drowned on purpose.

The water is cool, and the cold has a shape. Near the floor it is heavy and still and a few degrees short of comfortable; the light down here is dim green-grey, the light of a swimming pool at dusk. Look up and the column brightens. Somewhere far overhead the surface is a moving ceiling of silver, wrinkled by wind, and the sun comes through in slow, wheeling bars that reach almost the whole way down because there is nothing in the water to stop them — no silt, no plankton bloom yet, hardly any life small enough to make the water milky. It is disconcertingly clear. You can see farther than seems right. You can see the wall.

The wall is the only horizon this place has, and only on the nearest side. It rises out of the floor at a shallow batter, a ramp of interlocking cast blocks — hexagonal, each one the size of a car, laid by machine in a pattern so regular it reads as texture rather than structure — climbing and climbing until it passes out of the water and into a sky you can only infer. Along its face, spaced with an engineer's indifference to the eye, run the intake grates: rectangular mouths of black steel, each bar as thick as a forearm, the whole grille ten storeys tall. Between one grate and the next there is a kilometre of nothing. Between one pump tower and the next, standing out on the floor like lighthouses that forgot to reach the surface, there are thirty.

And that is the emptiness of it. For a place so full of water, so full of sound, there is almost nothing in any given cubic mile. A tower. A cable in a concrete trough. A ladder bolted to a wall for reasons no one down here needs. A long red steel pipe, half a metre in diameter, lying on the floor for ten kilometres before it turns ninety degrees and goes somewhere else. Then water, and water, and the floor going on. The distances are the kind you stop trying to measure. Out beyond the last tower the floor simply fades into blue, not because it ends but because the water, clear as it is, has a limit, and this place does not.

It is loud. The loudness is the second thing, after the level floor, and it never stops. It comes up through the concrete before it comes through the water: a low, continuous, many-toned drone, the summed hum of pumps that are each the size of a church, distributed around a basin so large that their notes arrive out of phase and beat against each other. Underneath that is a deeper thing, more felt than heard, the pressure-thump of gates cycling somewhere over the horizon. And over both, near the grates, the intakes roar. Water pours through them in a solid, muscular sheet; you can see it as a distortion, a shiver in the light, and hear it as a hiss that swells to a howl if you drift too close. When the flow reverses — and it reverses, twice a day, on a schedule that has nothing to do with the moon — the howl drops to a groan, then to a rushing exhalation, and the whole water column leans.

Because this is what the place is for. It is a battery. The sea is out there, above the wall, and the basin is down here, below it, sixty metres below at the floor, and the difference is money. At night, when there is power to spare, the pumps push the water out and up and the level drops and the exposed blocks on the batter steam in the cold air. In the day the gates open and the sea comes back in, spinning the turbines in their halls inside the wall, and the level rises, and the fish know it.

The fish are the third thing. There should not be so many of them, in water this new, over ground this sterile — but the intakes have been sucking sea in for years, and everything small enough to pass the bars has come through with it, and much of it has stayed. Mullet in loose grey shoals graze the lime bloom off the seams, mouthing along the joints as if the whole floor were a single kilometre-long lip. Bass hang in the lee of the towers, heads to the current, waiting for the tide to change and bring them what it always brings. Clouds of sprat, thousands upon thousands, sweep past the grates in the flood and are gone into the blue before they can be counted, and behind them, almost always, something bigger: a shape that turns, a flash of white belly, a pulse of sound like a slapped door. Crabs have found the tie-holes. Anemones — dull orange, a few centimetres across — have found the cathodic anodes on the pipeline, where the metal fizzes faintly with protective current, and they open there in rows, indifferent to the electricity, the first colour on all that grey.

Above, where the water goes silver, there are shadows that fold themselves and drop: cormorants, chasing the sprat down twenty metres and more, trailing bubbles like a thrown handful of coins. Gulls patrol the surface in the thousands, drawn by the churn at the outfalls, where the turbine tailrace brings up everything the intakes killed and the water there is permanently boiling with birds. Seals come in through the gates on the flood — nobody planned for that, nobody can stop it — and haul out on the exposed blocks at low water, barking at the pumps, and come back down when the sea does. At the very edge of vision, sometimes, a great slow grey thing that is either a very large ray or a slab of something that tore loose and is drifting.

What there is very little of is green. A basin this size, this deep, this recently filled, is not yet a garden. On the upper batter, where the light is best and the water leaves the blocks bare for hours a day, a felt of algae has taken, and below it, in a band a few metres wide, threads of weed stream in the current — brown, ragged, tenacious, more colony than meadow. Someone tried eelgrass. There are the remains of the plots, geotextile squares pinned to the floor with steel staples near the shallowest wall, most of them bare, one or two sprouting a thin uncertain fur. Otherwise the floor is floor. A single strand of kelp, torn from somewhere outside and dragged in through a grate, can drift for an hour across the grey without meeting anything else alive and rooted.

Because it is so large, it has its own weather, and the weather comes down. On the surface, far up, wind gets up over forty kilometres of open water and builds swell that is real swell, steep and short, and on the days when it does, the ceiling of silver becomes a ceiling of lead and the light on the floor turns to nothing. Fog forms over the basin in the mornings when the water has been pumped down overnight and the exposed wall is cold, and sits there until the flood comes in. Rain, when it falls, falls on made water. In the winter the whole upper column chills and sinks and turns the basin over, top to bottom, in a single week, and the mullet go deep and the fizzing anodes are the only thing that seems to notice.

And the basin itself moves, all of it, all the time. The intakes on the north wall push, the outfalls on the west pull, and between them a slow gyre has set up, a current you can feel as a lean, that carries a drifting thing in a circuit of the whole basin in something like four days. Sound moves with it. Sediment — such as there is — moves with it, and is already beginning to bank, a millimetre deep, in the eddy behind each tower, the first draft of a landscape that in a thousand years might have shoals and channels and something like a bottom. There are already places, along the seams, where the sealant has begun to shed, and the concrete beneath shows the first grain of wear. Not much. A young place. Everything square, everything legible, everything still stamped with the date it was set.

Then the schedule turns. Somewhere, a gate you cannot see begins to open. The groan comes first, through the floor, and then a shiver in the water, and then the entire column shifts, all of it at once — the mullet lift off the seams and turn as one, the weed on the batter lies down flat, the cormorants come up, the bass at the towers draw in and hold — and the sea comes down the grates in a white roar, cold and clear and full of small silver things that were, a minute ago, somewhere else. The light on the floor brightens as the surface flattens. The level begins to rise. And out across the poured grey plain, tower after tower, grate after grate, over the horizon and past it, the same thing is happening everywhere, exactly on time.
