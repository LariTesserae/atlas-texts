The first thing is the sound, and it is wrong for the size of the place.

You come up onto the shelf through a notch in the rock, and the notch is narrow enough that you have to turn your shoulders, and then it opens and you are standing in a room. That is the only word for it. A bowl of stone perhaps forty paces across, walls rising on three sides higher than you can reach, the fourth side open to a drop and to air that has cloud moving through it. A room without a roof. And filling the room, wall to wall, is a roar that ought to belong to a river in flood.

There is no river. There is not even a stream. What there is, everywhere, is seep.

---

The walls weep. Not dramatically — no cascade, no white water — but the rock is dark with wet from top to bottom and it has been dark with wet for so long that the wetness has become a kind of skin. Water comes out of the stone in beads and the beads join and run in threads, and the threads are so numerous that the whole face is quietly moving all the time, like the surface of a thing that is breathing very slowly. Where the water runs it has laid down a rind: pale mineral crusts in the shape of dripping, tongues and curtains and little shelves, and on the mineral grows moss, and on the moss grows finer moss, and from the finer moss hang the liverworts in their flat green scales like the shed feathers of something. Everything the water touches is green. Everything the water does not touch is also green, because the air itself is wet enough to grow on.

The floor of the bowl is a mattress. You do not walk on it so much as sink into it, a hand's depth, sometimes more, into sphagnum and cushion moss and a gold-brown sedge that grows in single blades rather than clumps, each blade alone. When you lift your foot the hollow fills with water and then, slowly — over the time it takes to breathe three or four times — the moss stands back up and closes and there is no sign you were there.

That is how it takes everything. There is a fallen branch near the eastern wall, thick as a thigh, and it has been there long enough that it is now a ridge of moss in the shape of a branch, and if you press it it gives under your thumb, and if you pressed harder your thumb would go through into a brown pulp that is more water than wood.

---

It is warm. That is the odd thing for how high it is — the cloud says high, the thinness of the air says high, the way your ears feel says high — but the stone holds a heat that comes from somewhere below and gives it back all day and all night, so that the seep-water is warm on the fingers, blood-warm or a little under, and the air in the bowl is the air of a bathhouse, dense and soft and smelling of wet mineral and of the particular sweetness that moss makes when there is a great deal of it. Steam does not rise exactly, but the cloud that drifts in over the lip lingers here longer than it should, as though reluctant, and thickens, and then lets go and moves on.

The light comes down white and even through that ceiling of moving cloud. No shadows to speak of. Every green thing lit from every side at once, so that the mosses show all their colours — the yellow-greens and the black-greens and the greens that are nearly blue — without any of them being brighter than another.

---

And the roar.

You look for it. You would have to. Something that loud must be *somewhere*, must be a fall behind a wall, must be a torrent under the floor. You put your ear to the moss and the moss is loud. You put your hand flat on the weeping stone and the stone is loud — you can feel it in your palm, a tremor too fine to be a tremor, more like the surface of a struck bell a long time after the striking.

The sound is the seep. That is the first understanding. Ten thousand threads of water each making its own small noise against the rock, and the rock a bowl, and the bowl shaped over an age of this so that it gathers every one of those small noises and folds them together and hands them back. The whole room is a mouth saying one continuous note, and the note is made of water falling a finger's height ten thousand times a second.

The second understanding comes later, after you have been standing in it long enough that it stops being noise and starts being a place. It is that the sound has a *shape*. Somewhere in the bowl there is a point where it is loudest, and it is not where you would expect — not at the base of the wettest wall but out in the open, near the middle, where the moss floor is a little lower and a little darker and where, if you kneel and part the sphagnum with both hands, you find not more moss but a hole.

A throat in the rock. Not wide — you could not put your head into it — but going down, and dark, and the air coming out of it is warmer than the air around, and it is from this that the sound is truly coming, or it is into this that the sound is truly going; it is hard to be certain which. Every thread of water on every wall runs down and goes under the moss and finds its way here and drops into the dark, and the dark is shaped for sound the way a shell is shaped for it. The throat has been swallowing this water for longer than there have been mosses on the walls. The roar is the sound of that swallowing, and the bowl is the sound of the roar, and the two of them together have made this room what it is.

Standing over it the sound is so complete that you cannot hear yourself speak. You can watch your own mouth move, feel the voice in your chest, and it goes nowhere. The place is saying something so steadily and at such volume that no other saying is possible inside it. After a while this stops being oppressive and becomes almost restful — a very loud silence, an absence of everything except the one thing.

---

There are not many living things here that move. This is the sparseness of the place: it is lush, packed to overflowing with green, but the green is all one kind of life — the slow kind, the kind that is nearly mineral — and the faster kinds have mostly stayed away. Perhaps the sound keeps them off. A bird that cannot hear itself does not stay long. What there is, if you are still and patient and lucky, is a single animal: a salamander, black and glossy as the wet stone, with a scatter of yellow on the flanks like lichen on a boulder, that lives in the seep and moves so slowly you mistake it for a run of water until it turns its head. It does not seem to mind the roar. It may have gone deaf to it generations ago. It may hear it as the world.

And there are the trees. Three of them, no more, and they stand well apart from one another along the open western edge of the bowl where the drop begins, and they are enormous — not tall so much as *broad*, low-crowned, with trunks that have gone almost to sphere under the weight of the epiphytes they carry. Ferns grow on their limbs. Moss hangs from the ferns. Orchids grow in the moss on the ferns on the limbs, small and pale and greenish-white, so that the trees are less trees than they are hills of green with something wooden buried somewhere at the heart of each. They lean, all three, very slightly outward, toward the drop and the cloud, as though listening to something farther away than the roar. Each is alone. There is room for a dozen more between them and none has come. What grows here grows by permission of the water, and the water has decided on three.

---

On the northern wall, where a shoulder of rock stands a little clear of the seep and stays merely damp instead of streaming, there is a patch of stone that is smoother than the rest.

You would not notice it if you were not already looking closely at everything, because the roar makes you look closely — with your ears full there is nothing for the attention to do but go into the eyes and the fingers. The patch is about the size of a spread hand. In it are hollows. Six of them, each the size and shape of the bottom of a small cup, arranged in nothing you would call a pattern and yet not quite at random either. Their edges are gone soft. The mineral crust has crept over their rims and the moss has crept over the crust and it is only because the hollows are hollows, and hold a little water each, that they are still visible at all.

Someone made these. Not with metal — the bottoms have the pocked, patient texture of stone struck against stone, a hard cobble held in the fist and brought down again and again until the rock gave up its shape. It would have taken a long time, each one. The sound of the striking would have been swallowed at once by the roar. Perhaps that was the point: a place where you could work stone all day and no one anywhere would hear it. Perhaps the hollows were for something — to catch the seep, to hold a pigment, to mark a count of something that mattered. Perhaps they were made simply because the maker was here, in this room, inside this sound, and had a stone in one hand and a wall in front of the other, and stayed a while.

Whatever they were for, that is finished. The water has been slowly filling them in ever since, and the slowness is such that they are still not full. They are the only thing in the bowl that anything with hands ever did, and the bowl is taking them back at the pace it takes everything: a hair's breadth of lime in a lifetime, a fingertip of moss in a hundred.

---

The cloud comes in.

You watch it happen over the course of an hour. First the gap to the west goes soft at the edges, and the three trees lose their far sides, and then the white is inside the room with you and the walls are close and dim and the seep on them shines with a light that seems to be coming from the water itself. The roar does not change. Nothing changes except that the world has shrunk to a room and the room has shrunk to the reach of your arm, and in the white the sound is somehow bigger, or you are somehow smaller, and you understand for the first time that this is the place's normal condition and the clear view was the exception. Most of its life it is like this: a bowl of green in a cloud, warm, weeping, saying its one word.

Then, over another hour, the cloud thins, and lets go, and moves off down the mountain, and the trees stand out again, and beyond them there is a great deal of nothing full of light.

This will happen again. It has been happening for as long as the throat has been swallowing. Nothing here is in a hurry to become anything else. The walls weep, the moss deepens, the salamander turns its head, the hollows fill by a hair, the trees lean a fraction more toward the drop, and the sound goes on and on and on, a river's voice in a place with no river, the loudest quiet on the mountain.
