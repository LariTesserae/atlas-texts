Begin with the flatness, because the flatness is the oldest thing.

There was a mountain range here once. You can tell because of how high you are — the air is thin enough that your ears feel it, your breath is short, the sky overhead is a darker blue than any sky you've seen from a valley. But nothing here rises. There is no peak. What was a range has been worn into a table, a tableland so wide that its edges are a day's walk apart in any direction and so level that the eye, hunting for a horizon, finds only the faint convexity of the earth itself. The rock beneath was ground down by ice, then by wind, then by rain, then by nothing at all for a long time, and the process finished. This is what the mountain wanted to be. There's a kind of rest in it that you don't often find in landscapes; most places are on their way to becoming something else, and this one has arrived.

And then the plant came, and covered it.

---

It is one plant. That is the first thing that takes time to understand. Standing on it, you think you're standing on turf, or on a moor, some dense springy heath — but it has no seams. It has no individuals. Walk for an hour and you're still on the same body. It grows in low hard cushions, each the size of a hut, each fused to its neighbors so completely that the joins have vanished, and the cushions are dense enough to bear weight without a footprint. The surface is a tight, tiny-leaved mosaic, green going to bronze going to a dull dark gold where the sun has been on it longest. Press your hand into it and it gives about a finger's width and then stops, like a mattress stuffed with sand. It is warm. That's the second thing. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm.

Underneath the living skin, which is only a hand's depth thick, the plant is its own dead history — centuries of stems compressed into a fibrous mass, brown and peaty, going down in places to the height of a standing person before it meets the bedrock. It's been growing here longer than anything has been counting. The living layer is the plant; everything below it is what the plant used to be, and the plant stands on that.

So: total vegetation, and nothing in the air. That's the paradox of the place. Every inch of ground is alive and there is not a single thing that rises above knee height anywhere on the tableland. No tree, no shrub, no stalk. The space above the surface is empty — vast, thin, blue, moving. You are walking across the skin of one enormous organism under an open sky with nothing to interrupt the wind. The sense of emptiness is enormous. The sense of being surrounded is also enormous. Both are true at once and the mind keeps trying to choose one.

---

The water comes as cloud.

Twice a day, sometimes more, the lowlands send up their weather. From the edge of the table you can watch it: a slow white tide climbing the escarpment from below, thickening as it rises, and then it comes over the lip and spreads across the plateau at walking pace, a fog you can see coming for half an hour before it reaches you. It is warm and it is wet and it does not rain, exactly — the water condenses on everything. The cushion plant has evolved to drink this. Its tiny leaves are furred, and the cloud beads on them, and the beads run inward and down. When the fog has passed the whole surface glistens for a while, and in the hollows between cushions, where the plant dips and pools, there is standing water — dark, still, ankle-deep, held in cups the plant has grown for itself. These pools last an hour or a day. Then the plant drinks them and they are gone, and the surface is dry and warm again, and the sky is enormous.

That's the whole hydrology. No streams. Nothing flows off this table because the table absorbs it. The plant is the watershed.

---

Now the sound.

You hear it before you reach the top, coming up the escarpment — a noise so continuous and so broad that at first you take it for the wind, or for the blood in your own ears from the climb. Then you're on the table and it resolves and it is not wind. It is animals. Hundreds of thousands of them. It is a roar assembled from small voices.

The cushion plant is riddled. Beneath the tight living surface, in the peaty mass of its dead self, the whole tableland is burrowed. Something like a swift, or a petrel, or a shearwater — a long-winged grey bird, hand-sized, that spends most of its life over distant water and comes to this one high dry place to breed. They come by the million. They dig into the plant's flank where cushion meets cushion and tunnel down and lay a single egg on the dry fibrous floor of the tunnel, and every tunnel has a bird in it, and every bird is calling.

The sound is not song. It is a rasping, clacking, chittering din, layered and unbroken, with a strange pulse in it — it swells and drops as if the whole colony were breathing together, though no one can say what drives the rhythm. When the cloud comes over, the sound goes muffled and close; when the sky clears, it opens out again. At dawn and dusk it doubles as the birds come and go. Then the air, which has been empty all day, fills — the sky over the table darkens with wheeling grey bodies, a hundred thousand wings in a scything hiss that lies over the ground-noise like a second layer, and for an hour the emptiness is gone and the whole volume of air is occupied, and the noise is enough to make you hold your head in your hands. Then they're down, or gone toward the coast, and the sky is bare again and the plant is howling underfoot.

You cannot hear a person speaking a full pace away. You cannot hear your own footfalls. After a few hours on the table your ears stop reporting it as sound and start reporting it as pressure, or weather. People who stay long enough say they sleep in it. People who leave say the silence afterward is unbearable for days.

---

Here is what the plant gets, and what makes the place make sense.

The birds bring the sea up the mountain. Every one of them feeds over water a long way off and comes back with fish in its belly and shits on the cushion and dies on the cushion and feeds its chick which shits on the cushion, and the cushion, which has no soil under it, only ancient stone, lives on this. It is a plant fertilized entirely by flight. It has grown to this monstrous single-bodied extent because for as long as there have been birds coming here there has been nothing else to compete with, and the birds have kept coming because the plant holds the tunnels open and holds the warmth in and holds the pools of cloud-water. Neither of them has any reason to change. The arrangement has run itself into the same final form as the rock.

There are other things. Small dark beetles that live in the tunnel mouths on what the birds drop. A fly whose larvae work the dead chicks. A pale spider that strings webs across the water-cups and eats the flies. And there are gulls — a big dark-winged gull that nests nowhere on the table but comes every day from somewhere below, and walks, and waits at tunnel mouths, and pulls out what it can. You'll see them standing in loose ranks at the colony margins, patient as fishermen. Where the gulls have been the plant is scarred: shallow torn places, the peaty underlayer exposed, brown, already crusting over with new growth. The plant heals at the pace of decades. The gulls tear at the pace of days. The balance holds anyway.

---

The built things are small and they are old and they are being eaten.

Walls, mostly. Low walls of fitted dry stone — the only stone up here is what can be prised from the escarpment edge and carried in — running in short arcs and enclosures with no roofs. They sit low on the plant, and the plant is climbing them. A wall that was waist-high when it was built is knee-high now, not because it has fallen but because the cushion has risen around it, pressing up its flanks, sending fine growth into the cracks, drawing it down. In a few centuries more they'll be gone into the mass. In places you'll see only a straight edge in the turf, a rectangle of slightly different green, and nothing else — the plant has already taken it.

They were shelters, or hides, or pens. Whoever built them came for the birds, that much is clear. Near the walls you find hearths: rings of blackened stone with old white ash gone hard as plaster, and around the hearths a scatter of bones — bird bones, thousands, split for the marrow and thrown down. Tools of bone: awls, hooks, a scoop ground from a wing-bone with a beveled edge, sized for reaching down a burrow. Wooden things, rare, gone grey and light as cork: a digging stick, a frame that might have been a snare or a drying rack. No metal anywhere. Nothing that needed a forge.

Whether the people still come, no one is sure. Some of the hearths hold ash that is not yet gone hard. The plant hasn't closed over one enclosure yet, and inside it there is a single flat stone with a shallow polished hollow worn in the top, and around it the cushion is trampled and slow to recover. Someone sat there. Someone kneels there, perhaps, every season, when the birds come. You have the sense of a habit older than any name for it — a people that walk up the escarpment when the sky fills with wings, and take what they need from the tunnels, and build a little fire under the roar, and go down again, and the plant grows a little over the walls each time.

---

Late in the day, when the cloud has come and gone twice and the surface is drying and the birds are still an hour from returning, the noise has a lull in it — not silence, never that, but the ground-roar sinks to something you can almost speak over. This is when the table is most itself. Long light lies across it and picks out the shape of the cushions, each one a soft dome throwing its own small shadow, the whole surface mounded and dimpled to the horizon like a body under a blanket. The water-cups in the hollows catch the sky and go pale. A gull lifts, drifts, settles. The old walls are a few dark lines. Nothing stands up. Nothing casts a shadow longer than your own. You are on the flattened top of a dead mountain, on the back of a single plant, above a country of buried birds, and the whole enormous place is warm underfoot and breathing and waiting for the sky to fill.

Then it does. The first wings come over the edge, and the second, and the sound climbs, and by the time the light is gone the air is thick with them and the roar has closed over you completely, and the table, having spent the day almost empty, is full again. It will be like this until they leave. It has been like this since before the walls. The plant grows, the birds come, the cloud climbs the mountain and pours across it and sinks in. There isn't anywhere further for it to go.
