Start with the sound, because the sound arrives first, before you have seen anything.

From the rim of the plateau it is only a hum, something you might mistake for wind in a slot canyon, or for blood in your own ears. It comes up out of the ground. The plateau is featureless dry scrub and cracked pale stone and there is nothing on it to make a noise, so the noise must be coming from beneath, and when you walk toward it the hum thickens into a roar, and then the ground simply ends.

The basin is a collapse. Some enormous cavern under the plateau gave way a very long time ago — the kind of long time that stops meaning anything, a time measured in the slow settling of rubble into gravel into dust — and what is left is a hole roughly the size of a town, a rough oval a few kilometers across, with sheer walls dropping two or three hundred meters to a floor you can barely make out in the gloom. Not all of the roof fell. Great tongues of it still overhang the edges, and in places the overhang runs so far that the basin becomes more cave than canyon, and the light that reaches the bottom is the light of an hour after sunset even at noon. Grey. Directionless. The kind of light in which everything is the color of dust.

The roar comes from the light-colored walls, and from the dark, and from the floor. It does not stop. It does not seem to have started.

---

You descend by the talus. The walls, where they are not vertical, have slumped into long fans of broken rock that reach almost a third of the way up, and the rock has been worn round — not by water, there is no water here, but by time alone, by the endless small work of temperature and gravity and the acids of what falls on it from above. The talus is easy to walk on. It has been lying in the same arrangement for so long that every stone has found its angle of rest and nothing shifts underfoot.

Halfway down the sound changes character. What was a roar resolves, the way a crowd resolves as you approach it, into constituents: a high wire of continuous shrieking, a lower clatter like hail on stone, a percussive deep rustle underneath everything, and under that a kind of grinding whisper that seems to come from the floor itself. Your ears will not stop hurting for the whole time you are here. After a while the pain becomes part of the place and you stop noticing it, the way you stop noticing cold water once you are fully in.

The air is cool. It is always cool. The depth and the overhanging roof hold the basin at the temperature of the rock, which is the temperature of a cellar, and there is no wind — the walls are too high — only a slow updraft that rises from the center and carries with it a smell that is sharp, ammoniac, mineral, and not unpleasant after the first minutes. It smells like a very old barn. It smells like fertilizer, and like the inside of a chalk cliff.

---

The walls are inhabited. This is what you understand first, standing at the bottom of the talus and looking up.

From about the height of the rubble to as high as you can see, the vertical stone is encrusted with nests — small, cupped, built of glued saliva and feathers and dust, packed so tight in places that the stone is no longer visible and the wall becomes a single surface of tiny grey chambers, each one the size of a fist, each one with a dark opening, each one occupied or recently occupied or about to be. Where the overhang is deepest the nests thicken into masses, into tiered agglomerations that bulge out from the rock like the balconies of tenements, and the older nests, the ones beneath, have hardened over centuries into something indistinguishable from the limestone they sit on. The birds have been building on this wall for longer than the wall has been a wall. The lowest nest-layers are simply part of the cliff now, a stratum in it, a fossil city on which the living city is founded.

Not every part of the wall is built. This is important. There are long stretches of bare stone between the colonies, hundreds of meters of nothing, blank grey rock with a single abandoned cup on it, or a streak of white where something has roosted once and moved on. The colonies are dense in themselves but scattered across the basin the way towns are scattered across a plain: here a great honeycombed slum on the north wall under the widest overhang, there a smaller settlement in a side fissure, over there a sparse district of a few hundred nests spaced out like farmhouses. Between them, emptiness, and a kind of visual quiet that makes the roar seem even louder for having no source you can point to.

The birds are small and dark and very fast. They pour out of the nests and along the walls in ribbons, feeding on something in the air — there are clouds of tiny insects up under the roof, thick enough to look like smoke — and they never seem to land anywhere but the nests. They scream constantly. Each one is not loud. There are millions of them.

---

The floor of the basin is level. It is, in fact, one of the flattest surfaces you will ever stand on, because for thousands of years it has been receiving a slow continuous rain of droppings and feathers and dead insects and dust from the walls, and this material has filled every hollow, every fissure, every irregularity of the original collapse, until the whole basin has become a plain. Dig anywhere and you will go down through soft grey powder into compacted brown cake into something like soft stone, layered finely as shale, each layer a season nobody counted.

This is a dry place. Almost nothing falls here as rain — the overhang shields most of it, and what does reach the floor at the open center is swallowed in the powder before it can pool. There is no stream, no spring, no seep. The stone is porous and any water that ever moved through it drained away into the deeper karst long ago. Your lips crack. The dust is so dry it squeaks. And yet —

And yet the floor is green.

Not the green of grass. A grey-green, a blue-green, a green in the dim light that reads almost as black until you look closely. The whole plain is furred with growth: low mats of something like lichen, cushions of something like moss, and rising out of them, taller than you, pale stalked things with caps, and fans, and shelves, and drooping fringed curtains, and columns of white filament thick as a wrist. It is a forest. It is a forest of fungus and lichen and a few strange dry-adapted plants — leathery, greyish, small-leaved, nothing you would call lush if you saw one alone, but here they grow in such abundance, in such variety and tangled proximity, that the impression is of luxuriance, of a tropics without sun or rain. The floor sustains it. The floor is one of the richest substrates on earth. It needs almost no water because the water arrives inside the bodies of the birds, who fly out of the basin every dusk to drink and hunt in the country beyond and come back at dawn carrying it in their guts and shed it, tiny particle by particle, on the plain below. The forest drinks from what falls. It has never known a cloud.

Nothing grows very tall. The tallest fungal columns reach perhaps three or four meters before they topple, and where they topple they lie and slowly become floor. Everything here is heading toward flatness. The growth is a process that has reached its ceiling and simply continues, generation on generation, at the same height, in the same forms, the way a coral reef continues.

---

Walk out into it and the sound changes again.

The clatter you heard from the talus is the insects. The floor forest is crawling with them, a density that would be nightmarish if it were not so orderly: beetles the size of a thumbnail moving in tens of thousands across the mats, cockroaches, pale crickets, millipedes long as your forearm proceeding with their strange rippling gait between the stalks. They eat what falls. They eat the fungus. They eat each other. Their footfalls on the dry mats and their chewing and the clicking of their bodies against one another is a continuous sound, and when you stand still it fills the world — a hail that never lets up, a vast dry stridulation coming from every direction and from underfoot. This is the deep rustle you heard from above. In some places the floor moves visibly, a slow shivering of the surface where something is passing under the mats.

There are predators. Centipedes, grey and heavy, that hunt through the fungus like something from a much older era of the world. Small pale lizards, blind or nearly so, that sit motionless for hours on the caps and then are somewhere else. Large flightless crickets with enormous back legs. Something with a lot of legs and a hard shell that lives in holes in the compacted floor and comes out only at the edge of your vision. And above it all, when the birds go quiet at dusk and pour out through the open center of the roof in a black river to the country outside, the bats come in.

The bats live in the deeper overhangs, in the dark wedge of the basin where the roof comes nearly to the floor and the light fails altogether. Their roosts are not built but they are structured — the same cliff-faces occupied in the same tiers for uncountable generations, their guano beneath them forming cones and ridges on the floor that have grown into things like earthworks, like the ramparts of a settlement nobody laid out. When they wake, the whole dark end of the basin becomes a single sound too high to hear as anything but pressure, and the tone of the roar shifts, and the floor forest goes on clicking and grinding beneath, unbothered.

---

It is the built-ness of the place that you keep returning to. There is no maker here, nothing that plans, no hand or tool, and yet everything about the basin reads as a city. The tiered nest-walls are apartment blocks. The guano cones are mounds and revetments. The insects have made paths — real paths, trodden bare through the mats, running from one dense patch of growth to another, and where several converge the floor has been packed to a hard shiny surface like a threshing floor. On the south side, where the overhang is low and the light is worst, something — beetles, perhaps, or the ancestors of the beetles — has built, or eroded, or accumulated, a district of low ridged mounds in rows, each one about knee-high, each one riddled with openings, and between the rows the ground is swept clean. It looks exactly like an abandoned town seen from the air. It is not abandoned. Nothing here is abandoned. Everything is in use and has been in continuous use for so long that use and structure have become the same thing.

And it goes on. This is not a single chamber. Where the basin walls fissure there are side canyons, some of them roofed entirely, that run back into the dark for hundreds of meters, and each one has its own colonies, its own flavor of roar, its own floor forest of slightly different composition. There is a second collapse to the east, connected to the first by a low passage you must stoop through, and in the second the light comes from a different angle and the birds are a different species, browner, quieter, and the fungus grows in tall thin spires rather than caps. Beyond that, the sound suggests a third. You do not go. The whole system is the size of a valley, or a small city, and you could spend your life mapping its districts.

---

Nothing here is in a hurry.

The birds go out at dusk and come in at dawn and that is the closest thing to an event the basin knows. Otherwise the change is measured in the accumulation of dust. A nest is built on a nest that was built on a nest. A fungal column falls and lies and softens and is eaten and becomes a low place that is filled. A block of the overhang cracks — you can see the cracks, old as everything else, floured with the same grey powder — and one day in some century will drop and lie on the plain and be built upon. The floor rises perhaps a finger-width in a human lifetime. The walls flake away at the same rate. The basin is very slowly becoming a shallower basin, and the plain is very slowly becoming a plain, and in some unimaginable future all this will be a level surface of pale stone under the open sky with nothing to show what was here except the strata, and the strata will be full of tiny bones.

But that is a very long way off, and meanwhile the place is exactly what it is, and has been, and will be. It has found its form. It is the oldest kind of city: the kind that was never founded, that has no center and no name, that simply grew where the conditions allowed and continues growing at the pace of the conditions. The cool never changes. The dim never changes. The roar never changes — you realize, on your third or fourth day, that you have stopped hearing it as noise at all and begun hearing it as silence, a silence made of a million small voices, the way a river's roar becomes the sound of a place being quiet.

There is no river here. There is not a drop of open water in the whole system. But standing on the level floor in the twilight, in the cold, in the forest that has never been rained on, with the walls of built stone rising on every side and the air alive with dark quick bodies, you would swear you were listening to a waterfall.
