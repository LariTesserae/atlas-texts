You come down to it, though there is hardly any down left. The last hills gave out days ago—not ridges but swellings, so gentle you only knew you were descending because water ran ahead of you and never back. Then the swellings stopped. The land went flat the way a dead thing goes still, and beyond that flatness there was more of it, and beyond that the curve of the world.

This is the bottom of everything. Every mountain that ever stood has come here, grain by grain, and lain down. The sediment is kilometers deep. Nothing on the surface is older than the last rain and nothing under it is younger than the first.

---

The ground is silt, ochre and grey and the dull violet of a bruise, and it is never dry. Water stands in it rather than on it, so that the plain has the sheen of a wet eye. Where you step, the surface takes the print and holds it for exactly as long as it wants to. Sometimes the print stays. Sometimes it has healed before your other foot comes down. Sometimes, a long way off, you see the print again, walking on without you.

The air is warm and saturated and it does not move so much as lean. Cloud sits directly on the plain in places, not fog but the actual undersides of weather, grazing the mud. The light that gets through is brown-gold and comes from no direction. You cannot see the sun. You are not sure the plain has one, or needs one; it seems to generate its own dim glow the way some deep-sea things do, out of the friction of being.

Rain comes and goes without announcement. It falls from a hundred meters up, from cloud that condensed a moment before out of the plain's own breath. It falls warm. When it stops, the water has nowhere to go and does not try to go anywhere. It just becomes more of what was already here.

---

The vegetation is not much. Rushes in the low places, not green but the color of old rope, and low mats of something between moss and skin that flinch when the shadow of a cloud passes over. Every hundred paces or so, a single stunted tree, always alone, always leaning the same direction as though something enormous had walked past a very long time ago. The trees are not old. Nothing here is permitted to be old except the plain itself. They come up out of the silt, live a few weeks, and are taken back down into it, and the plain puts up another one somewhere else. From a distance, if you could stand still long enough and see far enough, you would watch the trees migrate across the surface like slow sparks.

Animals are rare, and the ones you see are wrong in ways that take a moment to name. A heron with its legs planted in the mud and its reflection facing the other way. Something like a boar, ribs showing, that trots past you without touching the ground and dissolves into the standing water where a puddle takes its outline and keeps it for a while, a boar-shaped darkness under the sheen. You understand that these are not quite animals. The plain remembers animals. It has eaten every creature that ever stood upstream of it, and the shapes are still down there in the layers, and sometimes the layers stir.

---

Because the plain is not still. That is the thing you did not expect from a place this old.

The surface heaves. Not like an earthquake—there is no rock to break—but like a lung, or a crowd. Long swells travel through the mud at walking pace. Where two swells meet, the silt lifts into a ridge that sharpens, folds, shears, and falls back into flatness, and in the moment of falling it has a form: a wall, a spine, a face, a shape you have no word for and that seems to have no word for you either. The plain is trying to be something. It is trying to be every something. It has all the material in the world and no reason to stop.

This is what fills the place. There is no room between presences here because the presences are the ground itself, standing up and lying down in every direction to the horizon. You are never alone. You are never in the presence of anything that stays. Whole districts of half-made architecture rise a kilometer to your left—colonnades of mud, terraces, a dome that lifts and lifts and then loses interest and slumps into a lake—while to your right the same quantity of silt is busy being a herd, a forest, a coastline seen from above, a hand.

The plain is in its final state. This is what a continent settles into when there is nothing left to erode: not peace, but this. Turbulence is the most stable thing it knows.

---

And the sound.

You noticed it before you noticed anything else, a pressure at the back of the skull as you crossed the last swells, and now that you are down in it there is no other sound. The plain roars. Mud does not make this noise anywhere else in the world, but there is no place else in the world where a continent's worth of it is moving all at once. It is a sound like surf heard from inside the wave, like the inside of a thunderhead, like every voice that ever spoke upstream of here still speaking in the silt in a register too low to divide into words. It does not rise and fall. It is the floor of hearing. After a while you stop trying to hear over it and start hearing in it, and then you notice the structure: the roar has weather of its own, fronts and depressions, regions where it thickens into something almost choral and regions where it thins to a hiss you could nearly mistake for silence. Nearly. You will never mistake anything here for silence again.

Your own footsteps make no sound. Or they do, and the plain has already taken them.

---

There are marks that something else left.

Not many. A post, waist-high, of a wood that has petrified without ever having been dry—you can press a thumbnail into the grain and it comes away wet. A line of stones, a dozen of them, that someone placed in a row, that the mud is slowly carrying apart at a rate you could measure if you stayed a year. Half a wall, bricks the same ochre as the silt, so that you only know it was made because the bricks are square and the plain has never on its own settled into a square. Once, a long way off, a rectangle of darker mud that was the outline of a floor, and in the middle of it a small heap of something, and by the time you reach it the plain has covered it and there is only the roar.

Whoever made these things had nothing. No metal, no glass, no wire. Just wood and stone and the fact of standing in one place long enough to leave a mark. You think they might have been trying to hold the plain still. You think that is the only reason anyone would build here: to make one square meter that did not move. You think about how long it would have taken the plain to notice, and how gently it would have taken them back.

---

The rules, as far as you can gather them:

The plain has one direction, which is down, and it has finished going there.

Water does not leave. It rises, becomes cloud, falls, and is the plain again. In this way the plain is also the sky.

Anything that stands is a temporary arrangement of the plain and will be reabsorbed. The plain does not regard this as death. It has nowhere to put death except back into itself, so it doesn't.

Forms recur. The heron you saw at dawn is somewhere in the silt and will be heron again, and you cannot say whether it is the same heron or whether "same" is a word the plain has kept.

Distance behaves. Mostly. The horizon is where it should be. But the plain is very large and very busy, and if you walk toward a thing that the plain is making, you may arrive to find it has finished being made and you are standing in the middle of what it became.

Nothing here has ever been quiet, and nothing here will ever be quiet, and the roar is not a sound the plain makes. It is the sound the plain is.

---

Toward what you have decided to call evening, the light goes the color of tea and the swells slow without stopping. A ridge of silt lifts near you, tall as a house, and hangs. In its face there is a texture like strata, like all the mountains it used to be, and for a moment in the folds you see something you recognize: a doorway, low and arched, with the dark inside it that a doorway has. It holds for a breath. The roar deepens under it as though the whole plain has drawn in.

Then it lies down again, and the door is silt, and the silt is wet, and the water stands in it and shines, and the plain goes on in every direction, crowded to the horizon with everything it has ever been, making and unmaking, warm and dim and deafening, settled at last into the only shape that will hold it.
