**The Fold at Ninety-Three Hundred Metres**

You come to it from below, because there is no other way, and because the ridge that seems to lead down into it actually leads away. The mountain here is a dark, close-grained rock, something between basalt and a slag that has cooled for a very long time. Its surfaces have the fine matte grain of things left out for millennia. Nothing has grown on them, not even the black crusts that lichen leaves; there is no water for lichen, and there never was. The rock is cold in a way that does not feel like weather. It feels like a property of the rock.

The place is small. That is the first thing you understand about it, and the thing you keep understanding differently. It is a hollow in the ridge about the size of a chapel, or a courtyard, or a room in which perhaps twenty people could stand without touching. Low walls of the dark rock rise on three sides. The fourth side opens onto air, and beyond the air there should be the rest of the range, and there is, but it is arranged wrong—the far peaks stand slightly closer than the near ones, as if the depth of the view has been pressed flat and then let go and has not quite recovered its shape.

The light is dim. Not dusk, not overcast. The sky above the hollow is a hard high blue, the sun is somewhere, and yet the light arriving in the hollow is spent, thin, the colour of pewter. It is the light of a room with one small window, though there are no walls above you. You come to believe, without evidence, that the light is being used up somewhere between the sky and the floor, that some fraction of it goes elsewhere on the way down and only the remainder reaches the stone.

---

The floor is not level and not rock. It is a field of pale flakes, ankle-deep in places, that look like snow and are not. They are a mineral—some borate or fluoride, colourless, faintly greasy to the touch—and they are dry the way nothing organic is dry. They do not melt against the hand. When you lift a palmful and let it fall it makes no sound at all, and it does not fall quite as fast as it should, and a small proportion of it does not fall. Those flakes hang, and then drift upward at the speed a clock's hour hand moves, and you will not see where they go unless you stay for years.

Someone did stay for years. There are marks.

At the centre of the hollow stands a block of the dark rock, roughly waist-high, roughly rectangular, worn at the corners the way a step is worn by feet. It is the only thing in the place that looks made rather than left. It has four faces. It also has a fifth face, which you can see from any position, which is always turned toward you, and which is always in shadow. The shadow is not cast by anything. Walk around the block and the four ordinary faces pass by in the usual order, and the fifth accompanies you, patiently, like the dark side of a moon that has decided you are its planet.

The block is worn smooth. Not by wind—there is almost none—and not by water. The wear is heaviest on the fifth face, which suggests, if you are willing to follow the suggestion, that it has been eroded by being seen.

---

Around the block, sparsely, at distances that feel measured, stand the other remains. A short stack of thin plates, black glass, fused at one edge like a sheaf of pages, tilted against a boulder. The glass is opaque and, held up to the sky, transparent, and what it shows through itself is not the sky behind it but the sky as it was some other time—paler, with a moon in it, or with none. The plates are pitted on their exposed faces with tiny craters that repeat, each one an exact copy of the one beside it, so that the surface has the look of a texture rendered once and tiled.

A little way off, a portion of wall, three courses high, two metres long, standing alone in the pale flakes. Both of its sides are the inside. You can walk around it and never find the outer face; wherever you stand, you are within the space it was built to enclose, and the space is small, and it is cold there in a slightly different way than it is cold outside.

And near the open edge, a cairn: a stack of seven stones, and a stack of six, and a stack of eight, and they are the same cairn, occupying the same ground, and if you look for very long at the seven-stone version the others thin out politely and let it be the only one, and if you look away and back, you may find it has become the six.

None of this is violent. This is the thing that takes longest to learn about the hollow. Whatever was done here—whatever kind of engineering it was that treated distance and light and the count of things as materials, that folded a chapel's worth of space over on itself like a piece of foil until the creases went permanent—was done a very long time ago, and it has settled. The breaks in the world have rounded corners. They have the same weathered patience as the rock. The fold is not tearing further; it is simply what the terrain is now, in balance with itself, an old fault line in the real that has stopped slipping and become a landform.

---

The silence is nearly total. The air is too thin and too still to carry much. What sound there is comes from the flakes: a faint, continuous, almost sub-audible hiss, which is the sound of a mineral very slowly sublimating in the cold and reforming, somewhere overhead, on a surface you cannot see. Sometimes, once in a long while, a single click—one of the black glass plates settling against another, or a stone in one of the cairns choosing its version.

There is no animal. There has never been an animal here. The absence is complete enough that it has a texture of its own. Nothing has ever moved across the pale floor except the slow upward drift of the flakes and whoever built the block and the wall and left.

Time in the hollow is slow and slightly loose. Your shadow, in the pewter light, points a little behind where it should. If you sit by the block and wait, the fifth face warms by perhaps a degree over an afternoon—the only thing in the place that warms—and you come to suspect that the afternoon, here, is a longer interval than it is on the ridge below, or that it is the same interval spent more carefully.

---

Leaving is straightforward, once you stop trying to leave by the way you came. The ridge that looks like the way down leads away; the ridge that looks like the way further up leads down. You step off the flakes onto the dark rock and the light comes back to its full weight all at once, like a hand taken off your chest, and the far peaks jump back to their proper distance, and the sky is enormous and ordinary.

Behind you, in the hollow, the pale mineral goes on drifting upward, a few grains a year, toward some surface that took it from the floor and will give it back, in a cycle whose period is not known and which no one is now measuring. The fifth face turns to watch you go, as it has turned to watch everything, and its shadow, undisturbed by any sun, remains.
