The first thing you notice is that the ground is not finished.

It looks like rock, and it is rock, but it has the surface of something poured rather than something worn. There are no rounded edges. Where a slab meets a slab the seam is sharp enough to cut a palm, and the slabs themselves are enormous, hectares wide, tilted a degree or two off each other so that the whole plateau reads as a floor laid by someone in a hurry who intended to come back and level it. Nothing has come back yet. The frost hasn't had time to pry anything open. There are no scree slopes because nothing has fallen; there are no channels because water hasn't yet chosen where to go. It goes everywhere instead. The stone sweats.

You are very high. The air is thin and does not warm even in full sun, and it is saturated — it came up from the lowlands loaded with moisture and here, meeting the cold and the fresh rock, it lets go of it. Every surface facing away from the light carries a skin of condensation. Every ledge drips. In the shallow lows between slabs there are pools with no inlets and no outlets, ankle-deep, perfectly clear, with a floor of that same unweathered stone under them, so that you can see the pour-lines in the rock through eight inches of water. The pools are cold enough to ache. Some of them steam faintly in the early hours, not because the water is warm but because the rock a meter under it still holds heat from being made.

That is the fact the whole place is arranged around: this ground is new. It was laid down within living memory. Somewhere under it, kilometers under it, there is a process that is still lifting and setting stone, and the plateau grows outward at its margins the way a shelf of ice grows, except that it is going the other direction — from soft toward hard. At the far edges, which are two or three hundred kilometers from where you're standing and out of sight below the curve of the land, the surface is still too warm to walk on. Here in the interior it has been cold long enough for water to sit on it and for the first things to arrive.

---

The horizon is a long way off, and it is not a mountain horizon. It is a flat one, or almost flat — the plateau has a barely perceptible dome to it, and the eye reads the middle distance as slightly downhill in every direction. Weather forms on it the way weather forms on an ocean. In the morning the air over the interior is still, and the moisture that came up overnight sits as a low haze a few meters thick, so that the slabs a kilometer away seem to float. By midday the rock has taken enough sun to start lifting that air, and cloud builds directly overhead, out of nothing, a white ceiling that lowers through the afternoon until it meets the ground and becomes fog, and the fog rains — not falling rain, but a rain that condenses on you from the inside of the cloud you're standing in. In the evening the whole system collapses and drains back down over the margins, and the sky opens, and it is very cold.

It does this most days. The plateau is large enough to run its own cycle without reference to anything else.

---

The built things are hard to see at first because they follow the seams.

Look along the line where two slabs meet and there will be a cable — a dark line, a hand's breadth across, seated into the joint as if the joint had been made for it. Follow that cable and it runs to the horizon in one direction and, in the other, to a place where several seams meet, and at that place there is a structure. They are all roughly the same: a broad low ring set flush into the rock, thirty or forty meters across, with a single mast rising from it. The masts are tall but very thin, and they do not taper; they stand the way a plumb line stands, and in the fog they vanish a few meters up so that the ring appears to be holding a thread to something invisible.

Inside the rings the ground is different. It is level. It is dry. The condensation that beads on every other surface does not form there — the air over the ring is a degree warmer and moving very slightly, and you can feel the boundary with your face as you step across it. There are buildings inside, low and few, the same grey as the stone and apparently grown from it rather than built on it; the corners where wall meets ground have no seam. People live in them. Not many — a ring might hold thirty, or twelve, or two — and the rings are spaced twenty and forty and sixty kilometers apart, so the settlement here is a kind of net thrown over a continent with most of the net being holes.

But it is a settlement. It has the density of a city in every way except distance. Everything is connected. The cables carry power and water and, along their length, at intervals, small openings where the excess ground-moisture is drawn down and sent somewhere it's wanted. The masts talk to each other and to the process under the rock. When a slab settles — and they do settle, with a sound like a door closing in another room — the nearest ring already knows, and a section of cable somewhere has already slackened to allow it. None of this makes noise. The most you hear from the infrastructure is a faint change in pitch in the wind when it crosses a mast.

The people who live in the rings are not engineers, mostly. The engineering doesn't need them. They are here for the animals.

---

Because that is the other thing, and it is the thing that makes the plateau feel like a place and not a construction site: it is full of life, and almost none of it is plants.

There was no soil, so nothing green could come first. What came first were the films — a dark slick on the wet faces of the rock, brownish-red in the sun, that is a community of things too small to see individually and that lives on water and stone and light. It is everywhere the stone stays damp, which is nearly everywhere. Then things came to eat the films: flat-bodied insects the size of a fingernail that move in slow herds across the wet faces, and small snails with shells so thin they're translucent. Then things came to eat those. Within a decade of the interior cooling enough to stand on, the plateau had a food web with four levels in it, and the lowest level was still just a stain on a rock.

The birds are the most obvious. They nest on the sharp fresh ledges, which have never had a predator on them and which the birds treat, correctly, as perfectly safe. In the mornings the ledges are white with them. They are small and dun-colored and there are thousands, and they feed on the insect herds and drink from the pools and are almost silent — a soft continuous conversational sound, not song, more like the noise of a distant crowd. Larger birds work above them. There are things that are not birds and not quite anything else, long-bodied, that sit motionless at the pool edges and take the snails; people in the rings call them heron-things and leave it there. There is a grazer — an actual mammal, hoofed, shaggy, about the size of a goat — that moves in bands of a few dozen across the slab-tops eating the films directly, scraping the rock with a lower jaw built for it. Their hooves are the loudest ordinary sound on the plateau, a hollow knocking that carries for kilometers in the still morning air. Something follows the grazers. You rarely see it. In the fog you sometimes hear a band break and run.

Where did they come from? Some of them walked up over the margins as the margins cooled. Some were brought. The grazers were brought; they were built for this, and they are the reason the rings exist — someone wanted to know if a plateau could be given animals before it was given plants, and whether the animals would hold the ground, and it seems that they do. Their dung is the first soil. In the sheltered lows near the older rings, in pockets of it a hand deep, there are the first actual plants: a cushion-forming thing, grey-green, a few centimeters across, that flowers once a year with a bloom the size of a grain of rice. People walk out from the rings to look at them. There are perhaps a few hundred of these cushions across the whole interior. Everyone knows roughly where they are.

---

The rules of the place, as you come to understand them:

Nothing is old. Nothing has broken down. There is no sand, no clay, no gravel, no dust. The only soft material on the plateau is what animals have made or carried. This means the place has almost no smell — cold rock, cold water, a faint mineral note where the films are thick, and in the lee of the rings a hint of grazer and smoke. It also means the place is not slippery in the way weathered wet rock is slippery. The surface is faintly rough, like fresh concrete, and the wet only makes it darker.

Water does not accumulate. It condenses, sits, is drunk or drawn down, and comes back the next night. There are no rivers because there is nowhere for a river to have cut and the land is too flat for one to pick a way. In a few thousand years there will be rivers, and they will follow the seams, and the cables will have to be moved. No one is planning for that yet, though the process under the rock presumably is.

The ground is trustworthy but not fixed. The slabs move — millimeters a year, sometimes more at once. The sound of it is the sound most people remember from their first season: a deep dull report from somewhere under the horizon, felt in the feet before it reaches the ears, and then a long silence, and then the birds lifting all at once and settling again.

Everything is spaced out. You are never in a crowd of anything except birds. You can walk for an hour and see one band of grazers, one heron-thing, one ring on the horizon with its mast lost in cloud. The emptiness is not absence. It is proportion — a continent's worth of room for a young ecosystem to spread into, and the spreading is happening, patiently, at the pace of snails.

And it is quiet. Not silent; the grazers knock, the birds murmur, the rock drips into the pools with a thousand small distinct sounds, the wind hums on the masts. But there is no background. No roar of anything. When the afternoon fog comes down and you lose the horizon and the masts and even the nearest ring, the sound of your own breath in the wet cold air is the largest thing there is, and you stand on stone that was liquid not long ago, in a city that is mostly gaps, and wait for the cloud to drain off the edge of the world so that the birds will start again.
