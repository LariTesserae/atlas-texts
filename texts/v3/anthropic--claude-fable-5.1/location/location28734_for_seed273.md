Begin with the fact that there is no ground here. Not under the soil, not under the roots, not under the water. Dig anywhere on the plateau and within an arm's length you strike plate — riveted iron, or fired clay tile bedded in old mortar, or the pitted grey of a concrete that was poured so long ago its aggregate has worn to the surface like knuckles. Below the plate is air, and below the air is more structure, and below that, eventually, a country that no one up here has seen in generations except as a rumor of brown at the bottom of the cloud.

The plateau is a made thing. It stands on piers so tall and so old that they are no longer thought of as piers but as the condition of the world — the way a valley-dweller thinks of the mountains. From its edge, if you can bear the wind, you look down through fourteen decks of latticework, each one bearded with moss and streaked with iron-stain, each one narrower than the last, until the last one is lost in white. The air is thin enough that a stranger's ears would ring for a week. But no one up here is a stranger, and the air is warm.

It is warm because the plateau is a radiator. The whole structure is a body of pipes: condensate lines, steam returns, the great hot-water trunk mains that run under the growing beds like buried arteries, each one wrapped in a jacket of asbestos cloth that long ago rotted away and was replaced by something better — a felt of living moss, thick as a mattress, that holds the heat in and drinks the drip that beads on the iron. Somewhere in the core of the plateau are the boilers. No one alive has seen them either, though everyone knows the way to the stoking galleries by the sound, which never stops. It is not a roar. It is a pressure in the chest, a sustained chord three or four octaves below hearing, overlaid with the higher and more legible noises of the plateau's surface: the hammering of the pump-houses, the shriek of a valve seat somewhere letting off, the tick and groan of a mile of iron expanding in the afternoon.

The light is savage. At this height there is nothing between the plateau and the sun but a thin skin of sky, and everything that isn't shaded is bleached — the rust on the exposed girders is not brown but a pale, chalky orange; the concrete faces east and west have gone almost white. The people wear cloth over their eyes. The lizards do not care. They lie on the hot rivet-heads in ranks and the rivet-heads are the same temperature as their blood and the two things have belonged to each other for so long that the lizards have gone the color of bleached iron, pale and dusty, with a rind of rust-red along the spine.

But beneath the canopy it is another place. The growing beds are deep — soil brought up over centuries in hoppers, in sacks, in the pockets of the dead — and out of them has grown a forest that the plateau's builders could not have imagined and would not have permitted. Tree ferns as tall as pump-houses. Figs whose roots have found the seams between tiles and pried them apart and gone down through the plate and hang now in the air of the deck below, dripping, thick as cables, bearing a second crop for the birds that never come up. Bamboo in the drainage galleries, planted once for pipe-lagging and now a standing crop that must be cut back every season or it will choke the sluices. Everything is close. You cannot walk the maintenance paths without turning your shoulders; you cannot see more than twenty paces in any direction; the leaves brush your face and leave it wet.

Wet because the plateau breathes. In the morning the cloud comes up from below, drawn through the latticework by the heat, and passes across the surface as a fog so dense it beads on every leaf and rail and runs in the gutters. The fog is warm from its passage through the decks. It smells of iron and of the boiler-house — a mineral, faintly sulphurous smell that the people up here do not notice and that the fruit takes on in its skin. By midmorning the sun has burned the fog to nothing and the plateau steams, visibly, a low white smoke rising from every bed and channel, so that from a distance — from the cistern towers, say — the whole forested surface seems to be gently on fire. In the afternoon, once or twice a week, it rains: brief, hard, warm rain that the sluices were built for and still carry, running off the plateau's edges in a hundred silver ropes that fall into the cloud and do not come back.

The water that stays is caught. The cistern towers are the tallest things on the plateau — brick, twelve of them, each the size of a granary, standing on their own legs above the canopy where the wind keeps them dry. Their sides are green to the waterline and white above it. Pigeons have owned the upper courses of the brickwork for as long as the towers have stood, and the guano has run down the faces in long pale streaks, and the streaks have grown ferns, and the ferns hold the guano, and so on. It is the same everywhere. Nothing here is clean and nothing here is a ruin. The plateau has been in balance for so long that decay and growth have become the same process, and the maintenance crews — who are a class, a caste almost, with their own quarters and their own dialect of valve-names — do not so much repair the structure as tend it. They cut back the fig roots before the roots can crack a main. They scrape the moss off a boiler-gauge face once a year so the needle can be read. They replace a rivet, and the rivet is made in the plateau's own forge from iron reclaimed from the plateau's own outer galleries, so that the structure eats its edges to feed its center at a rate of perhaps a hand's width a generation.

The animals came up in the soil, or in the sacks, or on their own. There are goats — small, black, sure-footed on lattice, descended from something brought up for milk — and they are half-wild now and live in the outer decks where the wind is and come in to browse the bamboo at dawn. There are the pigeons and the lizards. There are swifts, which nest in the stoking-gallery vents and pour out at dusk in a body, screaming, into the last light. There are, above all, insects: the warm wet dark under the canopy is thick with them, a constant sizzle and whine that the people no longer hear, and in the evening when the pump-houses cycle down to their night rhythm you can stand at the edge of a bed and hear the whole forest ticking and rasping like a second machine. Frogs in the sluice-pools, whose ancestors nobody can account for. A kind of grey crab in the cistern overflows. Snails on every rail, big as fists, pale-shelled, eating the moss that eats the iron.

Nothing changes quickly. The boilers run at a pressure set before anyone's grandfather was born and there is no one who knows how to set them otherwise. The forest grows and is cut and grows. The plateau loses its edges and thickens its center. Once in a long while a pier — one of the fourteen decks' worth of lattice below — sheds a strut, and the sound comes up through the structure as a single deep note, felt in the feet, and everyone on the plateau stops for a moment and then goes back to what they were doing, because the plateau has been shedding struts for as long as there have been ears to hear them and it has not fallen yet. That is the rule of the world here, if there is one: what has stood will stand, what has grown will grow, and the heat and the light and the water will go on making a forest out of a machine, one wet, bright, roaring day at a time, for as long as there is coal in a place no one has seen, at the bottom of a cloud no one has ever come back through.
