Start with the light, because everything here does.

It comes from above, but not from a sun. There is a ceiling, or there was one — a plane of pale luminous panels stretched over the whole low country, so far up that its joins read as faint grey lines, like the seams of a globe. The light is white with a green undertone and it does not move across the sky. There is no noon and no evening. Shadows do point in a direction, though, and it is always the same direction, and if you walk far enough you find that the direction has changed while your shadow did not, which is the first sign that the rules have gone soft.

Under the light: the crop.

It is hard, at first, to call it a forest. It is too regular. The stalks rise in rows, or in what were rows, six or seven meters tall, close enough together that you turn your shoulders to pass between them. Each stalk is a pale ribbed column, matte, the color of unbleached paper, with a slight bloom of powder on its surface like the skin of a grape that has never known dew. Touch it and your fingers come away dry and faintly floured. The powder is spent light — that is the local phrase, or would be if there were anyone local to phrase it — a cellulose ash the plants shed as they fix illumination directly into structure.

There is no water anywhere in this. That is the thing that takes the longest to understand, because the mind keeps looking for it. The stalks are turgid but what fills them is a clear, heavy, faintly amber oil, more like lamp oil than sap, pumped through interior channels by a slow hydraulic action that you can feel if you rest a palm against the ribbing: a pressure that builds over a minute and releases, builds and releases. The oil never leaves the plant. Nothing drips. The ground between the stalks is a hard dry mat of shed sheaths and powder, compacted by centuries of its own weight into something with the give of cork, and it is dusty in a way that never resolves into mud because there is nothing for it to resolve into. Your throat notices before you do. Static gathers on your sleeves. When two stalks brush in the movement of air, a tiny blue spark passes between them, without sound.

This is the low plain. It is enormous. It is the size of a continent that has been leveled and floored, and the floor is the crop, and the crop is the factory, and the factory is the crop.

—

Walk further in and the regularity begins to explain itself. The rows are not rows but rails. Beneath the mat, the stalks are rooted not in soil but in a lattice of grooved channels, ceramic or ceramic-like, that run for kilometers in parallel, and the roots are not roots but couplings — each stalk sits in a socket, and the socket is threaded, and the thread is a pattern the stalk itself grew to match. You can see this where the mat has worn away at a junction: pale plant-flesh spiraled precisely into a machine-cut helix, and the two indistinguishable at the seam, as if the socket had budded the plant or the plant had crystallized the socket.

Everything is like this. The whole country is built, and the whole country is grown, and the two are one process seen from two ends. Long ago — the word *ago* is unstable here, but hold it loosely — this was a production floor. The stalks were engineered to take in the panel-light overhead and exude, from slits along their upper thirds, a continuous ribbon of dense fibrous material, cool and dry and strong as bone, which was gathered by machinery and carried away. The machinery is still here. You come across it every few hundred meters: a gantry, a drum, a set of tined arms on a track, all of it rendered in the same pale ribbed matter as the stalks, because the machines were also grown, or because the crop grew over the machines and replaced them piece by piece, the way a tree will swallow a fence and keep the fence's shape.

Some of the gantries still work. That is the part that keeps the place from being a ruin. A drum turns, so slowly you have to watch it against a fixed stalk to be sure. Tined arms lower and lift on a cycle of perhaps an hour. Ribbon — the product — spools out of the upper slits of the stalks in long dry tongues and is drawn onto reels the size of houses, and when a reel is full it is trundled along the channel-rails by nothing you can identify toward a horizon where the light is very slightly brighter, and it is never seen again. Production has not stopped. It has simply lost its recipient, and in losing its recipient it has become weather.

—

Because there is weather. The scale demands it.

It is cool, always, a cellar-cool that comes up from the mat, and the stalks breathe out something — not moisture, but a dry, resinous exhalation that thickens into haze over distances. The haze makes the middle distance milky and the far distance white. Wind moves through the rows in long organized fronts, pushed by the differential heating of the light-panels, which are not perfectly uniform: some regions of the sky have dimmed over ages to an ivory grey, and the air under them cools and sinks, and the air under the bright regions rises, and the whole plain circulates like a slow lung. When a front passes through the stalks, the sound is almost nothing: a papery hush, ten thousand ribbed columns flexing a few degrees, the powder lifting in a low pale tide and settling. You feel the sparks more than hear them. The hair on your arms rises.

There are storms, of a kind. Where a large region of panel has gone dim, the stalks beneath it starve and their oil goes cloudy and they begin to produce ribbon of poor quality, brittle, laced with small grey flaws. The gantries reject it — some old standard still enforced by mechanism — and the rejected ribbon is not carried away but drops, and it drifts, and in a strong front it lifts and travels as a soft dry blizzard that can go for days, filling the channels, coating everything in loose fibrous grey. Then the bright regions bake it and it cracks and sublimes and the plain is clean again, or as clean as it gets. Long ago the crop learned to harvest this: you see stalks near the dim zones that have grown lateral combs, fine as gill-plates, to catch the drifting rejected fiber and feed it back into their own structure. This is what erosion looks like here. Not wearing down but reincorporating. Nothing has been lost from this place in a very long time. It has all just been moved.

—

Now the strangeness, which has been present from the first step and simply not named.

The light comes from the ceiling and is fixed into the stalks, and the stalks produce ribbon, and the ribbon is carried away toward the brighter horizon. But the panels have been shining for so long, and the crop has grown so total, that the fixation has begun to run the other way. The stalks, having exhausted every other substrate, have started to grow into the light itself.

You notice this first as a lensing. Between certain rows the air is not haze-white but slightly warped, as if a sheet of very clear oil hung in it, and looking through it the stalks beyond are nearer than they should be, or further, or repeated. Step into one of these sheets and there is a faint resistance, like walking through a cobweb you cannot see, and on the other side the direction of your shadow has changed and the row you were following is now two rows, both of them yours. The shadows themselves have grown. Look down: the shadow a stalk casts on the mat is not flat but very slightly raised, a felt of dark fiber a millimeter thick, and it is growing along the ground at a pace you could measure over an afternoon, and where two shadows meet they have begun to knit. Some of the oldest stalks stand now in shadows so thick they are almost stalks themselves — dim, soft, wrong-angled — a second crop of darkness under the first crop of light.

The ribbon, too, is not entirely what it was. Where the reels have been left too long on their rails, unspooling in the wind, the ribbon has continued its function without its factory: it fixes what falls on it. A reel abandoned near a warp will have wound into itself a stretch of distance, and if you unroll it — it is heavy; don't — you find the ribbon is longer on the inside than the outside. Reels have been found, or would be found, that contain a few hours. Not a record of hours. The hours. Set your hand on the fibre and your hand is cool and it is earlier.

The horizon where the reels are taken is brighter because this is where the fixed light has accumulated most. The crop is densest there. The stalks stand in contact, then merge, then become a single ribbed wall, and the wall has a slight curve to it that a careful eye reads as the beginning of a very large cylinder, and the cylinder is bright inside. Whatever the ribbon was for, that is where it went, and it is still going, and the plain — continental, cool, dry, sparking faintly in the wind — is the machine that feeds it, or the root that feeds it, and the panels above are the same thing coming around from the other side.

—

Things live here, but not many, and none of them are quite fauna.

There is a maintenance form: a low, flat, many-legged thing the size of a hand, pale, made of the same ribbed matter, that runs along the channel-rails checking couplings. It does not eat. It moves by the same slow hydraulic build-and-release as the stalks and is probably a stalk, in the way that a seed is a tree. You will see one every few kilometers. It does not notice you, but if you stand in a channel it waits, without impatience, until you leave.

There is something that lives in the deep shadow-felt under the oldest stalks. Nobody has described it. It shows as a movement of the dark against the dark, and where it has passed the felt is combed smooth. It may be the shadow's own maintenance form. It may be the shadow.

And once, perhaps twice in a lifetime of walking, something comes down from the ceiling — a drift of shed panel, or a thing shaped like a shed panel, pale and plate-like and turning, that descends over an hour into the crop and is received: the stalks around its landing lean in, the combs open, and by the next cycle of the gantries there is a new socket in the rail with a new pale spiral in it, and the dimmed patch of sky above is, very slightly, less dim.

—

Stand still long enough and the place settles around you.

The hush of a front moving through, two rows over. The click of your own collar as static leaves it. The ceiling seamed with grey lines, unmoving. Powder on your sleeves that will not brush off because it is not on your sleeves but grown into them, the first few fibers, taking the light. Your shadow at your feet, a little thicker than it was, pointing toward the bright horizon that is also behind you.

Nothing is wet. Nothing is loud. Nothing has been lost. The crop is making its ribbon and the ribbon is making the world and the world is a floor of pale columns under a white sky, cool and enormous and very quiet, going about the long dry business of becoming itself.
