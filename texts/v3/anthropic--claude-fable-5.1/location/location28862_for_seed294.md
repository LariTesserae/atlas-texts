The plateau is a bowl, and the bowl is full.

Somewhere above four thousand meters, where the air is thin enough that the sky over the water is a hard, unbroken blue-grey and never seems to hold a sun, a valley the size of a small province has been filled. Not by a river, or not only by a river: by snowmelt, by centuries of rain that had nowhere lower to go. The water is the color of a knife. It is cold in the way that high water is cold — not freezing, but so close to it that the surface has a viscous, oily look, and where the wind lifts spray it seems to hang a moment before it falls.

And the wind does lift spray. The wind is the first thing. It comes over the eastern rim in a steady, ripping pressure that has not stopped for as long as anything here has been alive, and the whole surface of the drowned valley runs before it in short grey ridges, tens of kilometers of chop, a field being combed. Nothing on the surface holds still. Where the water meets the rim it slaps and sucks against stone.

The stone is the second thing, and it is everything.

There is no rock in this valley that has not been handled. The rim is not a ridge but a wall — a wall so long and so thick that it took a mountain to make it, and it was made from the mountain. Below the waterline the walls step down in terraces, each one a lip of fitted blocks, some the size of a body and some the size of a house, laid without mortar and without machines, split along their grain and set so closely that the joints hold water. There are stairways that descend forty meters and end in a paved floor; there are cisterns within cisterns, square shafts opening into the terraces like the mouths of wells; there are causeways that run straight out across the basin at a depth of ten meters, wide enough for six to walk abreast, joining nothing to nothing, their ends softened into rubble. Even the floor of the valley, where it can be seen at all, is paved. The builders quarried the ground they stood on and laid it back down flat.

They had no tools that survive. What they had, it seems, was time, and hands, and the patience to fit stone to stone until the shape of a whole highland had been rewritten. Then the water came up over their work and stopped when it reached the rim, and it has stayed at the rim since, and the wind has been trying to empty it ever since.

It is old in the way that only stone under cold water gets old. Nothing here rots. The edges of the blocks are not sharp any more — the endless turbulence has taken the corners, filed the stairs into ramps, rounded the causeways like the backs of animals — but the plan is intact. The place has settled into the form it will keep. Whatever the valley was for, it has finished being for, and it is now simply what it is: a stone bowl at the top of the world, and a body of water in it that will not lie still.

Because below the surface it is not calm either. That is the thing you expect and do not get. In the first few meters the light is a milky grey-green, dim, the daylight scattered by a haze of rock flour that has never once been allowed to settle. Then the cold gets into the water in curtains — dense sheets that form along the walls at night and slide down the terraces like something poured, kicking the silt up off every step as they go. Visibility opens and closes. One moment the causeway is there, a long dark spine running off into fog; the next the fog has swallowed it and there is only a moving greyness with a current in it, and the current is not the current that was there a breath ago. The water in the deep shafts turns over. Bubbles of something come up from the cistern mouths and break apart before they reach the surface. The whole basin breathes in slow, violent, invisible weather, and the stones hold.

And the water is full.

Nothing grows here — nothing green, nothing with a stem. The cold and the grinding silt and the mineral sting of the water have seen to that. There is at most a dark, thin film on the sheltered undersides of blocks, more stain than life. But the animals came anyway, or were here first, and they are everywhere, at a density that has nothing to do with plenty and everything to do with movement: they are scattered, and they never stop.

Amphipods, first — pale, hunched, the size of grains of rice — not in patches but in weather of their own, drifting clouds of them that blow across the terraces and pile in the lee of the walls exactly as sand would. Where the light strikes them they glitter briefly and are gone. Fish move through the amphipod clouds, small ones, colorless, in shoals so large they behave like single bodies: a shoal will pour down a stairway, fill a cistern to its brim in a rolling, silent boil, then reverse and pour back out with no signal that can be seen. Larger fish sit in the joints of the masonry with only their heads out, grey against grey. In the shallowest terraces there are frogs, big, loose-skinned, with a look of having been folded up wet and left, who sit on the submerged steps in the dimness and do not move for hours and then are simply somewhere else.

And there are the builders that still build. Along every terrace there are small fish, hardly a hand long, who spend their lives carrying pebbles in their mouths. They lift them from one place and set them down in another; they make low rings and mounds and clear little courtyards of bare paving; and at night, when the cold water comes down the walls and the currents shift, all of it goes — the pebbles scatter, the courtyards fill — and in the morning they begin again. There is not a loose stone in the valley that has not been moved a thousand times. Only the great blocks stay where they were laid. The place is being rebuilt constantly and is never different.

On the surface, where the wind is, there are birds: long-necked divers that ride the chop and vanish and come up thirty meters off with something silver, and thin waders that stand in the flooded top terrace up to their bellies in the slap of the waves, leaning into the gale, waiting. When they take off they do not go far. There is nowhere to go. Beyond the rim the ground falls away for a very long distance, and the wind has swept it clean.

Listen and you hear almost nothing. At the surface the wind is a roar that the ear gives up on within an hour, and under it the roar becomes a low, tremendous hum with no direction. Below that, in the grey water, there are only small sounds: the tick of amphipods against stone. A pebble set down. A pebble knocked loose and rolling two steps and stopping. Once in a while, from very deep, a hollow shifting knock as some block that has been thinking about it for a thousand years moves a finger's width. Nothing echoes. The silt drinks it.

This is what the place is, then. A made thing, finished. Water on top of it that has never been quiet and never will be. Creatures who move everything that can be moved and cannot touch anything that cannot. It is dim at noon and dim at dusk and dark, entirely, at night, and the cold goes down the walls, and the fish empty and fill the cisterns, and the wind comes over the eastern rim with the sound of the sky being torn slowly along a seam, and in the morning the small stones are all somewhere else, and the valley is exactly where it was.
