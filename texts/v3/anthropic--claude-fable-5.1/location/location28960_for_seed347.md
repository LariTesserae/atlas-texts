From above it looks like a wound that has been dressed with green. The plain runs flat for miles, thin grass over pale rock, wind, nothing to stop the eye — and then the ground simply isn't there. A trench, perhaps two kilometres long and three or four hundred metres across, dropping away with edges so raw they still show the colour of the inside of the stone. Nothing has softened them. The limestone at the lip is white and angular, snapped rather than worn, and where the roof of the old river cave gave way the fracture planes still stand as clean as the faces of a broken plate. It happened recently, in geological terms — somewhere between a grandparent's lifetime and a few of them. The cave had been swallowing a river for a very long time, dissolving its own ceiling from beneath, and one wet season the ceiling came down along most of its length in a series of falls, some of which the animals on the plain would have heard as a long low complaint under their feet, and then the river was open to the sky.

Cold air does what cold air does in a hollow. It slides down the walls at night and pools, and the trench holds it. Even in the warm months the floor is many degrees colder than the plain, and most mornings the whole thing is filled to the brim with fog so dense and level that from a distance it looks like a lake of milk sitting in the ground. Around midday the fog thins from the top down and you can see into it. It never entirely leaves. It condenses on everything, so that the whole place is permanently beaded and dripping, and the drip is close to the only sound.

---

Descent is possible only where the collapse produced ramps of rubble — great slopes of blocks the size of rooms, tilted, wedged, some of them still creaking against each other when the ground is very wet. Everything down the ramp is covered. This is the first thing you understand about the place: there is no bare rock. Not because the rock is old and worn, but because in cold, saturated, shaded conditions moss moves faster than stone does. The blocks wear coats of it a hand deep — liverworts in slick dark sheets, cushion mosses in domes, hair mosses standing upright like a pelt. Ferns come out of every crack. Their fronds are stiff and bluish and hold water in their curls. The green is so total that the angularity of the blocks reads through it only as a kind of upholstery over furniture: you can see the sharp corners under the moss the way you can see a knee under a blanket.

The walls weep. Groundwater that used to feed the cave still arrives from the plain along the same bedding planes, and now it emerges into the open air along horizontal seams the full length of the trench. In some places it runs in sheets, so thin they look like polish; in others it drops from a projecting lip in strings of beads, or gathers in a mossy sponge and squeezes out below in a steady clear trickle. The seepage is cold enough to numb a hand. It has already begun to lay down calcite over the moss it runs through, so there are places where the moss has been turned, from the inside out, into pale stone lace that is still growing green at the tips.

---

And on the walls, in every band where the rock is firm enough to hold and wet enough to work, is the city.

It is made by birds, mostly, or something very like birds — small, dark, quick, coming and going out of the fog in silence. Their nests are mud, worked from the clay that the collapse exposed, carried up beakful by beakful and pressed into place, each cell a bottle with its neck pointing outward. But they do not build singly. They build against each other, generation on generation, so that the wall is faced with a continuous crust of galleries — cells fused to cells, layered, the older ones underneath and roofed over by the newer, until the accumulation stands out from the rock a metre or more in the thickest districts. There are streets in it, of a kind: horizontal ledges where the birds land and walk before entering, and vertical seams where water has been allowed, or trained, to run down without flooding the cells, the mud on either side of these seams thickened into little levees. In the oldest tiers the mud has taken on calcite from the seepage and gone hard and pale, and moss has colonised the ledges, so the lower city looks less like nesting and more like a ruin that has been kept up. At the height of the season every cell is occupied. Every ledge has a bird on it. The wall is a wall of eyes.

There is no room between things. That is the character of the place more than any single feature. The moss is packed to the point of layering over itself; the ferns grow through the moss; the galleries cover the rock; the birds fill the galleries; snails — enormous numbers of them, dark, glossy, thumb-sized — travel the moss in slow crowds; and beneath the snails, in the moss itself, there is a whole further population of small pale things that never see light. On the ramps of rubble every gap between blocks is somebody's burrow. There is nowhere to put a foot that is not already the property of a living thing.

---

The floor is where the river is, and the river is where the other builders are.

The stream that made the cave comes into the trench at the upper end, where the collapse stops and the roof still stands, so that the river appears out of a dark arch, already cold and already carrying its load of pale silt. It would have run straight down the trench floor in a narrow braided channel. It does not. Something has taken it in hand.

At intervals of forty or fifty metres the whole length of the trench, the river is checked by dams. They are made of sticks — the stiff stems of the tall shrubby willow-things that grow along the water — woven, packed with mud and moss and stones, curved gently upstream, and they hold. Behind each dam the water spreads into a pool, shallow, still, milky with silt that settles out so that each successive pool is clearer than the last. The dams are high enough that the pools step down the valley like a flight of stairs, and the drop over each one is deliberately broken into many small threads of water rather than one fall, which is why the river makes almost no noise. Whatever built them did not want noise, or did not want the water to cut, or both. Overflow channels have been left at the sides, lined with flat stones pressed into the mud. Where a channel has begun to erode its bank, there is new material heaped there, still green, still leafed.

The builders are heavy, low, dense-furred animals that move almost entirely in the water and are almost never seen moving. You see the result of them: a ripple with nothing at the front of it, a wet track up a bank, a stick being pulled slowly along the surface of a pool by a submerged something. They are silent, and they live in lodges of the same woven stuff built into the dams themselves, so that the dams are also houses, and the houses are also spillways. They have been at this for as long as the trench has been open. Their oldest dams are grown solid with moss and have small trees on them and are indistinguishable from islands until you notice that they are all the same shape.

Between the pools, on the flats the dams have created, there is a meadow — not grass but a dense low sedge and a spreading cold-country herb with thick leaves — and the meadow is grazed. Something the size of a small deer, grey, with a coat dense enough to shed the constant wet, moves through the fog in small groups and eats with its head down and does not look up often. When it does look up it is because there is a stalker in the reeds: a long-legged, long-necked bird the colour of the fog itself, which hunts the pools for the pale fish that live in them and will take a snail-crowded frog or a bird fallen from the galleries or anything else that is slow. The mud along the pool edges is written over with tracks — the deer-things', the heron-thing's, small hands with claws, and a great many that have been made and then filled with water and then made again on top.

---

The rules of the place are few and firm.

It is cold. It is always colder here than the world it is set into, and the cold falls from the rim and settles, so the floor is colder than the walls and the pools coldest of all. There is frost on the sedge some mornings when the plain above has none.

It is wet. Water comes from above as rain and fog, from the sides as seepage, and from below as the river; and none of it is allowed to hurry. The moss holds it, the dams hold it, the fog holds it in the air. Everything drips, and nothing runs.

It is young. The rock is sharp under the green, and it is still moving. Some nights there is a fall — a block that has been working loose for years finally lets go from a wall and comes down through the galleries and the ferns onto the rubble slopes, and the sound of it is enormous and brief in that muffling air, and by morning the birds have begun to rebuild over the scar and by the end of the season there is moss on the new faces. The city is repaired the way the meadow regrows. Nobody mourns the lost district; the new one is simply started.

It is quiet. Not silent — it drips, and the water threads over the dams, and there is the soft tearing sound of the grazers pulling sedge, and now and then the dry clatter of a bird's wings shaking off wet — but everything that lives here has learned to be quiet, because sound carries in the fog and everything that lives here is either hunting or hunted. The birds do not call. They go in and out of their galleries in a steady soundless traffic like bees. The heron-thing stands so still that the fog beads on it.

---

Over a day it changes. The morning is white and nothing can be seen and the drip is loudest. By late morning the fog has sunk to the level of the pools and the walls stand clear above it, the galleries alive with traffic, the moss shining. Afternoon is the brightest hour — a grey, even, shadowless light that comes down from the whole width of the sky and makes the wet surfaces glow rather than glitter. Then rain, usually, brief and cold, running off the rim in temporary falls that hit the rubble and are drunk instantly by the moss. Then the fog begins to form again, first over the pools, then rising. At dusk the deer-things come down to the water and the builders come out and there is a great deal of movement that is almost entirely without sound, and then the cold pours in and everything settles into itself and waits.

Over a year it changes more. The rains come in a season and the river rises and the dams take the strain, and some go, and the pools below fill with silt and are rebuilt. The birds leave for part of the year and the city stands empty and the heron-things walk the ledges picking at what was left. Ice forms on the pools and the builders live under it. Then the birds come back, and the first thing they do is repair.

Over a century it will change most. The edges will round. The walls will lean back. The moss and the calcite, working together, will bury the lowest galleries entirely in stone. The dams will become terraces and the terraces will become soil and the willow-things will become a wood. It is happening now, at the speed of living things, in a hole in the ground that is still surprised to be open to the sky — a city with no makers' names, kept, without a plan and without a single tool, by everything that lives in it.
