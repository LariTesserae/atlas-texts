The country around it is flat and cold and gives nothing away. Fen pasture, drained a long time ago and then half-abandoned to rushes; a sky the colour of pewter that is nevertheless very bright, the way winter skies at low latitudes are bright, lit from everywhere and from nowhere. You could walk this land for a day and see only the horizon, a few thorn trees leaning east, the white glint of standing water in the wheel ruts. The ground is a few feet above the sea and has been for as long as there has been a sea.

Then, without a rise or a marker, the ground opens.

It is a hollow, roughly round, perhaps thirty paces across at the lip and narrowing slightly as it goes down, so that the whole thing has the proportions of a very wide well or a very shallow room. Limestone, probably — something soluble, something that gave way underneath a long time ago and let the ground slump down into a pocket. That was before anyone was here. Everything since has been the work of hands.

Because the walls of the hollow are built. Not lined — built, the way a town is built, upward and inward and over itself. Drystone, no mortar, no dressed faces: the flat grey stone of the country split along its bedding and stacked, course on course, into terraces and cells and stairs and lintelled openings. The first tier sits on the floor of the hollow. The second is set back onto the first's roof. The third onto the second. Seven or eight tiers in all, depending on how you count the half-levels, rising nearly to the lip, so that from the pasture above you look down into the top storey of something. Ladders of split ash, silver with age, lean between the tiers. Narrow steps go up the corners in flights of nine and eleven. There are cisterns — square, stone-lined, set into the treads of the wider terraces — and channels no broader than a thumb cut along the tops of walls to feed them. There are shelves, and hearths without soot, and doorways a person could pass through only bent double, and behind the doorways there are chambers going back into the earth, dark at the throat and then, oddly, not dark, because the stone inside is pale and the light gets in somehow around the corners.

Nothing is more than three feet from something else. The city has been packed into its hole the way a wasp packs a nest, every gap filled, every surface used. Where there is a wall there is a niche in it, and where there is a niche there is something in the niche.

And everything that is not stone is green.

This is the first thing that unsettles: the lushness in the cold. It is very cold down there — colder than the pasture above, the way a cellar is cold, a stillness of air that has settled and will not be lifted. Breath hangs. Frost furs the north-facing ledges and never seems to go. And yet every joint between the stones has moss in it, thick as a thumb, dark green and bright green and a green so yellow it is nearly gold; liverworts spread flat and glossy on the damp treads like wet leather; ferns come out of the drystone in fans and shelves, hart's-tongue and polypody and spleenwort, their fronds rigid with cold, beaded on the underside with water. Lichen has taken every stone face that the moss has not — grey-green rosettes, black tar-spots, an orange crust on the lintels that catches the light like paint. Ivy has come down over the lip of the hollow and made its way along the top tier, but it has not gone further, as if it knows where it is not wanted. Down at the floor, in the last tier's courtyards, there are plants that have no business being green in this cold: a sorrel with red-veined leaves, a low clumped saxifrage in flower, the small white stars of it open and stiff.

Nothing is dead. That is what it takes a while to notice. There are no brown fronds, no rotted stalks, no drifts of fallen leaves in the corners of the terraces. The vegetation is all at once and all alive and has been, one begins to suspect, for a very long time.

The water is what keeps it. There is no stream here, no spring, no rain worth the name — the pasture above gets what the fen gets, which is a little, seldom. What the hollow has is condensation. Its air is cold and still and the stone is colder, and the moisture that is in the air comes out of it onto every surface. The walls sweat. The undersides of lintels hang with drops that do not fall and then, at long intervals, do. The moss holds the water and lets it go by the drop into the channels, and the channels lead it to the cisterns, and the cisterns are full — every one of them, to within a finger of the rim, water so clear and so still that the square of pale sky in each looks solid enough to lift out.

The light is the other unsettling thing. It comes straight down out of the bright sky, and the stone, being pale and wet, throws it back upward, and so do the cistern surfaces, and so, faintly, do the frost and the water-beads on the ferns. There is no direction to the light and therefore very nearly no shadow. The doorways are a grey a little deeper than the walls; the undersides of the ledges are lit from below. It has the quality of light in snow, or in fog — an evenness, a bleaching — except that the air is perfectly clear. You can see every stone. You can see the grain in the split ash of the ladders and the single hairs on the moss. It is not gentle light. It is the light of a place that has nothing to hide because it has finished hiding everything.

The city is inhabited, though not by whoever built it.

Every niche is a tenancy. Wrens — many wrens, more than there should be — sit in the smaller holes, one to a hole, feathers puffed against the cold, watching. A slow-worm lies along the inner edge of a lintel where the frost does not reach. Toads have the lower doorways, one in the mouth of each, their throats moving. Snails are on everything — the big banded ones, the small glossy dark ones, the tiny white ones like grains of rice — packed into the mossy joints in dozens, their shells beaded like the ferns. There are mice in the second tier, or the signs of them, and in the third a stoat that comes out of one hole and goes into another without apparent purpose. A heron stands on the topmost terrace on the south side, grey against grey, utterly still. Below it, in a chamber whose doorway has been widened by digging, there is the black smell of a badger and, deep in, the badger.

None of them make a sound.

Nor does anything else. The hollow takes sound and does not give it back. The moss and the packed stone deaden it; the still cold air will not carry it; the lip of the hollow shuts out the wind and whatever the wind might have brought. A drop falls from a lintel into a cistern and the sound it makes is so small and so quickly gone that it seems less a sound than the idea of one. The wrens do not sing. The heron does not move. Your own footfall on the moss of a terrace is absorbed before it has reached your ears. If you speak, the word goes about a foot from your mouth and stops. It is not that the place is quiet. It is that the place is silent in the way a room is silent when everyone in it has agreed not to speak.

The rules of the world, as far as they can be made out from standing in it:

Nothing here changes. The cisterns are full and they stay full; the channels drip and the level does not rise. The frost is on the north ledges and it does not melt in the brightness and does not thicken in the cold. The saxifrage is in flower and does not go over. The wrens are in their holes and the heron is on its terrace and when, later, you look again, the wrens are in their holes and the heron is on its terrace — not the same wrens, perhaps, or the same, there is no way to tell, but the number is the number. The badger is in its chamber. Something has been arrived at here, some balance between stone and water and cold and green and creature, and the balance holds, and every part of it is at rest inside its holding.

Things that are put down stay. On a shelf in the fourth tier there is a wooden mallet, ash-handled, its head a single knot of some heavier wood, worn glossy where a hand held it. Moss has grown up around the handle in a collar and stopped there. It has not grown over. Beside the mallet is a bone point, and beside the point a coil of twisted bark cord, and the cord is not rotten. In a niche on the second tier, three flat stones are stacked in a way that means something and the meaning is not available. There are shell-lamps — scallop shells with a black residue in the hollow — set at intervals along the ledges, and it is not clear how a lamp would ever have been needed here or whether anything in them has ever burned.

The builders worked with what they had. Stone split along its bedding with wedges; ash and hazel cut with flint and fire-hardened; cord of bark and sinew; bone for the fine work. The mallet on the shelf is the most complicated object in the place. And yet the walls are true, the corners are plumb, the steps are cut to a rise that a body finds without thinking, and the channels fall at an angle so slight it can hardly be seen and yet the water goes where it is meant to go and nowhere else. These are people who knew stone the way they knew their own hands, and who had time — or who found, in this hole, a place where time behaved differently and they could take as much of it as the work required.

Where they went is not a question the place will answer. There are no bones, no hearths with ash, no midden. Perhaps they finished. Perhaps that is what this is: a thing finished, a settlement that reached the state it was built to reach, and the builders, having brought it there, stepped up the last flight and out onto the pasture and left it to the wrens and the toads and the cold bright silence, the way a potter leaves a pot.

Or perhaps they are still here, in the state of everything else here, and the stoat that goes from one hole to the next is a citizen going about its business, and the heron is a watchman, and the badger in its chamber is a magistrate of long standing, and the whole crowded, sodden, frost-rimmed, lichen-painted, moss-jointed, ash-laddered warren of it is a town at its full and proper population, going on as it has always gone on, requiring nothing from anyone, having no more to say.

You climb out. The flat country is there, the thorn trees leaning, the pewter sky. Behind you the ground closes over without a rise or a marker. The pasture is bright and cold and windy, and the wind is loud, and you find you had forgotten it could be.
