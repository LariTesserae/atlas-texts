The mountain is still growing. That's the first thing to understand about the pit — the peak it sits in was not here two centuries ago, and the rock around you has never known rain. It came up too fast for weather to catch it. The edges of every fracture are still knife-edged, glassy, the color of a gun barrel, and if you brush a wall with a glove the glove comes away with a fine dark dust that has never been wet in its existence and never will be.

You enter from above. There is no other way in. Near the summit — high enough that the sky at noon is a bruised violet and the horizon curves — a gas bubble the size of a chapel once cooled inside the andesite and then, at some point in the mountain's brief convulsive life, the roof of it split. The split is a throat perhaps four meters wide. A ladder of welded rebar goes down it. Below the ladder, the throat opens, and the pit is there, and the pit is full.

Full is the word. Full to saturation, the way a hive is full. The bubble's interior is maybe thirty meters across at the widest and perhaps forty deep, and every surface of it has been colonized by construction: galleries bolted to the curved walls in ascending tiers, catwalks that ring the void at seven or eight levels, gantries, ducting, cable runs strapped in bundles as thick as a thigh. None of it is quite plumb. Everything leans a little inward, toward the center, because the center is where the work is, and the work is the reason the pit exists at all as a habitable place.

The mountain is trying to close.

That's what the machines are for. Not mining, though there's some of that; not refining, though the kilns are real. The whole industry of the pit — the hydraulic jacks the size of tree trunks, the screw props with their grease-black threads, the cross-braces of forged steel wedged from wall to wall — exists to hold the bubble open against rock that is still under compression, still settling, still being shouldered upward by whatever is happening in the crust below. Every day the walls move. Not much. Millimeters. But the props groan and must be re-tensioned; the jacks must be bled and re-pumped; a brace that was true on Tuesday is bowed by Friday and has to be pulled and a fresh one driven in before it fails. The pit is a wound the mountain keeps trying to heal and a small town of machinery keeps prying back open.

Why anyone would want it open is a question the place does not answer directly. You notice the kilns. You notice the racks of crystal near the vents, growing in the dry breath of the rock — sulfur, or something adjacent to sulfur, translucent and amber and the size of fists. You notice that some of the ducts lead down, below the lowest catwalk, into a dark that the lamps do not reach. Perhaps that's it. Perhaps the pit is just the vestibule.

It's dim. The throat above admits a column of gray daylight that reaches about halfway down before the haze swallows it — a haze of rock dust and vent gas and the exhaust of small engines. Below that, sodium lamps in wire cages give everything the color of old brass. Indicator lights blink on the jacks: green, green, amber, green. Somewhere a welding arc flares blue for two seconds and goes out and leaves a violet ghost on your retina. Your eyes never fully adjust because the light never holds still; the machines shift, the shadows shift, the column of daylight tilts as the afternoon moves across the summit.

And it's quiet, in a way that takes time to understand. The air up here is too thin to carry much. A hammer blow on a brace two levels up arrives as a small, flat *tik*, as if heard through a wall. The engines — squat, oil-fired, driving the hydraulic pumps — thud rather than roar, and mostly you feel them, a pulse in the catwalk grating under your boots, a tremor in the handrail. When a prop fails, which happens, the sound is a single sharp report like a branch breaking and then a long low complaint of steel taking up a load it wasn't meant for. Then silence, and the small sounds of people moving quickly. Mostly what you hear is your own breath, and the whisper of gas escaping from the fumaroles, and the birds.

The birds are everywhere.

Choughs. Hundreds of them, black, red-legged, yellow-billed, who found the pit the way they find everything warm and enclosed at altitude. They roost in the gantries. They walk the catwalks with a proprietary swagger. They steal — bolts, washers, gloves, anything bright — and they've learned to time the jack-bleeds, because the venting hydraulic fluid draws insects and the insects draw the birds. Below them, on the walls, the rock is crawling: jumping spiders no bigger than a fingernail, black and hunched, thousands of them, living on springtails and on the moths that get blown up the mountain in the thermals and fall stunned into the pit's warmth. Mites in the grease. Small pale beetles in the kiln ash. A colony of pikas has established itself in the void behind the second-level ducting and can be heard, faintly, chittering, when the engines idle. There are lammergeiers that come to the throat at dusk and look down and do not enter, and there is one that did enter, once, and now lives on the lowest gantry with a wing that won't fold right, fed by the crew on cut-offs of whatever they eat.

It is a great deal of life for a place with no plants. The only thing here that photosynthesizes is nothing; the nearest green is three thousand meters below. What grows on the pit's walls instead is a crust — orange near the vents, fading to gray-white and then to a sooty black — that is not lichen and not moss but some bacterial film that eats the sulfur and the warmth and asks for nothing else. It is the base of the pit's food chain, insofar as it has one: the springtails graze it, the spiders eat the springtails, the birds eat the spiders and the moths, and the whole vertical ecology hangs on the heat that the mountain leaks and the machines keep accessible.

Because it is cool, not cold. That's the other thing the pit offers. Outside, on the summit, the air will kill you in an hour and the rock is cold enough to weld skin. Inside, the vents keep the bubble at something like a cellar in autumn. You wear gloves and a jacket. Your breath doesn't fog — the air is too dry for that. Nothing fogs. Nothing drips. Snow that falls into the throat sublimes before it reaches the second catwalk, and the crew carry their water up in sealed drums and guard it like currency.

There are perhaps forty of them at any time. You see them in glimpses — figures in dust masks and headlamps moving along the tiers, two of them wrestling a new brace into position with a chain hoist, one crouched at a jack with a pressure gauge, one on the lowest level doing something with the crystal racks. They don't talk much. It isn't worth the breath. They communicate by tapping on pipe, by hand signals across the void, by a system of colored lamps whose grammar you never learn. They move like people who have long since stopped being surprised by the ground shifting under them.

And it does shift. Stand still for ten minutes on any catwalk and you'll feel it: a tremor, a sense that the whole cage of steel has flexed and resettled. Dust sifts down from above in a fine continuous rain. Every so often a piece of the wall lets go — a plate of dark glass the size of a door — and falls through three levels of gantry, and the choughs erupt in a black cloud and settle again within a minute. The crew look up, assess, go back to work. Somewhere a new prop is already being carried toward the gap.

What comes to you, standing there, is that this is not a place that will last, and everyone in it knows this, and the knowledge is what gives the pit its peculiar energy — not frantic, exactly, but *unceasing*. The mountain is still rising. In another century the throat will have closed or the bubble will have been squeezed to a seam or the whole summit will have shed itself in one great shrug. The jacks buy time. The kilns run on borrowed time. The birds, who don't know this, roost on the warm steel as if it were a cliff that had always been there, and the spiders hunt across a crust that grew over rock that was molten when their great-grandmothers were alive, and the one lammergeier on the lowest gantry watches the daylight column swing across the pit and does not seem to want to leave.

You climb back up the rebar ladder. The last thing you feel, before your head comes out into the killing air of the summit, is the ladder itself flex slightly under you — the throat, tightening, by some fraction of a millimeter, as the mountain goes on becoming itself.
