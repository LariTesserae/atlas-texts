The first thing is the sound, and the sound is the last thing too, so it may as well be said now: a single note, held. Not loud the way a factory is loud, with its clatter and gaps. Loud the way a wall is a wall. You feel it in the fillings of your teeth before you feel it in your ears, and after a minute your ears give up and hand the job to the rest of you.

The room is not large. Nine paces one way, eleven the other. It is a chamber inside something, and what it is inside of is too big to see from here. There is a sense of it, though — the way a small bone knows it belongs to a large animal. The floor is iron plate, cast in sections and bolted at the seams, the bolt heads still carrying the faint hexagonal sharpness of the forge. Nothing has worn them. Nothing has rusted them. The air here holds no water at all, and iron without water simply waits.

High. You know it from the way your breath comes shallow and from the light, which enters through a row of angled louvers along the upper wall — white, hard, unbroken by anything green or gray between here and the sun. Where the light strikes the plate floor it does not warm it. The cold is a steady, mineral cold, the kind that belongs to altitude and to metal. Frost does not form because there is nothing to make frost from. If you breathe on the wall a ghost appears and is gone in the same instant, taken by the dry air like a debt collected.

The note comes from a pipe. It runs floor to ceiling in the center of the chamber, a cylinder of riveted steel as wide as three men standing shoulder to shoulder, and it hums. Somewhere below, something turns at a fixed speed — a turbine, a fan, a shaft that has never once varied — and the pipe is the length it is, and the air inside it is the temperature it is, and so this note and no other. There is a valve wheel bolted to a stub off the pipe at chest height, spoked, painted a red that has never faded because there is no weather in here to fade it. It has not been turned. There is no evidence it can be turned. Its purpose seems to be to stand there being a valve wheel, the way the pipe's purpose is to stand there being loud.

Against the far wall there is a bench. Nothing on it. Between the bench and the pipe is a floor drain, dry, its grate perfectly clean. Between the drain and the louvered wall, an eyebolt set in the plate, with nothing tied to it. These four things — pipe, wheel, bench, drain, eyebolt — and the wide iron floor around them, are the whole of the furniture. The emptiness between them is not an absence. It is measured. Someone laid this room out on paper before it was cast, and the spaces were drawn in first.

Here is where it becomes strange.

The floor is not entirely bare. In a ring around the base of the pipe, and in a second broader ring some distance out, and in odd lobed islands beyond that, fine gray grit has gathered into shapes. Fronds. Rosettes. Things like the skeletons of ferns, like moss seen too close, like the branching of a lichen drawn by an engineer who had heard of lichens but never touched one. They stand a finger's height off the plate, and they do not move. You crouch to look, and the sound comes up through your knees, and you understand: the grit is metal dust, the slow filing-away of the machine's own bearings, sifting up from below through the drain and the seams. And the note has been sorting it. Every particle that lands is shaken by the standing wave until it finds a place where the floor is still — a node — and there it stays. The pattern is the sound, made visible and left to grow. It has been growing for as long as the note has been sounding, and it has never changed shape, because the note has never changed.

You could call it vegetation. It is patient, it branches, it colonizes, it is arranged by an invisible law into the only forms that law permits. It is gray and cold and made of engine.

There is other life, but only just. In the seam where the louvered wall meets the floor, where a little of the morning's warmth pools before the metal gives it back, three real plants grow. They are very small. Something like a sedge, something like a dwarf cinquefoil, one thing you cannot name with leaves like scales. They are the color of plants that receive too much light and too little of everything else. Their seeds came up through the drain, or in on someone's boot a long time ago, and they have found the one place a seed could hold, and they tremble. Constantly. Every leaf vibrating at the frequency of the pipe. They have never known a still moment and they have never known a wet one and they are, in their way, doing well.

Nothing else lives here. No insect could cross this floor without being shaken into a node. No bird could bear the note. The room does not miss them.

Stand for a while and the sound stops being a thing that happens and becomes the place itself. It has no beginning you could point to and it makes no promise of an end. The louvers hold their fixed angle. The light moves across the plate with the day, the only motion in the room, and even that is a kind of stillness, since tomorrow it will move exactly the same way. The red wheel waits. The gray ferns stand at attention around the humming steel. Your breath goes up and vanishes. You have not adjusted to the cold; you have simply stopped counting it, the way you stopped counting the sound.

When you leave — through the low hatch in the wall opposite the bench, the only way in or out — the silence outside will hurt worse than the note did. That is the last rule of the place, and it is not written anywhere.
