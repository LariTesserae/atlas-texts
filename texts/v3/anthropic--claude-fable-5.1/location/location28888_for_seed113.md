Somewhere below the water table, under a hill that does not know it has been hollowed, there is a room the size of a cathedral, and the room is a city, and the city is not finished.

You would come into it from above, through a shaft that is more chimney than stairwell, and the first thing that would meet you is the heat. Not the heat of sun but the heat of something setting — the deep, even warmth that comes off a slab of concrete a day after it is poured, or off a compost heap, or off a wall with a fever. The air is thick enough to feel against the face. Water has come out of it everywhere: on the shaft walls, on your sleeves, beading on the rail that runs down beside the steps, a rail that is smooth and cool and slightly too soft under the hand, as though it has not entirely made up its mind to be metal.

Then the shaft opens and the room takes you.

It is a single vault, perhaps eighty meters across, perhaps sixty high at the crown, and it is nearly empty. That is the first fact of it. There is more air in here than anything else. The dome is not carved; nothing in this place has been carved. It has the look of something laid down in layers, the way travertine is laid down at a hot spring, ring on ring on ring — except that travertine is the color of old bone and this is the color of wet slate, and travertine is ridged and stepped and this is *fair*, smooth as a cast, drawn upward in ribs that meet at the top like the fingers of two hands. Where the ribs join there is a slit, and through the slit comes a grey light that could be the sky, very far away, or could be something the vault is doing on its own.

The floor is not level. It steps down in broad terraces from the wall you came in on, six or seven of them, each about the height of a chest, and the terraces are streets — you understand that before you understand anything else, because they have kerbs, and gutters, and at intervals a rectangle of slightly different texture where a door will be. The edges of everything are knife-sharp. Nothing has been touched. Nothing has been rained on, or walked on, or leaned against. Where two surfaces meet they meet exactly, with the crispness of a thing that came out of a mold an hour ago, and the corners hold small perfect pools of water that have not yet found their way out.

The water is what builds it.

This is the rule of the place, and it takes a while to see because it is quiet in its working and loud in its result. Along the base of every terrace runs a channel, and the channels are full, and the water in them is moving fast and heavy and *thick* — it does not splash the way water splashes. It has a mineral weight to it, a syrup's laziness at the edges, and where it slows in an eddy it leaves something behind. A film. A lip. Over hours the lip becomes a kerb, becomes a step, becomes the sill of a window that no one has asked for yet. The water knows where to become stone. It runs along a channel and then, at a point that is not marked by anything you can see, it climbs — it goes up a wall in a sheet a finger's width thick, against every argument gravity has, and at the top it spreads and stills and sets, and the wall is a hand's breadth taller than it was. You can watch it. You have to hold very still and watch for a long time, but you can.

Whatever tells the water where to go is not in the water. Dip a cup and it is only water, hard and warm and tasting of iron and something like pencil lead. Pour it on the terrace and it puddles and does nothing. Whatever tells the water where to go is in the *room* — in the shape of the place, or in something under the floor, or in the slit of grey light at the crown — and the water is only obedient. There is a story here, if you want one, about a city that was drawn once, at some other scale, by something that thought at a different pace, and then handed to a spring to build. The spring has been building. The spring is not finished.

And this is where the sound comes from. The room is loud. Not with anything you could point to, but with the sum of all of it: the channels running under every kerb, the hiss where the water climbs, and, underneath, a constant irregular ticking, a knocking, a crack every so often like ice on a pond — the setting stone, contracting as it cures, finding its stresses and giving them up. The dome throws all of this back down. Stand in the middle of the lowest terrace and it is like being inside a shell held to the ear, except the shell is roaring, and when a large piece somewhere across the room settles with a sound like a dropped plank, the echo of it comes back to you four times, each one a little softer, each one from a slightly different direction.

The plants have come up in the wet.

No one planted them; or someone planted them so long ago, in the drawing, that it amounts to the same thing. They are the plants that come first anywhere there is warm water and no light to speak of and no one to eat them: liverworts in flat green scales along the gutters; a moss so dark it is nearly black, in cushions on every north-facing sill; a fine grey-green fern with fronds like the skeletons of fish, standing in every crack the fresh stone has opened, thick enough in places to hide the kerb altogether. Down on the lowest terraces, where the channels widen into what will be cisterns or plazas or some third thing the city has not yet decided, there is a rush — a hollow-stemmed, jointed, hip-high rush, pale as bamboo shoots, standing in the shallows so densely you cannot see the water under it. It sways, though there is no wind. The water moves it from below.

They are all wet-shining, all a little too big for what they are, the way plants get when they have never known a dry day or a frost or a mouth. There is nothing here that eats them. There is no insect on the moss, no snail track on the ferns, no bird in the vault and no fish in the channels — you could stand in the roar for an hour and never see a single thing that moves of its own will except the water and what the water moves. The lushness is uncanny because of that. It has the fullness of a garden and the stillness of a museum. Everything grows and nothing stirs.

The light is the last thing you sort out. It is dim, evening-dim, though there is no evening down here. Some of it comes from the slit in the crown. More of it — you notice this only when you stop looking for it — comes from the stone, from the *freshest* stone, the sills laid down this hour and the kerb the water climbed while you were watching. Where the mineral is still setting it holds a faint slate-blue glow, a cold light in a warm room, and as it cures the glow goes out of it. You can read the age of a wall by its light. The upper terraces, the older ones, are nearly dark. The lowest, where the work is thickest, hold a low tidal shimmer like a city seen from a hill at dusk, and the pale rushes stand in it and throw no shadows because it comes from underneath them.

Over a day — and you would need to stay a day to see this — the room breathes. The water rises in the channels, and the air grows so thick that it rains, a little, from the crown; the ribs of the dome weep in long threads that fall sixty meters and land with the sound of applause. Then the water drops and the air clears and the ticking of the stone grows faster, more urgent, and a new lip appears on a new sill. The fern that was in a crack this morning is in a windowsill by night, because the window came up around it. The rush on the lowest terrace loses an arm's width at the edge where the water thickened and set over its roots, and gains an arm's width at the other edge where a channel widened. The city is building around the garden and the garden is spreading into the city and neither of them knows the other is there.

There are stairs that end in the air. There are doorways with nothing behind them yet, an inch of solid slate where the room will be. There is a colonnade, on the third terrace, of eleven columns, standing at the intervals of a twelve-column colonnade — the twelfth has not come up, and the water runs past the place where it should be and does not climb, and there is no way to know if this is patience or a mistake.

And there is all that air. That is what you would remember: not the stone or the ferns or the roar but the emptiness in the middle of it all, the great hot dim volume of nothing with the city curing at its edges, a street plan holding its breath, waiting for the drawing to finish itself and for something to arrive — or waiting, perhaps, for nothing, and content to go on being built.
