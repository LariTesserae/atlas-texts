The door is steel, the kind that has been painted over so many times the rivets have become soft hills under the enamel, and it opens inward onto noise.

Not a sound so much as a pressure. It arrives before anything else — before the eyes have adjusted, before the wet air has found the back of the throat — and it does not resolve into components. There is no rhythm in it to count. It is water falling onto water and onto concrete and onto itself, and it has been falling for one hundred and thirty years without the interruption of a single second, and the room has been shaped by that falling the way a shell is shaped by the animal that made it.

The room is small. That is the first surprise after the noise. Something that loud should need a cavern, and this is a chamber perhaps eleven metres long, seven wide, a little taller than a chapel. It is a box of poured concrete set somewhere inside the body of a much larger works — you came down two flights of iron stairs and along a corridor to reach it, and the wall to your left, if you could put your ear to it and hear anything at all, would be humming with the deeper business of the plant. This is only an outfall. A place where used water is let go.

The water comes from a single mouth. High on the far wall, a circular opening lined in cast iron, perhaps a metre and a half across, its lip flared like the bell of a horn. From it the discharge pours in a solid, glassy column — not spraying, not scattering, but one continuous braided rope of warm water the colour of weak tea, dropping four metres into a basin below. The basin is the floor. The whole floor is a shallow rectangular pool, waist-deep at the far end where the column strikes it and shelving up toward the door until it is only a skin of water over concrete, a centimetre, a film. There is a raised walkway along the right-hand wall, cast in one piece with the room, and this is where you stand. There is nowhere else to stand.

Everything is made. There is no soil in here, no stone that was not mixed and poured, no timber, no living thing that was planted. And yet the room is green.

The spray does it. Where the column hits the basin, a mist rises and stays — the air is saturated, warm, near body temperature, and the mist has nowhere to go but to settle slowly on every surface and drip and re-form. Over a century of that, the concrete has grown a pelt. The walls are furred with moss to the height of a tall man, a dense emerald velvet that holds water like a sponge and weeps it back out in slow lines. Above the moss, where the wall is only damp rather than drenched, liverworts spread in flat overlapping plates like scales. Ferns have taken every horizontal crack and every place where the concrete has spalled — maidenhair mostly, their black wire stems and small green fans nodding constantly in the moving air, and one large hart's-tongue that has rooted in the failed mortar of the iron mouth itself and hangs its strap leaves down beside the column like a curtain half-drawn. Along the walkway's edge, where the rail bolts have rusted and split the concrete, a species of small pale grass that should not be able to live without more light has found the daylight sufficient and grows in tufts, bleached almost yellow.

The light is ordinary. That is worth saying because everything else about the room is so intense. Above, the ceiling is not a ceiling but a grating — heavy cast-iron bars set in a lattice, the top of a shaft that rises through the works and opens, somewhere far above, to the sky. Daylight comes down it grey and even, the light of an overcast afternoon that could be any afternoon of the year. Nothing in the room casts a sharp shadow. The mist diffuses everything. In this light the moss is not a dramatic green but a real one, the green of something alive and slightly bored.

Look at the concrete where the moss does not cover it and you will see how old the place is — not in years, but in the sense of a thing having finished becoming. Concrete newly poured is grey and sharp-edged and holds the ghost of its formwork. This concrete has been washed by warm mineral water for so long that every edge has gone. The lip of the basin is rounded like a river cobble. The walkway's corners have been eaten back to curves. Where the reinforcing bars run close to the surface the water found them long ago, and the concrete over them fell away, and the bars themselves rusted and swelled and then, having nothing more to lose, stopped — they stand out now from the wall like the ribs of something fossilised, brown-black, hard, no longer changing. Everything that was going to fall from this room has fallen and been carried down the drain. Everything that was going to dissolve has dissolved. What is left is the shape water could not take away, and it will hold this shape for a very long time.

The minerals in the water have been building as well as unbuilding. Where the column strikes the basin there is a rim of pale deposit, calcite or something like it, smooth and faintly ridged, the beginning of a thing that in ten thousand more years might be a proper travertine terrace. On the iron mouth, below the flared lip, a stalactite of the same pale stuff has grown — only the length of a finger, and probably every year of the room's life went into it. The pipe is corroded to a soft brown skin but not through; it was cast thick, by people who expected it to be used, and it is still being used.

There is almost nothing in here. That must be understood alongside the noise and the green. One pipe. One pool. One walkway. One drain — a square iron grate at the shallow end of the basin, near the door, where the water leaves as quietly as it can given the circumstances. There are no valves, no gauges, no ladders, no signs. Whatever controls this outflow is elsewhere and always has been. The room does not do anything. It only receives. Between the mouth and the drain there is the column and the mist and the air, and the air is the largest thing in the room, and it is empty.

Something lives here besides the plants, but not much. A frog, a single common frog, sits on the calcite rim at the edge of the falling water with its throat pulsing, and has probably sat there or somewhere near it for its whole life, and its parents' before it; it must have come down the shaft as an egg on a bird's foot, or in a season of flood from the grating above, and found the one place in the works that was warm all winter. It does not move when you look at it. Nothing in this room moves for you. Higher, in the angle where the grating meets the wall, there is the pale scribble of a spider's web that has caught mist and not much else. Once — perhaps — a wren, which will have come down the shaft after the spider and gone up again. That is all the animal life. The frog and the spider and the rumour of a wren, and the moss growing at the speed of moss.

Stand for long enough and you notice the sound has a temperature. It is not the cold roar of a mountain fall. The water is warm, warm enough that steam would rise off it on a cold day if there were ever a cold day in here, and the roar is thick with that warmth, a bathhouse noise, a boiler-room noise, softened at the edges by the moss that eats the high frequencies and leaves the great low body of it intact. You feel it in the walkway through your boots. You feel it in the rail. If you spoke you would not hear yourself and you would soon stop trying, and the room would go on saying the one thing it has always said.

This is what a century and a third of equilibrium looks like. The plant above was built to run and it runs, and its cooling water was always going to be let out somewhere, and this was the somewhere, and nobody has had reason to change it. The flow was set when the pipe was set. The temperature is the temperature of the process. The mist rises at the rate the column makes it and settles at the rate the moss drinks it and the two rates met each other long ago. No gradient runs through this room. It is not decaying and it is not growing. It has arrived.

The door behind you is still open, and beyond it the corridor is dry and comparatively silent, and the noise pours out through the gap like water itself, and it does not diminish, and it does not stop.
