There is no light here, so begin with the air.

It is warm the way the inside of a mouth is warm — not heat exactly, but body-temperature, close, so that after a few minutes your skin stops being able to find the boundary between itself and the room. The air is thick with water that has not yet decided to fall. It stands in the dark at the exact point of saturation. Breathe in and your lungs fill with something nearer to weather than to air; breathe out and your breath makes no cloud you could see, but you feel it join the rest, indistinguishable, absorbed.

You came in through a fissure high on the mountain, well above the last of the trees, where the rock was cold and the wind moved and there was grey daylight on lichen. The crack went down and sideways and closed over your head, and somewhere in the first twenty meters the temperature turned. The cold mountain is above you now. This is a pocket in it, a single sealed chamber, about the size of a large room in a house, and it has been warm and dark and wet for longer than the mountain has had a shape.

Put your hand on the wall.

It is not what you expect from a cave. Water-carved stone is smooth; this is not smooth. Under your palm there are edges — thousands of small, hard, faceted edges, some so fine they catch on the ridges of your fingerprints. The wall is not being worn away. It is being *added to*, or was, or is at some rate too slow to count as happening. Everything in here is deposit. Everything is crystal growing outward from stone, mineral coming out of water that came out of rock. Nothing has been softened, because nothing has ever moved fast enough here to soften anything.

Move your hand a little. The wall becomes something that hangs — a curtain of stone thinner than a fingernail, ringing very faintly when touched, damp on both sides. Move it again and there is a cluster of what feels like frozen coral, branched and re-branched, brittle, each branch ending in a point. This is the density of the place: there is almost nowhere to stand that is not within reach of a dozen of these forms. Columns come down from a ceiling you can barely reach and rise from a floor you cannot see, and between the columns are the branched growths and the curtains and the needle-beds, and between those are smaller versions of the same, and everything is beaded with water. The room is crowded. It is crowded the way a thicket is crowded, but without a single leaf. You move through it sideways, hands first, and you brush against stone at every step, and where you brush, the stone is wet.

The dark is total. Not the dark of a moonless night, which has a grain to it, a residual grey your eye can work with. This is dark with no floor beneath it. Hold your hand in front of your face and there is no hand. After a while the eye stops trying and the rest of you comes forward to do its job.

So: sound.

It is almost none. Somewhere in the room — you cannot fix where, the shapes of the stone confuse it — a drop falls into water. You wait for the next one. It comes eventually, long enough later that you had begun to think you'd imagined the first. The interval is not regular. It is not irregular either. It is just very long. Between drops there is your own blood, and the small wet sounds your clothes make, and under those a kind of pressure that is not sound but that the ear registers anyway: the mass of the mountain on all sides, the hush of a space that has been closed for so long the air itself has settled.

Your echo is wrong. Say something — it comes back almost immediately, softened, as though the room were padded, and it comes back from too close. The stone forms are so many and so near that sound cannot travel far enough to become an echo. It just dies, gently, a few meters out.

There is a pool.

You find it because the floor, which has been rough and slightly upward-crusted the whole way, suddenly is not, and your foot is in warm water to the ankle before the message reaches you. It is the same temperature as the air. Reach down and the surface is perfectly still — not merely calm but *set*, so that your hand entering it feels like an intrusion into something that had been finished. Beneath the surface the water is clear; you know this because your fingers touch nothing until they touch the bottom, which is smooth for the first time in this whole place, smooth and slightly ridged like the inside of a shell. This is where the water goes. Not away. Here. It condenses from the air onto the stone, gathers, runs down the columns and the curtains, and comes to rest in this basin, and from this basin it evaporates back into the air, and the air condenses it back onto the stone. The pool does not rise. It does not fall. It has been exactly this deep for as long as the temperature has been exactly this.

That is the thing you begin to understand, standing shin-deep in the dark: nothing here has a direction. Outside, water runs downhill and stone wears away and time can be read in the wear. Here the water goes around. The heat comes up from somewhere below the mountain, steady, and it has been steady, and the room reached the temperature and the wetness it was going to reach, and then it kept them. The crystals grew out of the water until the water held no more than it gave back, and then they stopped, and they are still stopping, in a present tense with no forward edge. If you came back in a thousand years the drop would fall into the pool at the same unfixable interval, and the curtain would ring at the same faint pitch, and your footprint on the rough floor would not be there because you never left one — the crust is too hard — but the water you displaced would have gone back exactly to where it was.

There are people in here, or the marks of them.

On a shelf of rock beside the pool, at about the height of your hip, your hand finds three stones stacked one on another. They are river-smooth, which nothing else in this room is — they were brought. They have been there long enough that the bottom one has been cemented to the shelf by the same slow deposit that made everything else, and long enough after that for the deposit to stop. Beside the stones there is a small shaped hollow in the rock, and in the hollow your fingers find a residue that is not mineral: greasy, and when you bring your fingers to your nose, faintly and impossibly still carrying the smell of burned fat. A lamp. Someone sat here in the light of a stone lamp and looked at this room, and you cannot imagine what they saw, and you will not see it.

Further along the wall, low, there is a flake of stone lying on the floor that is not from this room either. It has a sharp edge and the edge has a pattern of small deliberate scallops. It has not been moved since it was set down. Nothing has moved it. Nothing here moves anything.

And on the wall above the shelf, pressed into what must once have been a skin of soft clay over the crystal, there is a hand. Your hand fits into it — a little large, the fingers a little longer than yours. The clay went hard and the deposit came over it, a translucent film you can feel but only just, sealing it. You would expect, in a living cave, for the film to have thickened over the centuries until the hand was buried and lost. It is not buried. The film is as thin as a fingernail. It got that far and then the room held still, and the hand has been half-covered, neither taken nor kept, for a length of time you cannot estimate because there is nothing in here to estimate it against.

Follow the wall past the hand and you come to something alive, or that was alive, or that lives so slowly the distinction breaks down.

A root. It comes through the ceiling — a crack you find by following the root up as far as you can reach — and hangs down into the room, thick as your wrist where it enters and dividing, dividing, into a beard of fine pale threads that hang almost to the floor and are wet all over. Somewhere far above, on the outside of the mountain, in daylight and wind and the cold, there is a plant. This is what it sent down when it found a warm wet crack, forty or a hundred or more meters of blind descent, into a room where nothing grows because nothing green can, and it hangs here drinking the air, and it is the only thing in the room that came from the sky.

Around the root's threads, and only there, the stone is furred. A fungus, or a bacterial mat, something whitish that comes off on your fingertips and smells of nothing. It feeds on what the root gives off. It has spread exactly as far as the root's wetness extends and not a centimeter beyond, and it too has the feel of a colony that reached its edge and stayed.

You are not alone in the pool.

It takes a long time to know this. You stand with your legs in the water and after many minutes something touches your ankle — not a brush, a *placement*, a small cool weight that settles against you and does not go away. Bend and put your fingers to it and it is a creature the length of your thumb, soft, with limbs, and it does not startle. It has never needed to startle. Nothing in this room has ever come at it fast. It has no eyes to find; the head is smooth. It lets you touch it for a while and then, at some pace that cannot be called a decision, it is not there anymore, and the water where it was is already still again.

It is the only animal you will find. The pool is small and warm and has almost nothing in it, and the room is sealed, and whatever lives here lives on the fungus and the fungus lives on the root and the root lives on the sky, one thread of daylight arriving underground as sugar. There is not enough for more than a few.

Stand for a while longer.

What is strange about this place is not any single thing in it. It is the arrested quality — the sense of a room caught mid-word. The stone is all growth and no growth. The forms are sharp, new, unfinished, and also ancient, and there is no contradiction because they are unfinished *permanently*. The hand on the wall is half-preserved and will stay half. The lamp went out and the smell has not faded. Even you: you are the warmest thing in here by less than a degree, the wettest by nothing, and the room has already begun to absorb the small disturbance of your arrival, and when you go it will close over the space you took without ever having noticed you were in it.

The drop falls into the pool. You wait. Eventually another. Somewhere above you there is a mountain and weather and a long slow future of wearing down. In here the mountain is being born, very slightly, and has stopped, and keeps stopping. When you finally turn to grope back toward the fissure, you find you have to think to remember which way, because the room is the same in every direction — crowded, warm, dripping, dark — and it does not want to give you a difference to steer by.
