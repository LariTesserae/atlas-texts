The mountain gave up being a mountain a long time ago. What is left is a shield of grey stone lying under the sky like the lid of something, so worn that the eye can find no slope to it, though the lungs know they are high — the air is thin and tastes of iron and wet cloth, and the cold is the particular cold of altitude, which does not bite so much as steadily subtract. Nothing on the shield rises above the height of a knee. Cushion plants, moss campion, a hard grey lichen that has been growing on the same square inch since before there were names for anything. The rock between them is smooth as a riverbed, and in fact that is what it is: the bed of ten thousand years of rain running off in every direction at once, having nowhere in particular to go.

You walk toward the settlement for a long time before you understand that you are already in it.

---

It begins as a change in the moss. The cushions thicken, darken, take on a greenish-black wetness and a spring underfoot. Then you notice they are growing on something — a low kerb of pale stone, chalky, honeycombed, with the texture of a wasp's nest that has been dipped in cream and left to harden. The kerb runs straight for a while and then turns a corner. Another runs parallel to it, a few paces away. Between them the ground is bare and level and faintly channeled, and if you did not know better you would call it a street.

It is a street. There is no other word. It leads between walls that rise as you follow it, from knee-height to waist to shoulder, and the walls are made of the same pale stuff, which up close is not stone at all but the mineral ghost of vegetation — you can see the pattern of moss fronds in it, the hollow tubes where reeds once stood, the fine cast of grass blades, all turned to a porous limestone the colour of old teeth. The walls are hollow. Rap one and it sounds. Lean on one and a fine dust comes away on your coat, and beneath the dust the surface is damp.

The streets meet, divide, meet again. They are not laid out by anything that thought in grids, but they are not random either: they follow the shallow braid of channels that water took across the shield in some era when the springs still ran, and so they have the logic of a delta, or of veins on the back of a hand. Where two channels joined, there is something like a square. Where a channel pooled, there is something like a hall. The walls enclose spaces — dozens of them, then hundreds — each a room-sized chamber with an opening in one side, and the openings are all at about the height of a chest, and roughly the same width, and this is the first thing that is wrong with the place, though it takes a while to become wrong. Water does not care about doorways.

---

The town is empty in a way that ordinary empty towns are not. There is nothing in it. No rubble, no floor, no threshold. The chambers are hollow to the ground and the ground inside is the same smooth rock as outside, cushioned with the same moss. Nothing has been taken away because nothing was ever here. Whatever was inside these rooms when they closed over — reeds, sedge, the dark ropey moss that still grows in the wet corners — rotted away centuries or millennia ago and left its outline in the stone, so that you are walking through the negative space of a marsh that no longer exists, cast in its own mineral and stood upright.

Between the chambers there is a great deal of nothing. The town is hamlet-sized, perhaps — you could cross it in a quarter hour — but its emptiness is out of all proportion to its size. You can stand in one of the squares and see clear through six doorways to the grey shield beyond, and through six more to a sky the colour of pewter, and there will be nothing in any of the rooms between, not a leaf out of place, only the wind moving through them like a person going from room to room looking for something.

That is the second thing: the sound. The town is loud.

---

At first it is only wind, which on the shield is constant and unobstructed and has the flat unbroken roar of a thing that has never been made to turn a corner. But in the town the wind must turn corners, and the corners are hollow, and every hollow has its note. The chambers are close enough in size that the notes are close in pitch, and they beat against one another, so that the air is full of a low throbbing that you feel behind the sternum more than in the ears. Above that, the openings whistle — a hundred openings, each a little differently, a bristling of thin cold tones. Below it, deep enough that it is more like a pressure than a sound, some large enclosed volume somewhere near the centre resonates when the gusts come from the west, one slow note every few seconds like the breathing of a sleeper in another room.

And under everything, water.

It is not raining. It rains here perhaps one hour in five, but the intervals are not silent. The whole town is a condensing surface. The walls are cold and porous and the air that comes up the flanks of the old massif is saturated, and so the stone sweats continuously, and the sweat gathers in the hollow tubes and honeycombs inside the walls and falls through them, drop by drop, into cavities you cannot see. Each drop rings. The walls are drums and flutes and cisterns all at once. Put your ear to one and you hear a small interior weather — patterings, a trickle, a sudden hollow plink like a stone dropped down a well, then a long resonant hum as the water finds a tube the right length to sing. Take your ear away and you realise you were hearing that hum all along, from every wall, and that the throbbing you took for wind is partly water, thousands of drops a second, a town-sized instrument playing itself very slowly to nobody.

When the rain does come — and it comes suddenly, a grey wall walking across the shield, and hits the town like a hand on a table — the noise is extraordinary. Every surface rings at once. The doorways moan. The moss on the walls swells visibly, darkening, and streams begin to run down the streets exactly where the streets are lowest, which is to say exactly where they have always run, and you understand that the town is not finished. The water is carrying dissolved stone. Where it drips from the lip of a doorway, there is a small pale tongue of new deposit, soft as chalk, which will be hard in a year and part of the wall in a century. The rooms are still being built. Very slowly, they are closing.

---

There are living things here, but you must look for them.

Snails, first — small, translucent, with shells the same colour as the walls, grazing the algae in the wet channels. There are more of them inside the chambers than outside, in the shelter, and their trails on the moss are the only marks anything has made here in a long time. Then, if you are patient, a bird: a single black shape that comes over the shield low and fast, wings angled back, and drops into the town and vanishes. It is a chough, or something like a chough, and it lives — this is the third strange thing — in the chambers, in the high honeycombed part of the walls where the tubes are wide enough to nest in. There seem to be only a few of them. They do not call, or rather they do, but their calls are a thin descending whistle indistinguishable from the doorways, and so you hear them constantly and never know it. Once you have seen one you will see it again, or one like it, going into a wall and not coming out.

On some afternoons the cloud comes up over the edge of the shield, which is not far — the town sits perhaps a hundred paces from the place where the level rock simply stops and the grey air begins, and the sound of water falling a very long way rises out of that air day and night, part of the town's chord. The cloud fills the streets to the height of the walls and no higher, so that you can stand in a doorway with your feet in fog and your head in clear cold light, and the whistling of the openings changes as they fill, dropping in pitch, going soft and woolly, until the whole town is muttering to itself under a white blanket. Then the cloud drains back over the edge and the light comes down flat and even and colourless, and the walls steam, and the note rises again.

At night there is ice. A skin of it on every wet surface, so that in the first hour of morning light the town crackles as it thaws, a fine ticking under the roar, and the moss is stiff and grey until the sun reaches it.

---

You could stay a long time working out the rule of the place, and it would be this: water made it, in the shape of what was there. The shield is a slow dissolving lens of limestone under a thin rind of harder rock; the springs that once broke through it carried the stone in solution and set it down again wherever the flow slowed, over whatever was growing — and the things growing were moss and sedge, in clumps, in the wet channels, spaced as living things space themselves, each clump making its own small dam, each dam its own pool, each pool its own thickening wall. The chambers are the size of the clumps. The doorways are where the flow came in. That is all. Nothing planned it.

But the doorways are at chest height, and they are the same width, and they face inward toward the squares more often than they should. The chambers are the size of rooms and not of anything else. When the west wind comes, the great slow note from the centre of the town is one you find you are breathing in time with before you notice. And there is a place, near what you have started to call the square, where the moss on the floor of a chamber is worn in a shallow oval as though something has lain there, though nothing lives here but snails and a few black birds, and the moss grows back in a season, and the oval is still there.

You are not asked to resolve this. The town does not resolve it. It goes on sweating and ringing and building itself a little higher, closing its rooms a grain at a time, out on the level top of a mountain that finished being a mountain before anything had eyes to see it, and the light comes down grey and even, and the cloud rises and drains, and the water goes on saying the one long thing it has been saying since the springs failed — which sounds, if you stand in the right doorway, in the right wind, almost like a word.
