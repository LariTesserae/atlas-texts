You come down into it from the last low rise of the old country, and the first thing you notice is that the light has stopped behaving. It is not night. It is not overcast in the ordinary way either — the sky is a smooth lid of vapor, pale and even, and it ought to be brighter than this. But the basin drinks the daylight. The air itself seems to hold it back, so that everything below the rim sits in a kind of standing dusk, the color of pewter and wet slate.

The basin is wide. Hours across, if it can be crossed at all. It was floodplain once, or estuary — the ground is that flat, that black, that soft underfoot. Your boots sink a finger's depth and come up clean, because nothing in this soil has had time to rot. It was underneath something until recently. You can tell by the smell: not decay but mineral, the smell of a stone freshly split.

And out of that soft new ground the city has come up.

It does not fill the basin. That is what makes it hard to call a city at first. The structures stand far apart — a quarter mile, half a mile, sometimes further — each one alone in its own long field of ferns, so that from the rim the whole thing looks like an orchard planted for trees that have not yet grown into each other. But it has streets. You can see them if you look for the seam rather than the surface: dark ribbons of compacted ground running from one structure to the next, arrow-straight, meeting at angles that are almost but not quite right angles. Nothing has walked them yet. The ferns lean over the edges without crossing.

The structures themselves are the newest things you have ever seen. That is the only way to say it. The tallest are perhaps twelve stories, and they have the proportions of something that has been extruded rather than assembled — long, faceted, tapering unevenly, like a crystal grown in a solution that kept changing its mind. Their edges are sharp enough to cut. There is no weathering on them, no staining, no rounding at the corners where rain would run, no green film at the footings where things would grow. Around the base of each one the soil is heaved up in a fresh raw collar, the way earth heaves around a post that has been driven in from below. Some of the collars are still cracking. Some are still, very slowly, rising.

The material is dark and it does not want to be looked at. Not glass, not stone, not metal, though it has qualities of each. Up close the surface is matte and very slightly warm, warmer than the air, and if you put your palm on it you find it is a little too smooth for a surface — as though there were nothing there to be smooth. Light that falls on it does not so much reflect as arrive and stay. This is, you realize, where the daylight has gone. The city is quietly full of it. That is why the basin is dim.

At night the structures give a fraction of it back. Not from windows — there are no windows — but from the whole surface, a faint pewter glow, no brighter than a moonless sky. So the difference between day and night here is smaller than it should be. Day is a dusk in which the towers are dark. Night is a dusk in which they are not.

Between the towers, the green.

It is a young forest of old plants — ferns as tall as a person, horsetails in dense stands, something with broad glossy leaves that has no name you know, all of it a deep humid green that reads nearly black in this light. It is lush without being crowded. It grows in the way things grow when there is no competition yet and no one has told them how tall to be. You can walk through it. It parts and closes behind you and drips.

Everything here drips. The basin is warm, the air is close to saturated, and the structures are always a few degrees off from the air around them, so they sweat. Water beads on their faces and runs down in sheets and gathers in the raw collar around each footing, and from there it finds the low seams of the streets. The streets are becoming channels. They were not designed as channels — or perhaps they were, and this was always the plan, that the city would make its own canals by the simple patience of condensation. In the deepest parts of the basin the water has already collected into long still pools that mirror the towers perfectly and make each one seem twice as tall and half as real.

There is no river. No source, no outflow. The water arrives out of the air each morning and leaves back into it each afternoon, and in between it moves, slowly, along routes the city seems to have anticipated.

It is very quiet. Not silent — there is the drip, constant, a hundred different tempos; there is the tick of the ferns adjusting; and now and then, from somewhere inside the nearest tower, a sound like a large stone being set carefully into place. It comes perhaps once an hour. It does not echo. After it, the tower is fractionally different, though you cannot say how.

Living things are scarce, and what there is keeps its distance. Small pale frogs at the edges of the pools, no larger than a thumb-joint, which do not call. A single grey heron that has found the city acceptable and stands motionless in the shallows for the whole of the afternoon. Insects — yes, but few, and they avoid the towers entirely, swinging wide around each one as though there were a wind there that you cannot feel. Once, far off across the basin, something larger moves along one of the streets, low and deliberate, and is gone behind a stand of horsetail before you can decide what shape it was. It moved the way the towers grow: as if it were the only thing that had ever needed to be there.

You learn the rules of the place by walking it. The streets are longer than they look; a tower that seems a quarter mile off takes an hour to reach, and the one behind you is closer when you turn than when you left it. Your shadow, in what light there is, falls toward the nearest structure rather than away from the sky. The ferns are always the same height as you. None of this is threatening. It is simply how the basin has decided to work, and the ferns and the frogs and the heron have already accepted it.

What the city is for is not apparent. It has no doors. It has no obvious center, though the streets tend, over miles, to bend a few degrees toward the west, where the vapor lid is fractionally thicker and the towers, if you count them, stand fractionally closer together. It is either very early or very late in its own life — either not yet inhabited or inhabited by something that has no more use for windows than a stone does — and it is impossible from inside to tell which.

What is apparent is that it is not finished. The collars rise. The channels deepen by the width of a hair each day. The ferns push a little further into the streets and are, each night, a little further back. Somewhere a stone is set. The light comes down through the vapor and does not reach the ground, and the basin holds it, and waits, and drips.
