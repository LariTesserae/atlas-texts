The ground is the first thing, and it is the whole thing. It is pale, the colour of old bone or new cement, and it is warm under the hand at any hour. It is not rock. Nothing here was ever rock. Every surface you can stand on was laid down, thin film over thin film, by things too small to see, and if you kneel and put your face close to it you can find the seams: bands finer than paper, some grey, some faintly rust, some the yellow of dried lemon rind, each one a season's work. The stone is hollow-sounding in places and dense in others. It rings very slightly if you drop a pebble on it — but there are no pebbles here, only the pieces you break off yourself, and those crumble before you can drop them twice.

This is a country of deposition. It goes on to every horizon and past them. From any point you can walk for days and the character of the ground will change — it will step up into low terraces, it will run out into broad shallow aprons, it will bulge into domes and blunt chimneys — but it will not stop being made, and it will not stop being warm, and you will not reach an edge.

The building is done by mats. They are the only large life here, if "large" is the word for a sheet of cells a few millimetres thick. Where the water is, they are: rubbery, olive and rust and bottle-green, sometimes a violent orange where the flow is hottest. They pull mineral from the water and lay it down beneath themselves, and they climb on what they've laid, and so the ground rises. Slowly, at the pace of a fingernail. Fast, at the pace of a continent. The whole plain is maybe half a metre higher than it was in a human lifetime, and there is no sign it intends to stop.

The water comes up from below. This is the rule everything else follows. Somewhere under the built ground there is heat and there is water, and the water rises through the porous stone and comes out at the surface wherever it can. It does not come out everywhere. It comes out in places — a vent here, a low seeping rim there, a chimney the height of a person that runs a thin sheet of hot water down one side and nothing down the other. Between the places is dry ground, and the dry ground is most of it. You walk a long time across warm empty pavement to reach the next wetness. When you do, you hear it before you see it: not a sound of running water exactly, but a change in the texture of the quiet, a faint continuous tick and seep, the sound of water moving where it barely has room to move.

Where the water pools it makes terraces. Each pool has a rim, and the rim is where the mats are thickest and the deposition fastest, so the rim grows, and the pool deepens behind it until it spills, and the spill starts a new rim below. The terraces are never quite finished. They have the look of something still being drawn — edges soft, rims still building, the shapes of next year's pool already sketched in a dark wet fan of new mat spreading over old pale stone. The water in the pools is clear and hot and very still, blue-grey over the pale bottom, greener where the mat is. It moves only where it overflows, and it overflows in sheets so thin you cannot see them, only feel the film of heat on the stone give way to a film of wet.

Then the pool goes dry, because the vent beneath it has clogged itself with its own deposit, and the water finds another route, and the mat in the abandoned pool dies back to a dark crust and the crust bleaches to the same pale as everything else. So the plain is scattered with old pools — dry basins, dead rims, chimneys that no longer run — and it is scattered equally with new ones, and the two are hard to tell apart from a distance. The plain remembers where it was wet. It does not mourn.

Rain comes, but rarely, and it is the plain's own rain. The ground is hot; the air above it rises; on some afternoons the rising air builds towers of cloud that go up white and then grey and then almost black at the base, and for an hour a column of rain stands somewhere out on the flats, and you can see it from a great way off because there is nothing to hide it. Sometimes it walks. Sometimes it stays in one place and beats the stone there until the stone runs with sheets of hot water and the dead mats in the dry basins darken and swell as if remembering. Then it stops. The cloud collapses into rags and the rags burn off. In an hour the ground is dry again except in the low places, and the low places steam.

At the end of the night, before the light comes up, the stone is coolest — which is to say still warm — and the air is coolest, and there is a brief hour when moisture condenses on everything. The pale ground goes dark with it. The dry rims bead. The dead crusts in the old basins are wet through. Then the sun clears the horizon, flat and enormous, and the dew lifts off the whole plain at once in a low haze that you can watch move away from you. This is the loudest hour: a faint crackling from every direction as the stone dries and the crusts contract. Then it is quiet again.

There are plants, but not many, and none of them are ambitious. In the cracks where old rims have split — and they do split, from heat, from the weight of new deposit on old — there are tufts of something stiff and grey-green, reedlike, knee-high at most, rooted in the accumulated dust of dead mat. They grow only where a crack catches condensation and holds it. Down in the warm spill-channels between terraces, where the water is cooler and slower, there is a fine emerald moss, and in a few of the oldest basins, ones that have been dry for so long that a real soil has begun to form from their bottoms, there are low shrubs with leaves like leather and pale bark, standing far apart, each one owning its small territory of shade. They do not spread. The ground is too hot and too new and there is too little of anything to hold on to. They are guests. The mats are the residents.

Nothing moves that is not water, weather, or growth. There are no tracks in the wet places except the ones you leave. No shells in the pool bottoms. No sound at night but the tick and seep of the vents and, very occasionally, from a long way off, the crack of a rim giving way under its own weight and a pool letting go all at once — a soft rushing that lasts a few seconds and then is done, and somewhere out in the dark a new terrace has begun.

The light is ordinary. Full sun most days, hard and white off the pale stone, so that you squint; grey and bright under the storm towers; long and amber at the ends of the day, when every rim and chimney casts a shadow across the flats and the plain reveals how much shape it actually has. The colours are the colours of the deposit: bone, cream, rust, a green so dark it reads as black, one flaring orange that means *too hot to touch*.

It is a place that is not finished. That is the whole of its character. Not ruined, not worn — the opposite. Nothing here has been smoothed by time because there hasn't been enough of it yet. The edges are sharp where they've just cracked and soft where they're just forming, and there is almost nothing in between. Wind has barely touched it. Nothing has ever stood on it long enough to wear a path. It is a floor being laid, at continental scale, by the smallest possible masons, out of the water that comes up from below, in a heat that never lifts, under a sky that makes its own occasional rain and then thinks better of it.

You walk. The ground rings faintly. Ahead, at the limit of sight, a single column of rain stands on the flats and does not move.
