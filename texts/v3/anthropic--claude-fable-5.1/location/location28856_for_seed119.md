You come to it across flat country that has been made flat. That is the first thing to understand about this place, and it takes a while to understand it: the ground for a long way around is not ground. It is fill. Rubble packed down, then clay laid over the rubble, then more clay, tamped by feet and by mallets over so many seasons that it has taken on the grain and give of real earth. Grass has been discouraged. The surface is bare and slick and the color of a wet slate roof, and it holds water in a thousand shallow dents where the tamping was uneven — each dent a little grey mirror, each mirror showing a piece of the same low cloud. Nothing grows in the dents. A few tufts of rush have been permitted along the margins of the approach, like guards who have long since forgotten what they were guarding.

The sky is a lid. It sits close. The light under it is the light of an hour before dawn that never advances toward dawn, though the day is presumably going on somewhere above the cloud. Everything is cool to the touch and slightly damp and smells of clay, and of standing water, and faintly of animals.

The structure itself is not large. You could walk around it in the time it takes to eat an apple. It is a wall, roughly oval, higher than a tall person can reach and thicker at the base than at the top, so that in section it would look like the stump of a tooth. The wall is made of everything: river cobbles the size of two fists set in courses, and between and over the courses a mud plaster full of chopped reed, and where the plaster has fallen away you can see older plaster beneath it, and older again, so that the wall is a record of its own repair. It has been renewed so many times that it has stopped being straight. It swells and leans. Rain has been running down it for longer than anyone remembers and has left long dark stains beneath every irregularity, and in the stains a short moss grows — the only green, and not very green.

There is no door. There are steps. They are set into the outside of the wall, slabs of stone that project like teeth from a jaw, worn concave in the middle by the feet of whoever used to climb them, and slick now with a film of water. You climb. At the top the wall is wide enough to sit on, and the top course has been laid with flat stones set in pitch, and the pitch has gone grey and crazed and is speckled white with droppings. You sit. You look down inside.

It is empty.

That is the second thing to understand, and it also takes a while. The floor is a single continuous surface of fitted flags, each flag shaped by hand with a pecking stone until it met its neighbors, and the joints have been packed with a lime that has weathered to the same color as the stone, so that the whole floor reads as one dished grey plate. The dish is very slight. It falls perhaps a hand's depth from the walls to the center, and at the center there is water — a pool the size of a cart bed, so still that it looks like a hole in the floor through which you can see the cloud. There is nothing on the floor. There is nothing in the pool. There is a wooden post near one wall, adzed square and gone silver, with a cord tied around it whose other end goes nowhere. There is a stone the size of a loaf that someone once set down and did not pick up again. That is all. The open space between the walls is the emptiest place you have ever been. It is the emptiness of a room whose purpose has been fulfilled so completely that nothing further is required of it.

And it is deafening.

The sound comes from the walls. From the inside face of the wall, which you could not see from the approach and which is not blank. It is pocked. From floor to the pitch line it is riddled with niches, hundreds of them — perhaps a thousand — each the size of a cupped pair of hands or a little larger, each cut into the mud and cobble and smoothed on the inside until it is as slick as the inside of a shell, and each one occupied. The lower courses of niches, the ones that the water climbs to, belong to frogs and newts. The middle courses, which stay damp but do not flood, belong to snails the size of fists whose shells are the color of the wall, and to slow black beetles, and to some kind of grey toad that seems to have made a specialty of the corner niches. The upper courses belong to swifts and to small pale bats, which share, or take turns, or have some arrangement you are not able to work out. Under the pitch line, where the wall meets the sky, a species of swallow has built its mud cups so thickly that they form a continuous eave, and this eave is a hundred throats wide.

Everything is calling. The frogs make a sound like stones being knocked together underwater, and there are so many of them that the knocking has no rhythm; it is a texture, a continuous wet clatter. The toads underneath it produce a low pulsing that you feel in the stone you are sitting on. The swifts scream. The swallows argue. The bats, at this hour, are a sibilance at the edge of hearing, but there are enough of them that the edge of hearing is where the sound lives, a permanent hiss like sand poured on sand. And the wall itself amplifies all of it: the oval shape and the smooth dished floor were built, or have settled, into a form that throws every sound across the empty middle and back, so that a frog on the far side is as loud in your ear as one beside your knee. The emptiness is not silent. The emptiness is where the noise goes to be heard.

You begin, after a while, to notice the rule. The animals do not sound in the open. A frog that leaves its niche and crosses the floor to the pool does it in silence — moves, squats, drinks or spawns or whatever it has come to do, and returns in silence, and only when it is back inside the wall does it begin to knock again. The swifts that stitch across the oval do it without a sound and scream only when they land. It is as though the niches are the instruments and the animals are the breath, and breath outside an instrument is only air. Whether the place was built to do this, or whether the animals learned it, or whether it simply happened over a thousand repairs and a thousand generations until it hardened into law, there is no way to say. But it is the law. The floor is quiet and the walls are loud, and the pool in the center is the quietest thing in the world.

The water is the third thing. The pool does not receive rain, or rather it receives rain and does not rise. Rain falls into it and the level does not change. Nor does it drop in dry weather, though there is very little dry weather. The level is fixed at a line you can see on the surrounding flags, a darker band a finger wide where the stone is permanently wetted and has grown a black slime like the skin of a plum. Above that band the flags are grey and merely damp; below it they are under water. The water comes from below. It rises through the joints of the floor — if you climb down and put your face near the flags you can see it, a shine along the lime that thickens and beads and runs downhill toward the center in threads too small to make a sound. The whole floor weeps. The whole wall weeps too, from the inside, so that every niche has a dark lip where the moisture gathers and drips; it drips onto the floor, and runs to the center, and joins the pool, and does not raise it. Something under the flags is taking the surplus away. A drain, presumably. Laid by hand, once, of fitted stone, and silted since to exactly the width that lets through exactly as much as arrives. The place drinks and voids and holds its level as a body does. It has found its size.

The cool is the cool of that water. The walls sweat it and the floor sweats it and the air over the pool is heavy with it, so that your breath shows and your clothes grow heavy at the cuffs and the stone under you passes its chill up into your hips. When the light changes — it changes, though it does not brighten; the grey deepens toward the end of the day, and the cloud ceiling comes lower — a mist gets up off the pool and lies on the floor like a second, softer floor, knee deep, and the frogs cross it invisibly and their movement shows only as furrows.

The evening turn is the loudest. The bats come out of the upper niches in a stream that thickens the mist, silent, circle the oval twice in a body, and go over the wall. As they go, the swallows come in, fold, and begin to talk. The swifts have already gone quiet. And the frogs — the frogs, at the change of light, all begin at once, and for the length of perhaps twenty breaths the knocking does have a rhythm, a single enormous pulse that comes off the wall as one sound and hits the far wall and comes back, and the pool shivers under it in concentric rings from the outside inward, and your teeth hurt. Then it falls apart again into texture, and the mist closes over the floor, and the rain starts, because at this hour it always starts, a fine cold rain that patters on the pitch and the swallows' eave and adds nothing to the pool.

Somewhere under the fill, out beyond the wall, a heron lands on the tamped clay and stands in one of the shallow mirrors and waits. It is the only thing you have seen all day that is not inside the wall or on the wall, and it looks in from outside as you look in from the top, and neither of you goes down.

You could stay. The wall top is wide enough to lie on. The niches nearest your feet are occupied by a toad and a snail and something else that has drawn back too far to see, and none of them mind you, and the sound is so total that after a while it becomes a kind of quiet — the way a river becomes a kind of quiet. The place does not need you. It was made, every stone of it, and every stone has been handled and re-handled and set and re-set, and whoever did that is not here and has not been here for a long time, and the thing they made has gone on doing what things do when they are left: it has filled up with whatever would live in it, and it has found its level, and it has become loud.
