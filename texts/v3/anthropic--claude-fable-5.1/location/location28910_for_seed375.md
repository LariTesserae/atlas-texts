You come down to it from the rim, and the first thing that changes is the air. Up on the plain there is a wind, thin and constant; the moment you step below the lip of the basin the wind stops as if a door had shut, and the air that replaces it is cool and perfectly still and smells faintly of struck flint. The floor of the basin is white. Not snow-white, not salt-white exactly; a chalky, matte white with a grain to it, like unglazed porcelain, and when you kneel you see it is made of tiny plates lying flat against one another, each one sharp at its edges, none of them yet worn round.

The light is low and comes from everywhere. There is no sun visible, only a high, even haze the colour of dilute milk, and the basin is deep enough that the rim cuts off most of the sky. Shadows do not fall so much as pool. You can see clearly for perhaps a hundred paces, and after that things soften, not into fog but into a kind of grey indifference, as though the distance simply stopped bothering to render itself.

The town sits in the middle of the floor. You can walk around it in under an hour.

Its edge is the strangest thing about it, or the first strangest thing. There is no outskirts, no scatter of half-built things or rubble apron. The white floor runs flat and then, along a line so straight you could lay a rule to it, the floor becomes a street. A curb rises to the height of your hand, square-cut, and beyond the curb are walls. The walls go up two storeys, three in places, in a material that is the same white as the floor but denser, closer-grained, faintly translucent at the thin edges so that where a wall stands between you and the haze it glows very slightly from within, like a fingernail held to a lamp.

Everything is sharp. That is the second thing. The corners of the buildings could cut cloth. The sills of the windows have arrises that catch the little light there is in a fine bright line. Nothing has slumped, nothing has spalled, no lintel has sagged. There are no stains where water ran, because water has never run here; there is no water in this basin, there has not been water in this basin for longer than the basin has had a floor. The town has the look of a thing unwrapped this morning.

And it is empty. You walk in along the first street and your footsteps make no sound. You stop, deliberately, and scrape your heel against the curb, and the sound is absorbed so quickly you are not sure you heard it at all. The material drinks noise. You clap once and the clap goes nowhere. The silence is not the absence of sound but a positive thing, a medium, and after a while you stop testing it because it feels rude.

The streets run straight and meet at right angles. The blocks are of a size a person could stand at one corner of and see the far corner. The doorways are all the same height, a little taller than you, with a lintel and a raised threshold, and they are all open; there are no doors, no hinges, no pins, no marks where anything was ever fixed. Inside, the rooms are square and bare and the floors are the same white plate-grain as the basin. Some rooms have a second room behind them. Some have a stair, cut into the wall as a series of ledges, going up to a room with a window and nothing else. Nowhere is there an object. Nowhere is there a mark that says a hand did this, a hearth, a peg, a trace of soot. The town has all the forms of habitation and none of its residue.

You begin to notice, block by block, that it is not finished. Or rather that it is not fixed. At the far end of one street the wall of a house does not meet the ground cleanly but flares at its base into a lip, a soft white skirt, still faintly wet-looking though there is nothing wet in it, and above the lip the wall is thinner and more translucent than the walls behind you, and its top edge, instead of running level, breaks off in a fringe of small pale spires like the edge of a sheet of frost. It is going up. It is going up while you watch, though you cannot see it move; when you look back at it after walking the length of the block and returning, the fringe has knitted itself into a straight line and the wall is a hand higher and the spires have moved up with it.

That is when you start looking for the builders, and it takes a long time to find one.

They are small, the size of a thumb joint, and the same white as everything else, and they do not move quickly. You find the first one on a windowsill, in the angle where the sill meets the jamb, and you would have taken it for a chip of stone except that it was not there a moment ago. It has no eyes you can find, no limbs you can name. Its underside is flat and glistens very faintly, and where it has been there is a track, not a scrape but an addition: a thin ridge of new material, laid down and already hardening, following exactly the line of the joint. It is grouting the window. It is finishing the work. When it reaches the corner it pauses, and something at its front end tests the angle, and it turns ninety degrees and goes on.

There are not many of them. In a whole street you might count three. They work alone and do not appear to signal to one another and yet the walls they raise meet at corners and share lintels and arrive at the same height. Whatever holds the plan is not in any one of them. You find yourself wondering whether it is in the ground.

Further in, near what you would call the centre if the town had one, there is a plaza. The buildings around it are older, or at least more complete; their walls have the deepest translucence and the sharpest lines. And here you see the third thing, the thing that makes the silence feel less like emptiness and more like held breath. One wall of the plaza has been eaten.

Not broken. There is no rubble. Where the wall stood there is a scallop cut out of it, smooth-sided, the size of a door, its inner surface glassy and clean as though the material had been dissolved rather than struck. The edges of the scallop are the only rounded edges in the town. Around its base the floor is scattered with little white grains that are not plates like the rest but spheres, perfect and dull, the size of seed pearls, and when you pick one up it is warm. The only warm thing in the basin.

You do not see what did it. You see, once, at the edge of your hundred paces of clarity, something low and long move across the mouth of a street and not come out the other side. It made no sound. Afterwards the wall at that corner has a new curve in it, and two of the small white builders are already on the curve's lip, laying their ridges, straightening it back toward a line.

So the town is a contest, or a conversation, between a thing that makes right angles and a thing that makes curves, and neither is winning, and the result is a settlement at the exact moment of its emergence, held there. Every wall you see is somewhere between being raised and being consumed. The plaza has been rebuilt many times; you can see it in the layering of the translucence, thin bright laminae like the rings of a shell, dozens deep. The town is young everywhere and nowhere new.

There is vegetation, if that is the word. In the angles where the street meets the curb, and in the scallops the eater leaves before they are refilled, something grows: grey-green, crisp, branching in flat fans no bigger than a hand, rooted in nothing you can see. It does not seem to want light; the largest fans are in the deepest doorways. When you touch one it does not bend but flexes as a whole, like a leaf of mica, and returns. The builders route around it. The eater, judging by the untouched fans inside the older scallops, does not eat it. It is the one thing in the town that neither party seems to have an opinion about.

Cold gathers as the haze darkens. There is no evening in the usual sense; the milk overhead simply thickens toward grey, and the walls' inner glow becomes for a while the brightest thing there is, so that you walk down streets faintly lit from within their own stone, every edge drawn in a hairline of pale light. The builders do not stop. You can hear them, or you convince yourself you can: the very smallest sound, a tick at the edge of hearing, like a single crystal deciding on its shape. Then not. Then again, somewhere else.

Nobody has lived here. Nobody will. The doors are the height of a person and open onto rooms a person could sleep in, and the stairs are cut to a person's stride, and the whole town waits with a perfect and total patience for an occupant it has no way of knowing exists, being built and unbuilt by two kinds of small blind creature that have never seen a house.

You leave by the line where the street stops. The wind takes you back at the rim, and the sound of it, after the hours below, is almost too much.
