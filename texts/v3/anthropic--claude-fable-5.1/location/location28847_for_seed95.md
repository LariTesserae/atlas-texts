The first thing to understand about the tableland is that it is finished. Not abandoned, not ruined — finished, the way a river delta is finished when it has spread itself as thin as it can go. Whatever mountain stood here once has been taken down to its root and the root has been polished. What remains is a plateau perhaps forty miles across, sitting so high that the clouds live below it, and so worn that from any point on its surface you can see almost to its edges: a faint darkening of the horizon where the rock finally gives out and drops into the white.

The rock is a pale, reddish stone, fine-grained, warm to the hand. It has been rained on and dried and rained on again for so long that it no longer has a texture, only a sheen. There are no boulders, because everything that could fall has already fallen and everything that fell has been ground into the shallow basins that dimple the whole surface like the marks of fingertips pressed into clay. In each basin there is a little sand, and in the sand, if the morning fog has been generous, a lens of water the width of a hand. This is where the water lives. It comes up from below at dawn, when the cloud sea underneath the plateau swells and breathes over the rim and lays itself down on everything, and by mid-morning it has gone back, leaving every surface beaded and every basin filled. Some days it also rains — a warm, brief, almost apologetic rain that arrives from nowhere and leaves before it has finished falling. There are no streams. Water has nowhere to go here; there is no slope for it to follow. It sits in its hollows and is drunk or is breathed away.

It should be cold. It is not. The air has the thick, mild warmth of a greenhouse, and it never varies by more than a few degrees between the middle of the night and the middle of the day. Nobody who has stood on the tableland has been able to say why. The stone gives off no heat. The sun is not unusually strong. The warmth is simply a condition of the place, like the flatness — one more thing that has settled and will not be changing.

What grows: not much. A black lichen on the north-facing lips of the basins, crusted and slow. A grey moss that lives only where the fog pools thickest, in a crescent along the western rim. A low plant with thick, silvery leaves that opens for perhaps an hour after the fog lifts and then closes again, and which is never found more than a few paces from the next one but never touching. That is nearly all of it. The green, such as it is, is not the point of the place.

The animals are the point of the place.

You do not see them at first, because there is nothing to see them against. They are the color of the rock — they have become the color of the rock — and they lie so close together and so still that the eye reads them as terrain. It is only when one of them exhales, and the beading of moisture on its back shivers and runs, that you realize the low hummock in front of you is a shoulder, and that the shoulder is one of ten thousand shoulders, and that the entire plateau from where you stand to the farthest darkening of the horizon is alive.

They are large, most of them. The nearest thing to describe them against is a tortoise, but the comparison fails in every direction: they are furred, thinly, the fur matted down and mineralized until it has a grain like slate; their legs are short and folded beneath them and rarely seen; their heads are small and low and turned down to the stone. Their backs are broad and gently domed, and on each back, in the shallow depression between the shoulders, the fog leaves a pool of water that the animal will later reach round and drink from, slowly, without lifting its body. They graze the lichen. They graze the moss. They move — they do move — at a pace that you can only confirm by marking the position of a shoulder against a basin's rim and coming back the next day. In the course of a year one of them might cross a distance you could walk in an hour. In the course of a life, whatever that is, it might cross the plateau.

They are not alone on their own backs. In the mineralized fur live smaller animals — something like a sloth's idea of a lizard, long-toed, slow-eyed, the same color as everything — and these graze the lichen that has taken root on the large animals' shoulders, and drink from the shoulder-pools, and are never seen to leave the back they were born on. On the small animals live smaller animals still, flat and shelled, and on these, if you look with your face nearly against the surface, a dust of something that moves. Every presence is a landscape for another presence. The density is total. There is no square yard of the tableland that is not layered three or four lives deep, and there is no life on it that is not also a place.

And above, in the warm still air, something is always drifting. They look like seeds and are not seeds. They have no wings; they rise on the morning fog, hang for hours in the mid-air where the temperature never changes, and settle in the evening onto whatever back is below them. They are the only fast thing here, and they are not fast. Thousands of them at a time, a soft grey weather of bodies, drifting a few feet above the herd and casting no shadow worth the name.

Given all this, the silence is the strangest thing. Ten thousand large animals, and no calling, no bellowing, no scrape of horn. They do not vocalize. Perhaps they never did; perhaps they gave it up when there was no longer any distance between them worth shouting across. What sound there is comes from water: the tick of fog-beads gathering and letting go, the faint continuous drip from ten thousand backs into ten thousand basins at mid-morning, a sound so widely distributed that it seems to come from the air itself. Beneath that, if you are quiet, the breathing — a long low pressure in the ears rather than a sound, the whole plateau inhaling at roughly the same time and, minutes later, letting go. Once, on a still afternoon, a foot moves, and the hush of horn on polished stone carries for half a mile.

There have been people here. Not many, and not recently, and not — this is the odd part — with anything in their hands. There are stone rings, knee-high, a few paces across, built from the only stones the plateau has to offer, which is to say fragments broken from the rim and carried inward. Carried; there are no marks of dragging. The rings are too small to have been houses and too far apart to have been a village, and each one is now occupied, in the way that everything here is occupied, by an animal lying in the middle of it as if it had been built for that purpose. Maybe it was. There are also grooves in the rock, straight, shallow, running for a hundred yards and stopping, and these are in the wrong direction for water and too regular for a foot. There is one standing slab, three times the height of a person, out toward the eastern edge, with no marks on it at all, and a hollow worn into the stone at its base where the fog collects, and an animal has lain in the hollow and drunk from it for so long that the slab has a grey shadow of lichen up its lee side where the animal's breath has been.

You will find, if you walk long enough, a scattering of bones. They are not disturbing. They lie among the living exactly as the living lie among each other, close, layered, the color of the ground, and nothing has moved them, and the fog beads on them each morning as it does on everything. It is hard to tell how old they are. It is hard to tell how old anything is. The animals themselves seem to have no young — none have ever been seen, though in a herd this size one would expect them everywhere — and so either they are born somewhere out of sight, or the small animals on their backs are what the young look like, or the herd is simply the herd, the same ten thousand that lay down here when the mountain was a mountain and have been waiting it down ever since.

The days are alike. The fog rises, spreads, beads, retreats. The silver-leaved plants open and close. The drifting things go up and come down. In the long middle of the afternoon the light lies flat and even over everything, without glare and without shadow — the sun is high but the air has a thickness that softens it — and the whole plateau looks like a single animal resting, breathing under a skin of stone. Then the fog comes back over the rim in the last hour, and the horizon loses its edge, and somewhere very far off a foot moves and the sound of it arrives, soft, a minute later.

If there is a rule here, it is this: nothing comes to the tableland to change. Things come here to stop, and stopping takes a very long time, and while it is being done the fog is enough to live on.
