The first thing is the warmth, and it comes before the seeing. You step down out of the ordinary air into something that has been holding the same temperature for longer than anything has been counting, and it closes over your skin like a hand that has been resting on a stone in the sun.

It is a hollow. Not a cave — the sky is still above it, somewhere, a grey coin at the top of a wide, low-walled throat — but the walls lean in and the air is so thick with suspended water that the light coming down loses itself halfway. What reaches the floor is a soft, sourceless dimness, the color of wet ash, with no shadows sharp enough to name. You can see perhaps nine or ten paces in any direction, and there is not much more than nine or ten paces to see. The place is the size of a barn, or a chapel with the roof gone, and it is entirely full.

Full of stone, first. The floor is not a floor but a series of rimmed basins, each the width of a cupped pair of hands or a washtub or a bed, stacked and nested and overlapping so that there is no flat ground anywhere. Their lips are ridged like the edge of a shell, pale where they stand above water, ochre and rust and a deep liver-brown beneath it. The mineral has built itself in layers, each pool spilling into the next lower pool over a smooth apron that looks poured and is, in a sense, poured — the water carries the stone in solution and sets it down grain by grain, and has done so until there is no distinction left between where the spring is and where the rock is. Everything is spring. Everything is rock.

And the walls are the same. They weep. Not in drops but in a continuous, silent film that darkens the surface and pearls along every ledge and folds itself into miniature terraces on the way down, so the walls are just the floor turned upright, and the boundary where one becomes the other is somewhere under a lip of stone you cannot quite find with your eye.

The water itself is warm — not hot, you could keep a hand in it indefinitely, and the temptation to do so is strong — and very clear where it is still, and faintly milky where it moves. It smells of iron and of eggs, but gently, an old smell that has stopped being sharp. Where it emerges, near what must be the deepest part of the hollow, there is a seam in the rock the width of a finger, and the water comes out of it at a rate you could measure with a bucket and a patient afternoon, and that rate has not changed. That is the thing about this place, the thing you understand slowly: the water comes in at exactly the speed it leaves. The pools do not rise. The pools do not fall. Somewhere below the lowest basin there is a crack that takes it away at precisely the pace the seam gives it, and the stone that dissolves upstream is the stone that is laid down here, and the stone that is laid down here is the stone that slumps and dissolves again a little further along, and the whole assembly has settled into a form so balanced that it no longer has a direction. It is not growing. It is not wearing away. It has arrived.

There are no plants. This is the second thing you notice, after the warmth, and it takes longer to notice than it should because the place does not feel dead. It feels occupied. But there is no moss on the weeping walls, no fern in the cracks, no algae greening the pool edges. The mineral load in the water is too high, or the light is too low, or both, and whatever falls in from above — a leaf, a seed — is coated within days and becomes a small pale relief on a basin floor, a leaf-shape in stone that is not a fossil, only a leaf that stayed. In the shallowest pools, where the film of water is thinnest and the light is least dim, there is something: a skin the color of old brass, faintly ridged, that clings to the mineral and shrinks back from your finger like the surface of hot milk. It is the only living thing that is at home here, and it is barely anything. It has been doing exactly this for as long as the water has been coming.

And yet the space is packed. There is no room in it. The basins press against each other, the walls press in, the mist presses down. Every surface has grown a coating, a frill, a lip, a beard of dripping stone. Stalks of mineral rise from some of the pool floors where a bubble stream has been coming up at one point for a very long time, and the stalks have been trimmed level at the water surface by the same water that built them. Nothing here has been left alone long enough to become simple. The density is not of things arriving but of things having stayed — an accumulation without a single addition you could point to. If you set a stone down here it would be, within a season, part of the place, and it would be as though you had never done it.

Someone did set things down. Not many. You find them by hand more than by eye, because the light flattens everything into the same grey-brown. A row of shallow notches cut into a sloped wall on the way in, spaced for a person's stride, each one softened at its edges and lined with the same pale crust as the pool rims, so they might be steps or might be a natural stairway of ledges, except that natural ledges are not spaced for a stride. A bowl-shaped hollow ground into the lip of one of the upper basins, deliberately, wider and smoother than any of the others, with a groove worn beside it as though a vessel had been rested there again and again. In an alcove above the reach of the drip, a shelf of set stones, three of them, fitted without mortar, and on the shelf nothing — but the shelf is dry, and it is the only dry thing in the hollow, and that is its purpose. Near the seam where the water enters, a cord has been tied around a knob of rock, some plant fiber twisted by hand, and it has been so long in the vapor that it is a cord of stone now, a stone loop holding a stone knot around a stone knob, and you cannot tell whether it once held something or was itself the offering.

There are no fires. There has never been a fire here — nothing would burn, and there is nothing to burn, and the walls are unblackened. Whoever came brought their tools already made, worked the stone where it was soft, used what the place gave them — warm water, dry shelf — and did not try to make it into anything else. Their marks have been half-taken back. The place did not resist them. It absorbed them, and went on.

The sound is the sound of a very large, very slow clock with no hands. A drip from the left, then a longer wait, then a drip from behind, and under both a single continuous note so low it is more felt than heard — the seam, the water leaving the rock. Now and then a bubble comes up through one of the deeper pools and breaks with a sound like a small mouth opening. Nothing echoes. The mist eats it. If you speak, your voice goes out into the grey and does not come back.

Something lives here beyond the brass skin, but only just. A small fly, wingless or nearly so, slate-colored, that runs along the wet lips of the basins and stops and runs again; you see one, then a second an hour later that may be the same one. In the deepest pool, when you hold still long enough for the water to settle to glass, a shape the length of a fingernail joint, pale and jointed, moving across the ochre floor with no interest in the light or in you. It has not seen anything else in a long time. It does not appear to need to.

You stay longer than you meant to. It is easy, in warmth and dimness, with nothing changing. The grey coin overhead does not brighten and does not darken — the mist holds the light at one level regardless of what the sun outside is doing, and you find you have lost track of whether it is morning. The steps are behind you, up through the leaning walls, and the shelf is dry, and the bowl in the basin lip is waiting for whatever was set in it. The water comes in and the water goes out. The stone builds and the stone dissolves. Your footprints, where you crossed the shallows, are already filling in with a faint pale bloom, and by the time you reach the top of the notched wall and turn to look back, there is nothing down there but the hollow, full and still, the same as it was before you and the same as it will be after, keeping its one warm note against the rock.
