You hear it long before there is anything to see, and you hear it wrong. From below, on the shoulder of the mountain, it sounds like a rockslide that refuses to finish — a grinding that swells and falls off and swells again, coming from somewhere inside the cloud that sits on the summit ridge like a lid. The ridge itself is bare, grey, unfinished-looking: not the smoothed, lichened grey of old mountains but the chalky, sharp-edged grey of stone that has not yet decided what it is. There is no path. There is a scratch in the scree, and then another, and then a flat boulder where someone once knapped a core and left the flakes in a small fan, and then the noise takes over your ears entirely and you stop being able to think about anything else.

The place is a cauldron. Eight or nine metres across, perhaps twelve deep, sunk into the crest like a thumbprint in wet clay, and full to the brim with water. Water is the room. There is almost no air in it — a skin of spray, a metre of churning whiteness above the surface, and then the cloud closes down and the fall arrives out of nothing, a column of water as thick as a person, dropping straight from the fog with no visible source, hammering into the pool at a point slightly off-centre. Where the fall lands the pool is not water at all but a permanent explosion, a white crater that keeps blowing open and refusing to fill.

The sound in the air is bad. The sound in the water is worse. If you enter — and the rim is such that you cannot approach without entering, the stone shelves off under an inch of moving water and then there is nothing — the noise stops being something you hear and becomes something you are inside. It comes through the chest and the jaw. It is the sound of a drum being struck from within. The fall does not roar so much as beat, an irregular pulse, because up in the cloud something is metering it: it surges, chokes, surges again, and each surge arrives in the pool as a pressure wave that you feel in the soft parts behind your eyes.

And yet the light is ordinary. That is one of the strange things. Under the cloud you would expect gloom, but the fog is thin and the sun is on top of it and the whole room is lit with a clear, even, sourceless brightness, like a kitchen at midday. The water is transparent to the bottom. You can see everything. There is very little to see.

---

The pool is nearly empty. This is what takes time to understand. Its walls are smooth and pale and curve down to a floor you can just make out, and in the whole volume of water — enough to fill a small chapel — there is almost nothing: no weed, no silt cloud, no fish. A few dark fragments, deep down. The white column of the fall, hanging in the middle like a pillar that is also a wound. Yourself. The space between things is enormous relative to the space itself; it is a small room built around a great absence, and the absence is loud.

The walls are the second strange thing. They are not worn. Every other plunge pool on every other mountain is a record of destruction — gravel-scoured, undercut, potholed by stones the water spun for ten thousand years. This pool is the opposite. Its walls are being *added to*. The water here comes down from the cloud carrying something dissolved — lime, or something like lime, or something for which there is not yet a name — and where it slows and swirls against the rock, it lets go of its burden and the burden becomes stone. The walls have the texture of candle-wax, of cooled sugar, of the inside of a kettle. They are furred with a pale crust that is still soft in places, that gives slightly under a fingernail and then, a hand's breadth away, is hard as tooth. Run a palm down the curve and it comes back grey and gritty and faintly warm from friction, and by the time you have climbed out the grit has set on your skin into a fine crackle that you have to pick off.

So the cauldron is closing. Slowly, from the outside in, the room is growing its own walls inward, and the fall keeps blasting the centre open, and between the two of them — the depositing water and the destroying water, the same water — the shape of the place is negotiated moment by moment. Nobody made this. It is still being made.

---

The turbulence does the making. Watch it for long enough and the surface stops being chaos and becomes something closer to a workshop with a single, furious craftsman.

The fall drives a standing column of bubbles down almost to the floor. That column shears against the still water around it and throws off vortices, and the vortices go spinning out to the walls and up, and where each one touches the crust it leaves a mark — a dimple, a whorl, a raised lip — because the eddy slows the water just enough for the water to shed a little stone. So the walls are recording the weather of the pool. Every gust in the cloud above becomes a change in the fall becomes a new pattern of eddies becomes a new ridge of wax-rock, and the ridge, once it exists, steers the next eddy. The room is learning its own shape by being repeatedly struck.

Shapes rise on the surface. Foam builds into a ridge, the ridge becomes a spire, the spire leans and stands and is knocked flat. A whirlpool opens by the north wall, a proper funnel with a black throat, screams for half a minute — you can hear its own note inside the larger noise, a higher, narrower shriek — and then the fall surges and the funnel is filled and gone and a mound of upwelling water bulges in its place. The pool builds and unmakes with the impatience of a child at a sandpit. Nothing lasts thirty seconds. Everything is trying.

Sometimes the pulse of the fall drops almost to nothing — the cloud holding its breath — and for two or three seconds the room is only a very loud room, not a deafening one, and the water clears of bubbles and hangs, and you can see straight down to the floor. Then you can see what the dark fragments are.

---

They are the built things.

Someone came here. Not recently, not once. They left a ledge: a shelf pecked into the eastern wall just below the waterline, a hand's width, a body's length, with the tool marks still crisp on its inner face — short, overlapping, hammerstone blows, the rhythm of a person working by feel in a place where they could not hear their own strikes. The ledge is being swallowed. New crust has grown over one end of it and rounded the edge, and in another few years, or decades, or however this place counts time, there will be a bump in the wall and no ledge.

On the floor, under seven or eight metres of moving glass, there are stones that did not fall there. Six or seven of them, arranged in a rough ring, each about the size of a loaf. They have grown together at their bases. Whatever they marked, they now mark the place where they are becoming one stone.

Something lies in the ring. In the clear moments you can see it is a tool — a haft of dark wood and a head of something paler, bone or antler, lashed with a cord that has gone to a soft white sleeve of mineral. It is not corroding. It is being preserved, precisely, in the act of vanishing: the crust follows every fibre of the binding, every facet of the head, and thickens, so that the shape stays and the thing goes. In another century the tool will be a stone in the shape of a tool with nothing inside it.

Higher up, near the rim on the sheltered side, someone has scratched into the soft crust. Lines. A row of short vertical strokes, then a gap, then more. Tallies, or a fence, or the fall itself drawn over and over. The oldest strokes have already blurred under a skin of new stone; the newest are sharp and still show the pale grey underneath. Somebody has been keeping a count of something here for a long time, and the wall has been keeping a slower count over the top of them.

Nobody is here now. You wait, and nobody comes, and the marks say nothing about when the next one will be added, or whether it will be you.

---

Living things are almost entirely absent, and the ones that are present seem to have arrived by mistake and stayed by stubbornness.

A single willow, no taller than your knee, grows out of a crack in the rim where the spray keeps it wet and the wind keeps it flat. Its leaves are stiff and grey-green and coated on the upper surface with the same fine crust as everything else, so that the tree glitters slightly and clicks when the gusts move it. It is alive under the glaze. It may be very old. Below it, on the wet lip, a mat of dark moss holds on where the water runs thin, and the moss too is turning to stone from beneath — its lower layers already mineral, its upper layers still soft, so that it grows on top of its own monument.

In the pool: something small. You see it first as a flicker against the far wall, a pale comma moving against the current where no comma should move. An amphipod, or a thing like one — a curled, transparent crustacean the length of a thumbnail, colourless, eyeless, working its way along the wax-rock with dozens of legs. Then another. There are never more than a few. They graze the fresh crust where the mineral is soft and take something from it, and where they have grazed the wall is smoother, so they too are shaping the room. They have no business at this altitude. They are here.

And once — once in a long watching — a bird. A dipper, brown and white-throated, arriving low out of the cloud, landing on the spray-wet rim and bobbing in that way they have, and then simply stepping off into the maelstrom. It goes under. You see it, in a clear moment, walking on the floor of the pool among the ring-stones with its wings half-open, hunting the transparent things. It stays down an impossible time. Then it is out and gone, back into the fog, and the pool is empty again, and it is as if the room needed to be witnessed and had arranged for it.

---

The rules of the place, as far as they can be learned by standing in it:

The water comes from nowhere. Above the cloud there is no lake, no glacier, no catchment large enough to feed a fall like this. The column arrives from the fog and there is no more to say.

The water makes stone, and it makes it in proportion to how much it is disturbed. Still water here would build nothing. The turbulence is not incidental; it is the mechanism. Violence is how this place accretes. The louder it is, the faster it grows.

Whatever enters and stops moving will be kept. The tool, the ring, the ledge, the moss. The pool has no way to reject; it can only enclose. If you climbed out and sat on the rim for a week you would come away lacquered.

The centre will never close, as long as the fall keeps falling. So the room's future is a shaft: a narrow throat of water going down into an ever-thickening body of stone, all of it built from the record of its own storms, all of it inaudible from the outside except as that grinding in the cloud that the few people who cross the ridge take for a rockslide and hurry past.

It is cool in the water, not cold. There is a faint mineral warmth in it, as though the act of becoming stone gives off heat. Your hands go numb anyway, from the vibration more than the temperature.

When you leave — and you have to, because the sound is disassembling you — you climb the east side by the drowned ledge, which holds, and haul out onto the raw rim, and the noise drops from inside your body back into your ears, and the wind takes the spray off you, and you look back and can see nothing at all. The cauldron is below the lip. The cloud is above it. From ten steps away there is only bare grey ridge and a sound like the mountain being made, and you find, later, on the underside of your forearm, a thin new skin of stone that you did not notice forming, that flakes away under your thumb like frost.
