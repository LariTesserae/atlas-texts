You hear it before anything else, and for a long time it is the only thing.

From a distance — from the last of the salt flats, say, where the ground still belongs to itself — it is a single low note, so wide that you feel it more in the sternum than the ears, like standing near a dam spillway with your eyes closed. It does not rise or fall. It has no rhythm you can find. Closer, it separates a little: a grinding under everything, a hiss over it, and inside both a texture of clicks and impacts so dense they fuse into a hum, the way a million people speaking becomes weather. Closer still, and it stops being a sound you hear and becomes the medium you are in. Shouting does not work. Speech does not work. Whatever lives here has stopped bothering with voices.

The city begins without a wall. The flat simply gets busy. Cracked white mud gives way to cracked white mud with ribs in it, then plates, then low pale shelves that lift knee-high and run in curving parallels toward the horizon, and by the time you understand you are standing on construction rather than ground, there is no longer any ground to compare it to. It goes on in every direction. It has gone on for longer than the geology under it has been dry.

---

It is low. That is one of the first things. Nothing here reaches higher than a few storeys, and most of it is lower — a rolling terrain of terraces, cells, corridors, roofs that are also floors, ramps that turn into ceilings. You can see a long way across it in the hazed light, and the far edge is not a skyline but a continuation, fading into the wet grey of its own exhalation. The city does not rise because it does not need to. It has the whole surface of the world to spread across, and it has spread.

Everything is wet. Not flooded — the water table sits a hand's width below the lowest floors and shows itself in seeps, in the dark weeping line along the base of every wall, in the pools that gather in any depression that stays still long enough to be a depression. The air is warm and saturated, the temperature of blood, and every cool surface streams with condensation. Water beads on the undersides of lintels and drops in a constant fine rain into the passages below. Where it pools it is faintly milky, holding particles of the city in suspension, and where it dries it leaves rings and crusts of mineral white. Steam lifts from the warm floors in the morning and from the cooling walls at dusk, and the whole plain smokes gently at all hours, so that the sun, when it is visible, comes through as a bright soft disc without edges.

The material is one material. Or it presents as one — a pale, dense, slightly translucent substance somewhere between ceramic and packed chalk, greyish-buff where fresh, stained where old. It is cool and slightly gritty to touch and it takes water like stone takes water, darkening, holding it, giving it back slowly. Where it has stood a long time it is streaked rust-orange and copper-green from whatever trace metals the water carries, bloomed with the black-and-ochre varnish of microbial crusts, pitted and polished by wear. There is no plant on it anywhere. There is nothing green. What grows here grows flat and slow and mineral: shelf fungus in the deep damp corners, pale and rubbery; slimes that streak the wet channels; the varnish. That is the whole of the flora. The rest of the living surface is animal.

---

And it moves.

That is the second thing, and it takes longer to see than the first because it moves at the speed of things you think of as still. A wall is not where it was. You did not see it go. You look back along a corridor you walked an hour ago and it is a different corridor — narrower, or opened out into a court that was not there, or closed entirely, its floor now a low ridge that will be a wall by evening. The pale material does not crack and crumble; it *goes*, in a slow plastic flow, thickening here and thinning there, welling up out of a floor in a smooth lens that becomes a bench, a parapet, a room. You can watch it if you hold still and stare at one edge for a minute — the way you can watch the minute hand of a clock. A line advances. A curve rounds. An opening you might have stepped through a moment ago is now a slot for your arm.

It does not do this by any visible means. Nothing pours it. No mechanism is exposed. The material simply behaves as if it were always in the act of deciding what shape to be, and never finishing. Whatever governs the deciding is inside the material itself, or under it, or is the material, and no one here seems able or inclined to say. It is the kind of technology that has stopped being technology and become geology: you do not ask how the tide works.

The roar is this. It is the sound of a continent-sized surface of stone slowly reshaping itself, all of it, everywhere, at once. Every grain shifting against every grain. Every fresh edge shearing, every seep of water hissing as it hits a warm new floor and steams off, every pool draining as its basin lifts and every new basin filling, every crust flaking, every wall settling into itself with a sound like a very slow landslide — none of these events loud, all of them countless, all of them continuous. Stand in one place and the roar seems to come from under your feet. Move, and it comes from under your feet there too.

---

The city is full. There is no empty room in it. Whatever pocket of space exists is occupied — by water, by steam, by crust, by something that has moved in — and the pockets themselves are closing and opening all the time, so that the occupants are constantly displaced and re-housed. Nothing is ever settled and nothing is ever unoccupied. It is a saturated solution that never precipitates.

The animals understand this. They are everywhere, and they are the only things here that seem to have a plan.

Waders stand in the milky pools, tall grey birds with red legs, and lift and resettle whenever a pool begins to lift under them. Small dark swallows nest in the fresh eaves — they favour the newest overhangs, the ones that have just formed and will hold for a day or two, and when a nest goes they have already begun another in a newer overhang thirty paces off. They have been doing this so long that they build fast and light and do not mourn. They scream, of course, thousands of them, but the roar takes it.

There are crabs. There are pale eyeless fish in the deeper channels, fish that travel with the water when a channel drains and re-forms, and things like large slow rasping snails that graze the mineral varnish off the old walls and leave clean pale tracks. There are swarms — clouds of something small and glassy that follow the advancing edges of construction the way gulls follow a plough, rising and settling, feeding on what the shearing exposes. There are rodents or something in the role of rodents, and the things that hunt them. There is a kind of large, slow, heavy-shouldered grazer, pale as the walls, that eats the shelf fungus in the deep damp corners and moves out into the open only to cross from one damp corner to another, and there are always more of them than you expect once your eye learns their shape.

None of them are startled by the movement. A wall begins to rise under a sleeping grazer and it shifts and resettles without waking. A pool drains and the waders walk to the next one. The fauna here have never known ground that stays still, and they have made the instability into a set of rules as reliable as seasons: the new eave holds two days; the low court fills by evening; where the crust is thick the wall has been still long enough to be safe; where it is clean the floor is moving and will not bear a nest. They read the city fluently. It is possible they are the only things that do.

---

There is weather that belongs to it. The heat of all that motion, and the steam of all that water, rise off the plain together, and above the city the air is always turning — a perpetual lid of low cloud that thickens through the afternoon into something dark and heavy and then lets go, hard warm rain that hammers the pale terraces and sheets off them into the channels and is taken back into the water table within the hour to seep out again as condensation on the walls. Lightning walks through the cloud and its thunder is lost completely; you see the flash and hear nothing you can distinguish from the rest. Then it clears, and the disc of the sun comes back through the haze, and the whole plain smokes.

Nothing arrives here from outside the system. The water is the same water. The heat is its own heat. The city is a closed circulation the size of a continent and it has been running long enough that the closure has become its own kind of ground.

---

Because it is old. That is the last thing, and it takes the longest to see, because the movement pulls the eye and the movement is the newest thing about it. But look at the material where it has stood — the varnish is thick, the pitting is deep, the copper-stain has run down into the seams and been partly re-absorbed and run again. Look at the animals: the swallows that build fast, the grazers that do not wake, the fish that travel with the water. Those are not adaptations of a generation. Look at how the turbulence itself has a form. The walls flow, but they flow in a range of shapes — arches of one proportion, terraces of one height, channels of one depth — as if there were a grammar that the shaping has learned and no longer departs from. Wild rivers braid across their floodplains and every braid is different and the river is the same river. The city is a rapid that has been standing in the same place for so long that the standing is older than the rock around it.

It is not becoming anything. It is not finishing. It is not, so far as anything here can tell, going wrong. It is in the state it settled into, and the state is churning, and the churning is warm and wet and deafening and packed edge to edge with things that live in it and know its rules, and it goes on to the horizon and past it, smoking gently under its own sky, remaking itself with a roar that has not stopped in longer than anything has been listening.

You will not be able to go back the way you came. There is no way you came. There is only the way there is now, and by the time you have taken it, there will be another.
