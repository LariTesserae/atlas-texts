**The Warm Throat**

Very high, above where trees stop, above where the last cushion plants give up and the ground becomes nothing but cinder and frost-shattered rock, there is a hole in the mountain that breathes.

You know it first by the smell. Sulfur, but not the sharp match-strike sulfur of a fresh vent — something older and rounder, a sulfur that has been mixing with wet stone and living bodies for a very long time. Then by the warmth, which arrives as a contradiction. At this altitude the air is thin enough that your pulse knocks in your ears and the wind has a mineral edge that dries the inside of your nose. Then you round a fold of cinder and step into a column of breath: moist, body-warm, smelling of guano and hot rock and something faintly sweet, like fermented fruit, though nothing has fruited within a day's walk of here.

The hole itself is not large. A person could stoop through it. Around its lip the black scoria is bleached tan and rust from centuries of steam, and the rock there is smooth in a way the surrounding mountain is not — not carved, not worked, just worn by the passage of warm damp air and by ten thousand years of small bodies landing, gripping, launching. The edges have been softened the way a step is softened by feet. Just inside, on a knob of rock at exactly the height a hand would fall, there is a place polished darker than the rest. You could take that for a coincidence.

---

Inside, the throat drops and widens. You have to go on hands and knees for a body-length and then the ceiling lifts away and you are standing, and the darkness is total.

Not dim. Not gloomy. There is no gradient of light here to speak of — the entrance behind you is a gray coin that does nothing for the room, and after twenty steps even that is gone around a bend. What you have instead is heat and sound and moisture, and these do the work that light does elsewhere. The chamber is not vast. It is the size of a small church, or a large barn: perhaps thirty paces across, with a ceiling you can sense rather than see, a ceiling that is *close* in the way a ceiling is close when it is full of something.

It is full of something.

The sound is the first thing that overwhelms. It is not a roar — nothing in here is large enough to roar — but it is a saturation, a pressure, a thousand small sounds stacked so thickly they become a single texture you can feel on your skin. Chittering, at a pitch that sits right on the upper edge of hearing and then slides above it and comes back. The dry rustle of wing-membrane against wing-membrane. Dripping, everywhere, in a hundred different tempos, water falling from the ceiling onto rock and onto bodies and into pools. A low, wet, continuous seething from the floor. And under all of it, something so constant it takes minutes to notice: a soft rhythmic exhalation from deeper in the mountain, the sound of steam moving through a fissure, a breath every eleven or twelve seconds, never hurried, never late.

---

The ceiling is bats. That is the simplest thing to say and it does not begin to say it.

They hang in a continuous shag, body pressed to body, so densely that when one shifts, a wave of adjustment moves across the whole colony like wind over grass. In the warmth they do not torpor the way high-mountain bats elsewhere must. They stay active, half-awake, grooming and squabbling and rearranging through the whole of the day. Their heat is part of the room's heat. If you stand still you feel it coming down on your scalp, a faint radiant press, along with a mist of exhalation and a steady faint rain of droppings that patters on the floor and on your shoulders and does not stop.

There is more than one kind. There is a small dark kind with a folded leaf of a nose that clusters tightest and lowest, around the warmest part of the ceiling directly above the vent. There is a larger, paler kind with long ears that keeps to the cooler margins and hangs a little apart, in loose curtains. And there is a third kind, which you would not identify as a bat at all by sound, because its call is a low churring almost like a frog's, and which hangs in the deepest cleft of the ceiling in a mass so dense and so long-established that the rock beneath them has been dissolved by centuries of ammonia into a soft pitted crust.

They leave at dusk. That is when the room's sound changes character — not louder exactly but organized, the chittering resolving into a purposeful clatter as the colony peels off the ceiling in layers and funnels toward the throat, a black river running upward and out into the cold thin evening, out to hunt across the high slopes where insects rise on the day's last thermals. And they return before dawn, and the room fills again, and the sound goes back to its texture. But not all of them leave, ever. There are always the nursing mothers and the old and the sick, and there is the third kind, which does not seem to leave at all and which no one has seen feeding. What it eats is one of the room's small mysteries.

---

The floor is the second overwhelming thing, and it is worse.

Guano, a meter deep or more, warm and wet and steaming faintly where the heat from below comes up through it. And in the guano, life at a density that does not seem possible. Beetles — a black, glossy, flightless kind with a rounded back, moving in such numbers that the surface of the floor is never still. Pale cockroaches. Springtails in drifts like spilled flour. Small white crickets with antennae longer than their bodies, blind, feeling their way across the tops of the beetles. Mites that you cannot see individually but that you can feel, a faint continuous tickle where your hand touches anything. The whole floor is a single slow organism made of ten thousand small ones, digesting what falls from above.

The seething sound is this. If you put your palm flat on the ground you feel it as a vibration.

Above the floor, on every surface of the walls up to about shoulder height, the rock is furred. Not with plants — there is no light for plants, and the room's vegetation, such as it is, consists of a few pale filaments of fungus threading through the guano and a leathery rind of something bacterial that grows around the vent itself, in bands of gray and dull orange, and that is the only thing in the room that could be called a garden. The fur on the walls is a crust of small snails, shells thin as fingernails and nearly transparent, packed shoulder to shoulder, rasping at the microbial film that grows where condensation runs. Between the snails move harvestmen with legs like hairs, and small pale spiders, and a kind of centipede the color of old teeth that is the room's chief hunter at this level and moves with a disturbing calm.

And there are the frogs. This is the room's real strangeness, the thing a naturalist would not believe without seeing. Down where the floor meets the wall, in the shallow pools of condensate that collect in every hollow, live frogs — small ones, the size of a thumb-joint, mottled brown and nearly eyeless, their eyes reduced to dark dimples under the skin. They should not be here. There is no way for a frog to reach this altitude; the slopes outside would freeze them in an hour. But they are here, and they have been here long enough to lose most of their eyes, which means they have been here since before the mountain was as high as it is now, since a time when the slopes outside were warmer, and they have simply stayed while the world lifted and cooled around them, keeping to the one place that stayed warm. Their calling is the churring you took for the third kind of bat. Or perhaps the third kind of bat has learned to sound like them. In the dark it is not easy to tell where a sound is coming from.

---

Water moves through the room without ever arriving as rain. It condenses. The steam from the vent rises through the mass of warm bodies, cools against the ceiling where the rock meets the outside air, and forms droplets that swell and fall. Every surface is beaded. The bats are damp. The walls run. Where the water finds a channel it has, over the long time, carved shallow grooves down the rock face, and along these grooves the snails cluster thickest, and at the bottom of each the frogs wait in their pools. The pools drain slowly into the floor, into the guano, which holds the water like a sponge and steams it back up again. The room is a closed cycle, or nearly. A little water leaves as vapor through the throat. A little comes in as snowmelt seeping through cracks in the roof, a few drops a minute, cold, so that there are spots on the floor where the warm floor-life parts around a small chilled circle.

The heat is steady. It has been steady long enough that the whole room has reached a kind of settlement with it. The vent does not surge; the mountain here is old and its fire has burned down to this one patient exhalation. Nothing in the room is new. The colony's ceiling-crust is centuries thick. The frogs' eyes took a long time to go. The rock has been etched and softened by ammonia and warm water until there is not a sharp edge anywhere below the height of your head. Everything that could wear has worn, and now it simply continues.

---

There are no structures here. Nothing has been built or stacked or arranged. But the room is not unvisited.

On a shelf of rock just inside the throat, above the reach of the floor-life, there are things. A few flakes of dark glassy stone, the kind that chips to an edge, lying where they were struck. A small blade of the same stone, dulled. A length of cord made from twisted fiber, mostly rotted. A gourd, split and empty. And bones — small bat bones, cracked and sucked, in a scatter that is old and a scatter that is not old at all.

Someone comes. Not often. The polished knob at the entrance says that hands have gripped it for a very long time but that they grip it perhaps once a season, perhaps once a year — enough to shine the rock, not enough to groove it. They come up out of the cold, through the throat, into this warmth, and they take a few of the sleeping bats from the low margins of the colony where the pale long-eared kind hangs loose enough to reach. They eat them here, in the dark and the noise, because it is warm here and warm nowhere else on the mountain. They leave the flakes of their knives where they fall. They have never made a fire in this room; there is no soot on the walls, no char in the guano. There would be no point. The mountain is the fire.

Whether they know about the frogs is not clear. There is no sign that anything has ever been done to the frogs. Perhaps in the dark they have never noticed them. Perhaps they have noticed and decided the frogs are not theirs to take.

---

If you stay in the room long enough — an hour, half a day — the noise stops being noise. It becomes a weather. You stop hearing the individual drips and the individual chitters and start hearing the room as a single ongoing statement, a loud steady presence like surf, with the mountain's breath underneath it counting off the intervals. The heat goes into your bones. The dark, which at first felt like a lid, becomes a medium, something you are suspended in rather than pressed by. Your skin learns the room: the ceiling's radiant warmth above, the floor's vibration below, the wall's beaded coolness to the side, the faint stirring of air toward the throat that tells you which way out is.

And the animals take you in. Not gently — the mites find you, and the beetles climb your legs, and the droppings never stop falling — but without alarm. You are one more warm body in a room that has always been full of warm bodies. The colony adjusts around your presence the way it adjusts around a shift in the vent. There is a slight lean in the sound, a moment's reorganization, and then the texture closes over again.

The room does not know it is high. It does not know it is on a mountain, in a cold thin air, above the line where anything green can live. It knows only that it is warm and dark and wet and full, and has been, and will go on being, as long as the mountain keeps breathing at its own slow pace, one exhalation every eleven or twelve seconds, never hurried, never late.
