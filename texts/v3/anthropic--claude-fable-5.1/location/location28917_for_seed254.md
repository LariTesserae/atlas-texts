You hear it before the stairs end. Not a sound so much as a floor beneath other sounds — a low, continuous pressure that comes up through the iron treads and into the bones of your feet, and then, as you go down, into your knees and chest and the roots of your teeth. Water falling and being caught. Iron turning. Something electrical humming at a pitch just too low to be a note.

The stairwell is a cut-and-cover shaft lined in glazed brick, brown to shoulder height and cream above, and every brick is furred. Not dirty — furred. A moss the colour of old copper coins has taken the mortar lines and then the faces, and it holds a skin of condensation so that the walls shine faintly in the light of the lamps. There are lamps every second landing: wire-caged bulbs on brackets, most of them working, throwing a tired amber that reaches about three metres before the wet air drinks it. Where the light lands the moss is thick as a folded blanket. Where it doesn't the moss thins to a dark velvet, and then to a black slick of liverwort that you would not want to trust your hand to.

The air is warm and it gets warmer as you descend. It smells of wet mineral and bruised leaf, and under that of hot varnish, the particular odour of an electric machine that has been running a very long time.

---

The stair lets you out onto a gallery, and the gallery hangs over the hall.

It is not large. You could shout across it if shouting meant anything down here. Perhaps forty paces long and half that wide, with a barrel-vaulted roof of riveted plate rising two storeys, and the whole of it organised around a single fact: the river comes in at one end and leaves at the other, and on the way it turns three machines.

The penstocks come through the upstream wall as a rank of great iron pipes, each broad enough to walk inside, curving down in a slow elbow and vanishing into the floor. Their bolts are the size of fists. Every bolt head wears a cap of moss, and the moss along the top of each pipe has been growing so long and so undisturbed that it has become a ridge, a green spine, with ferns rooted in it — hart's tongue, mostly, glossy and undivided, their strap leaves hanging down over the curve of the iron like tongues from a row of exhausted animals. The pipes are warm. Not hot; warm the way a body is warm. The water inside is moving too fast to cool.

Below, on the turbine floor, the three generators stand in a line. They are the height of a two-storey house, cylindrical, cased in cast-iron panels that were once painted a railway green and now show that green only in patches where a panel has been recently opened and closed. Everything else is grown over. The generators wear their moss the way a stone wears lichen, in maps and continents, and where a casing seam leaks a little warmth the moss thickens to a cushion and the cushion sprouts fern. At the base of each machine, where the bearing housings give off their steady heat, there is a ring of *Selaginella* — a lacy, creeping club-moss, pale green, almost blue in the lamplight — growing precisely to the edge of the warmth and no further. Three green haloes on the floor. The floor itself, between them, is no longer visible. It is a spongy carpet, ankle-deep, that gives under a boot and closes again behind it. Walkways have been kept open through it, and these are the only places where you can see the original surface: cast-iron plate with a raised lozenge pattern, worn smooth in the centre of each path.

The machines are turning. You can't see it — there is nothing exposed to see — but you can feel it in the gallery rail, a tremor that never varies, and in the air, which the rotors move gently through vents so that the ferns on the far side of the hall lean very slightly towards you, always, as if the whole room were listening.

---

The noise has layers, once you have been in it long enough for your ears to give up trying to hear anything else.

Lowest is the turbines, a roar without edges that seems to come from the floor and the walls equally. Above it is the water: the tailrace at the downstream end, where the spent river boils up out of the draft tubes into an open channel and pours away under a grille into the dark. That is a wet, chaotic sound, white and full. Above that, the electrical sounds — the three generators each humming at a pitch a hair apart from the others, so that the hums beat against each other in a slow throb, a swelling and dying every few seconds like something breathing. And above everything, tiny and constant, the drips. Thousands of them. Every rivet in the roof has grown a stalactite of pale mineral and every stalactite has a drop at its tip, and the drops fall onto iron and moss and standing water and make a rain that never stops and never gets heavier.

The moss takes some of the noise. You notice this. The hall should ring and it doesn't; the vault should throw echoes and it swallows them. Somebody, long ago, would have needed to put their mouth against your ear to be heard, and now perhaps they wouldn't even manage that. Nothing is said here. There is no room in the air for it.

---

Off the hall, through openings in the side walls, is the rest of the place — and this is where it stops being a machine and starts being a settlement.

Whoever built it built to stay. A gallery runs the full length on each side at first-floor height, and off the galleries are rooms, and the rooms are terraced back into the rock in two and in places three tiers, connected by iron stairs and short ladders and, at the upstream end, a tramway with a hand-winch that once brought stores down from the surface. There is a control room, with a wall of dials behind glass gone milky, and the needles in the dials still standing at their marks. A workshop, with a lathe under a dust-sheet that has become a moss-sheet. A canteen with long tables, benches attached, an urn on a shelf, and the shelf and urn and tables all one continuous soft green form now, so that the room reads as furniture only if you already know what furniture is. A bunkroom with twelve iron frames. A washroom with white tiles, and the white tiles are the greenest room of all, because the washroom had the most light and the most standing water. A tiny room with one chair and a shelf of swollen books, which might have been an office or might have been a place to sit.

Cables run everywhere along the walls in cleated bundles, and roots run beside them, and you have to look twice to tell which is which. The roots come down from above — from the shafts, from the surface, from the trees that have found a warm, wet, unresisting seam in the ground and sent their feeders down it for decades. In several places they have come through the brick and now hold the brick together. In the workshop a root as thick as a forearm has wound itself around a broken pipe-bracket and taken over its work; the pipe hangs from the root. Nobody arranged this. It's just what happens when a place is left to its own logic for long enough.

---

The light is the light of a place that runs on its own electricity and spends it carefully.

The caged bulbs in the hall and on the galleries give the amber pools. There are also, at the upstream end, two ventilation shafts that go straight up to the surface, and daylight comes down them — a grey, filtered, distant daylight that has passed through whatever grows at the top of the shaft and arrives as a green-tinged column, thin as a rope, moving very slowly across the floor over the course of a day. Where these columns fall the plants change. The mosses and ferns give way to things with ambitions: bramble, pale and leggy, feeling its way along the floor; a sycamore seedling every year in the same spot, putting up its two leaves and lasting a season on borrowed light and then folding back into the carpet; ivy, coming down the shaft wall in a continuous sheet and spreading across the floor in a fan, exactly as far as the light will let it and not one leaf more.

The most remarkable growth is under the lamps themselves. Each bulb, on for decades, has made a small garden directly beneath it — a bulge of moss and fern pressing up toward the warmth and the light, in one place tall enough that the frond tips actually touch the wire cage and have browned there. Look along the gallery and you see it: a row of green pillars, each one reaching for its own small electric sun.

Between the lamps and the shafts, in the greater part of the space, it is dim in the way that the inside of a cupped hand is dim. Shapes, not detail. Your eyes adjust and then adjust again and still you find yourself moving with a hand out.

---

There is nothing alive here that moves under its own power.

This is the thing you notice last and it is the strangest thing about the place, though there is nothing strange about the reason. The water comes in through screens, and the screens are fine, and so no fish. The shafts have grilles and the grilles are intact, and so no bats, no birds. And the insects — there should be insects, in warmth and wet like this — but the hall has been sealed for a very long time, and what came in on the wind as spores could come, and what needed to fly or crawl in could not. You look for webs in the corners of the vault and there are none. You look on the undersides of the fern fronds for the small brown scatter of things eating and there is none. The ferns are perfect. Not one leaf has a hole in it.

So nothing here eats anything. That is the rule the whole place runs on. The plants grow, and die, and lie where they die, and the wet holds them and the warmth breaks them down and the next year's growth comes up through them. The floor is not a floor; it's a shallow peat, decades deep, built entirely out of moss that nobody has ever grazed. Cut into it with a knife and you'd find the layers, the seasons, thin dark bands where the light in the shafts fell short one winter and the growth stalled. It is a record kept without any intention of being one.

---

Time here is measured in three ways and none of them is fast.

The water rises and falls with the season on the surface, a hand's breadth between spring and late summer, and you can read it on the tailrace wall as a band of paler moss that gets wetted and dried. The columns of daylight lengthen and shorten and swing. And the plants do what plants do at the rate that plants do it — a fern frond takes a fortnight to uncurl; a mat of moss takes a year to thicken by the width of a coin; a root takes a decade to close its hand around a beam.

Everything else is constant. The machines turn at the speed they turned at when they were set going, because the water has not stopped and there has been nothing to stop them. The hum does not vary. The drips do not vary. Someone, sometimes, must come — a panel is fresh-painted; a walkway has been kept clear; the dust-sheet on the lathe, however green, was put there by hands — but whoever it is leaves no more trace than that, and the place closes over their passing the way the carpet closes over a footprint.

It is not ruined. That's important. Nothing has failed. The roof holds, the penstocks hold, the current goes up its cables into the dark and out to wherever it goes. It's simply that the building was made of iron and brick and warmth and water, and only one of those four things was ever going to win, and it has, and the other three have made their peace with it. The green is not eating the machine. The green is the machine's overcoat now, its lagging and its packing and its rust-proofing, and the machine goes on under it, roaring quietly in its blanket, turning the river into light and heat and giving the heat and a little of the light back to the moss.

Stand on the gallery long enough and the noise stops being noise. It becomes a kind of silence with a floor to it. The drips fall, the ferns lean toward you, the three hums swell and die and swell, and nothing anywhere in the place does anything it hasn't been doing for a very, very long time.
