The mountain is new. That is the first thing to understand about it, and everything else follows. It was not here in the sense of having a shape when the last long weather ended; it came up in a single sustained motion over a few thousand years, the way a knuckle comes up when a hand closes, and the rock has not yet learned what wind does to rock. The ridges are edges. The summit block has faces that catch light like broken pottery. Nothing has rounded. There are no scree fans at the bottoms of the cliffs because the cliffs have not had time to shed anything worth calling a fan; there are only a few slabs lying where they fell, still fitting the wound they left above.

It stands high enough that the air is thin and the sky is a hard, depthless blue that somehow reads as normal daylight — bright, even, no drama to it — but the cold is constant. Not killing cold. The cold of a cellar in a stone house, of the inside of a bell. Water does not freeze here so much as hesitate.

Because there is water. That is the second thing. Warm, moist air comes up the south side of the range from lowlands that cannot be seen from here, climbs, and lets go of what it carries against the young stone. Everything weeps. Every north face has a film on it. Where two slabs meet there is a bead and where a fracture runs there is a seep, and in the early hours the whole upper mountain steams gently as the first light finds the wet surfaces and the fog boils off them and re-forms an arm's length away and settles again. Nothing grows in any of this. There is no lichen. There is no crust of algae in the seeps. The stone is too fresh, the chemistry too raw, the cold too steady, or perhaps something else keeps it clean. The wet rock is exactly as gray or exactly as black as dry rock, only shining.

---

Partway down the east side of the summit block, a fracture opened during the uplift and never closed. From outside it is a dark seam, a hand-width wide at the top and widening below, choked partway down by a fallen wedge of stone. Behind the wedge the fracture opens into a cavity.

It is not large. A tall person could stand in it if the ceiling allowed, and in the center it does. The floor is perhaps four paces across in one direction and six in the other, roughly, if you count the shelves and the low overhang where the ceiling comes down to meet a tilted slab. It is the size of a room in a modest house. That is all the room there is.

The light comes in through the seam above — direct sun for a few hours around midday, gray diffuse light the rest of the day — and it is enough to see by. Plenty, in fact. The wet walls throw it around. There is a curious quality to illumination in a wet stone chamber: it arrives from everywhere at once, low and even, so that shadows are soft and everything is legible without anything being lit.

The walls sweat. There are places where the seep has organized itself into a steady vertical line, a thread of water running down a face and pooling at the bottom in a shallow basin the fracture happened to leave. There are places where the water does not run but beads, thousands of beads on the ceiling, each one holding a bright point from the seam, and one lets go every few seconds with a sound you could not hear from outside.

And in the cavity, along the walls and out across the floor and up into the ceiling where the beads hang, is a city.

---

Not for anything human-sized. Understand that first. The structures are on the scale of the space they are in — the tallest of them rise to about the height of a knee, and most are lower, and the smallest are the size of grains and are not visible as individual things but only as a texture, a sort of frost, on the surfaces of the larger structures.

They are pale, mostly. Off-white, bone, a color that could be ceramic or could be salt or could be neither. Some are gray-blue where the water runs over them constantly. A few, up near the seam where the sun reaches at noon, have a faint warmth in the surface that might be pigment or might be a thin skin of something doing work.

The shapes are not decorative but they are not the shapes of any machinery either. They are more like the results of a long argument between structure and purpose. Towers that are also channels — hollow, with water moving down inside them, visible through openings where the wall thins. Low broad things with corrugated tops that catch the falling drops and route them. Bridges, if that is the word, arching from one shelf of rock to another across the floor, a finger-width wide, and along the top of each a groove, and in each groove a moving thread of water. Lattices climbing the walls that follow the seep-lines exactly, so the seep now runs through the lattice rather than across bare stone, and the lattice is dark and glistening with it. Up in the ceiling, hanging from the bead-points, are fine inverted structures, pale stalactites that are not mineral, that reach down toward the floor and stop, and every one of them is wet along its whole length.

There is a great deal of empty floor between all this. The city is not crowded. Structures stand with space around them — bare wet stone, sometimes a shallow pool, sometimes a channel cut or grown into the floor — and the eye can rest. It has the feel of a town in a valley that could hold far more, where every building has a yard. Things are placed. Nothing is jammed.

---

Stand still and you will begin to see that it moves.

Not dramatically. Not in a way that would trouble anyone who came in expecting a cave. But the city is a machine for water and the water is in constant motion, and a machine for water in a place where water arrives from every surface has a great deal to do.

Along the bases of the towers there are apertures that open and close. Not on a rhythm; in response. When the noon sun comes through the seam and the ceiling steams and the drip-rate rises, the apertures widen and the corrugated collectors take on a faint sheen of movement as they tilt — a few degrees, no more — to meet the drops. In the late afternoon, as the light thins and the cold hardens and a rind of frost starts to form on the shadowed wall, something along that wall gets slightly warmer and the frost never quite arrives. You can watch the frost try, bloom in a small fern-pattern, fade, try again a few centimeters over.

The water is being moved uphill in places. This is one of the things that is not right about the city, and it is the only thing that is not right. There is a channel on the floor that runs into the base of a hollow tower and the water in it goes in and, a moment later, water comes out of an opening two-thirds of the way up the tower and spills into a bridge-groove and travels across the room. There is no pump. The tower does not hum or tick. The water simply goes where the structure wants it to go, and the structure appears to be a single solid thing with no moving parts inside. Once you notice it you notice it everywhere: water in these channels arriving places that gravity would not have taken it, and always quietly, and always unhurried.

It is very quiet. Outside, the wind on the summit block would be a constant presence; inside, the fracture is so narrow and the fallen wedge so well placed that the wind is a faint pressure in the ears more than a sound. What there is: the drops. A drop into a pool. A drop onto a collector, which sounds different — a small dull sound, no ring. The running threads of water, which are almost silent but not quite; if you hold your breath there is a whisper in the room the way there is in a house where a tap has been left slightly on. Now and then, from somewhere in the mass of the city, a small tick, as if a structure has settled or grown by a fraction and something has taken the strain.

Because it is growing. The frost-texture on the surfaces is not frost. In the bright hours it thickens in places, a pale rime along the edge of a tower or the lip of a channel, and by the following day the edge is a fraction higher or a fraction wider. Whatever is building the city builds it out of what the water brings — dissolved mineral leached from the raw new stone, a little carbon from the air, something from the sun that comes through the seam for those few hours — and it builds slowly, and it does not stop, and it appears to have an opinion about what the room should become. The channels are being straightened. A pool that was here last season has been given an outlet. The ceiling structures are lengthening toward the floor by a hair each week and there are places where the floor structures have begun to reach up toward them, and it is not hard to see that eventually there will be columns, and that the columns will be wet on the inside, and that the room will be less a room and more an interior.

---

There is very little living here. The stone outside is bare. The room is bare of anything green or gray or crusted. But it is not entirely without animals.

On the north wall, low, in the angle where a shelf meets the floor, there is a small dark thing about the length of a fingernail. It is some kind of arthropod — many-legged, slow, glossy with the film of water it lives in — and it moves along the base of a lattice with the sureness of something that has a route. It is drinking, or eating, or cleaning; it puts its front end against the pale material at intervals and stays there, then moves on. The structure does not appear to mind. Nothing closes against it. It may have been here before the city was; it may have arrived with the water; it may, in some way that is not obvious, be part of how the city knows what it knows. It is the only one of its kind visible. Once, in the afternoon, there is a second: a small pale mite, drifting on the surface of the largest pool like a seed on a pond, borne to the outlet and carried into the dark inside a tower and not seen again.

That is the fauna. Two things, and one of them gone.

---

What the city is for is not written anywhere and no one is here to say. It would be easy to invent a purpose — a reservoir being built against some future dryness, a seed of infrastructure waiting for the mountain to stop rising so that something larger can begin, a test, a garden of one plant that is a building. But the room does not offer any of this. It offers a small cold space in a young mountain where water arrives from every direction and something patient has taken that water and begun to make a place.

Toward evening, the light in the seam goes copper for a few minutes and every bead on the ceiling holds a copper point. The drip-rate falls. The apertures at the bases of the towers narrow. Somewhere in the walls a faint warmth holds the frost at bay. The many-legged thing has gone into a gap at the base of the lattice and can be seen there if you crouch, folded, still. Water continues to run in the grooves along the bridges, thin as thread, uphill where it must, and the sound of it is the sound of a house at night where someone, in another room, is very carefully not making any noise.

Outside, the mountain goes on rising, so slowly that in a lifetime you would not notice, so surely that the fracture will one day widen and the wedge will one day fall and the room will open to the wind. The city knows this or does not. Either way it is building for it. The newest structures, near the seam, have a slightly different shape from the old ones — broader at the base, lower, with their channels turned inward — and the pale rime along their edges is thicker, this week, than anywhere else in the room.
