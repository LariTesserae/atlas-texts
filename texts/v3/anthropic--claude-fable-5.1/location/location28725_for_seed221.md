The mountain is not here. That is the first thing to understand about the place. Somewhere below, a long way below, there is rock, and there is a slope, and there is the reason anyone would build at this altitude at all — but none of that reaches the chamber. The chamber sits on the mountain the way a cup sits on a table. Everything you can touch was brought up.

It is small. Five paces one way, perhaps seven the other, and the ceiling is close enough that a tall person would keep their head bent. Poured concrete, still in its forms. The plywood shuttering has never been struck; the ties still run through it, the wedges still hold, and where the form was overfilled the concrete has bulged out over the top edge in a grey lip that has not finished slumping. Rebar stands up out of the slab in a forest of rusted stalks, waiting for a second pour that is not coming. A pallet of cement sacks against one wall has gone hard from moisture. The floor is a slab with a broom finish that was only half done, and the broom is still there, lying where it was set down.

None of it has cured. This is the strangeness of the material — it is wet. The concrete sweats. Press a thumb into the wall and it comes away grey, and the mark stays, and stays. The whole room is at the moment of setting, the moment when a thing stops being a fluid and becomes a substance, and it does not pass through that moment. It has been about to harden for as long as it has existed.

And so there is water everywhere. The concrete gives it up continuously. It films the shuttering and beads on the tie-rods and gathers in the low corner of the slab in a pool that never rises past the second reinforcing bar. Overhead, where the ceiling meets the form, a row of droplets hangs and falls and hangs again. The air itself is saturated; it is so cold and so close to freezing that each breath comes out as a plume and does not dissipate, only sinks. Frost has taken the upper walls, the cement sacks, the steel of the scaffold that runs along the north side — a lace of it, thin, white, complete, that neither thickens nor melts. Below the frost line the walls weep. There is a border, quite sharp, between the part of the room that is ice and the part that is water, and it runs level all the way round at about the height of a person's chest.

The thing that keeps it this way is the generator.

It sits in the middle of the slab on a pallet of its own, a squat diesel unit the size of a chest freezer, yellow once, and it runs. It runs at a single speed. There is no surge to it and no lag; it does not idle up or hunt or miss. It holds one note, a low hard note that is more felt than heard, and layered on top of that a second note from the compactor plate that is still switched on beside it, whose rubber mounts have long since stopped absorbing anything, so that it hammers the slab it is sitting on at a rate you can count. The slab does not compact. The generator's fuel gauge shows something under a third, and it shows this always. A bare work lamp hangs from a hook in the rebar, wired to the generator, and it is the only made light in the room: a flat orange sodium glow that leaves the corners unlit and gives the wet concrete the color of clay.

The noise fills the room to the walls. It is the loudest thing that has ever happened here and it has never stopped happening. It shakes the water off the ceiling and into the pool, it shakes the frost off the scaffold and onto the frost, it makes the drops on the tie-rods dance in place. Stand in it for a minute and your teeth find the frequency. Stand in it longer and you stop hearing it as sound and begin to hear it as a kind of pressure, the way you would feel altitude.

The animals came for the warmth.

There is nothing else warm on the mountain. The chamber is not warm either, not really — but the generator's block is, and its exhaust manifold is, and the slab within an arm's length of it is a few degrees above the rest, and on a mountain this high and this cold a few degrees is a country. They came in through the hatch, through the gap where the shuttering does not quite meet, through the conduit sleeve that was cast in the wall and never had anything run through it. They came from below. They kept coming. There is no sign that any of them ever left.

They are on everything. That is what you see first, and then what you see is that there is no surface in the room that is not partly them. Moths on the rebar, so many and so close that the bars appear furred, their wings folded, each one beaded with condensation like the steel it sits on. Moths on the cement sacks, moths on the lamp cage, moths in the frost, gone stiff and grey, indistinguishable from the frost until you look. Bats hang from the underside of the scaffold planks, from the tie-rods, from the lip of overfilled concrete, in a continuous layer, some of them holding onto other bats. There are birds — small ones, finches and something like a wren and something with a long bill that should not be at this altitude — roosting in the scaffold, on the broom handle, along the top edge of the shuttering, shoulder to shoulder, feathers slick. Mice in the folds of the sacks. Something the size of a fox curled in the dry space between the pallet and the generator's frame, its back against the block. Frogs in the pool. Frogs on the wet slab around the pool, wet-backed, dozens of them, packed nearly edge to edge. Beetles in the tread of the compactor plate. A snake, or two snakes, or one long one, laid along the exhaust pipe where it runs out through the wall.

They are still. Not dead — the bats' ears are up, the birds' eyes are open and take the lamp, the frogs' throats are full — but held. The cold has them, and the noise has them, and whatever it is that has the concrete has them too. They do not breathe in any way you can see. They do not flinch when the water falls on them. When a drop hits the back of a frog it beads there and sits, and the frog is exactly as wet as the slab, and after a moment you cannot say where the one ends. The moths on the rebar do not stir when the compactor hammers. They are of a piece with the room, one more layer of made material, laid on last and set at the same moment as everything else.

You count and lose count. The density is such that there is almost no room for you, and you understand, standing in the one clear patch of slab by the hatch, that if you took a step you would be stepping on something, and that it would not move.

A little green has found its way in. Not much: a skin of algae on the plywood forms where the water runs, a tuft of grass rooted in the mud of a bootprint in the slab, a wisp of something pale and threadlike growing on the wettest of the cement sacks. It is the least present thing in the room and it also does not grow.

Outside the hatch — the hatch is a square of grey a shade lighter than the sodium glow, and that grey is the sky, close, colorless, the light of a place above weather — there is a small wooden platform and a rail and then nothing, and the nothing is white. Cold comes in through the hatch in a steady sheet and meets the exhaust heat and makes the frost line. Neither side gains.

These are the rules, as far as they can be told. Water leaves the walls and enters the pool and the pool does not rise. Frost forms and does not accumulate. The generator burns fuel and the gauge does not move. The compactor strikes the slab and the slab remains soft. The animals arrive at warmth and remain at warmth, and the room can always hold one more, and it is always full. The concrete is about to set. It is a place that was made and then held at the instant of its making, the last instant before it would have become finished and ordinary, and everything that has ever come to it has been taken into that instant too, moths and mice and the fox-sized thing, and laid down, and kept.

It is very loud. It is very cold. It is not going anywhere.
