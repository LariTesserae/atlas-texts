The first thing is that you are below the water and the water knows it.

Not below a surface you can see — below a level. Somewhere above your head, perhaps eleven metres, perhaps fourteen depending on the tide, is the height at which the sea would stand if it were allowed to. It is not allowed to. A wall runs the whole rim of the basin, a long grey-brown thing of rubble and clay, and everything inside the wall lives in the space the sea used to occupy and has not been told it can't have back. The ground here is not land. It is bottom. It is the floor of a bay that has been drained for forty years and has not yet finished becoming anything.

You stand on it and it gives. Not mud exactly — mud implies a stage after this. This is sediment that was laid down under water in flat annual layers, never compacted, never dried, and it has spent four decades slowly discovering air. The top few centimetres have formed a skin, grey, salt-bloomed, split into polygons the width of a hand. Step on the skin and it holds for a moment and then the plate tilts and the underside is black and glistening and smells of sulphur and low tide and something sweeter, older, a smell that has been shut in the dark since before there were people to name it. Water comes up through the crack immediately. Not fast. It doesn't need to be fast. It just rises to the level it wants and stops, a small bright coin in the grey, and in an hour there is a pool there, and in a day the pool has a green film on it, and in a week there are things swimming in it.

Everything is wet like this. Not raining — it rarely rains — but the whole basin sweats. The sea is on the other side of the wall and the sediment beneath your feet is connected to that sea by a hundred metres of saturated silt, and the pressure differential is enormous, and so water comes up. It comes up in the polygon cracks. It comes up in slow domes where the ground lifts and splits and weeps. Along the foot of the wall it comes out of the rubble in a thousand threads, each one no thicker than a finger, each one running down and joining others, so that the base of the wall for its entire length — and the wall is long, it takes a day to walk round, this basin is the size of a town and its outer districts — is a continuous seep, a fringe of water that never dries and never quite becomes a river.

The air is thick with it. That's why the light is the way it is. The basin sits in a hot country under a sky that would be blistering elsewhere, but the wall makes a bowl, and the bowl fills with its own exhalation, and the sun comes down through a lid of pale vapour and arrives grey. Not dark. Dim. A permanent late-afternoon that lasts from dawn to dusk. You lose the sense of what time it is almost at once. Distances go soft. The far side of the basin is a suggestion. The pumping stations along the wall are dark shapes with a haze around each, and you can't tell if the haze is steam or exhaust or just the general breath of the place.

You can hear them though.

You can hear everything. The sound is the thing you notice second and then never stop noticing. The wall holds it in the same way it holds the fog. The pumps are the floor of it — there are eleven stations, big squat brick and concrete houses set into the rim, and each one runs a bank of electric centrifugal pumps that have not stopped in your lifetime, and they make a sound that is not a roar exactly but a pressure, a low continuous felt thing that you register in your sternum and the roots of your teeth. Above that is the water they lift. Each station discharges over the wall through great riveted iron pipes, and the water hits the sea on the other side, and the sound of that comes back over the wall as a kind of endless collapse, a wave breaking that never finishes breaking. And behind that, on the other side, the actual sea, the real one, working at the outside of the wall the way it has worked at it for forty years, patiently, and the wall transmits it. Put your hand on the rubble and you feel the sea. Put your ear to it and you hear it directly, a slow enormous respiration, and it's the most frightening sound in the basin because it is the only one that doesn't care about you.

But those are only the mechanical sounds, and they are not the loudest.

The loudest is alive.

Look up — not far up, the fog is low — and the air is occupied. Look along the wall and the wall is occupied. Look at the sediment flats and the flats are occupied. Every ledge of the drainage cuts, every rubble slope, every exposed spar of the barge that was left stranded in the middle distance when the water went down and has rusted there since, every square metre of anything that isn't actually liquid has something standing on it, and the somethings are mostly birds, and the birds are screaming.

Nothing planned this. When you drain a bay you don't remove what lived in it, you strand it, and what you strand is food. In the first years the flats were carpeted with dying things and the birds came from a thousand miles round and never left. Now it has settled into a rhythm, but the rhythm is not quiet. The sediment is full of worms and burrowing shrimp, cousins of the old bay fauna that have adjusted to living in a floor that is now brackish groundwater instead of open sea. The pools that form in the polygon cracks fill with tiny fish that came through the silt as eggs. The seeps along the wall run with eels — real ones, long, brown, thick as your wrist — that have found a way through the rubble from the ocean and hunt along the foot of the wall in the shallow braided water, and you see them constantly, a dark ripple against the flow, and the birds see them too. Crabs by the tens of thousands, pale grey crabs almost the colour of the skin of the sediment, who have taken the polygon cracks as burrows and live in them so densely that at dusk the whole flat seems to shift and click. Flies, in clouds. Something that might be a kind of freshwater shrimp in the standing water and something that is definitely a salt shrimp in the water near the wall and no clear line between them.

And on all this: gulls, several kinds, in numbers you cannot estimate. Terns, sharp-winged, who dive into the pools. Long-legged waders picking along the seep-line, stilts and godwits and things you don't know, dense as a crowd at a station. Cormorants on the barge and on the pump-station roofs, hung out to dry, a hundred to a roofline. Ibises. At one end where the sediment has stayed wetter and a shallow lake has refused to leave, flamingos, thousands of them, a pink mass in the grey that you would not believe if you saw it in a photograph. Herons, solitary, standing in the crowd like they are somewhere else. Bats at dusk, in a river of their own along the wall. Small brown birds in the sparse salt-plants. Ravens on the pump-house chimneys, watching all of it.

They are all talking at once and the sound goes up and hits the fog and comes back down and hits the wall and comes back in and there is no gap in it. None. You cannot hear yourself speak. You learn to gesture. The workers at the pumping stations wear muffs and communicate with hand signals and a chalkboard, and when they go up to the top of the wall and stand on the outer edge looking at the sea, they do it for the relative quiet, and the sea is not quiet.

There is very little green. This is the thing that keeps the place from being a marsh. The sediment is too salt, too raw, too newly made. A few plants have managed it — glasswort in red-green mats along the higher polygons, a tough grey samphire, a crust of something like lichen on the pump-house walls where they've stood long enough — but the flats are bare for hectares at a time, and where anything grows it grows alone, a single fleshy stem standing up out of the salt-skin with a hundred birds around it. The colours of the basin are grey, black, rust, the pink of the flamingos, the white of gull-bellies and salt, and the bright unexpected green of the algae that films every standing pool and every damp rock and gives the whole floor, when the light hits it right, a faint sick shimmer like the inside of a shell.

The wall is not the only built thing, but it is the only one that matters. The rest is scattered — a pumping station every kilometre or two, and between them almost nothing. A row of survey stakes marching across a flat toward some datum, half of them fallen, all of them white with guano. The barge, which was a dredger once and is now a reef for cormorants, its winch drums seized, its hull rusted to lace where it touches the wet. A concrete gauging post standing up out of the middle of the flats with a painted scale on it, black lines reading in metres, top line well above your head, the water level it was built to measure long gone. Iron pipe on trestles, running from a station down onto the flats to some outfall that was meant to drain a low place and now just adds its own voice to the noise. An abandoned hut, tin roof, on a slight rise of sediment that has consolidated faster than the rest — someone's idea, decades ago, of where the first field would go. Nothing since. The field never came. The land isn't ready. The land may never be ready.

Because this is the rule of the basin and you feel it in every step: nothing here has finished being made.

The sediment has not yet turned into soil. The cracks have not yet turned into channels. Water that comes up out of the ground runs in whatever direction the floor happens to tilt and gathers in whatever hollow it finds, and the hollows shift, and the flow shifts with them, and there is no drainage pattern yet, only a hundred thousand shallow braids that reform every season. The pumping crews cut drains — long straight ditches, dug with a machine that has to sit on timber mats or it sinks — and within months the walls of the ditches slump and the ditch becomes a line of pools and the pools join and the line drifts. Nothing holds a shape. The rock underneath all this, when they've drilled to it, is limestone, old, honest rock, but it is fifty metres down and might as well be on another planet. Everything above it is provisional. It is geology in the moment before geology — the material is present, the water is present, the pressure is present, and time has not yet done what it will eventually do.

And time here is slow. That's the other rule. Forty years of pumping and the basin floor has dropped by perhaps three metres, from settling, from drying, from the slow squeezing of water out of silt that was never designed to bear weight. Three metres. The pumps run all day and all night and all year and they are not winning and they are not losing; they are holding. Every centimetre of consolidation is bought with a decade. The salt in the top layer is being leached down, very slowly, by the rare rains, so that a few years from now — or a few decades — some of the flats may become sweet enough for a grass to hold, and then a soil, and then, someone once believed, farms. The people who believed that are old or gone. The people who work the stations now do not talk about farms. They talk about the seep rate along the north wall, which has been rising. They talk about the sixth station, whose intake keeps silting. They talk about the eels, which they eat, and the ravens, which take their lunch.

There are cycles inside the slowness. The tide, on the other side of the wall, lifts the sea twice a day, and the pressure in the sediment lifts with it, and the seeps along the wall run a little faster for a few hours, and the pools on the flats swell by a finger's width, and the birds move up the wall a little and then down. The seasons: the dry months when the skin cracks wider and the salt blooms white and the flies are unbearable and the fog thins enough that once or twice you see actual sun, small and hard and far away over the rim; the wet months when the sky lowers to meet the fog and the whole basin is one grey breath, and the crews at the stations watch the intake gauges and the pumps are pushed up to their limit and the sound goes from deafening to a thing that seems to displace air. Every few years the sea does something on the far side — a storm, a surge — and for a day or two the seeps come through the wall as jets, and everyone stops sleeping, and then it passes and the level goes back and the birds return to their ledges and the noise settles to its normal roar.

You can walk it. Not across — the middle is too soft — but round, along the top of the wall or along the seep-line where the rubble gives you footing. It takes a day. At every point it is the same and at every point it is different: the same grey floor, the same weeping wall, the same solid mass of birds, the same pressure of sound; but here a pool has become a lake, and here a lake has gone back to skin, and here the barge, and here the flamingos, and here a pumping station with its doors open and a man in the doorway with his muffs on, nodding at you, gesturing at the sky, meaning the weather, meaning the tide, meaning who knows. The basin is big enough to hold all of these things at once and small enough that you feel the whole of it from any single point — the rim always visible as a darker line in the grey, the far stations always faintly there, so that you are never anywhere but inside.

Stand at the bottom in the last of the dim afternoon, with the water coming up round your boots and the crabs clicking and the birds going overhead in a sheet and the pumps in your chest and the sea behind the wall, and the sense you have is not of a landscape but of a held breath. Of something enormous, patient and very wet, waiting just above your head at the level it would naturally stand, and this whole loud crowded provisional floor existing in the gap. The sound is the sound of the gap being kept open. It has been kept open for forty years. It will be kept open tomorrow. Everything living down here has organised itself around the assumption that it will go on being kept open, and none of it has any idea, and the wall weeps, and the pumps go on, and the mud slowly, slowly, with the patience of a thing that has all the time in the world, thinks about becoming land.
