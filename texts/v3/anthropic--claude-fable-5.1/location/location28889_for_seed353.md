From below, the plateau looks like a stump: a tableland with a dark, level rim and vertical sides, rising out of the dry lowlands the way a felled tree's base rises out of a field. Get closer and the sides resolve into grain. Not rock. Bark, layered and fused, going up several hundred meters in a single continuous rind, split here and there by fissures that breathe warm air.

There is no way onto it that was not made. The cultivators — whoever they were — trained the ascents: ramps of buttress root spiraling up the flank, worn concave by ages of climbing, though nothing climbs them now. The ramp is warm underfoot. Everything here is warm underfoot.

At the top the sky closes. The rim is not an edge but a wall, and beyond the wall is a roof, and the roof is the canopy, except "canopy" is the wrong word for something this thick and this intended. It is a ceiling of interlocked bough, grown into vaulting, the joins sealed with a black gum that has cured over millennia into something like obsidian. No light comes through it. None is expected to. The plants here have not needed light for longer than the plateau has been flat.

---

You learn the place first by heat and by smell, because there is almost nothing to see.

The smell is resin, and beneath the resin, something like bread left too long in an oven — hot cellulose, caramelized sugars — and beneath that a sourness, fermentation, the exhalation of enormous slow digestions. The air is completely dry. It pulls moisture from the mouth. There is no water anywhere in this system and there has not been for an epoch; the plant chemistry moved on to oils, to waxes, to a dense amber sap that runs hot in the veins of the structure and never once turns to steam because there is nothing in it to boil.

The heat is not sunlight-heat. It comes from inside. The whole mass of the plateau is metabolizing, and the byproduct is a body-warmth that in places rises to a scorch. You feel the districts before you enter them: a wall grows warmer as you walk toward it, and the warmth has a pulse.

The light, what there is of it, comes from the same source. Where sap runs closest to a surface it glows a very dark red, the color of iron minutes after it stops being visibly hot. Seams of this run through the floor and up the columns like the veining in a leaf held to the sun, except the leaf is the size of a town and the sun is inside it. By these seams you can see your own hands, barely. You cannot see the ceiling. You can see the columns for two or three meters and then they go up into black.

---

It is all one plant. Or it was many plants grafted so long ago that the distinction has healed over. The floor is fused bark polished by its own movement. The columns are trunks — some the width of a house — that have been trained, cabled, and twisted into shapes that trunks do not take on their own. Some are hollow and hum. Some spin.

Because this is the thing: the plateau is a factory, and the machinery is the vegetation.

Walk into one of the halls and you find rows of what were clearly designed as pistons: cylinders of dense heartwood the height of a man, rising and falling in sockets of cork. The motion is slow, a stroke every few breaths, and the engine driving them is growth. Differential growth. One side of a cambium layer laying down tissue faster than the other, so that the whole assembly is forced to move, and when it has moved far enough a lignified catch releases and it falls back, and the cycle begins again. It is horrifically inefficient and it has run without interruption for longer than most mountains stay mountains.

Deeper in, flywheels: disks of grain-wood four meters across turning on axles of a black, glassy heartwood, lubricated by their own sap. Bellows of leaf, if leaf can be a thing the size of a barn door and the thickness of a mattress, inflating and deflating with a sigh you feel in the chest more than hear. Conveyors of braided root, moving. Moving *something*: pressed blocks, dark, faintly translucent, each the size of a brick. They come out of one wall and go into another. If you pry one loose it is warm and dense and smells of the whole plateau at once. It is cellulose and wax and stored heat. It is fuel, or building material, or both, and the factory is making it for the factory. The product of the system is the system's own next thousand years.

---

There are no animals. Nothing crawls on the columns, nothing flutters against the ceiling, nothing has died on this floor for so long that the floor has no memory of it. There is no smell of anything living that is not plant. The quiet is not the quiet of a forest, which is full of small lives making small noises. It is the quiet of a very large machine running well: the tick of contracting wood, the hiss of gas from a vent, the long low thrum of the flywheels which is more a pressure than a sound. You can hear your own footsteps all the way across a hall, and then the hall takes them and does not give them back.

And then something falls.

This is the other thing about the plateau: it will not stay still. The heat drives growth at a speed that would be obscene in a temperate forest. Walls thicken. Sockets close. A column that has been spinning since before there was a word for spinning begins, over the course of an hour, to bind, because its axle has grown a millimeter past tolerance — and the whole assembly groans, and stops, and the growth pressure that was turning it has nowhere to go, and the column splits along its length with a sound like a building giving up, and comes down.

It comes down slowly. That is the mercy of wood. A tower of punk and heartwood leaning, tearing loose from the ceiling vault, folding in on itself, collapsing into a soft avalanche that spreads across the floor in a cloud of hot dust. The dust is the plateau's weather. It hangs in the red glow, cellulose fine as flour, and it does not settle for hours because the rising heat keeps lifting it. In the worst districts you walk through a permanent haze of it, sawdust storms, the air the texture of hot breath, visibility a meter, every surface furred.

Before the dust has cleared, new growth is already in the wound. Pale shoots the thickness of an arm pushing out from the fresh-broken faces, blind and fast, groping toward whatever the pattern says should be there. Within a season there will be a column again. It will not be quite the same column. Nothing here is ever quite rebuilt — it is regrown to a template that has drifted a little each time, so that the machines are copies of copies of copies, rounded, simplified, some of them by now doing things that make no mechanical sense but which the pattern remembers as necessary.

---

It is old in the way that only something never-ending can be old. There are no ruins here, because ruin requires the thing to have stopped. Instead there is erosion of a peculiar kind: the erosion of an organism that has worn its own shape smooth from the inside. Doorways that were once tall enough for the cultivators have closed to slits. Stairs have grown into ramps. Signage — there were signs, panels of fine-grained bark scored with something — has healed over into a raised smoothness you can feel but not read.

And the terrain has flattened. The plateau top was once, you can tell, a landscape: hills of structure, valleys between halls, towers. Now it is nearly level. The high things fell and were regrown lower; the low things filled with the dust of the high things and were built on. What is left is a plain of packed machinery under a black ceiling, stretching further than the glow will let you see, district after district of pistons and wheels and bellows and conveyors, all of it touching, all of it warm, no gap between any two presences that you could put a hand through. In the far distance, where a whole quarter is coming down at once, the red glow brightens and pulses with the fresh-torn sap, and the thrum in the floor changes key.

---

At what must be intervals, though there is nothing here to measure them by, certain of the closed doorways open.

They are the old doors, the ones the cultivators would have used. A section of wall softens, splits along a seam that has been waiting, and swings inward on a hinge of living tissue, and for a while — an hour, a day — stands open onto the next hall. Warm air moves through. Dust drifts. Nothing comes, and nothing goes, and the door closes again and heals shut, and the place where it was is smooth by morning, if there were mornings.

The factory is keeping the hours. It has kept them so long it no longer knows what they were for. It grows its bricks and carries them from one wall to another and burns them, or stores them, or eats them; it opens its doors for shifts that ended before the sea below it dried; it brings down its towers and puts them up again a little wrong; it holds its enormous heat in the dark and does not sleep and does not stop. It is a plant, or it is a machine, or it is a town that grew so completely into itself that there is no longer anything in it but the town.

Standing at the rim, on the way out, you can put your hand flat against the outer bark and feel, through a wall the thickness of a hillside, the slow strokes of the pistons on the other side. In, and hold. Out, and hold. Warm as skin. Older than the shape of the land.
