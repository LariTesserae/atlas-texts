You arrive on it from below, because there is no other way to arrive. The shelf sits at the height where the air begins to give up its water, and so the last thousand metres of the climb are through cloud that has stopped being cloud and started being something more like a substance you walk inside. It is not raining. Nothing is falling. The water is simply present in the air at the same density it is present in the rock, and the difference between the two has become a formality.

Then the ground levels and you are on it, and the first thing you understand is that it is not finished.

---

The stone here is young in the way that geologists mean, which is to say it is younger than most things that have ever been alive. It came up out of the earth not long ago — a few thousand years, perhaps fewer — as a great blister of uplift, and it has not yet decided what shape it is. There are no valleys because nothing has had time to cut them. There are no rounded hills because nothing has had time to wear them. What there is instead is the shelf itself, tilted a few degrees, a plane of dark basalt and glassier things, broken along fracture lines that run straight for kilometres because nothing has interfered with them yet. Where two fractures meet the rock has heaved up in blocks the size of houses, sharp-edged, faces still bearing the dull sheen of stone that has never been touched by sun.

It has never been touched by sun. That is the second thing.

The shelf lies far enough toward the pole that the night here is not a night but a season, and it is that season now, and has been for a long time, and will be for a long time more. There is no moon in this sky, or if there is the cloud has taken it. What light exists comes from below the visible, from things you sense more than see: a slight greyness where the fog is thinnest, a difference between the black of the rock and the black of the air that is not colour but texture, the way you can feel a wall in a dark room before you touch it. Your eyes, after an hour, begin to do the thing eyes do in true darkness, which is to invent. You stop trusting them. You start listening.

---

And the shelf is loud.

It is loud in a way that takes time to separate into its parts, because at first it is simply a pressure — a roar with a grain to it, the way surf has a grain. But you stand still, and the cold gets into your feet, and gradually the roar comes apart.

Under everything is water. The rock weeps. It weeps from every fracture and every pore, a slow steady exudation of water that has been sitting inside the uplift since before the uplift, warmed slightly by depth, rising now through the cooling stone and emerging at just above freezing into air that is well below it. It runs in sheets down the block faces. It pools in every depression and the pools do not freeze, because the rock beneath them is still giving up heat, but they steam, and the steam is what the fog is made of. The sound of it is a hiss and a trickle multiplied by the whole width of the shelf — a hundred square kilometres of stone dripping at once — and it forms a floor under every other sound, so constant that within a day you will stop hearing it, and within two you will notice when it changes pitch, because the pitch tells you the temperature of the rock, and the temperature of the rock is a kind of clock.

Above the water is the city.

---

You should not call it a city. Nothing in it was designed for you and none of its rooms would fit you. But it is built, and it is dense, and it covers the shelf in layered organised construction from one edge to the other, and there is no other word.

It grows out of the water.

That is as much of the mechanism as can be given honestly. Something was put into the groundwater of this uplift — put into it or grown into it or written into it, the distinction is not clear — before the rock finished rising, and now as the water emerges and cools and gives up its dissolved load of minerals, the minerals do not deposit at random. They deposit according to instructions. The water knows where the walls go. It comes up through a fissure and where an ordinary spring would leave a lumpen apron of travertine, this one leaves a wall: a thin true wall, plumb, of pale grey mineral with a surface like unglazed porcelain, rising a fraction of a millimetre a day. Where two flows meet they leave a corner. Where a flow finds a lip to run over it leaves a stair. Where it pools before running on it leaves a floor, and the floors are level, and they are level to a tolerance that no builder using tools would bother to achieve.

The result, after a few thousand years of this, is a settlement of impossible age and impossible youth at once. The oldest parts, near the centre where the springs first broke through, stand ten and fifteen metres high — towers, or things shaped like towers, with the interiors hollowed into cells and galleries where the water ran inside them, and with exteriors fluted and ribbed where it ran down. The newest parts, at the edges, are ankle-high: the first courses of walls, the outlines of rooms, whole neighbourhoods laid out in stone no taller than a kerb, waiting. Between them every stage of intermediate growth. You can walk from a finished district to an unbuilt one in twenty minutes and pass through the entire history of the place, and it is all being built still, at the same speed, everywhere at once, in the dark, with no one watching.

It is warm to the touch. Not warm — warmer. The mineral conducts the last of the rock's heat. In this air that is enough to matter.

---

Which brings you to the third thing, and the reason the shelf is loud.

It is full of animals.

*Full* is inadequate. Every surface you can reach with your hand in the dark has something living on it, and the something is usually many somethings, and they are usually touching each other. The construction is warm and wet and shelters from the wind, and there is nothing else for a thousand kilometres in any direction that is any of those things, and so everything that could get here has got here and stayed.

The sound resolves, once you have spent a night among it, into layers.

The lowest and largest are the seal-things. They are not seals — the shelf is too far from any coast for seals, and these have never seen a sea — but they are that shape and that weight and that colour, and they haul themselves through the wet galleries of the old districts on flippers that are also hands, and they are the reason the floors of the lower city are worn smooth despite the city's youth. They lie in the pools where the springs emerge, packed so close that the pools appear to be paved with them, steam rising off their backs, and they groan. The groan is continuous and tidal and carries, and it is the note you will remember.

Above them, on the walls, are the climbing things: small, many-legged, armoured, wet, clicking. They graze the mineral surfaces of the construction, or graze whatever grows on the mineral surfaces — because something does grow there, in the dark, in the warmth, some film of bacteria or simpler that lives on the chemistry of the emerging water, and it is the only thing on the shelf that could be called a plant and it is not one. The climbers are the second layer of sound. They are quiet individually. There are, at a guess, many hundred million of them.

Above the climbers, in the upper cells of the towers and in the fog itself, are the flying things. Bats, or the local equivalent — leathery, warm, blind by preference, echolocating in a register at the edge of your hearing. They roost in the hollows the water left when it ran inside the walls, and there are so many that the towers, in the dark, seem to breathe: an exhalation of ten thousand wings when something disturbs a colony, a rush and a rustle that travels from tower to tower as one roost startles the next, and then the long settling.

And between all of these, everywhere, in every gap that is left, are the things that eat the others. Slow pale predators the size of dogs that move along the galleries taking climbers off the walls with a sound like tearing paper. Something in the pools that takes the seal-pups. Something in the fog, above the bats, that no one has ever seen and that is known only by a whistle and by the fact that bats sometimes fall.

There is no daylight and there is no plant and there is no season in the ordinary sense, and so there is nothing to hold any of this in check except the others. The shelf is a chemostat. The rock gives up heat and water and minerals at a rate that has not changed in living memory, the film grows at a rate set by the chemistry, the climbers grow at a rate set by the film, and everything above them is set by the climbers, and the whole system sits at its saturation and hums.

---

You learn the rules of the place by breaking them.

You learn that the water does not want to be interfered with, because when you dam a trickle with your hand the wall it was building stops, and stays stopped, and there is a scar in the pattern of the city that will be there when the towers reach thirty metres, a fault in the stone that says *someone stood here*. You learn to step where the seal-things have stepped, which is where nothing is growing.

You learn that the construction is not for the animals, and that the animals do not know this and do not care. Whatever instructed the water had some other purpose — the galleries are the wrong shape for anything living here, the stairs have a rise that fits no gait, there are rooms with no door and rooms with seven — and the fauna have simply occupied it, the way barnacles occupy a hull. They have not changed it. They cannot. The mineral is harder than tooth or claw and grows back over whatever is worn away. In ten thousand years the seal-things will still be groaning in pools of exactly this size.

You learn that the city knows you are there. Not in any way you can prove. But the water runs differently near you — pools where it did not pool, hesitates on lips it would otherwise run over — and when you leave a place and return to it the construction there has moved on in a way that is very slightly not the way it was moving before. Whether this is response or coincidence you cannot say. You are one warm body among several hundred million, and most of the others do not seem to be noticed. But you stand upright, and you carry a light for the first hours until you understand that the light is the loudest thing on the shelf and everything for a kilometre falls silent when you use it, and perhaps that is enough to register.

You learn that nothing here is fast. The city grows at the pace of a fingernail. The rock cools at the pace of a continent. The night is months old and has months to run. The animals breed and die and are born in numbers that would be a catastrophe anywhere else and here are simply the rate. The only quick things are the bats startling and the whistle in the fog and your own heartbeat, and after a while even your heartbeat slows to meet the place.

---

Stand at the edge, at the end.

The shelf runs out in a fracture cliff that drops into cloud, and beyond the cloud, you are told, is the ordinary world — cold, dark at this season, but ordinary, with lichen on the rocks and ice where ice should be. Behind you the city rises in the fog, tower behind tower, each one the residue of a spring that came up through cooling stone and left itself standing as it went, each one full of sleeping breathing wings. The seal-things groan in their pools. The water hisses on the rock. The climbers click. Somewhere near the centre a wall that has been rising for eleven hundred years puts on another layer of grey the thickness of a breath.

None of it needs you. None of it was made for you. It is not finished and it does not appear to be trying to be. It is only growing, in the dark, at the height where the air gives up its water, and the animals came, as animals do, to where it was warm.
