The heap is what you notice first, because there is nothing else at this end of the yard to notice. It sits at the low corner where the ground gives out toward the drainage ditch, a dark mound the height of a two-storey house, and a covered conveyor runs out to it on stilts from the direction of the furnace hall. Once a day, sometimes twice, the conveyor shivers, a motor somewhere behind the sheeting picks up its note, and a fresh load of slag comes down the chute and spreads across the crown of the mound with a sound like gravel poured into a bath. Then it stops. The heap steams for a little while where the new material lies, thin and white, and the steam leans over toward the ditch and comes apart.

That is the outside. The outside is not the place.

The place is under the heap's western flank, where the older slag has settled unevenly and a slab of it, glassy and the size of a garage door, has slid a little way down over the rubble beneath and hung up on something. It has left a gap. The gap is perhaps a metre high at its mouth and lower everywhere else, and you have to go in on your elbows, over material that wants to cut you. Nothing here has been softened by anything. The slag is months old, a year at the oldest, and every fractured edge is as clean as the day it cooled. It rings faintly under your hands, the way thin glass does.

Inside, it is warm. Not hot—the heat of a stone left out in afternoon sun, or the underside of an animal. It comes up through the floor and out of the walls, evenly, from all directions. The heap is still cooling from within. Loads poured months ago are down there in the core somewhere, still letting go of their heat a degree at a time, and this cavity sits in that slow exhalation. Your breath doesn't show. Your palms sweat immediately.

The roof is the underside of the slid slab, and the slab is glass. It is dark green shading to black, ribboned with brown where iron oxide ran in the melt, and it is thin enough along its edges that daylight comes through it. Not much daylight in any one spot, but the whole ceiling is faintly lit, a bruised green ceiling with the shadow of the yard's chain-link pattern laid across it, and between the glass and the sky you can sometimes see the movement of a pigeon or the indistinct shape of a boot going past on the crown of the heap above. The light is ordinary light—the same light as outside, fifty centimetres away—but it has been through the glass and come out sombre and underwater.

And the ceiling drips.

Rain falling on the heap works down through it, through the loose grains and the cinder and the fused lumps, taking on a taste of iron and sulphur as it goes. When it reaches the glass roof of the cavity it is still cool from the sky, and the air beneath the roof is warm and full of the heap's own damp, and so the underside of the slab is always beaded. The beads grow and join and run to low points and fall. There is no rhythm to it. A drop hits the floor, a long gap, three drops close together from different points, a gap, one. The floor where they land has been collecting them all this while, and in the six or seven places where the drips concentrate there are pools no bigger than saucers, rust-coloured, warm, and each one ringed with a crust of white and orange mineral where the water evaporates faster than it arrives. The crusts are building. If you came back in a month they would be visibly taller. The rock in here is being made while you watch, at a pace closer to the pace of the things living on it than to the pace of rock.

Because the cavity is full.

That is the thing that takes a moment to see, and once seen cannot be unseen. It is a small space—you could not lie flat in it without your feet in the entrance—and there is no part of it that is not occupied. Every surface. The floor, the walls, the low back where the roof comes down to meet the rubble, the glass overhead between the drips. It is not that there is a great deal of life here; it is that there is no room for any more.

Around the pools, in the damp orange ring, a mat of something has grown—a slick film, rust and grey-green and in places almost black, the colour of oil on a puddle. It is not a plant. It feeds on the iron in the water and the sulphur, and it is the closest thing this place has to a bottom rung. Springtails move across it, thousands of them, so small and so many that the surface of the mat appears to shimmer, and when you put your face close the shimmer resolves into individual bodies all going somewhere, none of them touching, none of them more than a body-length from a neighbour.

On the mat, midges. In the pools, their larvae, red as thread, coiled and uncoiling in water that would be too warm for them anywhere else. The midges lift off the mat in a haze when your shadow crosses them, and settle again in the same places, and the haze is the only motion in the room that makes a sound—a very slight collective whine, like a wire under tension a long way off, that stops as soon as they land.

Above the mat, on the sharp glass and cinder of the walls, spiders. Not a single web across the space, because there is no space; instead each has a small tented sheet in a hollow of the slag, a hand's width from the next, and the sheets are dusted with mineral from the drips so that they look like they have been there for years. They have not. Nothing has. Between the spiders, harvestmen with their bodies pressed flat and their legs laid out along the fracture lines of the glass. Between the harvestmen, woodlice of a pale blind sort, and small dark snails whose shells have gone thin and translucent from living on a substrate that gives them nothing to build with—you can see the animal moving inside the shell like a fist in a glove.

On the floor, in the warmest place, where the heat comes up strongest through a patch of fused cinder about the size of a doormat, there are salamanders. Nine of them, when I counted; there may be more under the edges. They are lying against one another, overlapping, tails across backs, in a loose pile the way puppies lie. They came up from the ditch, presumably, for the warmth. They are not a species that piles. Their skins are dark and wet and each is beaded with the same condensation as the ceiling, and they do not move much, and when one does the whole pile adjusts by a millimetre to close the gap it leaves.

Toads, three, in the back where the roof is lowest, wedged in. A grass snake, coiled in a fissure between two slabs, thick as a thumb, only its head visible, eyes open, watching the salamander pile with an attention that has no urgency in it. Wolf spiders on the salamanders' backs. Midges on the toads.

Nothing in here eats anything.

That is the part that is difficult to hold. Every animal here is either something that would eat its neighbour or something its neighbour would eat, and they are lying in contact, and the eating is not happening. Perhaps it happens at the mouth of the cavity, in the cool zone, where a toad going out to hunt in the ditch must cross the snake's line. Perhaps at night. But inside, in the warm, in the green light, there is a truce that no one has agreed to and no one breaks. The density has done something to them. A body against a body is a body against a body; the heat comes through everything the same; and there is no room, in any direction, for the space that a strike requires.

Things people have left, or lost, are in here too, half-taken. A thermocouple wire, red and white insulation, comes down through a crack in the roof from a probe that someone drove into the heap from above to log the core temperature, and the wire has a spider's tent on it and a run of snail-track and a line of rust where the copper is exposed. A survey stake, orange plastic, is embedded at an angle in the cinder floor and warm to the touch and has a toad sitting against it. In the back, an iron grate—a piece of a mould box, perhaps, that went down the chute with the slag—lies with its bars half fused into the glass, and the salamanders have used its shadow. A glove. An aluminium tag stamped with a heat number and a date eleven months ago. This is the whole built world of the place: things dropped from above and made part of it.

Overhead, through the glass, the motor picks up its note. The conveyor is running. You feel it before you hear it, as a fine tremor through the floor, and then the sound arrives, dulled and softened by all the mass above—the long pour of slag onto the crown, several metres up and over. Fine dust sifts down from the entrance. In the cavity, nothing startles. But over the next quarter hour the floor grows very slightly warmer; the pulse of heat from the new load is working down. The drips quicken as the roof warms and the condensation rate changes. The midges rise and hang and settle. The snake draws its head back into the fissure by a centimetre. One of the pools begins, very slowly, to steam.

Then the motor stops, and it is quiet again—drip, gap, drip—and the tremor in the floor fades to nothing, and the green light continues to come through the roof with the chain-link shadow lying across it, and the place goes back to what it is doing, which is building itself out of runoff and residual heat and whatever crawls in from the ditch to sit in the warm, all pressed together, a year old and already a world, with a ceiling of glass and rock forming on it at the speed of a snail.
