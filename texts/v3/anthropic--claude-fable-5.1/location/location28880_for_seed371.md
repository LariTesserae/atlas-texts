**The cleft on the shoulder of the mountain, a little after midnight**

You would not find it by looking. There is nothing to see. The cloud that sits on the upper mountain most nights has settled into every fold of the terrain, and above it there is no moon, and below it there is no town, and the ground itself—black glass and black cinder—gives nothing back. What you find first is the sound, and then the change in the air on your face, and then the ground drops away in front of you and you are standing at the rim of something.

It is not large. In daylight, if daylight ever reached the bottom, you could shout across it and be heard, though not tonight. A fissure, perhaps eighty paces long, twenty across at the widest, walled on both sides by rock that has not been outdoors long enough to learn anything about weather. It opened—the geologists who have not been here would say—within the last handful of decades, when the mountain shifted its weight and a seam parted along the shoulder below the summit cone. The walls are the inside of the mountain turned outward. They are still, in places, warm.

And it is full. That is the thing. At this altitude the slope outside the cleft is tussock and frost-shattered stone and the occasional everlasting flower gone grey and stiff. Inside, the fissure is packed to the brim with growing things, layered and leaning and touching, a wet green throat in a bald mountain.

---

Start with the sound, because the sound is what the place is made of.

Underneath everything there is a hum. It is below the range of hearing and above it at the same time; you feel it in the arches of your feet through your boots, a steady pressure like standing on the floor above a pump room. On top of that, continuous, a roar—not a wind roar, though there is wind too, but a roar with a body to it, the sound of a great volume of gas being forced through an aperture too small for it. It has a pitch. It rises and falls in a slow cycle, perhaps forty seconds from trough to trough, and when it rises it fills the cleft entirely and you cannot hear your own footfall, and when it drops back you hear all the other sounds that have been going on underneath it: a hissing from a dozen points along the floor, sharper and higher, like steam bleeding from threaded joints; a ticking, everywhere in the walls, the sound of rock giving up heat; and water, always water, dripping from leaf-tips and stone lips into the dark.

Every so often something lets go. A bang, flat and hard, and then a clatter that goes on for a second or two as a slab of the wall exfoliates and comes down through the vegetation and into the cinder. The glassy rock here is under enormous internal stress—it cooled too fast, at the surface of a body that was still moving—and it relieves that stress in sheets. Nothing has had time to round it. Every edge in this place will cut you. Every broken face is fresh, conchoidal, a curve like the inside of a shell, and if you were to strike a light you would see it glitter.

You do not strike a light. Or you do, briefly, and the beam goes into the steam and dies a metre in front of your face, and you put it away.

---

The floor of the cleft is not level. It slopes from the north end down toward a lower chamber at the south, and it is stepped, because the fissure did not open cleanly; blocks dropped at different depths and sit now at different angles, and the vegetation has taken the terraces they make and filled them. You go down the rim on a scree of cinder that shifts and rings under you like broken crockery. The cinder is warm. Not hot—you can put your bare hand in it—but warm the way a thing is warm when it has been left near an engine.

At the bottom the air changes. Above the rim it is cold, the ordinary cold of the high mountain at night, frost beginning to grow on the stones. Down here it is cool, merely cool, and damp, and it moves. It moves constantly and from several directions at once, because the steam that comes off the floor rises and the cold air off the rim sinks to replace it, and the two meet and coil in the confined space of the fissure and make their own small weather. You will feel the temperature of the air change three times in as many steps. You will feel a current on your left cheek and then a current on your right. The steam does not rise in columns; it is torn apart and driven sideways, pressed against the walls, sucked down into hollows, blown back up.

Your skin is wet within a minute. Not rained on—there is no rain tonight, though it comes often enough—but condensed on. The place is a condenser. The steam is warm and the walls are cool and every surface in the cleft, every leaf, every hair on your arm, is a surface for the water to find and bead on and run off. There is no standing water on the floor; the cinder drains as fast as anything can land on it, down through the loose rock and back toward the heat, where it is boiled and sent up again. Only in the cups of the rock, in the axils of the big rosette plants, in the small dead craters of the old fumaroles, does water pool—dark, faintly warm, tasting of iron and of something sharper.

---

The plants.

Nowhere else on this mountain, at this height, could they grow like this. Outside the cleft, the giant groundsels stand alone, one every fifty paces, stunted, their rosettes clenched shut against the frost. Inside, they crowd. Their trunks—thick, spongy, wrapped in the brown skirts of their own dead leaves—stand close enough that you must turn your shoulders to pass between them, and their crowns, big loose cabbages of grey-green leaf the size of a spread hand, meet overhead and form a low ceiling three or four metres up. Water runs off them continuously. Put your hand on one of the trunks and it is warm to the touch, and wet, and gives a little.

Between the groundsels, the lobelias. These do not branch. They send up a single dense rosette of narrow leaves, waist-high or taller, and from the centre of the rosette a spike of flowers as tall as you are, packed with bracts that hide the flowers themselves. The spikes stand everywhere in the dark, upright, dense, hundreds of them, a crowd of them, and when the steam swings through they lean all together and come back. The rosettes hold water. Each leaf-cup at the base is a small reservoir, and in the cold outside, that water would freeze at night and thaw by day and the plant would survive it; in here, it does not freeze, and the cups are full and warm and the plants use the warmth and grow, grow much faster than they have any right to, and the spikes are taller than the ones outside by half.

Tree heathers—the old, twisted, hard-wooded heath of these mountains—grow out of the fractured walls wherever a ledge or crack has caught enough debris to hold them. They lean inward over the floor. Every branch is hung with lichen, the pale beard kind, long strands of it, sodden and dripping, so that the heathers look less like trees than like something that has been left too long in a damp cellar. Beneath them, on every horizontal surface and most of the vertical ones, moss. Moss on the cinder. Moss on the glass. Moss growing directly on rock that has no soil at all, making its own, because that is what moss is for.

And it is all packed. Density is the character of the place. The groundsels are near enough to touch each other. The lobelias fill every gap between them. The moss covers whatever the lobelias leave. The heathers close the sky. You are inside a body of vegetation so thick that in the roar and the dark and the steam you navigate with your hands, from trunk to trunk, and the leaves you push aside are always wet and always cold on the outside and warm on the inside, where they have been sheltered against the next plant.

There is nothing alive here that moves under its own power. No birds at this hour or any hour; no rodents in the cinder; not a beetle in the moss. The gas that comes off the floor is not all steam. Enough of it is other things—the sharp smell tells you, and a faint thickness at the back of the throat—that nothing with lungs, nothing with a nervous system, stays. The plants do not need to breathe in the way that matters. They take the warmth and the wet and the extra carbon dioxide and they make of it more plant, prodigiously, silently, in the dark.

---

Now go toward the noise.

At the south end, where the floor steps down into the lower chamber, the vegetation stops. It stops in a line, more or less, and the line is not straight—it curves and reverses on itself, and if you could see it you would see that it is a line between green and brown, and beyond the brown, black. Groundsels stand dead on the near side of it, their leaves cooked, their trunks split and dried. Beyond them the moss is a crust. Beyond the crust, the cinder is bare.

Here the ground is hot. You feel it through your boots. And in the floor of the lower chamber, in a bowl of rock perhaps ten metres across, is the throat.

It is the one thing in the cleft that gives light. Not much. A dull red, the colour of an element that has been switched off and has not quite finished cooling, coming from a fissure in the floor of the bowl that you cannot see into because the steam is too thick above it. The steam catches the red and is lit from beneath, so that what you see is a moving, sagging, tearing body of pale rose-coloured cloud, eight or ten metres tall, pressed against the far wall and boiling up it, ripped sideways by the down-draught, torn open for a second to show the wet black glass of the wall behind it, closed again. It is the only thing you can see, and it will not hold still for long enough to be looked at.

The roar comes from here. It has a shape, up close. There is a body of gas under this floor, under pressure, and it gets out through the throat, and the throat is not a fixed size. Pressure builds; you hear it build, the pitch climbing, the hum in your feet thickening; then something gives, and the volume of the roar jumps, and the steam is shot upward in a mass and hits the ceiling of the fissure and splashes off it, and a wave of heat comes across the bowl and touches your face, damp and mineral. Then it drops. The pitch falls. For twenty seconds it is merely loud. Then it begins to build again. Forty seconds, more or less, trough to trough. It has been doing this all night and the night before, but not identically; the interval drifts; the strength of each release is different; twice in an hour something in the timing catches, and there are two releases close together, and the second one is much larger, and a piece of the wall above the bowl comes down.

Around the throat, on the rock, in rings, the mountain is manufacturing minerals. You cannot see them in the dark, but you can feel them underfoot as a change in the crunch of the cinder: a crust, brittle, that breaks under your weight and lets your boot down a centimetre onto something softer and hotter. Sulphur, mostly, yellow in daylight, crystallising out of the gas where it cools, in a band; and inside that band, where it is too hot for sulphur to condense, other things, whites and rust-reds that the gas leaves behind as it passes. The bands move. A crust that formed last week is being cooked off tonight because the hot zone has spread; a wall face that was bare is growing a fur of crystal because the wind has been holding the steam against it for six hours. Tonnage is small, but the process is continuous, it never shuts down, and it runs to a rhythm.

It is, in short, an industrial site with no one in it. A boiler and a condenser and a chimney and a production line for chemical deposits, all running, all loud, in the dark, all night, and nothing made—not one thing in the cleft has been put here by anyone. The rhythm of the release is the rhythm of a piston. The hiss of the small fumaroles along the floor is the hiss of relief valves. The ticking in the walls is the ticking of a steel frame cooling. The wet heat that rolls off the throat and through the groundsels is the heat off the side of an engine, and it is doing what such heat always does, which is drive something: in this case, the growth of the plants. The steam has a charge to it, too—not enough to see, but enough that in the drier eddies the hairs on your forearm lift, and when you touch the wet groundsel trunk after standing a while in the flow there is a small snap.

---

What the place is, though, is not the engine and not the garden. It is the line between them, and the fact that the line will not stay still.

Every night the heat pushes outward and something dies. A groundsel that was green last week stands brown tonight with its crown cooked. A lobelia spike has fallen over, its rosette scalded to a slump. The moss at the edge of the hot zone has become a crust and the crust has cracked and lifted in plates. And every night, somewhere else, the heat pulls back, or a fumarole that was venting closes off because a slab has come down on it, and the ground there cools to merely warm, and within a month the moss is on it and within a year the lobelias, taller than any lobelia outside, taller than you.

The walls shed into the garden. Rock comes down and crushes what it lands on and the moss is on the rock within the season. The garden climbs the walls. Heathers root in the cracks and lever the glassy sheets loose and they come down and the heathers come with them. The steam swings north and the north end grows lush and swampy and dripping; the wind shifts and it swings south for a week and the north end dries and cools and frost gets into it from the rim. Nothing about the shape of the green is the same two nights running. What was passable last night is a wall of lobelia tonight. What was a lobelia wall is a fallen groundsel trunk with a hot cinder slope behind it.

The rate is the thing that would frighten you, if you could see. The cleft is young and it is not finished. The fissure is still opening—a centimetre a year, perhaps, perhaps more this year—and the floor is still settling and the throat is still finding its size. The vegetation has arrived into a place that is not done forming and is growing as fast as the place changes, and neither of them is winning, and neither of them will. In a hundred years the throat may have sealed and the whole cleft may be a cold, still, mossy hollow full of frost-clenched groundsel, silent, and you would walk past it. Or the fissure may have opened along its length and taken the whole shoulder of the mountain with it, and there will be no cleft, only a new raw scarp, black and glittering and sharp, waiting for its first moss.

Tonight it is neither. Tonight it is this: a wet green throat of packed plants in a cold black mountain, loud as a mill, lit only at one end by a dull red that no eye is here to see, tearing itself down and building itself up in forty-second cycles, in the dark, in the cloud, wet on every surface, and you standing in it with your hand on a warm trunk, waiting for the pitch to fall so that you can hear your own breath again.
