The air outside is too thin to carry a voice, and cold enough to crack teeth. That is not where you are. You are inside the summit.

The mountain is hollow at the top. Not a cave, exactly — nothing has worn this space out. It looks more like the inside of a geode, or the interior of a fracture that happened three days ago and never finished. The walls are fresh. Every face of rock is a clean break, glassy where it caught the light, still holding the shapes of the forces that split it. Run a hand along the wall and you will bleed. Nothing has softened anything here. There has been no time.

The rock is warm to the touch, and further down, hot.

---

It takes a while to understand what you are standing in, because the eye keeps trying to make it a cave and it keeps refusing.

There are streets.

Not many. A few dozen structures, stepped up the inside of the cavity in tiers, joined by stairs cut with great confidence and worn by nothing. Low buildings with flat roofs. Cisterns. Channels running along the edges of the walkways, cut deeper than they need to be, as though the people who cut them expected more water than this. Arches. A small plaza on the second tier with a column at its centre that does nothing you can identify. It is a town — a real one, with the untidy sense of having been added to and argued over and built on top of itself — and it is entirely contained within a space you could cross in a few minutes' walk.

And the town is older than the mountain. You can see this. The lowest buildings are of a different stone from the rock they stand on, brought from somewhere else, somewhere flat. Their foundations were laid on a plain. Then the plain went up.

---

The mountain rose under the town. It is still rising.

You can feel it if you stand still long enough: not a tremor but a pressure, a slow steadiness in the floor like the deck of a very large ship. Somewhere below — far below — something is lifting the whole mass of stone, and doing it without pushing. There is no sound of strain. The rock does not groan. Each day the summit is a hand's width higher than the day before, and the stone simply agrees to it, the way water agrees to find a level.

The heat comes from that. Whatever is doing the lifting leaves warmth behind as a residue, and the warmth climbs. The floor of the cavity, at the lowest tier of the town, glows faintly — not red, but the ghost of red, the colour a kiln holds an hour after it is shut. The lowest streets are bone-dry and hot enough that you would not want to sit on them. Dust there does not move. Nothing does.

---

The ceiling is different.

The roof of the cavity is only a few metres of rock from the outside, and the outside is nearly nothing — air too thin to hold heat, a sky that stays black even at noon. So the ceiling is cold. The hot air from the floor rises through the tiers of the town, carries whatever moisture it has drawn from the rock, meets the ceiling, and gives it up.

It rains inside the mountain. Not everywhere, and not often, but at the top, where the ceiling curves lowest over the highest tier, water gathers into beads and then into drops and falls. It falls onto the roofs of the uppermost buildings, which were built for weather, though not this weather. It runs off into the channels. The channels carry it down. The middle streets are damp — dark stone, a skin of water in the low places, a smell of wet mineral that is almost but not quite the smell of rain on a summer pavement. By the third tier down the water has begun to steam off. By the fifth there is none.

So the town has climates. Cold rain above, a temperate wet band in the middle, a desert of hot stone at the bottom. A whole gradient of weather compressed into the height of a church.

The cisterns on the middle tiers are half full. Something keeps them that way.

---

What keeps them that way is the hardest thing to describe, because you never quite see it.

The town's works still run. The channels do not merely drain; there are places where water goes into the wall and does not come out where you would expect, and other places where it emerges above where it went in. There are lamps — a few, along the main stair, set into the stone — that hold a dim amber light without heat and without any visible source, and they brighten and dim on a cycle you can eventually time: roughly a day, though not quite the day the sun keeps outside. In the plaza the column that does nothing sometimes does something: a faint sound, a single low note, and afterward the air is fractionally clearer.

And there are the tenders.

You will not see one directly. You will see that the dust on a stair is disturbed in a line. You will see that a crack that opened in a wall — the rock here is under such strain that it cracks constantly, small clean fissures appearing overnight like frost — has been filled by morning with something the colour of the surrounding stone, seamless, slightly warm. You will see a cistern lid that was ajar closed. Once, from the corner of your eye, movement on the upper tier, in the rain: something the size of a dog, or a large bird, or nothing at all, and when you turn it is only water running.

There is nothing alive here. Nothing has grown on these walls, no moss, no crust, no film. The stone is too new and the place too sealed. But the works keep working, and the tenders keep tending, and the town maintains itself for a population that is not in it and shows no sign of coming back.

---

Where they went is not written anywhere.

The houses are not ruined. Doors are shut. On one flat roof on the second tier there is a stone bench facing the way you would face if there were a view, and there is no view, only the wall of the cavity a few metres off, fresh and sharp and glinting. Perhaps there was a view, once. Perhaps the mountain was lower and the sky still had colour in it, and the ceiling had not yet closed. Perhaps that is what they were trying to do — to be higher than anywhere, to go up and keep going up — and it worked so well that it sealed them in, and they left through some door that is not a door, and the town kept rising without them.

Or perhaps they built the engine that lifts the mountain and are still down there with it, in the heat, and the town is just the part that shows.

---

It is very quiet.

The drips are the loudest thing: a slow irregular ticking from the top tier, each drop distinct, each one landing on a different roof or channel or stone and making a slightly different sound. Below that, if you hold still, a hum, so low it is more felt than heard — the lifting. Below that, nothing. The outside makes no sound because there is nothing out there to make one.

Every so often — a few times a day — the rock cracks. It is a sharp small sound, like a knuckle, and it comes from anywhere, and afterward there is a new bright line somewhere on the wall, and by the next dimming of the lamps it has been filled.

---

The light is what you get used to last.

There is the amber of the lamps, dim, along the stair. There is the ember-glow from the floor, which lights the lowest tier from below and throws every edge into shadow upward. And there is, in three or four places along the ceiling, a thread of something else: not a crack — the tenders would have filled it — but a place where the rock is thin enough that a little of the outside comes through. It is not sunlight. It is the colour of the sky at that height, a deep bruised violet with no warmth in it at all, and it falls in narrow slants across the rain on the upper tier and makes the falling drops visible for an instant each, like sparks going the wrong way.

Between the three lights nothing is fully lit. You move through the town in a permanent dusk, hot from below and dripping from above, on streets that were paved for a place that no longer exists, in a room at the top of the world that grows a hand's width taller every day and will not stop, and there is no one here, and everything is kept.
