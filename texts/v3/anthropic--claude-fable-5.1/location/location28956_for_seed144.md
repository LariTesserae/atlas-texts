The first thing you notice is that the heat comes from the wrong direction.

At this height the sky should be doing the work — thin, indifferent, a blue so deep it's nearly violet at the zenith, the sun a small hard coin that gives light without much warmth. And it does that. Your shadow is crisp as a stencil. The air, when you draw it in, is cold and insufficient and tastes faintly of struck matches. But the ground under your boots is warm, and a hundred metres further along the ridge it is hot enough that you feel it through the soles, and by the time you reach the lip of the first basin you are walking on something closer to a stove lid than a mountain.

This is the massif. Nobody who works here calls it anything else. It is a broad, blistered shield of dark rock that rises out of the surrounding desert like the back of some enormous animal that lay down a very long time ago and never finished dying. Its summit is not a peak but a region — kilometres of undulating black plateau, cratered and fissured, tilted here and there into shallow bowls and broken by low cliffs of columnar basalt that have weathered, over ages, into shapes like organ pipes and stacked plates and the ribs of ships. Everything has the look of having been settled for a very long time. The edges are softened. The fractures are old. Nothing here is happening fast, geologically, but everything is happening.

Because the massif breathes.

From every fissure, every crack between the columns, every pit in the skin of the plateau, steam rises. Not violently — this is not a place of geysers — but constantly, in thin white threads and lazy plumes and, in the deeper basins, in slow rolling sheets that pour over the rim like something being poured from a bowl. The rock is hot beneath because something is hot beneath the rock, and has been for longer than anyone has been counting, and the water that seeps down into it from the rare highland rains comes back up transformed: mineral-rich, sulphurous, scalding, and then, within a few metres of leaving the ground, cold.

That is the massif's particular trick. The air up here is so thin and so cold that the steam cannot travel. It rises a body-length, two, and then the mountain's own breath condenses on itself and falls back as a fine, warm, endless drizzle that never seems to come from the sky. The rock is wet everywhere. It glistens. It is hot and wet at once, which the body does not know how to interpret — like standing in the open door of a laundry on a winter night, except that there is no door and no laundry and the night is a noon so bright it hurts.

The water gathers. In the low places it collects into pools that steam gently and never overflow, because the sun and the dry air take back exactly what the mountain gives. Some are the colour of weak tea, some milky green, some so clear and hot you can see the pale mineral floor and the bubbles rising from it. Around their rims the stone is crusted with deposits — ochre, chalk-white, a rust-red that looks like old blood, a strange soft yellow — laid down in ridges and terraces over a span of time that makes the pools look like something carved deliberately, though nobody carved them. Between the pools, in the damp cracks, the only vegetation of the plateau survives: a low grey-green mat of something that is not quite moss and not quite lichen, and here and there, where the condensation drip is steadiest, a stiff hummock of sedge no higher than a hand. That is all. Sparse is generous. The massif is a place almost entirely without green.

It is not, however, a place without life.

You understand this gradually, because at first the surface of the plateau seems to move in a way that you attribute to the shimmer of heat and the drift of steam. Then you look down, close to your feet, and see that the warm black rock is not black. It is covered. Bodies — small, dark, dry-skinned, lying flat and still with their throats pressed to the stone — pack the surface so densely that there is no rock between them. Lizards, or things that occupy the place lizards occupy: long-toed, blunt-headed, their backs the same charcoal as the basalt, their flanks flushed faintly orange where the heat has reached them. There are thousands. Tens of thousands. They lie across one another two and three deep on the warmest ledges, motionless, and when your shadow passes over them a ripple of adjustment moves through the crowd — a few hundred heads lifting a centimetre, a few hundred eyes turning — and then subsides. They do not run. Running costs heat, and heat is the whole point of being here. They came up from the cold desert nights below, or their ancestors did, and found a mountain that never cooled, and they have not left.

The air, too, is occupied. Insects — small, dark, hard-bodied — swarm in the steam plumes, riding the updraft and falling back with the condensate and rising again, a slow fountain of specks that never disperses. Where they drift near the rock, the lizards take them without appearing to move. Above, birds hang in the thin air on wings that barely stir: broad, pale-bellied things that nest in the basalt columns and sweep the steam-tops for insects, and, higher still, something larger and darker that turns in slow circles and is watching the lizards, not the insects. Occasionally one drops. You see it as a fold in the air and then a brief disturbance on the crowded ledge, a ripple that is not the shadow-ripple, and then the bird is labouring back upward with something in its feet and the ledge has already closed the gap. It happens perhaps once a minute somewhere on the visible plateau. It has been happening for a very long time. The numbers do not change.

And then there is the ironwork.

You come upon the first of it in the second basin: a wellhead, squat and rusted, bolted to a concrete pad that has cracked and heaved with the slow breathing of the ground beneath it. From it a pipe as thick as your body runs off across the plateau on low steel trestles, dipping and climbing with the terrain, disappearing into a bank of steam and reappearing beyond it, heading for the eastern rim where, if you follow it with your eye, it drops over the edge and down toward the desert. The pipe is warm. It is also, like everything else, covered in lizards — they line it end to end, bellies to the metal, a living sheath of scale so complete that the pipe looks upholstered. Where the lagging has rotted away and the bare steel shows, they lie thickest.

The works were built, you gather, to take the mountain's steam and turn it into something useful far below — power, or heat, or pressure for some purpose whose need has long since passed or been met elsewhere. There is not much of it: the wellheads, of which you will find eleven if you walk the whole plateau; the pipeline; a squat turbine house at the eastern rim with a stack that no longer smokes; two or three sheds of corrugated iron whose roofs have been peeled back by wind and whose interiors are, again, floored with lizards. A cable-way once ran down the north face; its pylons still stand, wires slack, one cradle hanging halfway down like a dropped bucket. Scattered across the plateau are the marks of the people who built it — a bootprint fossilised in a mineral crust, a length of chain, a valve wheel painted red that the drizzle has kept faintly bright.

But the machinery is not dead. This is the thing that takes longest to grasp, because the massif is so quiet. Sound does not carry in this air. The steam hisses, but faintly, a sound more felt than heard; the drizzle patters, but there is nothing for it to patter on but wet rock and skin. The lizards make no sound at all. The birds call rarely, and when they do the cry arrives thin and flattened, as if from much farther away than the bird. So it is some time before you separate, from the general low breath of the mountain, a steadier note — a hum, or less than a hum, a regularity in the air — and trace it to the turbine house, and put your hand against the corrugated wall, and feel it turning.

Something inside still runs. Not the whole plant — one unit, perhaps, geared down, fed by whichever wellheads still hold pressure, driving a generator that sends its current down a line whose far end you cannot see and whose purpose you can only guess. Perhaps it still lights something in the desert. Perhaps it powers only itself — the pumps that keep it running, the fans that keep it from cooking. The mountain breathes into it and it turns, slowly, and the condensate drips from its exhaust into a channel that has been furred over the years into a soft grey stalactite of scale, and in the channel the water is hotter than anywhere else on the plateau, and there the lizards are so thick they have formed a mound.

They like the machines. That is the mild strangeness of the place, the thing that stays with you afterward. They do not merely tolerate the ironwork; they have organised around it. The pipeline is their highway and their bed. The wellheads are their hearths. When the turbine's note shifts — and it does shift, a few times a day, as the pressure in the fissures rises and falls with some rhythm of the deep rock — the whole plateau seems to hear it before you do. A wave passes over the crowded ledges. Heads turn toward the eastern rim. Then nothing. Then the note settles, and so do they. You cannot say whether they are responding to the sound, or to some vibration through the stone, or to a change in the steam that precedes the sound; you can only say that the mountain and the machine and the animals have come, over the decades of neglect, into some kind of agreement, and that you are not party to it.

Weather comes in the afternoons. It is the one violent thing. Clouds build out of nothing on the western rim — the mountain's own breath, lifted by the day's heat until the cold air can no longer ignore it — and boil up into towers that hide the sun, and the light goes grey and flat and the drizzle thickens into rain, real rain, cold and hard, that drums on the sheds and pocks the pools. The lizards do not move. The rain hits the rock and hisses to steam on contact, so that for an hour the plateau is a white field with nothing visible above knee height, and you stand in it with cold water on your face and heat coming through your boots and the mountain roaring, almost, as close to loud as it ever gets. Then the cloud spends itself. The sun comes back small and hard. The steam thins to threads. The birds drop out of the clearing sky and the ledges shift and close, and the pipeline hums under its coat of scale, and the water the storm left behind begins, immediately, to go back where it came from.

By evening the sky is dark before the ground is. You can stand on the eastern rim with the turbine turning behind you and watch the desert far below go blue and then black, and the cold come up the flanks of the mountain like a tide, and feel it reach you and stop — because here it can go no further. Here the rock is warm and will be warm all night. The lizards know this. They are already asleep, or as near as they come to it, a hundred thousand small hot bodies pressed to the breathing stone, and in the last light the drizzle goes on falling, from no sky at all, onto a mountain that has been doing this, without needing anyone to watch, for longer than there have been eyes.
