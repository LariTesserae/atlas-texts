The valley was high before it was drowned, and it is still high. That is the first thing to hold onto. The lake sits at an altitude where, in the country around it, there are only stones and lichen and the last dwarf birches, and the water surface is a grey lid pressed down over a place that once had a climate of its own. Standing on the ridge above it you would see a long dark eye of water between two arms of scree, three or four kilometres from end to end, and a glacier snout at the upper end that is the reason for all of it. The ice came down the side valley centuries ago and lay across the mouth like a dropped beam, and the river that had drained the valley for as long as the valley existed backed up behind it, patiently, a hand's width a year, until the pastures went under and then the hay meadows and then the roofs. Nobody moved the ice. Nothing moves it now. It has become a wall, blue-grey, crusted with rockfall, and it leaks just enough at the base to keep the level nearly constant.

Below the surface the light gives out quickly. Not all at once — there is a zone, the first ten or fifteen metres, where the water is the colour of smoked glass and you can see your own hands — but below that it fails into a brown-green dusk that never brightens, even at noon, even in summer. What reaches the bottom is not light so much as the memory of a direction. Up is slightly paler than down. That is all.

And it is cold in a way that has stopped being weather and become a property of the place, like its depth or its shape. A few degrees above freezing at the surface in August. Colder below. Cold enough that the water is very clear, very still in its lower parts, and very slow to let anything go.

---

The forest is what you come to first, descending. Larches, mostly, with some spruce on the northern slope where the snow used to lie longest. They stand where they stood. The water rose too slowly to knock them over and too cold to rot them, and so the trees drowned upright, and remain upright, a whole hillside of grey trunks going up into the dimness with their branches still spread. The bark has gone; what is left is smooth wood, silvered, with the grain showing like the muscle of something skinned. Their needles are long gone from the branches but they are not gone from the valley. They lie where they fell, in a deep soft layer on the old forest floor, still recognisably needles, still faintly rust-coloured if you bring them up to where there is light, because nothing down here has eaten them.

Nothing down here eats anything. This is the thing that takes longest to understand about the lake, longer than the depth or the cold or the dark. There are no fish. There never were — the river that fed the valley came over a set of falls too steep for anything to climb, and the valley people had never seen a fish that wasn't salted and carried in on someone's back. There are no snails on the stones, no larvae in the mud, no water-fleas hanging in the beam of a lamp. The ice sealed the outlet before anything could arrive. So the lake is a place where the whole apparatus of consumption is missing. Things fall and lie. Things grow and are not grazed. Things die and stay.

But the plants came anyway, or were already there and changed. On the old pastures, on the long flat terraces the valley people had cleared and walled and mown for a thousand summers, there are now meadows again. Water-starwort and stonewort and a kind of moss that grows in dense rolling cushions the size of a body, dark green shading into a green so deep it reads as black under any light you can carry down. They grow to a height and thickness they never reach in ordinary lakes, because in ordinary lakes something is always nibbling them back. Here they simply continue. On the steeper ground they form terraces of their own, lapping over the stone walls, so that the human terracing and the plant terracing have become the same set of steps. In the standing forest, the mosses have climbed the dead trees and hang from the branches in curtains and ropes, and in the dimness the drowned larches look leafed again, in a foliage that moves in a way needles never did — slow, heavy, sideways, like hair underwater, which is what it is.

And here is a strange thing, one of several. The living trees at the top of the slope — the ones that were just above the final waterline, that stood for their whole lives in the down-valley wind that funnelled off the glacier — grew bent. Every one of them leans the same way, trunks curved, branches trailing off downhill like flags. When the water rose those last metres and took them, the wind stopped, for them, forever. There is no wind at the bottom of a lake. But the trees did not die immediately. In that cold, oxygen-poor water, a larch takes a long time to understand that it has drowned. And they went on growing, for a season or several, into the lean. There are shoots on the uppermost branches, pale, needleless, that extend the curve of the trunk by a hand's length or two — new wood, laid down under water, obeying a wind that no longer touched it. The last thing the tree learned was the shape of the weather, and it kept the lesson after the weather was gone.

---

The village is further down, where the valley floor flattens. Twenty-odd houses, a barn for every two, a mill where the stream came in, a small chapel with a roof of stone shingles. The people who built it did not have much iron — a few blades, a few nails hoarded and re-used, passed down — and so almost everything is stone laid dry on stone, or wood pegged to wood, and both of those survive drowning better than anything with a metal joint would. The walls stand. The roofs mostly stand, because the shingles were heavy and the rafters were larch, and larch does not rot in this water. Moss has taken the walls in the way lichen once did, in circular patches that have grown together into a continuous pelt, and the doorways are dark holes in the green.

Inside, in the barns, there is hay. This is the second strange thing. The valley people cut and dried their hay in late summer and stacked it in the lofts, and the water came in autumn, when the lofts were full, and the hay is still there. It is not yellow anymore — it has darkened to a tobacco brown — but it is hay, in stacks, held together by its own compression, in water that has not moved fast enough in centuries to lift it. In one loft a wooden hay-fork lies across the top of the stack where someone left it. The wood of the fork is the same colour as the hay. A layer of fine grey silt, thin as dust, covers both.

The mill wheel is still on its axle. The stream that once turned it now comes in from above as something else entirely — but that is the sound, and the sound needs its own telling.

The chapel has a bell, and the bell is still in its arch, and the rope is gone, and no current in the lake is strong enough to swing it. It hangs in the dark with moss on its shoulders. It is worth noting that it is silent. It is nearly the only silent thing in the valley.

---

Because the lake is loud. This is the thing a visitor would not expect and could not be ready for. Sound travels through water four times faster than through air and much further, and the lake is a closed basin of rock that holds sound like a bowl holds a hum, and it is never, in any season, quiet.

In summer the loudness is the inflow. The river still comes over the falls at the head of the valley, but the falls are now under water — they pour not into air but into the lake, a column of colder, siltier, heavier water that drops through the warmer surface layer and slides down the drowned slope as a current you can feel before you can see it, a moving weight, and it makes a sound like a train heard through a wall. Continuous. It never pauses. The silt it carries settles out along its path in a fan that has been building for as long as the lake has existed, and the fan has half-buried the mill, and the mill wheel — this is the third strange thing — the mill wheel still turns. Not fast. A revolution in several minutes, perhaps. The turbidity current passes over it and it moves, wooden paddles catching the flow, grinding on its axle with a low groan that carries the whole length of the valley.

In autumn the sound is stone. The scree slopes above the waterline freeze and thaw and shed rock, and every stone that falls into the lake arrives as a report — a crack, then a long descending rush as it tumbles down the drowned slope, then silence for a second, then the next one. On a cold clear night in October the lake sounds like a slow rockslide that never finishes.

In winter the surface freezes, and the ice sings. This is the loudest season. Ice under tension cracks and booms and produces long ringing tones that drop in pitch as the fracture travels, sounds like a bowstring the size of a valley, like something enormous being struck in an empty hall, and every one of those sounds goes down into the water and fills it. Under the ice, in January, in the dark, in the cold, among the drowned trees with their curtains of moss, the noise is nearly constant: a deep percussive music with no player and no listener, the lake talking to itself about the temperature.

In spring the ice breaks and the snowmelt comes and the inflow doubles and the whole cycle turns over again. The lake is never still. It is old, settled, in its own state — it has been what it is for a very long time and will go on being it — and it is also never for a moment at rest. Currents move the moss curtains. Silt falls. Stones fall. Snow falls onto the surface and, where it doesn't freeze, sinks slowly through the upper water as slush, a second snowfall descending through the dark onto the branches of the drowned larches, dusting the moss white for an hour before it dissolves.

---

And the fourth strange thing, which you would find only if you went down far enough and looked carefully.

The lake does not mix. The upper water, stirred by the inflow and the wind and the seasons, turns over each year. But below a certain depth — around thirty metres, roughly the level of the chapel roof — there is a layer that has not been to the surface since the flood. It is denser, saltier from the leached stone, and it lies under the upper water like oil under water, and the boundary between them is visible. Not sharp. A shimmer. A plane of slight blur hanging in the dimness at the height of the eaves, so that the village stands with its roofs in one kind of water and its doorways in another.

Below the boundary nothing has any oxygen at all, and so below the boundary nothing changes. The hay in the lofts is above the line and has gone brown. The hay in the byres, below it, is a colour that is almost yellow. A wooden bowl on a table in a house below the line has a residue in it. The needles on the forest floor below the line are still the rust-red of the autumn they fell.

The whole lower valley is preserved in the water of the year it drowned — the water that was there the last autumn the people carried their hay up, before they carried their belongings out and up over the ridge and did not come back. The upper water is new every year. The lower water is that year. It sits in the bottom of the basin under the new water and the moss and the standing trees and the singing ice, in the cold and the dark, sealed by its own weight, and the sound of the falling stones goes down into it, and the sound of the mill wheel goes down into it, and nothing in it has moved, and nothing is coming to eat any of it, and it keeps.
