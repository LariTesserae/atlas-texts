**The Poured City**

There is no ground here that was not once liquid.

The mountain stands higher than mountains have any right to — so high that the sky at noon is a hard, dark blue, and the horizon has a curve to it if you look long enough — and yet the soles of your boots are warm. Not warm the way sun-struck stone is warm. Warm from beneath. The heat comes up through the rock as if the rock were a lid on something, which it is.

The city sits on the summit like a crust on a wound. From a distance, if there were a distance to see it from, you would take it for a single dark cone streaked with dull red and a paler grey where the newest surfaces have not yet blackened. Closer, the cone resolves into stories: terrace above terrace of foundry-halls, casting-sheds, condensers, cooling galleries, stacked so tight that their walls share bricks and their chimneys lean against one another like people asleep on a bench. There is very little open sky between structures. What sky there is comes down in slots and shafts. You can walk for an hour under roofs and still be outdoors.

---

The material of everything is the same material. The streets are glass — black, dense, faintly ribbed with the flow-lines of the pour that made them. The walls are glass, or slag, or a fused brick that was cast rather than laid. Iron is used for what must move or must hold a shape against heat: gantries, doors, the great ladles, the tie-rods that stitch buildings to each other across the narrow lanes. The iron is red-brown and sweats.

It sweats because of the inversion. At this height the air is thin and very cold — cold enough that the upper ironwork, the crane-arms and the roof-ridges twenty and thirty meters above the street, sits at or below freezing on a clear night. But the ground is hot. Between the two, at about the height of a second storey, the air is exactly warm enough and exactly cold enough that whatever moisture rises from below condenses on the first cold thing it touches. So the upper city drips. All day, all night, a slow irregular fall of water from the beams onto the warm glass of the street, where each drop flattens, hisses very faintly, and is gone within a minute — or gathers in one of the shallow depressions where a pour cooled unevenly, and sits there, a small dark pool, steaming, until it is gone by evening and replenished by morning.

These pools are the city's water. There are no rivers. There is almost never rain; clouds form below the city, not above it, and you can stand on a western parapet and look down onto their tops. People set out cisterns of cast glass beneath the heaviest drip-lines and collect what falls. The water tastes of iron and of something like struck flint.

---

Nothing grows. This should be said plainly, because it is the first thing the body notices and the last thing the mind accepts. There is no lichen on the walls, no moss in the wet corners, no weed in the cracks, because the walls are too new to have cracks and too hot for anything to take. The oldest exposed surface in the city is perhaps forty years old. Beneath it are older surfaces, and beneath those older still — streets that were streets, roofs that were roofs, whole districts that were lived in and then, according to the schedule, filled.

That is how the mountain was made and how it goes on being made. The vent at the summit — it is called the Throat, and it is not a crater but a cored shaft, lined, gated, gauged — brings up material from below at a rate that has not much varied in living memory. It does not erupt. It swells. It is drawn off through channels into the ladles and the ladles are walked out along the gantries and tipped, in a strict rotation, into whichever quarter of the city has been evacuated for filling. A quarter is filled over some years, a courtyard at a time. The glass rises up the walls like a tide. Windows are bricked as it reaches them. Then the roofs go under, and a new street is laid across the top, and new walls cast against it, and the population that was displaced moves upward and back into the district they left, now one storey higher than it was and standing on their own former ceilings.

So the mountain climbs. Perhaps a hand's width a year, averaged over the whole summit. The oldest people say the sky has grown noticeably darker in their lifetimes, and the air harder to breathe. They are probably right.

---

Because it is glass, and because some of the pours were clear, you can in places see down. Not far — a meter, two — but far enough. There are streets in the Kilnwards quarter where, walking at midday with the sun straight overhead, you see beneath your feet the tops of doorframes, the rusted curl of a hinge, the slope of a roof-tile field still in its courses. The colors are muted and swimming, as if underwater. Children lie on their bellies and try to read the signs on shopfronts that closed before their grandparents were born. Sometimes a figure seems to be in a doorway. Nobody agrees on what these are — the caught shadow of a beam, a slag inclusion, a person who did not leave in time. The filling schedule is strict and everyone knows it, and yet.

Deeper, of course, the glass is opaque, black, striated with the history of the pours: a fatter band for the year the Throat ran high, a thin greenish seam where some contaminant came up and was poured anyway. The engineers read these bands in the walls of the maintenance shafts the way others read scripture. There are no books older than the mountain, so the mountain is the book.

---

It is quiet. This too takes getting used to. A city of furnaces and ladles and cranes should roar, and this one does not, for several reasons which together produce a silence so complete it has a texture.

The air is too thin to carry sound well; a shout across a courtyard arrives thinned and late. The great engines that walk the ladles are hydraulic, worked by steam that is itself worked by the mountain's heat, and they move at the speed of the heat — a ladle-walk from Throat to pour-site can take a full day, the gantry-wheels turning so slowly you have to watch the shadow of a spoke against the wall to see the motion. There is no hammering because there is nothing to hammer; the material is poured, not worked. And the people have learned, in the way of people who live in a thin-aired place, to speak low and little and to use their hands for the rest. There is a whole grammar of gesture here that outsiders never quite master, most of it to do with heat, height, and direction.

What sound there is: the drip. The tick of glass contracting at nightfall, a sound like a small stone dropped on a plate, coming from everywhere at once and then from nowhere. Once a day, when a pour begins, a bell — not struck but bowed, a long iron note that seems to come up out of the street. The hiss of a pool going dry. Somewhere, always, the hum of the condensers, pitched just above hearing, felt in the teeth.

---

There is life other than the human. Not much. On the eastern shoulder, where the old fills are cool enough to walk barefoot and the slope drops away into the cloud-tops, live the lambs.

They are not lambs. They are the size of a large dog and low to the ground, pale grey with a faint pinkish translucency at the edges, like the ear of an animal held up to the light, and they have no eyes that anyone has found. They move rarely and slowly, and they eat glass — or seem to; they are found pressed flat to the striated seams where a mineral-rich pour meets a poorer one, and where they have been the seam is etched and matte. They are warm to the touch, warmer than the rock. When the filling schedule reaches a place where the lambs are, the lambs are moved first. There is no law about this; it is simply done. Some of the older casters say the lambs were here before the city, that the city was built around them, that the mountain grows because they need it to. The engineers say this is nonsense. The engineers move the lambs first anyway.

Above the summit, on windless days, there are sometimes birds. They do not land. They come up on the heat, higher and higher, circling, until they are dots against the dark blue, and then they go back down toward the world where things live. No one knows what they are looking for. Possibly they are only enjoying the lift.

---

The light is ordinary and strange. Ordinary because the sun is the sun and it rises and sets. Strange because everything it falls on is black and drinks it, so that even at noon the streets have a dusk in them, and the only bright things are the sweat-beads on the iron and the surfaces of the pools, which lie in the dim like coins. At the edges of the city, where the terraces stop and the raw slope begins, the newest fills are a different color — grey, matte, faintly frosted where they cooled too fast — and these catch the light and hold it, so that from within the city the edge appears to glow.

Beyond the edge: the shoulders of the mountain, the abandoned districts too cold now for anyone to want, half-sunk in old glass, going down into cloud. Below the cloud there is presumably a country, valleys and rivers and the green that no one here has seen except in the greenish seams of a contaminated pour. Supplies come up — ore is not needed, but iron is, and salt, and grain — on a rail that spirals the cone through a hundred kilometers of switchbacks, and the cars take three days to climb it and arrive coated in frost that boils off the moment they enter the summit air. The people who ride up with the cars look shaken and leave as soon as they can.

---

There is one more thing about the place, which is that it has no finished state. Nothing here has ever been complete. The mountain is not a mountain but a mountain being made; the city is not a city but the working face of one; the ground is not ground but the most recent surface of a process with no end written into it. This is not a metaphor the inhabitants use. It is simply the condition. They pour, and it cools, and it ticks in the night, and the drip comes down on it from the cold iron above, and in the morning there are pools, and in a year the street is a hand's width higher, and in a lifetime the sky is a shade darker, and the Throat is still swelling at the same slow rate, and the lambs are pressed against the seams with their eyeless faces, warm, waiting for whatever it is they wait for, and no one hurries, because nothing here has ever been in a hurry, and it has come this far.
