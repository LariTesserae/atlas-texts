The first thing is the light, and the second is how little it has to push through.

This is a high country — high enough that the sky above it is a dark, saturated blue that seems to have weight, and the sun comes down without much interference. Stone throws hard shadows. The air is thin enough that the eye reaches farther than it expects to: a ridge that looks like a morning's walk is a day and a half. There is no haze. Distance is not softened; it is only made small.

And yet it is warm. That is the first wrongness, or the first thing to notice. At this altitude the air should carry a chill in it even at noon, and the nights should crack stone. Instead the ground gives back heat like a body. Put your palm flat on the pale rock and it is the temperature of skin. The warmth is in the stone, not the air, and it does not go away when the sun does.

The land is a broad tableland broken into steps. Not a single plateau but a succession of them, each a few hundred meters lower than the last, descending toward a horizon where the steps eventually run out of light and become a blue smear that might be lowland or might be nothing. The steps are old. Their edges have rounded. Water has cut them, then stopped cutting, then come back and cut again along the same lines, and now there are shallow valleys running across each terrace like the lines on an open hand — each one finished, each one carrying a floor of fine pale gravel and a few boulders that will not move again in any span of time that matters.

Whether the steps were made or grew is not a question that has an answer anymore. Some of them are too even. Some of the risers, if you walk their base, have a texture under the weathering that is not the texture of stone breaking — a fine regular grain, like the surface of something poured. But lichen grows on it the same as on the rest, and the same small flowers root in its cracks, and if it was engineering once, the engineering has been outvoted by ten thousand years of wind and dew. It is terrain now.

Vegetation is thin and low. A grey-green cushion plant that grows in domes no larger than a fist, hard as cork, scattered across the flats at intervals of a few meters like a very sparse crowd. A woody shrub with silver leaves that turns its leaves edge-on to the sun at midday and flat to the sky at dusk. Along the valley floors, where the dampness collects, a coarse grass in tussocks. On the north-facing risers — the shaded faces of each step — a dark moss that stays wet to the touch even at noon, and ferns, actual ferns, in a place where ferns have no business.

Because there is water. It just does not stay.

In the afternoon, every afternoon, fog comes up. It arrives from below, from the direction of the smeared horizon, climbing the risers one at a time. You can watch it come: a white tide on the lowest visible step, then the next, pouring over each lip and pooling on each terrace before spilling upward to the one above. It moves against the sense of how fog should move. It climbs. When it reaches you it is cool and dense and smells of wet stone and faintly of iron, and it lasts perhaps two hours. Everything sweats. The rock beads. The cushion plants darken to near-black as they take in what they can. Water runs in threads down the risers into the valley floors and stands there briefly in shallow sheets, an inch deep, mirror-flat, and then by evening it is gone — sunk into the gravel, drunk, evaporated back into an air that never seems to hold it for long.

Then the fog thins, and pulls back down the steps the way it came, and the sky is dark blue again and the light is hard again and everything is dry within the hour. The heat in the stone helps with that.

The animals know the schedule better than you do.

They are everywhere, and they are quiet. A grazer about the size of a goat, long-legged, coat the color of the gravel, with a dark stripe down the spine — they move across the flats in loose groups of a dozen or twenty, always in motion, always spread out so that no two are ever near enough to touch. You will see forty of them from one vantage and not hear one. They do not call. Their hooves on stone make less sound than they should. Smaller things: a lizard with a flattened body and a frill along its jaw, which sits on warm rock in the morning and turns to face the fog when it comes, mouth open, and drinks the air. A bird like a small, heavy swift that never seems to land, that rides the thin air in long swoops just above the ground and takes insects off the tops of the cushion plants without slowing. Insects themselves — a lot of them, a shimmer above the grass in the valleys, a persistent slow traffic of something beetle-like across every surface. None of it buzzes. None of it hums.

There are predators. A cat-shaped animal, low and long, which spends the day lying in the shadow of the risers and works the edges of the herds at dusk. You will know it is there because the grazers, without any signal you can detect, will all be facing the same direction. It moves without a sound too. Everything here does. Not stealth, exactly — the whole world seems to have had its volume reduced. Drop a stone from shoulder height and it lands with a small flat tick, then nothing. Speak and your voice goes about as far as your outstretched hand and stops there, as if the air were made of felt. You can shout and the animals fifty meters away will not lift their heads.

It might be the altitude. Thin air carries sound poorly. But it is more than that, and after a few days you stop testing it.

The built things are few and far between, and they are where the water is.

On each terrace, always somewhere along the valley floor, there stands a pillar — one, sometimes two. Waist-high to twice a person's height. Dark, smooth, tapered slightly toward the top, planted in the gravel as if it grew there. They do not look like anything in particular. There are no markings. They are cool to the touch — the only cool thing in this landscape — and the ground around each one in a circle of a few meters is darker and damper than the ground elsewhere, and this is where the grass is thickest and where the sheets of afternoon fog-water last longest, sometimes until dark. The pillars are collecting something. Or they are pulling the dew point down around themselves, or they are doing something to the air that has no name yet. Whatever it is, they have been doing it for a very long time. Their surfaces are pitted by wind. A few are cracked. One, on a lower terrace, has fallen and lies at an angle in the gravel, and the damp circle around it has stretched and pooled at its lower end, and the grazers still come to it.

Around the pillars in the late afternoon there is always a gathering. The grazers stand in a loose ring, not quite at the wet edge, waiting for something — perhaps the cat to declare itself, perhaps just for the water to stand still. The lizards climb the pillars themselves and press their bellies to the cool surface. The swifts come lower here than anywhere. And on the pillars there is sometimes a faint ripple, a movement under the surface like a swallow, and the air near them tastes for a moment sharper, colder, cleaner, as if it had been brought from very far away and very high up and set down here without passing through the distance in between.

Farther apart than the pillars, and fewer, are other remnants. A stretch of wall a hundred meters long, knee-high, running dead straight across a terrace and ending in nothing at both ends. A shallow bowl cut into bare rock, ten paces wide, perfectly circular, holding fog-water every afternoon and a colony of small red crustaceans that live in the gravel at its center when it is dry. A place near the top of one riser where the stone has been made glassy in a rough square, and the glass is scored with hairline lines in a pattern that is either a map or a schematic or damage. Nothing that looks like a house. Nothing that looks like a road. Whoever left these did not need shelter or paths, or did not need them here.

Nights are brief and enormous. Without the fog the sky opens and the stars come down close, an absurd number of them, and their light is enough to walk by. The stone stays warm underfoot. The grazers lie down in their scattered dozens and the cat moves among the risers and there is, if anything, less sound than by day. Toward dawn a thin wind gets up and moves across the tableland from the high side to the low, and the silver shrubs turn their leaves, and the first light comes up not gold but white, immediate, as if a switch had been thrown.

Then the heat rises off the rock into the thin blue air, and the lizards climb out onto their stones, and far down the steps, on the lowest terrace you can see, a white line begins to form along the lip and thicken and start, very slowly, to climb.
