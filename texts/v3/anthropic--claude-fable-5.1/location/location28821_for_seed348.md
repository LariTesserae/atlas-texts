**The Lift Ground**

From the top of the dike it looks at first like a small industrial town somebody forgot to fill in. Six or seven buildings of dark brick and riveted iron, set far apart from one another on a floor of grey silt, with nothing between them but distance. A rail spur comes in over the dike on a trestle and stops. A row of transformer gantries stands off to one side like a piece of fence that got lost. The whole enclosure might be eight hundred paces across. The dike goes all the way round.

Then the sound arrives, or rather you notice that it has been there since before you climbed up, and that the climb was the last quiet thing you will do here.

It is one sound. It has the depth of a large engine and the surface of a crowd. If you stand still it resolves, slightly, into layers — a low iron thrum that you feel through the soles of your feet, a mid-register rush of falling water, and above that a shrilling, chittering, whistling density that has no gaps in it at all — but then it closes back up into a single thing. It does not rise or fall. There is no wind to carry it away and none to bring it in gusts. It simply occupies the enclosure the way water occupies a tank.

---

The floor of the enclosure is below the level of the land outside. Below the level of the river, too, which runs somewhere beyond the eastern dike, unseen. This is the reason for everything here. The silt would be river bottom if the pumps stopped, and the pumps have not stopped in living memory, or in the memory of anyone who left records, and so the silt has settled into a hard, cracked, ash-coloured pavement that takes a footprint only faintly and holds it for years.

Nothing grows on it. Not moss, not the grey lichen that takes to old brick everywhere else, not the scum that should be greening the standing puddles. The water that comes up through the lift is cold and heavy with dissolved rock from somewhere far under the delta, and it leaves the silt dosed with a salt that has a faint metallic sweetness on the lips and kills anything with a leaf or a root. In the corners where spray has drifted for a hundred years the ground has crusted white, then bloomed into knuckled formations the height of a boot, hard as tooth, that one could mistake at a distance for a small dead garden.

It is cool. Not cold. The lifted water is always the same temperature, a few degrees above the point where breath would show, and there is so much of it moving through the enclosure that the air simply adopts it. There is no summer here and no winter. Iron never warms enough to be comfortable to lean on and never chills enough to hurt. Everything sweats. The brick, the pipe casings, the insulators on the gantries, the handrails — all of it carries a permanent film of condensate that gathers and drops and is renewed. It does not rain within the dike so much as the dike's air is always at the edge of raining, and gives up a little of itself wherever it touches a surface.

---

The buildings are the pump houses. Each is a tall brick box with a slate roof and arched windows too high to see through from the ground, and each stands over a shaft. The largest, in the centre, is the old lift — a cathedral of a thing, its interior one open volume from floor to lantern, holding two vertical engines the size of houses whose flywheels turn at a speed that has not been adjusted since the governors were set. The others are later and smaller: squat electric houses with the motors humming inside behind louvred doors. The power comes over the dike on the gantry line from somewhere outside. There is a small keepers' terrace of five brick cottages along the north wall, a store, a boiler house with a stack that no longer smokes, a tally office by the rail head. The layout has the particular sparseness of places built in stages by engineers who each assumed more would follow. More did not follow. What is here is finished.

Everything has weathered to a stable state. The rust on the outdoor iron has gone past flaking and reached the deep, matte, almost velvety brown of iron that has decided what it is going to be. The brick has darkened and lost its arrises. The slate has silvered. The wooden sleepers of the spur have hardened and cracked and silvered in their turn. Nothing is falling down and nothing is being repaired; the place has arrived at the shape it can hold indefinitely, and is holding it.

From each pump house a discharge culvert runs, open-topped and lined with dressed stone, across the silt to the eastern wall, where the water goes up and over the dike in a great iron siphon and drops into the river outside. The culverts are the only lines in the enclosure that run anywhere. Everything else stands where it was set down.

---

And on all of it, and in all of it, and over all of it, are the animals.

This is what the sound is. The engines and the water make the floor of it. The animals make the rest.

The lifted water comes up from the rock full of life — pale, eyeless, soft-bodied things that have never known light: small fish with skin like wet paper, shrimp that are entirely transparent except for a dark thread of gut, blind snails, ribbons of flatworm, a slow white crab that lives in the iron of the shafts and comes up with the flow in pieces. The pumps deliver them by the ton, every hour, without variation, into the open culverts, and the culverts carry them across the enclosure in a cold current the colour of pewter, and everything else here lives on that.

Birds are the most of it. They stand shoulder to shoulder along every culvert edge, two and three deep, a continuous seam of grey and white bodies with bills bent to the water. They stand along every roof ridge and every window ledge and every gantry arm. They stand on the iron siphon in a line so dense it looks upholstered. There are the small hunched gulls with the rust-coloured heads, and a kind of heron no taller than a man's knee that fishes with a stabbing motion so fast it is only a blur and a flare of spray, and a squat black bird with a hooked bill that dives in the culverts and comes up with the pale fish crosswise, and dense whirling flocks of something finch-sized that never seem to land, that feed on the wing from the cloud of midges that hangs over every open shaft. The midges themselves are a presence you could mistake for smoke: columns of them, standing over the water intakes, so thick that when you look up through them the sky is grained.

Below the birds, on the silt and the stone, the eels. Long, dark, thick as a wrist, in numbers that make the ground move. They have found their way up the discharge culverts against the flow — or down into the enclosure from the river, or were always here — and they lie in mats in the wet margins and in the shallow standing pools, sliding over one another, taking the shrimp and the fish that the current throws up on the stone lips. Where a culvert bends there is always an eel-heap in the outer curve, a slow dark knot that has been in that curve for as long as the water has been turning there.

Then the small things that live on the leavings. Rats — grey, sleek, well-fed, sitting in the open in the middle of the day with their forepaws on a fishbone, unbothered. Beetles the size of a thumbnail, iridescent black, that carpet the guano beneath the roosts and keep it from ever growing more than a hand's depth. A brown bat in the eaves of the old lift that only comes out to hunt the midges at the top of the shaft lantern, so that the space under the roof is a permanent slow gyre of bats and insects over the flywheels. Spiders in every window arch, webs so thick with midge husks they hang like grey cloth. Something that lives inside the electrical housings and comes out onto the insulators to warm itself in the faint field-glow there — a soft-bodied, slow, many-legged thing with a bluish sheen that nobody has needed to name.

There is no part of the enclosure where you are not within arm's length of something alive. And yet — this is the paradox of the place — it is sparse. The buildings are far apart. The ground between them is bare hardened silt with nothing on it but light and the odd wandering rat. The animals are not everywhere; they are exactly where the food is, in precisely the density the food supports, and they are not anywhere else. Between the culverts the ground is as empty as a floor. Standing in the middle of one of those empty stretches, with the roar all around you and the nearest bird two hundred paces away along a stone lip, you feel that you are in the one sector of a hive that was built for nothing.

---

The rule of the place, which becomes clear the longer you stay, is that nothing here is in the process of becoming anything.

The pumps run at one speed. They lift a fixed volume of water at a fixed temperature carrying a fixed weight of pale life. The culverts carry it across the silt at a fixed rate and discharge it over the dike. Everything that lives inside the wall lives on that stream and has, over however many generations it took, adjusted its number to it exactly. There are as many gulls as the shrimp will feed and not one more. When a gull dies it falls into the culvert or onto the silt and is eaten by the eels or the rats or the beetles within the hour, and its place on the stone lip is taken by a bird that was until then standing in the second row. There is always a second row. There is no season for the young because the water has no season; there are always a few eggs, always a few fledglings tumbling from the ledges to be taken by the black divers, always a few new eels the thickness of a shoelace threading through the mats of the old ones. Birth and death are not events here. They are flow rates.

The birds do not migrate. Where would they go? Nothing outside the dike is as good as this. The eels do not go down to the sea. The midges hatch and mate and die in the one column of air above the one intake in a cycle that lasts a day and has lasted every day. If you came back in a year you would find the same number of everything in the same places doing the same thing at the same volume. If you came back in fifty years — the same. The place is not still. Nothing in it is still for a moment. But nothing in it moves in any direction, and the total of all that motion is a shape that does not change, the way a waterfall is a shape.

The keepers, if there are keepers, keep to the north terrace and are rarely seen. The cottages have lights in the evening. Somebody must oil the governors and clear the intake grilles of eel and check the gantry insulators. But it is not clear that anybody is needed to do anything, and the absence of any sign of maintenance — no fresh paint, no new iron, no repaired stone — suggests that whatever they do, they have done it so long and so consistently that it has become one more component of the equilibrium and leaves no mark.

---

The light is the light of an overcast day, most days, filtered through the permanent haze of condensate and midge. The colours are grey, brown, white, black, the pewter of the water and the very faint blue on the insulators where the strange things sit. Nothing is bright. Nothing is dim. The shadows of the pump houses lie across the silt at whatever angle the hour dictates and the birds shift to stay out of them, or into them, in a slow wheeling that is the only sign of time passing.

The smell is fish and cold iron and the sweet-metal salt and, under it, guano, but not as strongly as you would expect; the beetles keep it down. Wet brick. Ozone near the gantries. The eels, if you get close, smell of the river.

And the sound. The sound does not stop, and after a while you stop hearing it as sound. It becomes a property of the air, like the coolness. It presses gently on the eardrums. Speech is possible but pointless. The gulls do not call to one another so much as add to the whole; the engines do not thud so much as underlie it. When you climb back up the dike and drop down the other side, the sudden falling-off of it — the ordinary quiet of ordinary land, with a river somewhere and a wind — is so violent that for several minutes the world outside seems the strange place, and the Lift Ground, behind its wall, roaring in its cold equilibrium, its ten thousand birds standing in their fixed rows over the pale endless stream of the lifted water, seems like the place where things are simply, and finally, the way they are.
