From outside it is nothing. A low green swell in the floor of a basin, no higher than a barn, the kind of shape you would take for an old midden gone to moss. The ground around it is dry and cracked, pale clay, and the swell sits on the clay like something set down and left. It has no trunk that you could point to. It has no crown. It has a skin — pored, grey-green, faintly ridged, warm to the hand as an animal is warm — and the skin goes all the way around, and up over the top, and down again.

You feel it before you hear it. The clay under your feet is not still. There is a note coming up through your heels and into your shins, and by the time you are close enough to touch the skin the note is in your teeth and you have to unclench your jaw to keep it from ringing. Then you find the seam, which is not a door but a place where the skin has grown apart in two lips that never met, and you turn sideways and go through, and the sound arrives.

It is not loud the way a thing is loud. It is loud the way weather is. It has no source. It is the whole air, standing up.

---

Inside, the swell is a single room, and the room is far larger than the swell. This is the first thing that is wrong and you notice it with your body rather than your mind: you walked ten steps to cross the threshold and the far wall is a quarter mile away, dim and gold-green and curving up into a ceiling you can only infer. The floor is a shallow bowl of the same living material as the walls, spongy, warm, holding a few pools of clear water in its low places. Light comes through the walls themselves. They are thick — in places you can tell they are yards thick — but they are translucent the way a leaf held to the sun is translucent, so the whole chamber is lit from every side at once with an even, shadowless, late-afternoon light that never moves.

And the room is empty. That is the second thing. After the sound, after the size, the emptiness. There is nothing in the middle of it. There is nothing hanging from the ceiling, nothing rising from the floor. The air between you and the far wall holds only air, and the sound, and a few motes turning slowly in a draft you cannot feel. A vast wet hollow. Cathedral is the wrong word, though it is the word that comes; a cathedral is *for* something. This is a cavity. Something was here and dissolved, or something was never here, and the plant grew around the not-being-here and kept it.

---

The city is in the walls.

You do not see it immediately because the light is so even and the wall is the same colour as what is built into it, but as your eyes adjust to the scale you begin to make out that the surface of the chamber is not smooth. It is terraced. It is honeycombed. From the floor to perhaps a hundred feet up, and in some places much higher, the living wall has been worked — chewed, hollowed, plastered, pinched — into a continuous face of cells and galleries and ledges, each opening a hand's width or a head's width across, thousands upon thousands of them, running in tiers that follow the grain of the tissue the way streets follow the contour of a hill. There are broader shelves that read as avenues. There are vertical channels that read as stairs, though nothing that lives here would use stairs. There are places where the openings cluster densely and places where they thin out, and dark seams between districts where the plant's own tissue has grown over the openings entirely and sealed them, and above those seams the next tier begins again. The oldest quarters are inside the wall now, entombed a yard deep, still visible as darker cells within the translucent green. The city has been growing outward into the room for a very long time, and the plant has been growing outward around it, and neither has ever caught up with the other.

Nothing built this with a tool. You can see that too, once you look. The plaster is spit and chewed fibre. The edges are bitten. The ledges have the smooth, sagging profile of things shaped by ten thousand generations of small bodies pressing against them. It is a city the way a reef is a city.

And it is full. That was hard to see at first because the builders are the same colour as their work — the greys and greens and dull golds of the wall — and because they are small, and because in the shadowless light nothing casts an outline. But the wall is moving. Every opening has something in it or coming out of it or going into it. Furred things the size of a fist that climb head-down. Sleek things with membrane wings that cling and fold and unfold. Beetle-things whose backs are the exact glazed green of the plaster. Long-necked things with no eyes that you would call birds if they had beaks, which they do not; they have soft grasping mouths and they feed at the seams where the wall weeps. They are all calling. They have been calling since before you came in and they will not stop. This is the sound. It is not one sound; it is every sound they make, at once, from every direction, in a chamber shaped to hold it.

---

The city and the room and the sound are one system, and it takes a while to understand how.

The wall sweats. It is warm in here — not hot, but a heavy, breathing, mid-afternoon warmth — and the exhalations of a hundred thousand small animals and the slow respiration of the plant itself saturate the air, and the air lets go of its water wherever it touches the cooler upper wall. So the ceiling drips. Constantly. You cannot hear the individual drops; nothing individual is audible here. But you can see them, thin silver lines falling through the gold light, and where they land in the floor pools the pools shiver, and where they land on the ledges the builders come to drink. The whole city is arranged around this. The lowest tiers, nearest the pools, are the densest. The upper tiers are for those that can reach the drops before they fall. The dark sealed districts are where the wall stopped weeping, long ago, and the drinkers moved on, and the plant closed over what they left.

The plant, for its part, feeds on them. You can see that too. Where the eyeless long-necked things feed at the seams, the seams are also feeding on them — the tissue swells around their mouths, tender and flushed, and when one of them falls, as they do, old or sick or simply loosened by the vibration, it does not lie on the floor for long. The floor takes it. Softly, over hours. The pools clear.

The sound is the third partner. It is not incidental. The chamber was shaped — grew, was grown — to resonate, and the resonance is what holds the place together. You begin to feel this when you try to walk across the floor toward the far wall. The air resists you. Not like wind; wind has direction. The air *stands* in certain places, in tall invisible columns where the sound has piled up against itself, and in those columns your ears go dead and your chest compresses and you cannot take another step forward though nothing is touching you. You have to go around. And between the columns there are gaps where the sound cancels, thin vertical slots of near-silence, and in the silence the light is wrong, thinner, and the far wall is not where it was, and you step out of the slot quickly because you do not like what you feel coming from the direction the wall used to be.

So the room has streets too. Not the ones in the wall — those are for the builders. The room's streets are the paths between the standing columns of noise, and they move, slowly, as the calling shifts through the day, and the builders know them. You watch a flock of the membrane-winged things cross the whole width of the chamber and they do not fly straight. They fly a long curving route that swings wide around nothing you can see and threads a narrow gap you would never have chosen, and they arrive.

---

The longer you stay the less the room holds its shape.

It was a quarter mile across when you came in. It has been that since, but not steadily. When the calling swells — it swells in slow waves, some inner rhythm of the whole colony, a rising and falling with a period of perhaps twenty minutes — the far wall recedes. Not visibly; you don't see it slide. You just find, when you look again, that the terraces over there are smaller, more numerous, that there is more room between here and there than there was. And when the calling ebbs the wall comes back, and the districts you saw at the far edge are now nearer and larger and it is not clear whether they are the same districts. The room is exactly as big as the sound needs it to be. The plant grows to hold the noise; the noise grows to fill the plant.

Your own ears have long since given up on direction. But something else is happening to them, and it is not damage. You are beginning to hear the wall. Not the animals on it — the wall itself, the terraces, the shape of the honeycomb. It arrives as a texture in the roar, a graininess that thickens where the cells are dense and smooths out over the sealed districts, and if you shut your eyes you can still tell where the tiers are, where the avenues run, where the pools are — and the heard city is not the seen city. It is larger. It has quarters the eye cannot find, out past the far wall, out past where the far wall would be if it stood still. It has a lower level below the floor. It has a district directly above you that you cannot see because the ceiling is there, and yet you hear it, occupied and busy and dripping.

Open your eyes and the ceiling is there. Shut them and the district is. You come to suspect that the heard version is the true one, and the seen version is a kind of shell the light has grown to keep you from going mad, and that the builders — who mostly do not have eyes, or do not seem to use them — live in the true one.

And the drops. This is the last thing, and it is the smallest, and it is the one you will remember. You watch a drop fall from the high wall, a long silver thread, and you follow it down to a ledge where one of the furred head-down things has stopped, and it drinks. And then, a breath later — you would swear it — you watch the same drop fall. The same thread, the same ledge, the same small dark head lifting to meet it. Not another drop. The same. As though the sound, having nowhere to go, had caught the moment on its way past and carried it once around the room and set it down again, a little worn, a little softer at the edges, still wet.

Nothing here is in a hurry. Everything is in motion. The wall breathes and weeps and closes over its own history. The builders are born and call and drink and fall and are taken. The room swells and shrinks with the chord. Outside, a green mound sits on cracked clay and hums, and has hummed, and will.
