Begin at the surface, because the surface is the only place that has light, and it is not the place you are going.

From above it looks like a marsh that has forgotten which way is up. The canopy of weed lies on the water in rafts the color of wet slate, blade over blade, so tightly interleaved that a footfall would probably hold for a moment before it went through. Cold air. Grey sky, or the underside of a cloud that never lifts. The rafts extend to every horizon and past them, and there are no islands, no rocks, no birds standing on anything — only the weed and the slow heave of a swell that takes a full minute to pass beneath you. Weather forms over this. Fog lifts off it in the mornings and settles back at night. In the shedding season the whole surface goes brown and sinks a hand's width and the smell of it carries for hundreds of miles in every direction to nobody.

Now go under.

The canopy is a ceiling. Within the first body-length the light halves, and halves again, and by the time your feet find the tops of the stipes it is something less than light — a memory of green, a difference between up and not-up. Below that the water is black and very cold and moves very little. You are descending through a forest that grows upward toward a sky it has completely occupied, and the forest is rooted in walls.

Everything down here is a wall.

That is the first thing to understand and it takes a long time to understand fully. There is no seabed. There is no rock, no sand, no mud that was not first carried here in a basket. Whatever the ground once was lies under so many centuries of building that it has stopped mattering. What your hands find in the dark is fitted stone: courses of it, dry-laid, each block dressed just enough to sit true on the one below. The joints are tight the way only hand-fitted joints are tight — not with the sameness of things made to measure but with the particular fit of this stone to that stone, chosen for each other, tapped into place, wedged with a sliver. Some walls are terrace risers waist-high. Some are the outer faces of platforms taller than a house, stepped back every few courses so that the whole thing leans inward and holds itself. Some are simply lines, a single course wide, running out into the black for what might be miles, marking a boundary between two fields that were flooded so long ago the boundary is now the only trace of either.

Run your hand along a wall and you feel how long it has been here. The corners are gone. Every edge that a chisel or a pick once left has been taken off by water and by the slow pressure of the weed's holdfasts, which grip the stone with a patience nothing that lives above the surface can match. The blocks have rounded into one another. Whole faces have become a single undulating surface, the joints visible only as shallow grooves, like the veins on the back of a very old hand. This is not a ruin. Ruins are in the process of falling. This has finished falling and has come to rest. Wherever a wall could slump, it has slumped, and the slump has been stable for a thousand years. Wherever silt could gather, it has gathered, to a depth where it no longer gathers faster than the currents remove it. The place has found the shape it will keep.

And on every surface — every riser, every platform edge, every lintel and cistern rim and paving stone — the forest is growing.

The holdfasts are the size of fists and the size of heads, knotted things that have felt their way into every joint and are slowly, over lifetimes, prising the courses apart. From each one a stipe rises, thick as a wrist, smooth and cold and slightly yielding, up through fifty or eighty feet of dark to the light it has left the walls to find. There are so many stipes that you cannot move in a straight line. You move as through a crowd that is not looking at you. They touch your shoulders as you pass. They lean together above and close again behind. Between them, lower down, in the little light that reaches this deep — which is not really light but which the plants can still use — grow the things that need less: fronds of red weed so dark they read as black, crusts of a stony algae that has cemented itself onto the stone in pale lumpy sheets the way lichen takes a gravestone, and in the lee of walls, where the current is slowest, drifts of a fine filamentous growth that lies on everything like fur.

Deeper than that, in the rooms, there is a different kind of life.

Because there are rooms. The platforms are not solid. They are the flat roofs of buildings built on buildings, and the roofs have held, mostly, and beneath them are spaces where the water has not moved in a very long time. You find the doorways by feel: a gap in a wall with a lintel of a single slab, low enough that anyone going through would have stooped. Inside, the temperature drops. Inside, the stipes stop, because there is nothing for them to grow toward, and the darkness becomes total in a way it was not before — a darkness you can lean on. The floor is silt, deep and soft and untroubled, and it gives up a cloud when you touch it that takes an hour to settle. Along the walls, where the stone meets the floor, there is a slick, faintly gritty coating that is neither plant nor stone. It is the mats — the living film of things too small to have names that do not need light at all and breathe the slow chemistry of the silt. In some rooms the film has built itself up into low soft mounds over the centuries. In others it is only a smoothness under the fingers. It is the oldest life here and it will be the last.

Nothing swims. Nothing crawls. This should be said plainly. In the rooms the water is too still and too used-up; the weed above takes the oxygen for itself and the silt takes what is left. Out among the stipes, in the moving dark, a shell might drift down now and then — some small casing that grew and emptied up in the canopy and sank — but nothing lives down here that moves under its own will. The forest is uninhabited. It is a forest in the sense that the far side of a mountain is a forest: a fact of vegetation, not a home. You are the only thing here that turns its head.

Which is why you notice, when you do, the things the builders left.

Not many. They took their possessions with them or the water took them since. But here, wedged in the crack of a terrace wall exactly where someone put it down to free both hands, is a pick made from the beam of an antler, the tines gone soft as soap. Here, in a corner of a room beneath a fallen slab, a stone adze head with a shallow notch worn by a haft that rotted a hundred generations ago. Here, in the silt of a doorway, a scatter of small flat beads that might once have been a string. And once — you will remember this — a plate of hammered copper the size of a hand, so far gone to green that it crumbled in the current when you lifted it, and drifted away in flakes too small to follow. Simple things. Things made one at a time by people who knew how to make one thing at a time and did it for a very long time. There is no wheel in any of this. There is no arch. There is nothing that could not be done with a stone, a stick, a length of cord, and more hands than one.

That is the second thing to understand: the size of it against the smallness of the means.

Because you could travel here for months and not reach an edge. Follow one of the field walls out into the black and it will meet another at a corner, and that one another, and the terraces will step down and step up and the platforms will rise and fall, and the pattern will change — the blocks longer here, the courses set in a herringbone there, cisterns cut round instead of square — the way a language changes across a country. Drop a stone from a platform edge and there will be another platform below it. And below that. The whole of it is low. It was always low: a floodplain world, a place of wet fields and channels and raised houses, never more than the height of a tall man above the water that fed it. When the water came it did not have to come far. It came slowly, over lifetimes, so that people built the walls a course higher and then a course higher and then stopped, and the last courses are the roughest — hurried, undressed stones piled on the good work beneath them like a sentence that trails off.

The water is cold everywhere but coldest in the cisterns. These are the deepest cuts, square shafts sunk through three or four layers of building to whatever was below the beginning, and the water in them does not mix. Put your arm into one and the cold is a different cold, a cold with weight. The mats grow thick on the shaft walls. The silt at the bottom has not been disturbed since the day the last person drew from it. Whatever fell in is still there.

Listen.

Mostly there is nothing. The water is a thick quiet, the quiet of a held breath at scale. Under that, when you have stopped moving for long enough, there is the forest: a slow creak as a stipe bends against the lintel it has grown beside for a century, a soft repeated knock where two holdfasts have worked a stone loose and the current rocks it in its bed, a whisper too faint to be called sound where the fur-growth on the walls stirs in a current you cannot feel. Far off, sometimes — once a day, once a week — a deeper note, a settling. A slab giving up its last inch. It carries a long way in water this still. You will not hear anything twice.

And there is the long sound, which is not sound at all but which you learn to feel: the swell above, passing through the canopy, transmitting itself down the stipes as a slow shift of pressure, a minute in, a minute out. The forest breathes with it. The walls do not.

What changes here changes slowly enough that you would need to be very old to notice. The silt rises in the rooms by the thickness of a leaf each year. The holdfasts split a joint a hair's width per decade and the wall behind it eases and settles and finds a new stillness. Once in a great while the canopy above fails in some season of cold or storm and the light comes down, grey and brief, and for a year the red weed on the lower walls goes pale and reaches, and then the canopy closes and it darkens again. The copper goes on going green. The antler picks go on softening. The mats go on building their low soft mounds in the dark of rooms where no one has stooped through a doorway in a span of time you could not count on all the beads of all the strings that ever broke here.

There is no story in it anymore. That is what erosion means, in the end: not destruction but the completion of a shape. Whoever they were, they built a world by hand across a continent of low wet ground, course on course, and the water they built beside rose up and made the world its own, and then the forest came and stood on the walls and reached for the light, and everything that could happen after that has happened. What is left is a place that has come to rest. Cold. Dark. Enormous. Green-black at the top and black at the bottom and built, every inch of it, by someone, for something, out of stone lifted one at a time.

Go up now. Find the stipe you came down and follow it. The green comes back into the water first as a rumor and then as a color and then, when you break through the canopy, as a whole grey sky, and the cold air, and the weed lying on the swell to every horizon, hiding the walls.
