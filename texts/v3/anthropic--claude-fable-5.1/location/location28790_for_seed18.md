You hear it before you see it, and you hear it for a long time.

The last ascent is a stair bolted to the outside of a shaft — iron treads, a handrail worn to a bright groove by hands that came up before you — and the sound comes down the stair to meet you. At first it's a pressure in the chest more than a noise. Then it's a noise, a single low tone with no edges, the sound a mountain would make if a mountain were a pipe. By the time you're a hundred steps from the top you can't hear your own boots. By the time you reach the lip you've stopped trying to think in words, because words are made of sound and there is no room for any more sound here.

The shaft is the mountain. Whatever rock stood here once was long ago cased in plate — riveted iron, each rivet head the size of a fist, running in courses like the stone of a fortress — and the casing has been up here in the thin cold long enough that it has stopped being metal in any ordinary sense. It's the colour of the cliffs it replaced. It has lichen. It has, in the lee of the rivet lines, a crust of rust so old and so layered that it has become soil, and the soil has seeds in it, and the seeds have come up.

At the top the shaft opens into a chamber, and the chamber is almost nothing.

That's the first thing: how little there is. A circular room perhaps sixty paces across, walled in the same plate, roofed with a cowl of louvred iron that lets the sky in as bars of ordinary grey light. A floor of grating. Under the grating, darkness going down, and out of the darkness, the roar. That's the whole inventory. One wheel-valve on a stem near the wall, painted red a long time ago, its paint now a memory of red. One ladder to a catwalk that circles the room below the louvres. One bell hanging from a bracket by the entrance, swinging a little in the updraft, which you can see ring and cannot hear. A lamp in a cage that is either lit or not; in this light you can't tell.

Everything else is air, and the air is warm.

That's the second thing, and it's the one that makes the place. You are as high as it's possible to be built. Outside the cowl, the sky is the dark blue of true altitude, and the frost on the outer casing never fully leaves. But the shaft exhales. Whatever it is doing far below — no one who comes here knows; the wheel-valve turns a quarter turn either way and changes nothing you can detect — it pushes up a column of warm, wet, faintly mineral air, steadily, without pulse or surge, at the same rate it has pushed since before the paint on the wheel was red. The air comes through the grating and fills the room and meets the cold iron of the cowl and gives up its water.

So it rains here. It rains from the roof, gently, all day, all night, a fine drip off the louvre edges and the catwalk rails and the bell's shoulder. Where the drip lands on the grating it runs through and is carried back down into the roar and comes up again. Where it lands on iron it makes rust, and rust makes soil, and the soil is where the green begins.

The green is everywhere the metal has a ledge. Moss first, a deep wet emerald cushion on every rivet course, thick enough to press a thumb into. Then ferns — filmy ones, translucent as the lids of eyes, that could live nowhere else on this mountain and live here in profusion, unrolling off the walls in rows that follow the plating. Something with broad leaves has taken root in the accumulated rust behind the ladder and grown up along it as if the ladder were a trellis put there for the purpose. Along the catwalk the growth is heavier, a hanging fringe of it drooping over the rail into the warm column, so that looking up from the floor you see the sky through a green lace. Roots have found the seams between plates and gone in. It's an old occupation. The metal and the plants have reached terms.

The odd thing about the plants — one odd thing — is that they lean. Not toward the light, which comes evenly through the louvres, and not toward the wet, which is everywhere. They lean toward the centre of the room, toward the grating, toward the sound. The ferns arch inward. The moss is thickest on the sound-facing side of every rivet head. Whether they're following the warmth or following the vibration, or whether after this long there's any difference, you can't say. But the room has a bias in it, a slow lean of everything living toward the place the roar comes from, and you find after a while that you've adopted it yourself.

The fauna doesn't lean. The fauna rides.

There are birds here — small, dark, hooked-winged, the kind that never land anywhere else — and they live in the column. They come in under the cowl in the morning, or what serves as morning at this height, and drop into the warm air and simply hang there, wings set, letting the exhalation hold them. Dozens of them at any time, turning in the empty space above the grating in slow overlapping circles, never touching, never calling that you can hear, though their beaks open. They take insects. There are insects — a haze of them over the grating, hatched from the moss and the rust-soil, lifted on the breath of the shaft and eaten before they reach the roof. And there are the lizards, blunt and slate-grey and warm to the touch, which press themselves flat against the plate where it's hottest and do not move for hours, and which have learned that nothing here can hear them, and so have stopped bothering to be quiet. You'll see one scrabble across the grating with a clatter that in any other place would send everything running. Nothing runs. The birds keep circling. The ferns keep leaning. The roar is so total it has abolished the whole category of small noises, and the animals have grown up in the abolition.

So the rules, as far as they can be worked out:

Nothing spoken here is heard. People who come up together — and some do, though rarely, and never many — put their hands on each other's shoulders and mouth things, and after a while give that up too and just stand.

Nothing changes quickly. The valve doesn't do anything. The breath doesn't vary. The rain off the roof falls at the same rate as it has for as long as there's been moss to measure it by, and the moss measures it by getting thicker. The only cycles are the long ones: the ferns brown a little in what must be a dry season far below, when the breath comes up marginally less wet; the birds are gone for some weeks; a plate somewhere in the casing lets go a rivet, a single hard clang under the roar like a tooth dropped in a river, and a fern comes down with it, and another begins.

Everything here was made, and no one knows what for. The shaft goes down further than anyone has followed it. The bell was hung by someone who thought it could be heard. The wheel was painted by someone who thought it should be turned. Whoever they were, they built to last and then left, and what they built has become the kind of place that outlives its reason — a mouth at the top of the world, breathing warm on a garden it didn't intend, in a noise it can't stop making.

You stay longer than you mean to. Standing on the grating with the breath coming up through the soles of your feet and the rain coming down on your shoulders and the birds turning overhead in the one warm room on the mountain, the roar stops being a sound and becomes a kind of weather, and then a kind of quiet — the only quiet there is here, the quiet of a thing so loud that nothing can be added to it. Your teeth hum. The bell swings. A lizard crosses the floor, clattering, unafraid. The ferns lean in toward the dark under the grate, as they have for a long time, as they will after you go back down the stair and the sound recedes below you step by step until it's a pressure in the chest again, and then a memory of one.
