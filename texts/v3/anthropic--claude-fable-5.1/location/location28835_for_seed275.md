The first thing is the dryness. Not heat — the air is cool, cellar-cool, the temperature of a stone hand — but a dryness so complete that the lips split within an hour and the skin of the fingertips whitens and lifts. There is no water here. There has never been water here. The floor is a crust of anhydrite and halite, grey-white, powdering underfoot into a talc that hangs in the still air and slowly settles. Where the crust has been cut or broken it shows bands: pink, bone, a dull rust. Nothing dissolves it. Nothing ever has.

The space is vast. It is hard, at first, to accept that it is underground. The roof is somewhere above — three hundred meters, perhaps more — a slow vault of the same pale mineral, and it is lit. Not by shafts; there are no shafts. The light comes from below, from the things that are growing here, and it is thrown back down by the roof so that the whole cavern holds an even, shadowless, mid-morning light, the light of a cloudy day with the sun somewhere behind. It does not change. There is no dusk. The lit surfaces are simply present, and the eye, after a while, stops asking where the sun is.

The cavern runs for kilometers. You could walk a day across it and not reach a wall, only more of the same floor, and, spaced across it at long intervals, the growths.

---

They are plants. That is the only word for them, though they do not photosynthesize and they do not drink. They rise out of the crust wherever there is a seam of black beneath it — the rock here is veined with old tar, bitumen, a heavy dry hydrocarbon locked into the salt — and they draw that up, and it moves through them as a sap that is more like light machine oil than anything a leaf has known. Their tissue is pale, dry, hard; the surface has the grain and give of cork and the density of cured oak. Cut, it does not bleed. It smells of pitch and, faintly, of warm bread. It is warm, slightly, from the inside.

They grow in the shapes of buildings.

This is not a metaphor, and it is not a trick of the eye finding architecture in nature. The nearest growth is a colonnade — twenty-two columns in two rows, each column a single trunk risen straight from the crust and stopped at exactly the same height, and across their tops a continuous beam of the same tissue, grown from column to column so that the joins are not joins at all but seamless. The columns are fluted. The flutes are the plant's vascular channels, running up the outside, and they are spaced evenly. The beam's underside is flat. Along its upper edge, in a line, are what look like buds: fists of pale tissue that will, in time, unfold into something, a floor perhaps, a second story.

Nothing about the colonnade is finished. This is the second thing you understand about the place, after the dryness: it is all unfinished. Every structure ends in growth. The beam runs past the last column and continues on, unsupported, for eleven meters, tapering, and then stops in a soft blunt tip, still swelling. A wall beside it stands to shoulder height along most of its length and then descends in steps to nothing, the steps being simply the places where the growth fronts have not yet arrived. Doorways stand open with no doors in them — the doors come later, hinge and panel grown in place — and some doorways are only three-quarter arches, the keystone not yet met. There is a set of stairs that climbs toward a landing that does not exist. There are the beginnings of a bridge that reaches out over a low place in the crust and simply ends, midair, feathered at the end into fine roots-in-reverse, searching.

It grows while you watch. Not quickly — the pace is the pace of a minute hand, not a second hand — but if you fix your eye on the tip of the bridge and hold your breath you can see the searching filaments shift. Put an ear to a column and there is a sound inside it, a faint continuous creak, like a house settling, that never settles.

---

The growths are far apart. Between the colonnade and the next structure — a low round thing that might become a hall or a tank — there is a kilometer of empty crust with nothing on it but wind-ripples in the powder and the tracks of animals. The city, if it is a city, is spaced out across the cavern floor the way towns are spaced along a river, except that there is no river; there are only the tar seams, invisible beneath the salt, and the growths mark where they run. Some seams are richer than others. Over the rich ones the growths crowd together into something like a district: a dozen structures within a few hundred meters, connected by grown walkways, roofed streets, a spiral ramp that turns three times around itself and lifts its unfinished lip toward the roof. Over the poor seams there is just a single trunk, or a wall standing alone in the white, waiting.

From a distance a district looks like a town seen from a hilltop. Up close it is stranger. The proportions are not quite human. Doorways are too tall and slightly too narrow. Stairs have risers that force a long stride. There are rooms with sockets in the wall at the height of a standing person's chest, a row of them, smooth-bored, the size of a wrist, purpose unknown. Every district has, somewhere near its center, a wide shallow bowl grown into the floor, twenty meters across, with a lip, and nothing in it.

---

The light is theirs. Along the upper surfaces of the beams and the crowns of the columns the tissue changes: it goes translucent, then glassy, and glows. Not bioluminescence in the cold blue-green sense — this is a hot, white, working light, the light of a filament, and touching one of the glowing surfaces you can feel the current under the skin of it, a fine prickle. The plants are electrified. The oil-sap moving through them carries charge; you can hear it, if the cavern is quiet enough, as a hum just below the threshold of hearing, more in the teeth than the ears.

And they do work. This is the thing that takes longest to see, because it is so quiet.

At the edge of the largest district there is a shed — a grown shed, four walls and a roof, long, with one open side — and inside it, standing in a row, are six wheels. They are the plant. They have grown out of the floor on axles that are also the plant, and they turn. Slowly, silently, oiled from the inside, each one about the size of a millstone, and along the rim of each are grown teeth that mesh with the teeth of a smaller wheel, which drives a shaft, which disappears into the wall. What it drives on the other side of the wall, you cannot tell. The wall is unfinished. The shaft ends, for now, in a blunt green-white bud.

There are pistons elsewhere. There is a thing like a bellows, two meters tall, that inflates and collapses on a cycle of about a minute, and where its exhaust goes there is a chimney, grown, and from the chimney, nothing you can see — only a shimmer, a heat-flicker in the cool air. There is a whole long building, roofed but not yet walled, in which the floor is a bed of the plant's tissue rising in hundreds of small identical nubs, each nub slowly extruding a fiber, and the fibers are drawn up by a grown reel and wound, and the reels, when full, drop from their axles onto the floor and lie there in a heap of spools nobody comes for. The heap is large. It has been accumulating for a long time.

The city is a factory and the factory is a plant and the plant is building the city. What the wheels drive, what the fibers are for, who is expected to walk the too-tall doorways and stand at the too-high sockets — none of that is here yet. The place has the feeling of a set being built for a play whose company has not arrived, and may not, and the set does not seem to mind. It goes on. The bridge reaches. The stairs climb toward the landing that isn't.

---

It is not empty, though. Living things move through it.

Most are small. There are beetles the size of a thumbnail, pale, almost translucent, that swarm at the fresh growth-fronts and eat the searching filaments, and in this they may be doing a kind of pruning; certainly the growths that have the most beetles are the cleanest-edged. There are things like grasshoppers that live entirely inside the fluted channels of the columns and can be heard, very faintly, ticking. There is a flat grey lizard-shaped animal that presses itself against the glowing beams for warmth and does not move for hours. There are motes that drift, that might be seeds or might be animals, and settle on the crust and are gone.

And there are larger animals, and these are what give the cavern its motion. Herds of a pale six-legged thing, dog-sized, that moves across the open crust between districts in loose strings of thirty or forty, browsing on the fallen spools and the shed fiber and the occasional slab of tissue that a growth has abscised — because the growths do shed; a wall that has been superseded by a better wall will loosen at its base and drop, and lie on the crust, and be eaten. The herds are quiet. Their feet are broad and make no sound in the powder. They leave trails that cross the cavern in long curves from one district to the next, and the trails are the closest thing to roads the place has.

Something hunts them. You do not see it easily. It is the color of the crust and it uses the colonnades — the columns are exactly wide enough to hide it — and it waits. Twice you see a herd string tighten suddenly and flow away from a particular structure at a hard run, and once you find, on the far side of a half-grown wall, a place where the powder has been disturbed in a broad circle and there is a smell of pitch and something else, and pale hairs, and nothing more.

The predator's presence means the districts are used. Trails go around some structures and through others. The wide shallow bowls at the district centers are avoided by everything.

---

The cavern has weather, of a kind. Every few hours the roof sheds. There is no warning — a faint crackle overhead, and then a fall of dust, gypsum flour, sifting down across a few hundred meters of floor for the space of a minute, so that the light dims slightly and the air tastes of chalk and everything wears a fresh film of white. The growths shrug it off; their glowing surfaces heat enough that the dust does not settle on them, and a fine dry avalanche of powder runs continuously off the beams like sand off a drum. Everything else is coated. The herds are the color of the floor for this reason. So is the thing that hunts them.

Slower than the dust-fall is the pulse. Perhaps twice a day — though "day" means nothing; call it twice in the time it takes a person to grow hungry, sleep, and wake — the whole cavern warms by a degree or two. The hum in the teeth rises. The glowing surfaces brighten from mid-morning to something closer to noon, and the fresh growth-fronts, if you are watching them, visibly hurry, the filaments stiffening and extending. Then it passes and the air cools again and the growth returns to its slow settling creak. It is metabolism. The plant is breathing, on the scale of a landscape, and the city is its exhalation.

Slower still — you would have to stay a long time to see it — the districts move. Not the structures; those are rooted. But the seams beneath run off in one direction, deeper into the cavern, and the growth follows them. The oldest district, at the near end, has the most finished buildings, the most closed doors, the fullest walls; also the most shed slabs and the biggest heaps of uncollected spools, and here and there a column that has gone dark, its glow spent, standing among the live ones like a dead tooth. At the far end the structures are only stubs, only buds. The city is not standing still; it is walking, at the pace of a minute hand, away from where it began, toward whatever the seams lead to, building as it goes and leaving its earliest work behind for the herds to eat.

---

Stand in the middle of it for long enough and the quiet becomes the dominant fact. The hum. The tick in the columns. The powder-hiss of dust running off a beam. A herd passing at a distance with no sound at all. The creak, everywhere and nowhere, of things growing into the shapes of rooms.

It is a working place with nobody working in it, a lit place with no sun, a green place with no water and no green. The plant does not know it is strange. It has found a seam of old darkness under a mile of salt and it is making, out of that darkness, wheels and doorways and stairs, patiently, for someone. The bridge reaches out over the low place in the crust. The filaments at its tip lift, and feel the dry air, and go on.
