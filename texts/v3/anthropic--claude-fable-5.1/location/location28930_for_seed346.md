The ground comes first, because the ground is not finished.

It has the color of wet cement and something of its texture, but it is not cement; press a thumb into it and it yields very slightly and then resists, the way a thing does when it has decided to be solid but has not fully committed to the decision. Walk on it and it holds. Kneel and put your ear to it and there is a faint continuous sound underneath, not mechanical, more like the sound a room makes when someone in another room is thinking hard. This is the substrate being written. Somewhere below — there is no clear sense of how far — a process is laying down the rules by which stone will be stone, and the rock at the surface is the freshest output of that process, only recently promoted from proposal to fact. In places it is still tacky. There are footprints in it from no one.

The town sits on top of this like a mold sits on a casting, except the casting is still pouring.

It is small. You can walk from one edge to the other in the time it takes a kettle to boil, if you walk in a straight line, which you cannot. The buildings are shoulder to shoulder and then closer than that: they lean into one another, overlap, share walls that were not designed to be shared. Stone tenements four and five storeys high, with narrow windows and iron-colored fixtures, and between them, threaded through and over them, the infrastructure — pipes as thick as a torso, sleeved in something like frost-dulled glass, running along the eaves and diving into the street and rising again three doors down. They do not carry water. If you put your palm against one it is cold in a direction your skin does not have a word for, a cold that feels like it is going *past* you rather than into you. These are the conduits by which the substrate is served. They carry whatever it is the ground is made of before it is ground.

Everything is damp. The air is a few degrees warmer than the newly settled stone, and so the stone sweats. Condensation beads on every surface and runs down in slow threads and collects in the seams between buildings and freezes overnight and unfreezes by mid-morning, so the whole town is glazed, faintly, with a film that catches the light. The light is the one ordinary thing. It is grey daylight, the flat even daylight of an overcast afternoon in late autumn, shadowless, coming from no particular direction, and it does not change much. It does not seem to know about the sun. It is simply the amount of light a town like this should have, and so it has it.

Sometimes it rains. The rain here is uncertain about its terms. Mostly it falls the way rain falls, straight down, cold, pattering on the glass-sleeved conduits with a sound like fingernails. But at the edges of the town it hesitates — drops that come down and stop a hand's width above the stone and hang there, considering, and either finish falling or go slowly back up. Nobody has established which direction is correct. After the rain the streets are full of puddles that reflect the street from an angle that is not quite the angle you are standing at, so you see the underside of a balcony you are standing on, or the back of your own coat.

It is quiet. The quiet is structural. Sound moves through this place reluctantly, as if the medium for it had not been fully installed; a dropped coin makes its small bright noise and the noise reaches you a moment late and slightly muffled, as through a wall that is not there. Voices, when there are voices, arrive as murmurs regardless of distance. The loudest thing is the water — the drip and thread of condensation, the pattering conduits — and even that is soft, a sound at the edge of hearing, more felt than heard, like the building's pulse.

---

You notice the doubling after a while, though it has been there the whole time.

There is a lamppost at the corner where the main street kinks left. It is iron, or the color of iron, with a glass head that is lit though the light is not needed. There is also a second lamppost, in nearly the same place — offset perhaps two centimeters, at an angle perhaps a degree different — and a third, and behind those a blur of others too faint to count, all occupying the corner, all being the lamppost. From a distance they resolve into one. Up close they never quite do. The frost on the glass head forms in a pattern that belongs to all of them, a lace that is denser where they overlap and thin where they diverge, and the stone at the base of the post is worn — worn, in a town where the stone is new — in a smear of small overlapping footprints, as if many people had leaned against many versions of the same lamppost for many years.

This is the density. The town is not crowded with people. It is crowded with instances. Every doorway is several doorways that have not agreed on a threshold. Every stair has more steps than you can climb, laid over each other, so that going up feels like climbing through a stack of translucent stairs and the one your foot lands on is decided at the moment of landing. The windows are the worst of it, or the best: they show the interior of the room and also the interior of the room next door and also a room that is in neither building and cannot be, a room with a green wall and a chair, and all three interiors are dim and still and empty of anyone. Inside, everything is layered. Tables ghost through tables. There is always one more coat on the hook than there is hook.

None of this is violent. Nothing flickers. The overlaps do not resolve suddenly or fight for precedence. They simply coexist, patiently, like sediment. If you stand in one place for an hour you may see one of the lampposts drift a hair's width closer to another, or a stair align itself more exactly with the stair beneath it. The town is settling. It is settling the way ground settles under a house over decades, except that what is settling is not the ground but the *fact* of the town — its count, its dimensions, which of its versions is the real one. Somewhere in the writing of the substrate, the specification for this place was left open, or was written many times, and now all the drafts are here at once, waiting for the slow underlying process to reconcile them.

---

There is a street that runs the length of the town and ends, on both sides, in fog.

The fog is the boundary. It begins where the settled stone begins to lose its nerve — where the pavement goes from cement-colored to a paler, chalkier grey and then to something you would not want to step on, a surface with the sheen of skin on cooling milk. Beyond that there is no ground, but there is also no drop. There is a grey haze exactly the color of the daylight, and it is very cold there, colder than anywhere else, and the condensation on the last buildings before it is thick enough to run in sheets. This is the seam between what has been decided and what has not. The conduits run straight into it. You can see them for a few meters, dimming, and then you cannot.

People who live here — and some do, though you will rarely see them, and when you do they will be at the far end of a street, indistinct, a coat and a posture, walking away — do not go to the edge. Not from fear. There is simply nothing there yet. It would be like walking into a sentence before it has been written. Occasionally the fog gives up a little ground: a new stretch of pavement resolving out of the grey, damp and soft and printed with the same ownerless footprints, and a few weeks later a wall standing on it, and a few months later a building, stacked already with its several versions. The town grows at the pace of a fingernail. It has been growing for a long time. There is no sense of how long; the doubling extends to the clocks.

---

Very little lives here on purpose.

In the seams between the leaning buildings, where the condensation collects and the stone stays wet, there is a lichen. It is a dull greenish-grey, crustose, patient, the kind of thing that takes a century to cover a hand's breadth of rock — and it does cover a hand's breadth of rock, on stone that cannot be more than a year old, which means either the lichen is wrong or the year is. There is a moss in the shadow of the conduits, dark and cold and very slightly luminous when the light fails, which it seldom does. Once, on the roof of a building near the east fog, a small tree, leafless and gnarled, that when you look at it from the street is two trees and from the roof is none.

And there are the birds. Or the bird. It is corvid-shaped and coal-colored and it is the only animal you will see, and you will see it on the conduit outside the window and on the lamppost and on the cornice of the building opposite, and you will not be able to say whether it is three birds or one bird that has not settled how many it is. It does not call. It watches, and shifts its weight, and preens, and its several beaks work at its several feathers with a synchrony that is not quite exact. Now and then one of it drops from its perch and flies the length of the street toward the fog and does not come back, and the count on the cornice does not change.

---

There are rules, and you learn them by walking.

Distance is approximate. The main street is longer walked west than walked east, by perhaps a dozen paces; the side streets are worse. A doorway you have passed may not be there when you return, or may be there twice. Nothing is ever lost, exactly — the doorway is still in the town — but it may have shifted its emphasis to a different draft.

Cold accumulates in the overlaps. Where three walls occupy the same space the air between them is bitter, and ice forms in fern patterns on the glass that no ordinary frost would make, tessellated, many-jointed, as if the crystals themselves had been given more than one lattice to grow along.

The conduits are not to be opened. Where one has cracked — there is one, on the north side, low, with a hairline fracture in its sleeve — the wall behind it has gone soft again, dedifferentiated, back to the tacky cement-grey of the fresh substrate, and the fracture weeps something clear that is not water and does not run downward and where it touches the ground the ground forgets briefly what it is.

Nothing here happens quickly. That is the fundamental rule. The town is a slow reconciliation, a landscape in the act of being decided, and the decision is being made at the speed of geology by something that has all the time it needs. The reality breaking down here is not collapsing; it is *condensing*, drop by drop, the way the moisture condenses on the stone — many possibilities cooling against a cold surface until they can no longer stay in the air and must become one thing, or a few things, or a film of things that catches the flat grey light.

Stand at the corner with the lampposts long enough and you will feel the cold come through your boots, and the damp settle on your shoulders, and the quiet press gently on your ears. The coin you dropped a while ago will finally finish making its sound. The bird will shift on its several perches. And one of the lampposts — the second one, perhaps — will be, when you look back, very slightly more the lamppost than it was before.
