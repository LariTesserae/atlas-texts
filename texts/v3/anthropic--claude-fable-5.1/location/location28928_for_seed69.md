The first thing is that you cannot see it, and this is not a condition of the hour. There is no hour. Light does not happen here. A struck match makes heat against the thumb and a smell of sulfur and nothing else — the flame is there, you can feel it, but it gives nothing to the eye, and after a moment it goes out as though ashamed. Whatever this place is, it has settled the question of light a long time ago, and settled it in the negative.

So you know it first by the wet.

The air is full. Not humid in the way of a summer afternoon but saturated to the point of running, so that water condenses on the backs of your hands, on your eyelashes, on the underside of your jaw, and drips. Every surface weeps. Stone is slick. Bark is slick. The ground is not mud exactly but a warm, giving mat of something rooted and something rotting, and water stands in every low place and moves slowly through every sloped one, and when you stop walking you can hear it working its way downhill in a thousand small voices under the roar.

Because there is a roar. It is the second thing. It comes from everywhere and from particular directions at once, a layered noise like weather that never breaks: a deep, patient throb somewhere to the left that you feel in your teeth; a high sustained hiss that could be steam or could be a million leaves; a cracking and rattling in the middle register that never resolves into a rhythm; and beneath all of it, so low it registers as pressure rather than sound, something turning. Something very large turning, a long way off, and never stopping.

---

The land is high. You know it from the thinness that sits under the warmth — the air is warm and wet but it is not thick; your breath comes faster than it should. You know it also from the way sound behaves at the edges. Walk long enough in one direction and the ground stops, cleanly, and the roar in front of you is replaced by a vast soft absence with wind moving up out of it. The wind is warmer than the plateau and wetter. Something below is exhaling, and this whole tableland is the lip it exhales across.

How far the plateau runs is not a question the place answers. It has its own weather. Rain comes across it in fronts you can hear approaching for an hour — a change in the hiss, a smell of iron and crushed stems, then the drumming — and the fronts pass and are followed by others, and the water they leave drains and rises again as fog and comes down again, and the cycle is older than anything that walks in it. There are winds that blow one way for what feels like days. There are stillnesses so complete that the dripping becomes the loudest thing. At the scale it exists, it has stopped being a landscape and become a condition.

---

The forest is everywhere and it is not made of anything you'd have called a plant, though it does what plants do. The trunks are cool and fibrous and enormous, some too wide to reach around, some so close together you turn your shoulders to pass between them. Their surfaces are ridged in spirals. Higher up — you can feel this by climbing, by the way the trunk swells and branches — they fan out into flat, leathery structures that catch the drip and channel it, and the whole canopy is a roof of these, layered so densely that the falling rain arrives at the ground as a delayed, distributed weeping rather than as rain.

They don't grow toward light. Nothing here does. They grow toward heat. Put your palm flat on the ground and you feel it, faintly: the earth is warm from below, unevenly, and the forest is thickest where it is warmest, and thins into low rubbery mats and standing water where the warmth runs out. The trunks lean, all of them, in the same direction in any given stretch of ground, and the direction they lean is the direction of the nearest engine.

---

That is what the turning is.

You come on the first one by the sound growing out of the roar until it is the roar, and by the ground going from soft to hard under your feet, and then by the wall. It is metal. You know it before you touch it, from the way it gives sound back, and when you touch it, it is warm and wet and rough — not the roughness of manufacture but of long weather. Pitted. Furred with a moss-like growth that comes away in your fingers and smells of pennies. The wall curves. You follow it, one hand on it, for a very long time, and it does not stop curving, and somewhere inside it, through the plate, comes the slow immense stroke of something rising and falling. Once every few breaths. It has been doing this long enough that the ground around it has been worn into a shallow moat by the tremor of it, and the moat is full of warm water, and the water is full of things moving.

There are pipes leaving the wall. Thick as a body, running off into the forest at a height you can just reach, sweating and hot enough to hurt if you hold on. The forest has grown around them, into them, over them — in places the pipe is more root than pipe — but they have not been broken. Follow one far enough and it arrives at another engine. Follow that one's pipes and they arrive at others. They are scattered across the tableland at long intervals, a day or two's walking between them, each one a hill of iron in its own clearing of hard ground, each one thudding, and they are all connected, and they are all running, and there is nobody in them.

No doors that you can find. No lights, of course. No smell of fuel. What they burn, if they burn anything, is the water: the air goes into them wet and comes out of the high vents hotter and wetter, in plumes you can feel on your face from a hundred paces off, and it is this — you become sure of it, in the way you become sure of things in the dark — that keeps the plateau warm, and it is the warmth that lifts the water and makes the fog and the rain, and the rain feeds the engines. The whole roof of the world is a loop that closed a long time ago and has been turning ever since. Whether anyone built it to do this, or whether it was built to do something else and this is simply what it does now, the iron doesn't say. It has weathered past the point of intention. You can find the ghosts of rivets, the edges of plates, a stub where a ladder was, but they are the way fossils are in a cliff: shapes that used to be a purpose.

---

The animals do not care about the question either. There are so many of them.

They are in the water — the warm moat around each engine is thick with them, small quick bodies that bump your ankles and slide away, and larger slower ones that you hear surfacing with a sound like a bucket lifted from a well. They are in the trunks; something the size of a dog lives in the ridged spirals and clicks constantly, a dry rattling that is both the sound of its moving and a sound it makes on purpose, and the clicks come back to it from the bark and the pipes and the wet ground, and it knows where everything is. Nothing here has eyes. You can catch things — they are not especially afraid, since fear is a thing you learn from being seen — and run your fingers over smooth blank heads, over mouths and nostrils and the soft dents where an ear might be, and there is never an eye.

Some of them find their way by sound. Some by heat: there is a long low animal that moves through the mats near the pipes and stops when you come near and lifts its head toward you, toward your warmth, and stays lifted, tasting. Some by the water itself — pressure, current, the tiny shifts a moving body makes in a saturated medium. The canopy has its own tier of them, things that never come down, whose calls are a piping so high it is almost not sound, that you feel in your sinuses. And there are predators, because there always are: a silence moves through the clicking sometimes, a hole in the noise the shape of something that has learned to be quiet, and the clicking closes over where it went, and a while later there is a brief, wet commotion, and then the ordinary roar again.

They gather at the engines. Every engine is thick with life the way a reef is — the warm water, the hot pipes, the metal furred with whatever it is that grows on metal here. Colonies of something shelled have cemented themselves to the iron in ridges you can read with your hands like a coastline. The tremor from inside must be, to them, the way sunlight is to other things: the fundamental fact, the thing you orient by, the thing that was always there.

---

There are a few marks that are not engines and not living.

A wall, once, low and straight, running for forty paces through the forest and stopping. Cut stone, joints you can feel with a fingernail, roots through it. A flat place where the ground rings faintly hollow. A shape in the fog that resolves under your hands into a single upright post with a ring at the top, iron gone to lace with rust, standing alone in a marsh a long way from anything. Whoever left these did not stay, or did not stay in a form that leaves marks. The engines were the point. The rest was scaffolding, and the scaffolding is mostly gone.

---

And underneath it all, the rule. You test it again and again because it seems impossible and it holds every time. Strike sparks from the iron with a stone: you hear them, you feel the small hot bites where they land on your wrist, and you see nothing. Rub your eyes hard in the dark and you get no phosphenes. Whatever light is, it is not permitted here — not forbidden, exactly, but unable, like a sound that cannot travel through vacuum. The place is not dark the way a room is dark. It is dark the way the inside of a stone is dark, or the deep sea, and it has been that way long enough that everything in it has been shaped by the absence into forms that would not recognize the thing they lack.

You stand for a long time with your back against a warm iron wall, the stroke of the piston coming up through your spine, rain arriving in the canopy overhead and coming down around you as a thousand separate drips, the clicking things going about their business in the trunks, the low animal lifting its head toward your heat from somewhere by your knee, and the far engines answering this one across the tableland, thud, and after a while, thud, and the wind coming warm up over the edge of the world from whatever is exhaling below.

It does not need you to see it. It has never been seen. It goes on.
