The first thing to understand about the plateau is that it has stopped. Whatever it was doing — rising, folding, shedding itself in landslides down whatever flanks it once had — it finished long ago, and what is left is the part that could not be taken away. Quartzite, mostly, so old it has no fossils because it predates anything that could have left one. It stands somewhere above the weather most of the world knows, and its top is nearly flat: not the flatness of a plain, which is built up, but the flatness of a thing worn down until only the resistant floor remains. From the edge, if you could find the edge, the rock would drop into cloud and keep dropping, and the cloud would not show you the bottom.

You rarely find the edge. The plateau is large — a day's walk across, perhaps more, though nobody measures it that way — and its rim is nearly always inside the cloud that sits on the whole thing like a lid. The air is thin. The cold is not sharp; it is the cool of a cellar, of stone that hasn't been in direct sun for a very long time, and it is wet. Everything here is wet in the particular way of a place that never quite rains. The cloud moves through, and the cloud is made of water, and the water attaches itself to whatever is standing still. Fog drip. Every leaf, every wire, every rooftop and railing has a bead forming at its lowest point, and when the bead grows heavy enough it falls, and another begins. This is the sound of the plateau: not rain, but the aftermath of rain, continuously.

---

The rock itself is only visible in places — the pans, where water has stood for so long that nothing will grow, and where the surface shows a grain like wood, low ridges a finger-width apart running in parallel across acres. Everywhere else it is covered.

Moss, first. Deep moss, bolsters of it a meter or more thick, springing back slowly underfoot, holding water like a body holds blood. It is the color of moss in shadow: dark green going toward black at the base, rust at the tips of some species, a grey-green almost blue on the hummocks that catch what little light comes down. On top of the moss, or rooted through it into the rock's fractures, grow the trees, if they can be called that. None is taller than a person. They are twisted, their bark hidden under lichen that hangs in pale beards and grows in orange plates and in a black crust that looks like something burned. Their leaves are small and leathery, waxed against the cold. Between them, in any cleft that offers shelter, the tree ferns unfurl — the largest living things here, some three meters across the crown, their fronds heavy with condensation and slowly, constantly, nodding.

It is dense. That is the word for it. Nothing stands alone. Every trunk is a scaffold for six other species; every fern is a roof over a colony of smaller ferns; the moss on the ground continues up the trunks and across the branches and down the other side. Walking through it means pushing through it. A hand placed anywhere will touch four living things and come away wet.

And it is all in motion — not visibly, not moment to moment, but at a pace you can feel if you stand still long enough. The moss is growing. In the thin cold air with water arriving constantly, it grows at a rate that would be absurd anywhere else, and the plateau is not a static thing but a slow green tide moving over itself, burying, decaying underneath, rising. There is peat under the moss, meters of it, the accumulated dead of a very long time.

---

The town does not announce itself. You come upon it the way you come upon everything here: it resolves out of the cloud a few meters ahead, and you realize you have been walking along a wall for some time without knowing.

It is built of the plateau's own stone, cut in long low blocks, and it has been built on and over and against itself for so long that the layers are legible: older masonry rounded and moss-softened at the bottom, newer courses above it, iron above that. Nothing is tall, because there is no reason to be tall here and every reason not to be — the wind, when it comes, comes across the flat top with nothing to stop it — so the town spreads instead, and doubles back, and roofs over its own streets. Much of it is covered. You walk through passages where the ceiling is someone's floor, where pipes run overhead wrapped in sacking against the damp and dripping anyway, where a dim lamp every twenty paces marks the way. The lamps are electric. Their filaments glow a tired orange inside glass that has fogged from within. They do not so much light the passages as indicate where light would be.

The moss has come in with the people. It grows on the north faces of the walls, in the mortar lines, along the gutters, over the lower pipes. Crews go out with scrapers. In the older quarters they have given up, and the walls are simply green, and the town and the plateau have reached some understanding.

---

The town exists because of the works, and the works exist because of the cloud.

At the edges of the settlement, and standing out in the open plateau beyond it in long rows that disappear into the fog, are the screens. Each is a frame of iron the height of three men, holding a mesh — woven wire in the old ones, something finer and darker in the new — set at a slight angle to the prevailing drift. The cloud passes through the mesh and the water in the cloud does not. It gathers on the wires, runs down, collects in the trough at the base, and from the trough it flows through the channels.

The channels are everywhere. Stone-cut at first, then iron-lined, then simply iron: they run beside every street and under many of them, and they carry the plateau's harvest inward and downward through the town by a gradient so gentle it took generations to survey. Where the gradient allows, the water turns wheels. Small wheels, most of them, no wider than a cart, set into the walls of the passages and turning with a wet patience. Belts run from their axles into the buildings. Some drive dynamos, which feed the tired lamps. Some drive the mills. The mills are why the town is here.

What they mill is the peat.

It is cut from the deep beds in the plateau's shallow basins, hauled in on low iron rails by carts that move without much sound, and brought into the drying houses, which are the largest structures in the town and the only ones with real height — chimneys, brick, going up into the cloud and vanishing. It takes an enormous effort to dry anything here. The drying houses burn a portion of what they dry to dry the rest, and the smoke from the chimneys goes up and joins the cloud and comes back down, eventually, as more drip. The dried peat is pressed into blocks and bound and stacked and sent down — there are ways down, cable-lines off the rim into the white, and things come up the same way, and that is all most people in the town know about the lower world.

The pressing is done in the mills. The sound of it — a slow hydraulic groan, a thud, a hiss of released water — is the loudest thing in the town, and it is not loud. It arrives through the walls as a pulse more than a noise. Between pulses there is the dripping, and the turning of wheels, and the occasional scrape of a moss crew's blade against stone.

---

There are few animals. This is a place of plants and water and iron, and the things that live in it are mostly very small. The moss is full of them — springtails, mites, a pale beetle that comes out on the mesh screens at night and is found in the troughs in the morning, drowned, in numbers. Nothing sings. Once or twice a season a bird comes over, high, black against the slightly lighter grey, and does not land. There is a story that something larger lives in the far peat basins where the cutting has never reached, and people leave it alone, and it leaves them alone, and nobody has seen it in living memory. It may be a way of talking about the peat itself, which does move — it settles, it bulges, it breathes out gas that sometimes catches in the drying-house flues with a soft blue flame.

The people are few, too, for the size of the place. The town was built for more of them, or by more of them, and now there are streets in the outer quarters where no lamp has burned in years, where the channels still run because the channels do not need anyone, and the wheels still turn, driving belts into rooms where the machines have stopped and the moss has taken the floor. Nobody quite says the town is shrinking. It is more that the plateau was here first, and is patient, and the town has always been a temporary arrangement.

---

Certain things are understood here, though they are not written down.

That the cloud is the weather, and there is no other. It lifts sometimes — an hour, an afternoon — and the top of the plateau shows itself as what it is, a vast dark green flatness with the screens standing over it in rows like the stubble of some crop, and the sky above is a hard pale blue with the sun small in it, and everything is briefly too bright and too far away. Then the cloud comes back from the rim on every side at once and closes, and people are relieved, and do not say so.

That water only moves downhill, and everything here is downhill from something, and so everything is always moving, slowly, toward the mills.

That moss will grow on anything that stops. This is said of machinery and of people.

That the stone is older than anything, and does not care about the town, and will be exactly as it is now when the last screen has rusted through and the last chimney has fallen and the moss has closed over the passages and the wheels have gone still and green in their channels — and that this is not a threat or a grief, only a fact about the place, like the cold, like the drip, like the way the light comes down through several hundred meters of water and arrives grey.

At the far end of the town, past the drying houses, there is a place where the channels converge and the gathered water of the whole plateau is let go — over a lip of iron, down a stone chute, and out past the rim into the cloud, where it falls, and keeps falling, and no sound comes back.
