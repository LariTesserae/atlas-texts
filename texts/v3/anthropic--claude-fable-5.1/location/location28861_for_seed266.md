You come up onto it from below, always. There is no other way to arrive, because the Shelf is the top of everything: the upper flank of a single mountain so broad that from its surface you cannot tell you are on a mountain at all. The slope that brought you here — weeks of switchbacks through cold, wet, ordinary country — flattens somewhere behind you, and the ground simply becomes level, and stays level, and goes on being level to a horizon that is very slightly too close and very slightly curved.

The air is thin. That is the first thing. Your lungs work at it. But the second thing, which takes longer to understand, is that the ground is warm. Not sun-warm; the sun here is a small hard coin in a sky that goes indigo at the zenith and almost black if you tip your head all the way back. The warmth comes up. The whole mass of the mountain is still giving back heat it took in long before there was anyone to stand on it, and the Shelf sits over that heat like a lid on a stove that was turned off a thousand years ago. Stand still and it comes through your boot soles. Lie down at night and you will not be cold, though the stars above you are the sharpest, coldest stars you have ever seen.

There is no water. Whatever water this country once had went down through the rock or up into the black sky a long time ago, and nothing replaced it. There are no clouds, only occasionally a high thin haze of dust that turns the sun the color of brass for an afternoon. There has never been a river here. There are no dry beds. The absence is total and unremarkable, the way it is unremarkable that the sea has no trees.

---

The surface is mineral and it is old. Ages of wind and heat-swelling and night-shrinking have worked it smooth: long low domes of grey-brown stone with a satin finish, spaced by shallow basins floored with a crust that cracks into polygons the size of dinner plates. Where the crust has been broken — by a hoof, by a wheel — the fresh surface underneath is a startling pale yellow, and within a few days it dulls to the same grey-brown as everything else. Sulphur, iron, glass. In places the domes have weathered into standing shapes, and these look carved, and are not.

Everything is rounded. Nothing has an edge. The Shelf is a country that has finished happening. It sits in its own state, and the processes still at work in it — the sun on the stone, the stone on the sky — are so slow that a lifetime here registers as a single frame.

The colors are close to one another: grey, ochre, the dark violet of shadow in thin air, an occasional seam of black glass where something once ran molten and stopped. Distance does not blue things here. There is not enough air to blue them. A ridge sixty miles off is as crisp as a ridge one mile off, only smaller, and this does something to your sense of your own body: you feel enormous and very slow.

---

The towns are far apart. You see one from the next only as a plume — a column of dark smoke that goes straight up for a mile because there is nothing to bend it, and then spreads at some invisible ceiling into a flat grey table. On a clear day you can count five plumes on the horizon. Each plume is a town. Between them there is nothing but the domes and the basins and, moving over them, the herds.

Each town is built around a vent. The Shelf is pocked with them — fissures where the old heat comes up faster, where the ground is not warm but hot, where an egg would cook if there were eggs. The first settlers put their kilns over these vents and the towns grew from the kilns. Now each town is a ring of brick and iron structures around a central works: furnaces, calciners, long sheds where the yellow crust is crushed and roasted and drawn off as acid or as glass or as the fine white powder that goes down the mountain in sealed drums and is worth, per pound, more than anything the lower country produces. The furnaces are fed from the vents. Nothing here is burned. There is nothing to burn. The heat is simply channeled, and the engines that drive the crushers and the compressors run on that heat, turning in slow deliberate strokes, their flywheels as tall as houses.

The buildings are the color of the ground because they are made from it: fired blocks of the local stone, iron that has gone the same ochre as the crust. From a distance a town is invisible except for its plume. Up close it is all pipes — pipes on trestles, pipes running along the ground on sleepers, pipes going out over the plain toward the outlying vents and returning. The trestles have been there so long the wind has worn them into the same satin as the domes.

A town has perhaps three hundred people. They are quiet people. The air makes them so. Sound carries poorly here; a shout goes flat within a hundred yards, a bell is a dull knock. Voices are kept low because raising them is useless, and so the towns have a hushed, library quality, all the vast machinery turning with a soft rhythmic exhalation and hardly a word spoken over it. Between towns, where the rail runs, communication is by light: mirrors in the day, lamps at night, a lamp on a tower answering a lamp on a tower forty miles off across ground where a shout would die at your feet.

---

The rail. One line, gauge wider than anything in the low country, running from town to town in a loop that takes eleven days to complete. The locomotive is a heat engine too, its boiler charged at each vent town and carrying that charge across the intervals. It moves at walking pace. It is very heavy and very quiet and it does not stop for the herds, because the herds part for it, having learned over generations that it does not deviate.

---

The herds are everywhere and they are the strangest thing on the Shelf.

There are no plants here. There have never been plants. And yet the country is full of animals, more animals than the greenest valley below, moving over the domes in loose scattered bands of thirty or a hundred, spaced so widely that you are never out of sight of one and never crowded by any. The commonest are the grazers — low, broad, six-legged things with a shell like weathered brass and a heavy head that they drag along the ground. They eat the crust. They break the grey surface with a bony plate at the front of the jaw and lick up the yellow underneath, and they are drawn most of all to the fresh faces where the crust has been recently cracked, which is why they follow the rail, and why they gather in slow patient rings around the towns where the tailings are dumped.

The people say the animals were here before the towns. The animals, if you watch them long enough, do not seem to distinguish between the towns and the country. A calciner stack is a warm rock. A tailings heap is a rich seam. The animals come into the works at night and lie against the furnace housings for the heat, and in the morning the workers step over them. Nobody drives them off. There is a law against it, though nobody remembers the law being made.

Where the tailings are oldest the grazers are thickest, and the old workers say — quietly, the way everything is said here — that the tailings *become* grazers. That a slag heap left alone for forty or sixty years begins to move. That the heavy plated backs you see out on the plain are heaps that finished. You are not required to believe this. But you will notice that the oldest heaps around the oldest towns have a shape, and that nobody digs into them, and that there are always animals lying against their flanks.

There are other things. Something with a very long neck that stands on the domes at dusk, motionless, faced toward the sun until it sets, and then walks away. Things that live inside the pipe-trestles and are heard only as a ticking. And above all of it the flyers — you would not think anything could fly in air this thin, and they barely do; they have wings twenty and thirty feet across, membranes so fine you see the sun through them, and they do not flap but hang in the rising heat over the vents, circling at great height, coming down in the night to lie in the warm basins with their wings spread flat over the crust like sheets laid out to dry. In the morning they wait for the ground to give them the day's first lift. You can walk right up to one. It will look at you with an eye like a chip of black glass and not move.

---

Some things the Shelf teaches you, if you stay:

That the ground is the only warmth and the sky is the only cold, and everything alive arranges itself between the two. You sleep on the ground. You work with your back to the stone. Nothing lives on the tops of the tall structures; the tops of the stacks are the coldest places in the country, cold enough to hurt, though the base is too hot to touch.

That there is a ceiling. The smoke finds it. The flyers find it. It is perhaps a mile up, and above it the air is a different thing — thinner, colder, moving in a direction the air below does not move. Nobody has been above it and come back with anything to say.

That nothing here is finished quickly. A wall is built to last a thousand years because there is no reason it should not. The kiln you fire was fired by your grandmother's grandmother. The crust you break heals in a week and the mark of your breaking it is gone in a season, but the mark of the rail, which is older than anyone, is a shallow trough worn into the domes where the grazers have followed it for as long as there has been a rail, and this trough will outlast the rail.

That the silence is not empty. Stand still long enough and you hear the machinery, very faint, from the nearest plume: the slow exhalation of the engines, a beat perhaps every four seconds, like the breathing of something very large asleep. And then, under that, something you cannot place at first, and then can — the sound of the herds cracking the crust, a dry small snap repeated across miles of country, thousands of jaws opening the yellow ground, and the sound arriving so weakly and so evenly from every direction that it seems to come from inside your own head.

---

At night the lamps come on — one on each town's tower, and out between them, on the trestles, a lamp every few miles, so that the loop of the rail is drawn in the dark as a ring of dim orange points across a plain you can no longer see. The sky is full of stars to the horizon, and the horizon is close and curved, and the stars go down behind the edge of the Shelf as though the Shelf were the whole world's roof and you had climbed up onto it, which you have.

The ground is warm under you. Somewhere out on the plain a flyer settles, spreading itself flat. Somewhere a heap that has not moved in fifty years shifts by a quarter inch, and in the works nobody notices, because a quarter inch is not the kind of thing anyone here would notice, and because the engines keep their slow time, and there is a great deal of it.
