The first thing to understand is that it is a table, not a mountain. The land does not come to a point. It rises from somewhere far below, through cloud that nobody standing on it has ever seen the underside of, and then simply stops rising and spreads out, flat as a pressed leaf, for a distance that has no practical meaning. Walk toward the edge in any direction and you will not reach it. The horizon is level. The sky behaves as skies do over oceans.

The rock is old basalt, dark and pitted, and it is wet. Not rained on — wet in itself, the way a cold bottle is wet. The air up here is thin and saturated and the stone is always a degree or two colder than the air, so water simply appears on it, gathers in the pits, runs along the fracture lines, and beads off the undersides of overhangs in slow, continuous drips that have been falling for so long they have drilled cups into the ledges below. Every exposed surface weeps. There is nothing dramatic about it. It is the rock's ordinary condition.

But there are very few exposed surfaces. The plateau is covered. That is the second thing to understand: there is no bare ground, anywhere, and there has not been for a very long time. What covers it is a low forest, knee-high to shoulder-high, so dense that it holds a person up if they lean on it. It is not one plant. It is cushion mosses the size of cars, grown so tight they take the weight of an adult without denting; dwarf conifers with trunks as thick as a wrist and forty rings to the centimeter, bent flat by wind that no longer blows the way it did when they were young; tree-ferns whose crowns open exactly at the height of the canopy and no higher; liverworts in sheets; lichens in ruffs and beards and crusts that have themselves been colonized by smaller lichens. Where one organism ends another has already begun. Roots go into moss rather than soil because the soil, such as it is, is a meter of compressed dead moss resting on the basalt, and it is more water than solid.

The forest is cold to the touch and it is quiet in a way that takes some time to notice. The plants absorb sound. Footfalls do not carry. A dripping ledge two meters away is inaudible from the far side of a cushion. What you hear instead, once you have stood still long enough, is a very low hiss — not wind, but the collective transpiration of the whole surface, water leaving billions of leaves at once, so faint it may be imagined. Under that, if you put your ear to the moss, a ticking. That is the sound of the plateau working.

Because the third thing to understand is that the plateau is a machine, in the plain sense of the word: a system that takes in energy and materials at one end and produces something at the other, at scale, without pause, and has done so for long enough that its shape has been entirely determined by the doing.

It works like this. The moss cushions are hollow. Inside each one is a chamber, sometimes several, lined with the plant's own dead tissue, and in these chambers live colonies of pale, soft-bodied grazers about the size of a thumb joint, blind, with mouthparts like planers. They eat the moss from the inside at a rate exactly matched to its growth on the outside, and their waste — which is warm, and slightly acidic, and rich — is drawn back up by the moss through its own tissue. The cushion is therefore a boiler: it takes in cold water and light on the outside, heat and nutrient on the inside, and the two exchange across a wall a few millimeters thick. On cold mornings the cushions steam faintly, all of them together, and the plateau smokes like a field of cooling ingots.

The grazers do not stay in one cushion. There are corridors between them, tubes of chewed material wide enough for the animals and for the things that eat the animals: long, flattened, many-legged predators, dark red, that move through the tunnel system on a schedule so regular that if you cut a cushion open and watched, you could set a clock by the intervals between their passes. They do not hurry. Nothing here hurries. The prey does not flee because the tunnels are the prey's own construction and the predators are part of the maintenance — they cull, they open blocked passages, they carry material from one cushion to the next in their gut. When a predator dies in a tunnel, the moss grows through it in a season.

Above the canopy the work continues in a different medium. The plateau makes its own weather. Warm, wet air rises off the working forest all day and cools in the thin atmosphere and, in the afternoon, a cloud deck assembles perhaps two hundred meters up — a flat, grey, unbroken ceiling that mirrors the flat, green, unbroken floor — and rains gently back down onto the surface that made it. There is no thunder. Occasionally, where the cloud is thickest, a thread of static discharges downward into a tree-fern crown and the fern conducts it, silently, into the moss below, and for a moment a patch of forest the size of a room glows faintly through its own tissue, and then doesn't. The ferns are the tallest things, and this is why: they have grown to precisely the height at which they intercept the current, and no higher, because higher would draw more than they can carry. Their fronds are ribbed with a mineral the plant pulls out of the basalt, and the ribs are cool and slightly metallic and taste of tin.

Fauna is abundant, but you must look for it, because none of it is loud and most of it is inside something. Beyond the tunnel-dwellers, there are grey, fist-sized amphibians that live in the water-cups drilled by the dripping ledges, one animal per cup, each cup a lineage. There are flightless birds, the size of hens, black-feathered and nearly indistinguishable from wet lichen, that walk across the top of the canopy in lines of six or eight, pausing at intervals to drive their beaks through the moss skin into the chambers below and pull out a grazer. They walk the same routes their parents walked; the moss along those routes is a slightly different color. There are moths, at dusk, thousands of them, that do not fly so much as drift, rising on the same warm air that builds the cloud, and are eaten in the cloud by things you cannot see from below — you know only because their wings come down afterward, in the rain, like a fine grey mulch, and the moss takes them in.

Nothing on the plateau has been made. No stone has been shaped or stacked. No path has been cut that was not cut by an animal walking it ten thousand times. And yet the whole surface has the quality of a plant — in the other sense of that word: a works, a facility. Every process feeds a process. Every output is somebody's input. The rock weeps into the moss, the moss feeds the grazers, the grazers heat the moss, the moss steams into the cloud, the cloud rains on the rock, the ferns bleed the sky's charge into the whole circuit so that nothing seizes. The timing has been worn smooth. There is no slack in it and no waste, and there hasn't been for so long that the very weathering of the basalt — the pits, the drilled cups, the fracture lines that channel the seep — has taken on the form of the process rather than the process taking the form of the ground.

There are places where the surface has failed. Not many. A cushion, somewhere, will die, and its chamber will collapse, and for a while there is a hollow in the canopy with grey basalt at the bottom, weeping, ringed by the raw edges of its neighbors. These are the only places you can stand on stone. They are cold and they smell of iron and the drip is suddenly audible. And from the rim, if you kneel, you can watch the neighbors moving in — not fast, but visibly, over days, closing the gap with a kind of patience that is easy to mistake for indifference and is in fact the opposite.

Then it is sealed, and the plateau is whole again, and the hiss resumes, and the cloud forms in the afternoon, and the black birds come along their route and pause and drive their beaks down and lift their heads, and nothing at all has changed, at any scale you could measure, in any direction you could go.
