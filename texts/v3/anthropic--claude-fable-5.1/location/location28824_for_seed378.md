The first thing is the whiteness. Not snow — you would think snow, from the rim, looking down into the basin from the pass, but the air coming up at you is hot and heavy and smells of eggs and wet iron, and nothing that white could be cold in air like that.

The basin sits at altitude, a broad shallow bowl maybe fourteen kilometres across, a collapsed thing, an old caldera whose floor has since been paved over by its own water. Everything below the rim is sinter and travertine: mineral laid down by hot springs, one thin skin at a time, over a period the geologists have stopped trying to estimate. The floor of the basin is a terraced plain of it, chalk-white and cream and a pale sulphur yellow, stepping down in lobes and aprons toward a central lake that is not so much a lake as the place where the terraces run out of height. The water is everywhere. It rises through the floor in ten thousand places, most of them no bigger than a thumb, and flows across the top of the plain in a sheet a centimetre deep, so that the whole basin gleams as though varnished. Where it pools it is turquoise and near-boiling. Where it runs it is clear and merely scalding. At the lake edge, kilometres from the vents, it has cooled to something you could put a hand in for a count of three.

Nothing green grows here. The ground is too hot for roots, the water too mineral, the air too full of sulphur; whatever seeds blow in over the rim land on stone that is slowly being buried and are buried with it. What lives on the surface instead is the colour of the surface: mats of thermophile bacteria in the runnels, orange and rust and a deep blood-red where the water is hottest, fading through yellow to a grey-green in the cooler channels, like a temperature map painted directly onto the ground. They are the base of everything. They are the closest thing to a forest.

The light is the second thing. The basin is high and the sky above it is thin and very blue and the sun comes down through it with nothing to soften it, and hits the white floor, and comes back up. There are no shadows to speak of except under built things. The steam does not shade; it brightens. It rises off the pools and the runnels and the lake in a continuous low haze, never quite fog, never quite clear, and the light gets into it and stays there, so that the whole floor of the basin seems to be lit from within as well as from above. People who work here wear smoked glass over their eyes and still go partially blind by forty. The air is so saturated that it condenses on any surface cooler than itself — on your face, on the undersides of the pipe-runs, on the flanks of the buildings — and drips. It is not raining. It never rains here, quite. The basin simply sweats.

The town has no single centre because the ground will not permit one.

Under the white crust the plain is honeycombed with hot water, and the crust is thin in places and thick in others and the pattern changes over decades as vents seal and reopen. So the settlement — it is called the Works, or the Pans, or just the Basin, depending on who you ask — is spread across the plain in a wide scatter of separate installations, each built on a knob of old dense travertine that has tested stable, each connected to the others by raised timber walkways on iron piles that have been re-driven so many times the piles are more mineral than iron. From the rim you can see them all at once: the condensing towers, the evaporation pans, the pump-houses, the long low sheds of the drying floors, the dormitory blocks with their tin roofs gone white with scale. Between them, a great deal of nothing. Steaming white nothing, shot through with orange channels, and the walkways threading across it like stitching.

The industry is extraction. The water coming up through the floor is loaded with dissolved salts — borates, lithium, a dozen others — and the Works exist to get them out. The method is old and mostly passive: water is drawn off the hottest vents through iron mains and led downhill into shallow lined pans, acres of them, where the heat of the ground itself does the evaporating, and what remains is scraped up and carted to the drying floors and bagged. The steam from the vents also drives the pumps, through low-pressure reciprocating engines that were installed by the grandfathers of the current engineers and have never needed replacing because there is nothing here to corrode them faster than they can be re-scaled. There is a small turbine hall that makes electricity for the lamps and the telegraph. It is the only thing in the basin that hums, and you have to be inside it to hear it.

That is the third thing, and it takes a while to notice: the quiet.

You would expect a place like this to roar. Vents, steam, engines. But the vents here do not roar. The pressure is low and constant and the water comes up through a metre of porous stone that smothers it; a big vent makes a sound like breathing, and most of them make no sound at all. The engines run at eleven strokes a minute behind stone walls. The pumps are below ground. Steam escapes through the relief valves not in a shriek but a sigh, a long exhale that is over before you have turned your head. The walkways are timber and the workers wear soft-soled boots because hobnails eat through the boards, and so even footsteps are muted. And the air itself — hot, wet, thick — seems to swallow sound before it can travel. Two men talking on a walkway sixty metres away are visible in every detail and completely inaudible. You see the shape of their mouths.

Nobody in the Works raises their voice. It is not a rule. It is simply that nobody ever has, because it has never been necessary to compete with anything, and after a generation or two it has become a kind of manners. Orders are given by hand-signal across the pans. The shift-change is signalled by a lamp on the turbine hall going from white to red, and the whole scattered town watches for it. New hires are known immediately by how loud they are, and they either quiet down within a season or they leave.

There is a great deal alive in the basin, and none of it makes a sound either.

Start with the channels. In the cooler runnels, downstream of the bacterial mats, the water is full of a small pale crustacean, a kind of shrimp with no eyes and a transparent shell, that grazes on the mats and is present in such numbers that the water in the lower channels is visibly cloudy with them. They are the second link. Everything else eats them. There are flatworms, and a red-bodied annelid that lives in the sinter pores and comes out at dusk, and a small dark salamander-like thing with a frilled neck, the only vertebrate that lives in the water itself, which the workers call a stoker and which is in fact not an amphibian at all but something older, something with a bony plate under its skin that no one has classified because no one from a university has come up here since the telegraph was installed.

Then the birds. The basin is on a migration route and the birds have learned it. Long-legged wading things, pale grey, herons or something like herons, that stand motionless in the cooler pools with their feet on the threshold of what they can bear and strike at the shrimp with a movement so fast you only see the afterimage. There are hundreds of them. They are the most conspicuous thing in the basin after the buildings — you can see them from the rim, white on white, tall and still and scattered across the whole floor at intervals of thirty or forty metres, each in its own pool, none of them close to another. They do not call. Whatever species they are elsewhere, here they do not call; the workers say the hot air ruins their voices, but more likely there is simply nothing to call about, no forest to be lost in, no need to find one another when you can see every other bird in the basin from where you stand. When they fly, which is rarely, they go up in total silence, and the shadow they cast is the darkest thing on the plain.

There are lizards on the pipe-runs. Flat, grey-white, scaled like the sinter itself, basking on the iron mains where the metal is hot enough to blister skin. They eat a kind of fly that breeds in the drying sheds. There is something that lives in the lake — larger, never seen whole, a back that surfaces occasionally at the far end where the water is coolest and the steam thickest, and that the workers do not discuss and do not fish for. There are bats, or things shaped like bats, that come at dusk out of cracks in the rim and hunt low over the pans. And around every building, in the warm damp shadow underneath where the condensation drips, there is a slow crust of something that is not quite a fungus and not quite a mineral, that grows on the pilings in pale frilled shelves and that has to be scraped off twice a year or it will lift a shed off its footings.

None of this is hostile. None of it is even particularly interested in the people. It is an ecosystem that has been in its own equilibrium for a very long time and that has absorbed the Works into itself the way it absorbs everything, as one more surface for the water to run over and the minerals to build on.

---

Because that is the rule here, if there is one. The basin is an accretion engine. Every surface exposed to the water gains a skin of stone, and the water is on every surface. The walkways are re-planked every eight years because the boards go from wood to something like ivory and then crack. The pipe-mains are half a metre across on the outside and a hand's-breadth on the inside and have to be reamed. The buildings acquire drip-fringes under their eaves, white stalactites that lengthen and thicken until they become columns and have to be broken off. Anything left in the open — a wheelbarrow, a shovel, a boot — is white in a month and stone in a decade. The graveyard, on a stable knob out toward the eastern rim, has no headstones, because the ground makes its own: the dead are laid on the surface under a cairn of loose sinter and the water does the rest, and after a generation there is a smooth white mound with a shape faintly inside it.

The Works fight this constantly and lose slowly, and have made a peace with losing. The whole town is in motion, at a geological pace. Vents die and the pans downstream of them go dry and are abandoned and new pans are cut where new vents have come up. The walkways are re-routed. A dormitory block that stood for sixty years in the north of the basin is now a white hump with a chimney sticking out of it and the shrimp have colonised the runnel that flows through what was its kitchen. There are older humps than that, further out toward the rim, with square corners under their rounded skins, that nobody remembers building. There are things under the sinter that were here before the Works and that are being unmade and rebuilt into stone at the same slow rate as everything else, and the workers walk over them without knowing, because that is what walking here means.

And above it all, the light. The endless bright wet glare off the white floor, the steam lit from within, the herons standing at their intervals, the grey lizards on the hot iron, the far pale hump of the turbine hall with its one lamp, white or red. The condensation ticking off the eaves. Eleven strokes a minute behind a stone wall. A man on a distant walkway, raising one hand, and another man, further off, raising his in answer, and nothing, nothing at all, to hear.
