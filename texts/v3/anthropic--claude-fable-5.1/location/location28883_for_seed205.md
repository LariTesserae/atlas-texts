You arrive on it the way you arrive on a frozen lake: uncertain, at first, whether you are on ground at all.

Underfoot is a surface like cork, or like the pad of an enormous foot — faintly springy, ridged in long shallow swells, the colour of old tobacco leaf shading toward rust. It is warm. Not sun-warm; the warmth is coming *up*, through the soles, steady as a body's. Where the ridges crack, the cracks show a paler interior, fibrous, damp, and if you kneel and put your hand into one you feel it breathing — a slow push of hot wet air, then a pause, then a draw.

There is no soil anywhere. There is no rock. There is only this, in every direction, for as far as the haze allows you to see, and beyond that, and beyond that.

---

The plateau is one organism. Nobody who lives here says so, because it would be like saying the sky is one sky; but it is. It has been growing for longer than there have been eyes to see it, piling its own dead layers beneath its living skin until it lifted itself two thousand metres above whatever it first rooted in, a continent of rind and pith and slow ascending sap. Its edges — if it has edges — are somewhere beyond a horizon that visibly curves.

The height is why the air is thin and why the light comes down hard and white and undiluted, and the organism is why the air is hot. It burns its own sugar. The whole surface runs a fever, a few degrees above blood, and in the middle of the day when the sun lies on top of that fever the heat becomes something you wade through. Walking any distance, you learn to keep to the shade of the verticals, which are few.

---

The verticals are what break the plain, and they are very far apart. You will walk for an hour toward one, watching it not grow.

Some are stalks — grey-green columns thirty or forty metres high, unbranched, slightly bulbous at the crown, standing alone like the last posts of a fence that once enclosed something. Some are vents: low craters of thickened rind, three or four hundred paces across, whose centres are open black throats. Some are cups: shallow bowls of glossy living tissue, the only surfaces on the plateau that are not dry, holding a skin of water that never quite evaporates because the cup is cooler than everything around it, and the cup is cooler because it is where the organism sweats.

Between them: emptiness. Ridged, warm, tobacco-coloured emptiness, unmarked except by the wind-polished trails of things that have walked the same lines for a very long time.

It is the emptiest place you will ever be in that is also, at every instant, completely full.

---

Because the sound. You noticed the sound the moment you arrived and assumed it was wind, and then the wind dropped and the sound did not.

It comes up through the feet before it comes in through the ears. A roar with grain in it, like surf heard from inside a shell, like a city of engines under a floor. Stand still and it separates into layers — a low continuous drone that never varies; above that a chittering, ticking, rasping mass that swells and ebbs in waves that sweep across the plain faster than any animal could run; above that, sometimes, individual sounds: a sawing, a hollow knock, a long descending whistle from somewhere inside the rind that makes the hair stand up on your arms.

None of it is the plant. The plant is silent. The plant is a roof.

Beneath the roof, in the metres and metres of pith and fibre and old dead wood, in the sap channels and the gas chambers and the drowned galleries where condensation pools in the dark, the organism is inhabited the way a reef is inhabited. Borers with jaws the colour of amber. Blind pale things that swim the sap. Colonies that farm fungus in the rind's dead layers and sing to keep their bearings in the dark, all of them, all at once. Tens of millions of them under each square kilometre, and the plateau has a million square kilometres, and their sound is the constant weather of the place. There is no silence here. People born here have a word for the roar that means, approximately, *the air*; they have no word for its absence, because they have never met one.

It does things to you. After a day the roar stops being heard and starts being a pressure. After three days you find you have begun to speak with your hands.

---

Some of what lives inside comes out.

The vents are how you know. Walk toward a vent in the last hour of the afternoon, when the surface fever peaks and the interior can no longer hold its breath, and you will see the black throat begin to smoke. It is not smoke. It is a column of animals — winged, glittering, each the size of a thumbnail — pouring upward out of the plant in a stream so dense it has its own shadow, twisting like a rope in the updraft, half a kilometre high before the wind bends it over and pulls it out across the plain in a slow dark ribbon. The sound of the emergence is a shriek laid over the drone. The air around a venting throat is unbreathable, thick with bodies; the ground for a hundred metres is greased with the ones that fell.

Out on the plain, the ribbon comes apart. The swarm settles, mates, dies. By morning the rind in that direction is dusted with a shining crust of wings, and the crust is already being eaten by the things that come out at night.

Those are larger. You hear them before you see them — a wet clicking that moves through the drone — and then in the last light you see a shape unfolding from a crack you would have sworn was too narrow, an arch of jointed dun-coloured plates as long as a man is tall, then another arch behind it, then legs. They graze the fallen swarm. They graze the rind itself, planing off the outer dead layer with mouths like drawknives, and this is the second sound of the night, a long continuous rasp coming from every direction, as if the whole plateau were being sanded down. They are not aggressive. They do not seem to see well. If you stand still they will walk over your feet.

And then, very rarely, something else: a shadow passing over the plain in the middle of the day, too large and too slow to be cloud, that keeps its own darkness with it as it moves and is followed, at a distance, by a rippling change in the pitch of the drone, as though every buried thing beneath its path had drawn one collective breath.

Nobody has told you what that is. Nobody is going to.

---

The water is stingy and specific.

The organism exhales through the vents and through a billion invisible pores in the rind, and its breath is wet, and above the plateau the breath gathers. By late morning there is always a low ceiling of cloud, grey-white, close enough to feel, moving with the surface winds in long rolls. It rains from this ceiling, but not often, and not everywhere; the rain falls in isolated columns you can see approaching from twenty kilometres off, a dark shaft joining sky to ground, moving at walking pace, wetting a swath a few hundred metres wide and then passing on. The rind darkens where it falls, drinks, and is dry again in an hour. Nothing runs off. There is nowhere for it to run.

The real water is the dew. The plateau cools at night — not much, the fever keeps it tepid — but the stalks and the vent rims and any raised thing stand proud of the fever and cool further, and on their shadowed flanks toward dawn the water comes out of the air and beads and runs. Every vertical is a well for an hour a day. Every cup, the shallow sweating bowls, holds its skin of water permanently, and the water in the cups is faintly sweet and faintly warm and tastes like the inside of a green stem.

Under the rind, in the dark, there is more water than the whole surface will ever see. You can hear it, sometimes, when the drone thins: a dripping, an echo of something pouring.

---

People live here. Not many. You can walk for days and see no sign of them, and then you will come over a swell and there, in the lee of a stalk, will be a house.

It is made of the plant. Everything is made of the plant, because there is nothing else. The frame is the tough grey outer casing shed by the stalks, which peels off in long curved staves; the roof is layered husk, the papery sheaths that fall from the crowns; the walls are woven from the fibre of the pith, which pulls out in strands like coarse hair and can be soaked in cup-water and twisted and dried into cord that holds. The whole thing is lashed together — no nails, nothing metal anywhere. It stands off the surface on short legs, because the surface is warm and the surface is walked over at night by things with mouths like drawknives.

The tools are resin and bone. Resin comes from the cracks — the organism seals its wounds with a clear amber gum that hardens in a day to something that takes an edge and holds it. The people prise it up in slabs, warm it over a fire of dead husk, shape it, let it set. Bone comes from what emerges. A knife with a resin blade and a handle wrapped in pith-cord. A scraper for the rind. Long hooks for pulling grubs from the cracks, which is most of what they eat, along with the swarm when it falls, and the sap when they can reach it, which they do by driving a hollow stalk-spine into a soft place and waiting.

They cut into the plant for shelter as well. In the flank of every large vent rim there is usually a hollow, a room-sized cavity where someone has chipped away the dead outer layers down to the living fibre, which is cooler and which sweats. The walls of these rooms are marked — handprints, tally lines, the outlines of the large arched grazers. Some of the marks are so old the plant has half-healed over them, a glaze of new rind flowing across a drawing like ice over a leaf.

The people are quiet with their mouths and loud with their hands. They can hear — but the drone has taught them that the voice does not carry, and they have built their language in gesture and touch and in short hard percussive sounds made by striking resin on rind, which cut through the roar better than any word. A child learns to knock before it learns to speak. Two people conversing at a distance look like they are fighting, or dancing, or both.

They know where the vents will smoke and when. They know the shadow that passes over and they know not to be under it. They do not think of the plateau as alive in the way you would mean it, because to them its aliveness is not an interesting fact; it is simply the condition, the way the ground is, the way the air roars. They have a word for the whole of it that is the same as their word for *the place one stands*.

---

It is old, and it has settled.

You can read the age in the cracks. Where the rind has split deep enough you can see the layering — the living skin, and under it a hand's breadth of recently dead, and under that a darker denser stratum, and under that another, down and down past where the light goes. Each layer was once the surface. Each layer was once walked over and rained on and grazed and emerged from. The plant does not grow up so much as it grows *out over itself*, laying down a new skin each long cycle, and everything that was on the old skin — trails, husks, the fallen swarms, the marks people made — is sealed in and carried down into the dark.

Somewhere in the deep layers, kilometres below, there are house-frames of stave and husk that the plant swallowed whole. There are the drawings of the grazers. There are the bones of whatever drew them.

But it is not growing quickly anymore. It reached, long ago, the height at which the air is too thin and the cold at night too sharp for it to profit from another metre, and the wear now roughly matches the growth. The grazers plane it down; it lays itself back. The wind polishes the swells; the swells renew. The vents that smoke today have smoked for longer than the people can count, and the people cannot count very far, and do not see why they should. Everything here has already happened many times. The plateau is not becoming anything. It is being what it is, at length.

---

Toward evening the light goes amber and then rust, the colour of the rind, so that the ground and the sky are briefly the same and the stalks stand between them like black seams. The ceiling of cloud breaks up. Heat pours off the surface into the thin air in visible ripples. The drone deepens as the interior wakes.

Far off — a day's walk, two — a vent begins to smoke, and the column climbs and bends and lays itself across the sunset. Nearer, the first arched shape unfolds from a crack and begins to rasp. A knock rings out from the lee of a stalk, and another answers it: someone saying to someone else that the night is starting, that the ground is warm, that everything is as it was.

You lie down on the rind, because there is nowhere else to lie. It gives a little under you. It is exactly the temperature of your own back. Through your spine you feel the roar of the inhabited dark, layer upon layer of it, going down farther than you want to think about, and above you the sky clears and the stars come out enormous and strange in the thin air, and somewhere under your shoulder, very faintly, something is singing to keep its bearings, and somewhere under that, water is falling into water.
