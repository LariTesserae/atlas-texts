The first thing is that the ground is not ground. What you stand on, if you stand anywhere, is root — a braid of grey-barked root as wide as a road, slick with a film of green, and under it another root, and under that the mountain, which nobody has seen in living memory. The whole shoulder of the ridge has been taken by a single strangler that has long outlived whatever it once strangled. Its trunk is not a trunk but a hollow lattice a hundred feet across, a cage of fused stems with daylight showing through in places, and the cage is full of houses.

It is cool up here. The cloud comes through the lattice at intervals — not fog exactly, more like the air deciding to be water for a while — and everything it touches sweats. Bark sweats. Woven walls sweat. The undersides of the big leaves gather drops until the drops are too heavy and then they fall all at once, a small clatter, and the leaf springs back. There is no such thing as dry. There is only *less wet*, and the settlement is built around the pursuit of it: houses tucked under the overhang of the larger limbs, floors of split cane raised on pegs so the water runs beneath, roofs of overlapping palm that are less roofs than shingles of living frond still attached to their stems, trained down over a frame and lashed there while they grow. The houses drink. In the season when the tree pushes new leaves the houses get slightly larger.

Nothing here is joined with metal. The people have stone for splitting, bone for awls, and cordage — endless cordage, twisted from the bark of the tree's own aerial roots, which they harvest by cutting the tip so the root forks and then, years later, taking one fork and leaving the other. The lashings on the oldest platforms have been renewed so many times that the wood beneath has a smooth channel worn in it, a groove that fits the cord exactly, like a riverbed. Ladders are grooved by hands. The stone steps cut into the buttress roots — the only stone in the place, carried up from somewhere below generations ago — have hollows in them shaped like heels.

And it is loud. It is loud the way a waterfall is loud, so that after a while you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as weather.

The tree is fruiting.

It does this twice a year and when it does, everything on the mountain that eats comes to it, and the settlement, which exists precisely because of this, becomes something between a harvest and a siege. The fruit is small, purple-red, borne in clusters directly from the bark so that whole limbs look upholstered. It is ripening unevenly, as figs do, in waves that move around the crown following the light, and where the wave is, the birds are. Not a flock. A weight. Green pigeons so densely packed on a branch that the branch dips and they spill off the end in a sheet and land again further along. Barbets hammering their calls without pause, a hundred of them, so the sound has no gaps. Hornbills arriving in twos and threes with a noise of rushing air from their wings that you feel in your chest before you see them. Squirrels that are almost black. A troop of grey monkeys with faces like old men has taken a whole quadrant of the crown and defends it with shrieking, and the shrieking is answered from the other side by a second troop, and the two have been at it since dawn.

Underneath the birds, in the bromeliads that cluster in every fork like green cups, are frogs — small ones, brown, one to a cup — and the frogs are calling, a ticking that comes from every direction at once, and in the water in the cups are their eggs and the larvae of something that eats the eggs, and on the rims of the cups are snails the size of a fingernail moving through the algae. Flies rise from the dropped fruit in clouds that shift shape. Ants run the roots in two-way traffic, columns going out empty and coming back carrying purple. Beetles, moths in the shadows with wings like bark, a slow snake wrapped in a root crotch waiting for a frog to make a mistake. Bats will come at dusk in such numbers that the people close the leaf-shutters, not against the bats but against what the bats let fall.

The people are in it. They are on the limbs with long cane hooks, pulling fruit down into baskets slung under them; they are on the ground, which is root, gathering what the birds knock loose before the ants get it; they are shouting at each other across the hollow — *this side, this side is ready, leave that, the monkeys have it* — and their shouting is one more voice in the noise. Children run the low walkways with hand-slings, not to kill the pigeons but to move them, to push the mass of birds off one limb and onto another so the harvesters can get in. Where the fruit is thickest a kind of market has formed on a platform of lashed cane: baskets being emptied into bigger baskets, fruit being sorted by ripeness on mats, the split ones going into a trough where a woman is mashing them with a wooden paddle for a paste that will keep, the whole ones going into cane cylinders lined with leaf. Someone is arguing about weight. Someone is trading a stone blade for a share. A hornbill has landed on the edge of the platform, the size of a dog, and is regarding the sorting mats with obvious intent, and nobody moves to stop it because it is a hornbill and you don't.

Then the cloud comes through again and the light goes soft and grey and everything is briefly wet in a new way, and the birds go quiet for perhaps four seconds — a strange hole in the noise — and then it starts again, and while it was quiet a limb somewhere high up dropped an entire cluster at once and the fruit is still coming down through the leaves in a pattering that sounds like the beginning of rain.

It is a settled place. You can tell by what has been worn smooth and what has been worn away. The tree has been fruiting for longer than there have been people to harvest it; the people have been harvesting for longer than the oldest lashing; the birds have been coming for longer than either. The balance holds because nothing in it is trying to win. The tree makes more fruit than any of them can take. The people take some, the pigeons take some, the monkeys take some and waste more, the ants take what falls, the frogs take the flies that come to what the ants leave. The wood of the dead host, whatever it was, has rotted entirely away inside the lattice and become soil, and things grow in that soil in the dim interior of the cage — ferns, a pale orchid, a second small fig that will someday be a problem.

The people have a saying, which is not really a saying so much as an instruction, and it is the first thing they teach: *don't stand under a fruiting limb with your mouth open.* It means several things.

By afternoon the harvest platform is slick with pulp and the baskets are full and the ripening wave has moved around to the west face where the crown catches the last of the light, and everyone — birds, monkeys, people — has moved with it, so the east side, where the morning was, is now nearly empty, dripping, littered with skins, worked over by ants. The frogs are still going. The frogs have been going the whole time. Tomorrow the wave will be somewhere else and everything will go there. The tree does not have a schedule. It has a tendency, and the settlement has learned to live inside the tendency the way the houses live inside the lattice, taking the shape they're given, sweating, holding on.
