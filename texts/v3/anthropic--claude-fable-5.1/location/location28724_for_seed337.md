Start from above, because that is how you arrive: walking across a plateau of pale gray limestone, flat, thinly grassed, and then the ground simply stops. Not a cliff — a rim. Below it, the plateau has been taken away in blocks, and where it was there is a city.

The pit is the size of a valley. Its floor is perhaps forty meters down at the deepest point, shallower toward the edges, so that the whole thing has the profile of a shallow bowl that someone is still deepening from the middle outward. There is no rubble slope, no talus, no soil. Every surface is cut. The walls descend in right angles — benches, risers, terraces, the negative shapes of blocks that are now somewhere else. The stone is the color of new bone, faintly gold where the sun reaches it, and it has the particular unweathered sharpness of something split rather than worn. Rain has not had time to soften a single corner. Every edge would cut a hand.

This is the thing that takes time to understand about the place: it is not a quarry that people moved into. The city and the quarry are the same act. People here live in what they remove. A room is made by cutting a room-shaped block out of the floor; the block is then lifted, in pieces, to become the walls of a room higher up, on the bench above, where someone else's excavation has already left a flat place to build on. So the city sinks and rises at once. The lower you go, the newer the cuts, and the more the architecture is simply absence — a doorway that is a slot in living rock, a bed that is a ledge that was never anything else. Near the rim, the buildings are made of stacked block, mortared with lime slurry, three and four stories of it, and these are the oldest parts, though nothing here is old. Nothing here has been finished long enough to be old.

It is packed. That is the word. There is no empty ground anywhere in the bowl. Where there is not a cut, there is a wall; where there is not a wall, there is a stair, a drain, a block waiting on rollers, a pile of stone chips swept into a corner and already being used as fill. Households share the un-cut stone between them, so that a family's wall is also the raw mass their neighbor is slowly working into a second room. Walkways run along the tops of these party masses, a meter wide, with no railings, and from any point on them you can look down into a dozen roofless chambers at once, each at a different stage — one just scored with the outline of what it will become, one half-lowered, one occupied, with a mat and a jar and a smoke stain on the wall.

And yet it is quiet.

You expect noise from a city that is entirely under construction, and there is none, or very little, and the reason is the method. There are a few copper chisels here, soft and precious and constantly re-hammered, but most of the cutting is done without metal. A line is scored with a hard pebble and a straightedge. Along the line, a row of slots is worked a hand deep with stone points — patient, tapping work, a sound like someone knocking softly on a door in another room. Into the slots go wedges of dry wood. Then water is poured on the wedges and the people go away. Overnight the wood swells. Sometime before dawn the stone gives, with a sound that carries through the whole bowl — a single dull report, like a heavy door closing in the ground — and in the morning there is a new face, clean and slightly damp, and a block lying free, and a family looking at the slightly larger room they now have.

So the city grows at the pace of wood taking up water. Nobody hurries because hurrying is not possible. The loudest regular sound is the dawn cracking, three or four reports a morning from different quarters, and people lie in their ledges and count them and know roughly whose.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. It rains here in short passes, most afternoons in the cool half of the year, and every cut is a basin; the bowl would fill if it were not also a machine for draining. Channels run along the bottom of every wall, cut first, before anything else, because a room that floods is a room lost. They step down from bench to bench in tidy cascades that make no more sound than a tap left dripping, and gather in a long sump at the deepest point of the excavation, which is also, therefore, the newest part of the city and the part nobody lives in yet. The sump is a pool the length of a street, absolutely clear, pale green over the fresh stone. Everyone's water comes from it. In the mornings a haze sits over it and drifts up through the lower terraces and beads on every horizontal surface, so that the city wakes wet even when it hasn't rained, and the stone darkens and then pales again over the course of the day as the sun moves across the bowl. In shadow it stays dark. Certain walls have never been dry.

Where the seepage is steady — along a seam in the rock that runs diagonally through the western wall, weeping continuously — the only wild plants have taken hold: a thin green film of algae, and in a few of the older slots a fern no longer than a finger. That is all the unbidden vegetation the place has. It is too new. Lichen needs decades; this stone has had months. What else grows is carried down on purpose: soil hauled in baskets from the plateau and packed into cut troughs along the sunlit southern terraces, where people grow beans and a bitter green and a few stunted fig trees that lean toward the light. From above, these beds are the only color in the bowl that is not gray-gold or the dark of wet stone.

Animals are rare and specific. Small birds — a kind of martin — have found the niches left where wedge-slots were cut and never used, and nest in them by the dozen along the upper benches, so that at dusk there is a brief soft turbulence of wings and then nothing. Something with a long tail lives in the sump and eats whatever falls in. Occasionally a fox comes down from the plateau by the ramp on the eastern side, walks the terraces at night on the party walls as if it owned them, and leaves. There is nothing else. There has not been time for anything else.

At the rim, the plateau grass stops at a sharp line and the stone begins, and if you stand there in the late afternoon you can see the whole system at work: the shadow of the western wall sliding across the floor and the stone darkening after it, the drains threading their small water down toward the green pool, a block moving very slowly along a terrace on wooden rollers with four people leaning on it, someone on a lower bench pouring water from a jar onto a row of wooden pegs and then straightening up and walking away, done for the day. Smoke from thirty cooking fires going straight up in the still air of the bowl and then bending all together where the wind crosses the rim.

Tonight, in the dark, three or four times, the ground will make its sound, and the city will be very slightly deeper by morning, and very slightly taller, and still — for a long time yet — not finished.
