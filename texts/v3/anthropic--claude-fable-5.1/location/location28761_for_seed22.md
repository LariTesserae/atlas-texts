You hear it long before there is anything to see. The sound arrives across the old country as a pressure rather than a noise, a thing that leans on the chest, and the people who live within a day's walk of the edge have stopped noticing that they shout. Closer, it resolves. Closer still, it stops resolving and becomes weather.

The plain is new. That is the first fact of it. Whatever was here before — sea floor, marsh, a drowned river delta, the accounts disagree — was lifted or replaced or simply *decided* a few generations ago, and the stone has not had time to learn how to be ground. It comes up in broad low shelves, dark grey with a faint green tarnish, and every edge is a fracture edge. Nothing has rounded. Nothing has slumped. The shelves step down toward a horizon that never quite arrives, each step a few meters, each lip sharp enough to open a boot. Water has begun to find the cracks but has not yet agreed on where to go; it pools in the fresh joints, black and still, and in the mornings it sweats back out of the rock as the warm stone meets cooler air. The whole plain runs a little above blood-heat from below. Rain comes in short vertical columns you can watch cross the country from a long way off, and where it falls the stone hisses and steams and the pools brim, and then it is gone and the damp is left hanging.

Between the steps, almost nothing. This is the second fact. The plain is the size of a province and it is nearly empty. You can walk for an hour and the only things that change are the angle of the light and the pitch of the sound. The light is low — not evening, exactly, but a kind of permanent late afternoon under a sky that is banded. Long horizontal stripes of suspended dust hang at fixed altitudes, held there, not drifting, and the sun comes through them the colour of weak tea. The bands are where the sound is loudest. You learn this quickly: look up and see the dust standing in its shelves, and know that the roar is doing that.

Because the roar is the third fact, and the fact that explains the others.

It is not a machine sound, or not one machine. It is a chord — a sustained, enormous, many-voiced tone that shifts by fractions over hours the way a glacier shifts, and under it a floor of sub-bass you feel in your teeth and your knees and the fluid of your eyes. At its centre it is something past a hundred and thirty decibels and it does not stop. It comes from the nodes.

The nodes are the presences. They stand kilometres apart on a grid so regular that from any one you can see the next three as dark verticals against the striped sky, and beyond them, the suggestion of more. Each is the size of a small town stood on end. They are not buildings and not trees and not quite either's opposite. They began as something planted — a seed the size of a cart, the stories say, pressed into the fresh stone at the survey mark — and what has come up is a column of fibrous living material, ridged and bark-like at the base, broadening as it rises into galleries and shelves and long cantilevered arms that should not hold their own weight and do. Vegetation crowds every horizontal surface of them: broad glossy leaves, hanging root-curtains that drip constantly, moss so thick it is a landscape of its own, flowering things the size of doors. From a distance each node is a green cloud on a dark stalk. Up close it is a city block that is also alive and growing at a rate you can nearly watch.

They are growing *because* of the sound. This is the part the engineers who come to look at it cannot put into equations, or can, but the equations do not describe anything they recognise. The nodes sing. The song holds the air in standing waves across the entire plain — the dust bands are the visible edges of it — and inside that field, matter behaves as if it has been asked politely. The columns extrude upward along lines of pressure. Their arms reach toward neighbouring nodes along antinodes, and where two arms meet, kilometres apart and hundreds of meters up, they fuse, and the fused span thickens, and traffic — of what, it is not yet clear — will one day cross it. Water is lifted out of the pools in fine visible threads and carried up the columns without pipes. Stone dust from the still-cracking ground is drawn up into the bands and rains down at the nodes as a slow grey nourishment. The whole plain is a workshop and the roar is the tool, and the tool is also the worker, and the worker is also the thing being made.

No one lives here yet. That is the fourth fact, and it is the one that makes the place feel the way it does. It is a city under construction with a population that can be counted on two hands — a handful of tenders in acoustic shelters at the plain's edge, checking things that cannot be checked from inside. The nodes are hollow. Walk into the base of one through the arch that has grown itself an arch, and the interior is a vast dim shaft of green light and dripping and vibration, ribbed like a throat, spiralling up out of sight, and it is *made for something*, corridors and landings and voids the right proportions to be rooms, and all of it empty. Your footsteps do not exist. Your voice does not exist. The sound has taken the whole spectrum where voices live and nothing else can fit.

And yet the plain is full of animals. This is the contradiction that the place holds most easily. They came on their own. Nothing was seeded but the nodes, and within a decade of the first tone the air was thick with things that had never lived anywhere else — or had lived elsewhere, badly, and found here what they were for.

Most of them fly, or float. The standing waves hold small bodies as readily as dust, and there are creatures the size of a hand, membranous and translucent, that have simply stopped flying and lie in the pressure bands like fish in a current, thousands of them in each stripe, drifting slowly in the sky's slow rotation, feeding on the lifted water and whatever rides in it. Below them, in the wide near-silent volumes between bands — the node-gaps, where the pressure cancels and the roar drops to something merely enormous — flocks of dark, long-winged things that would be birds if their wings were not so obviously built to hear. They have no eyes to speak of. Their heads are all ear, cupped and folded, and they navigate the field the way a sailor reads swell, and they hunt the floaters at the band edges where the pressure gradient tumbles small bodies out into open air. Watching them work is watching a mathematics happen. They fold and drop and the floaters scatter and the field catches everything that was not eaten and puts it back.

On the ground, in the fresh cracks and at the dripping bases of the nodes, things that are deaf. Everything down here went deaf a long time ago or was born that way. Broad, flat, many-legged grazers cropping the moss in the perpetual rain-shadow of the columns, their carapaces vibrating visibly. Something like a heron, though its bill is soft and it feeds by feel in the black pools, standing motionless for hours while the ground hums under it. Small quick climbers in the root-curtains, moving up and down the columns in lines, taking the flowers, taking the fruit that the nodes have started to produce in the last few years without anyone having asked them to. The grazers are followed by things that follow grazers. At dusk — the deeper dusk, the one that arrives on top of the ambient dimness — the climbers go still and the low things move out from wherever they spend the day, and the pools ripple with something under the surface that has never been clearly seen and is, by the shape of the ripples, large.

The dampness runs through all of it. The nodes drip without ceasing; their leaves shed the lifted water back down in a soft persistent rain of their own, so that the base of every column is a rainforest floor a few hundred metres across, set in a desert of new stone. Walk out from under the canopy and in fifty paces the ground is dry and warm and sharp, and the only water is what condenses on your skin, and in another kilometre the next green cloud begins. Between them the animals cross fast and low or not at all. The floaters cross on the sky. The ear-birds cross on the gradients. The grazers do not cross. Each node, for now, is an island, and the arms reaching between them in the high dark are the promise that it will not always be.

There are rules. They are learned, not written, and the tenders at the edge pass them to whoever comes.

The sound does not travel outward the way sound should. It is loud at the nodes and loud in the bands and, oddly, no louder at the plain's centre than at its edge — as if it is not radiating at all but simply *present*, everywhere inside the field, at the volume the field requires. Stand in the exact cancellation point between four nodes and it drops to something you could nearly speak through. Step a metre to either side and it is back. There are creatures that live only in those points, small pale things that have not lost their hearing and cannot leave.

Distances shift. The grid is regular but the walk between two nodes is not always the same length, and the tenders have stopped trying to measure it. Something about the field. Something about the way the pressure holds the air, and perhaps the ground, and perhaps the person walking.

Light pools. It should not. The dust bands filter the sky to that tea colour everywhere, but at the bases of the nodes, in among the dripping, the dimness thickens into something you could nearly cup, and elsewhere — at the crack edges, at the broken lips of the stone shelves — it thins, and the fresh fracture surfaces glint as if lit from within. No one has explained this. It is possible no one is trying.

And the nodes are listening. That is the rule the tenders say last, and quietly, as if quiet meant anything here. The chord changes. Over hours, over seasons, it drifts, and the drift is not random and not weather; it follows things. A flock of ear-birds establishes a new hunting line and within a week the band they hunt has shifted a few metres to meet them. A grazer population booms and the moss at its node thickens. The tenders came here to build a city for people and the city is building itself around whoever has actually arrived, and the sound that shapes it is being shaped in turn by the wingbeats and the footfalls and the ripples in the black pools, all of it feeding back into a chord that is, slowly, learning what lives here.

So you stand on a shelf of stone that was not there a lifetime ago, in warm damp near-dark, with the sky standing in stripes above you, and the roar in your bones, and the green towers of an uninhabited city humming kilometres apart across a plain the size of a country, and everything that flies and crawls and drips around you belongs here more than you do. It is not finished. It is not for you yet, or perhaps not for you at all. It is a place being sung into existence, and the singing has not asked whose city it is, and the animals have not waited to find out.
