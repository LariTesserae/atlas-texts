You come down into it from the rim, and the first thing that stops being true is the ground.

Up on the rim there was a hard mineral crust, dry, salt-white in what little light there was. Twenty steps down the slope the crust becomes coursed stone — not tumbled, not natural, but set. Blocks the size of a torso, faces dressed rough, joints packed with a grey clay that is still soft when you push a thumbnail into it. The slope you are descending is a wall. It is a wall that has been built at the angle of a hillside, and it is built all the way down, and it is built all the way out, and there is no point at which it stops being built and begins being earth.

That is the rule of the basin, and it takes a while to accept: there is no substrate. Wherever you dig, you find more of it. The people who work here have driven shafts — not for anything, just to see — and the shafts go down through course after course, the joints getting harder, the clay turning to something like lime, the blocks getting larger and stranger and less shaped by hands you would recognise, and at no depth anyone has reached does the masonry give way to rock. The bottom of the basin is a floor. Under the floor is another floor.

---

It is dark. Not sealed-dark; there is a sky, or there is something overhead that behaves like a sky — cloud pressed down low and permanent, faintly luminous at the edges the way cloud is when a moon is somewhere behind it. Enough to tell a wall from an opening if you stand still and let your eyes go. Not enough to see a face. Not enough to see your hands unless you hold them up against the sky-glow.

The other light comes from the kilns. There are perhaps a dozen kilns across the basin at any time, each one a low beehive of the same grey block, each one throwing a dull red mouth into the fog. From the rim you can see them: scattered embers in a bowl of black, sometimes one going bright as somebody feeds it, sometimes one dying to nothing. You navigate by them. Everyone navigates by them. A kiln is a place-name.

Because of the dark, nobody here builds by sight. They build by sound.

---

The sound is what you hear from the rim before you see anything at all, and it does not stop, and it does not get quieter as you descend — it gets louder and it gets closer and it separates into its parts.

Stone on stone. That is the ground note: thousands of stone hammers on thousands of stone faces, dressing, fitting, tapping a block into its bed. Each tap has a pitch. A block that is seated true rings one way; a block that is rocking on a high spot rings another; a block with a hidden flaw rings dead. The workers do not look at the joint, they listen to it, and so the whole valley is a permanent inquiry — knock, knock, knock, a pause while someone shifts a wedge, knock — a sound of many thousand questions being asked of many thousand stones.

Over that, the calling. You cannot see who is beside you, so you tell them. The calls are short and have carry — a rising two-note shout that means *I am here*, a falling one that means *coming through with weight*, a rapid stutter that means *stop, the wall is moving*. They pass from mouth to mouth up the slopes and across the courtyards, relayed, so a warning raised at one kiln arrives at the far side of the basin a few breaths later, distorted, still understood.

Under that, the dragging. The blocks come from the older courses — there is no quarry but the basin itself, and so the deep layers are always being unpicked to build the high ones. Sledges of stone slab, hauled on ropes braided from hair, grind across the courtyards with a noise like a long tearing. Where the slabs have run for years they have worn grooves in the floor, and the grooves fill with water, and the sledges hiss.

And under all of it, hardly a sound at all, the water.

---

It is cool here, always. The stone holds the cold and the fog holds the wet. Every surface you touch is beaded. Breath from the workers, steam from the clay-pits where they tread mortar, the exhalation of the kilns — it all rises a little way and meets the low cloud and comes back down as a drizzle so fine you do not feel it fall, only find that you are soaked.

The walls sweat. That is what they say — a finished wall, a course that has been closed and pointed and rung true, will within a day begin to weep from its joints. Clear water, cold, faintly mineral. Nobody agrees where it comes from. Some say it is drawn up from below, from whatever the deepest floors are standing in. Some say the stone itself gives it up, that the blocks are wet in their hearts and it takes the pressure of a proper seating to squeeze it out. The water runs down the faces of the walls in the dark and collects in the grooves and the low places, and so the basin is laced with shallow channels that no one dug, that simply arrived where the building made room for them, and the workers have learned to leave them and step over them and cup their hands into them.

Every so often there is real rain. It comes without warning from the low cloud, hard, for a few minutes, and the whole valley changes its voice — the stone-knocking stops because you cannot hear the pitch in rain, and for the length of the shower there is only water on stone and the calling, everyone telling everyone where they are. Then it passes and the tapping starts again from one edge and spreads.

---

The density of the place is hard to convey from the rim, where it reads as a single dark mass. Down inside it is a different thing.

The walls are close. Not corridors so much as seams — gaps between adjacent structures barely wider than a body, sometimes narrowing to nothing so that you have to go back and find another way. Everything leans on everything. A wall that would fall alone is held by the wall beside it, which is held by the one beside that. The workers build in this way deliberately; they do not trust a freestanding thing. And so the basin is not a town of buildings but one building with many hollows in it, and the hollows are where the work happens, and the work fills them in, and new hollows open above.

You are never alone in a hollow. You feel the others before you hear them — a shift in the air, a warmth, the small sounds of a body moving. They brush past. Hands touch your shoulder in the dark to know where you are, and then you know where they are. Ropes cross at head height and someone is always paying one out. In the larger courtyards — there are some, where a great slab has been laid across many piers and a space kept open beneath — the crowding is nearly total: bodies treading clay in a circle, bodies passing blocks hand to hand up a stair, bodies asleep in the angles of the walls with the wet running past their feet. It smells of wet lime, of hair-rope, of smoke, of people.

And yet the place is not full in the way a hive is full. Nothing lives here but the builders. There are no plants; there is nothing for a plant to root in and no light for it. No insects worth the name. Occasionally something pale and soft in a seam — an eyeless thing the length of a finger that eats the lime-slime and is not, as far as anyone can tell, anyone's food. Once in a long while a bird comes down through the cloud, blind or lost, and hits a wall and is found in the morning by touch, and is a small event, talked about across several kilns.

The builders themselves are few, for the size of it. It is only that the size of it is so densely made.

---

The tools are stone and bone. Hammers of a hard dark cobble that has never been seen in the basin's own courses — they are handed down, worn to fit particular palms, and losing one in the dark is a grief. Picks of bone: long bones, cut and pointed, from what animal nobody now knows, quarried like everything else from the deep layers, where they turn up in the joints between the old blocks as though laid there. Wedges of fired clay. Sledges. Ropes. Lime burned in the kilns from a soft white stone that also comes from below.

There is no metal. There is no reason to think anyone here has seen metal, or that the deep courses, whoever made them, used any. The work is slow and it is careful and it is done with the body. A block is fitted by lifting it a dozen times, tapping, listening, lowering, rubbing the high spot with a coarser stone, lifting again. A wall a person tall might take a season. There is no hurry, because there is no end.

---

That is the other thing you come to understand, moving through it in the dark with your hands out.

Nothing here is finished, and nothing is meant to be. The whole of the basin is emerging — that is its condition, not its stage. The top surface everywhere is raw: half-courses, open beds of clay waiting for a stone, piers with nothing on them yet, stairs that stop in the air. A structure ringed true and closed and weeping from its joints is not complete; it is simply ready to have another course laid on it, or to be stripped in a generation for the blocks. The builders do not talk about what it will be. Asked, they will tell you what it is *being*, which is: raised.

Why raised, and toward what — the cloud gives no answer, and the calling has no word for it. There is a pattern, perhaps. From the rim, if you stand long enough for your eyes to open fully, the kiln-lights and the faint sky-glow catch on the ridges of the highest work, and there is a sense — no more than a sense — that the whole basin is a single form very slowly coming up out of its own foundations, the way a shape comes up out of the dark when you have looked at it long enough. Curved. Ribbed. Enclosing something. Tomorrow it will be a little higher. The deepest floors will be a little more hollowed out to feed it.

Down in the seams no one sees that. They hear it. The pitch of a good joint. The falling call that means weight is coming through. The rain, when it comes, stopping everything for a breath so that the only sound in the whole made valley is water finding its way down through stone toward whatever it is that the stone is standing on.

Then someone taps a block, and it rings, and the asking begins again.
