**The Roaring Shelf**

Start with the fact that it is high. You have to climb to reach the water.

The approach is from below, up a face of fractured basalt so recently cooled it still has the sheen of pouring — black, glassy, edged like broken bottles. Nothing has softened it. There is no soil, no lichen, no rounding of the corners. The rock is young in the way only rock can be young, which is to say it has been a rock for perhaps a few thousand years and has not yet been asked to be anything else. Handholds cut the fingers. The air thins. The sky goes from blue to a deeper blue to something that is nearly violet at the zenith, the way it does when you are high enough that the atmosphere is running out above you.

And then the lip of the plateau, and the water.

It is a sea. It is not a lake; the word lake is for things that end. This sits on top of the raised block of a continent the way a tablecloth sits on a table, and from the lip you cannot see the far side, and if you walked the shore — there is nowhere to walk; the shore is a knife edge — you would walk for months. Ten thousand feet up, a cold grey ocean, level as a spirit gauge, holding itself above the world.

You hear it before you see it, of course. You have been hearing it for days. But at the lip the sound stops being a thing you hear and becomes a thing you stand inside.

---

It is one note. That is the first thing to understand. It is not the noise of water — there is almost no surf, because there is almost no wind here that could raise it, and the surface of the high sea lies flat and shivering like the skin on a pot about to boil. The sound comes from underneath. It is a low continuous roar with a tone buried in it, something between a foghorn and the moment before a landslide, and it is so loud that the body stops treating it as sound and treats it as pressure. Your ribs vibrate. The fillings in your teeth. The water at the edge does not lap; it trembles in a fine standing pattern, a lattice of tiny ridges that never move, because the sea is being rung like a bell and the bell has been ringing without pause or variation for longer than anyone has been alive to time it.

Speech is impossible. It has been impossible since the last switchback. The few who work here use hands and chalk.

---

What rings it is industrial, and it is down there.

Somewhere on the floor of the high sea — the depth is not known exactly; soundings are useless when the whole medium is a tuning fork — sit the engines. Nobody who works on the shelf has seen them whole. What is known is inferred from the parts that break the surface: intake towers of riveted iron, spaced miles apart, standing up out of the grey water like the stacks of a factory that has been drowned but is still, stubbornly, on shift. They are streaked white with mineral scale. Steam does not rise from them; they are too cold. But the water immediately around each tower is a different colour, darker, and moves in a slow permanent gyre the width of a town, and that is where the note is loudest, and where the plating of the towers is worn to a mirror polish by nothing but vibration.

The engines pump. That is the general agreement. What they pump, and from where to where, is less clear. One theory holds that they are what keeps the sea up here at all — that a body of water this size, this high, should have found its way off the plateau ages ago, and the roaring is the sound of it being perpetually lifted back. Another holds that they are keeping the sea from freezing: the water is two degrees above the point, and never a fraction more or less, and the shivering is what keeps the crystals from forming, and if the note ever stopped the whole shelf would go white and hard in a single night. Both may be true. Neither can be checked, because you cannot get close to the towers; the water there shakes hard enough to burst a swimmer's eardrums in seconds and unfasten the bones of the inner ear shortly after.

So people stay at the edges, and build small.

---

The built things on the shelf are scattered with a great deal of nothing between them. Here a stone hut with a slate roof weighted by boulders, its windows facing away from the water. Here a winch and a spool of cable, rusted to a single object. Here a cairn that might be a marker or a grave. Here, once in a great while, a boat — iron, flat-bottomed, heavily ballasted, its hull shaped less for moving through water than for not being shaken apart by it. The boats go out only along the rim, in the shallows where the roar is bearable through padded hoods, and they go out for the animals.

Because the sea is full of them.

---

It should not be. There is no plant life on the shelf. Nothing green, nothing that photosynthesises. The rock is too new and too glassy, the water too cold, the altitude too fierce. The floor of the sea, where it has been glimpsed by dropped lamps, is bare black glass with grey mineral snow settling on it. And yet the water is thick with living things, spaced widely across an immense volume so that at any given moment there may be almost nothing near you, and then, without warning, a great deal.

They are deaf. All of them. That is the first adaptation and the one everything else follows from. Whatever came up here, or was put here, or arrived by some route no one has traced, lost its hearing within a few generations or died, and what remains are animals that live inside the loudest place in the world and experience it as silence. They have thick shells or thick skulls or no skulls at all. There are jointed things the size of a hand that cling to the intake towers and graze the mineral scale, thousands upon thousands, fluorescing a dull orange where the vibration is strongest — the shaking somehow feeds them, or something the engines exhale does. There are ribbons of translucent animals, filter-feeders longer than a boat, that drift with the gyres in single file and eat the grey snow. There are pale eyeless fish with a lateral line so developed it runs in a ridge along the whole body, that navigate by the standing wave the way a person might navigate by the grain of a floor. They can be caught by hand from the boats in the shallows, because nothing that lives here has ever been hunted by anything that came from outside, and they do not know to be afraid.

And there is the large thing.

It is seen rarely and never entirely: a back, or a fin, or a long slow displacement in the water off the northern towers, where the gyre is deepest. It is not aggressive. It is, if anything, indifferent. Those who have seen it agree that it moves in time with the note — that it rises and falls on the pressure like a body breathing — and that when it surfaces the sound does not change but the vibration in the rock underfoot does, briefly, as though something very heavy had shifted its weight against the bell.

---

Light is ordinary here. That surprises people. A place this strange, they expect gloom, or a strange colour to the sun. But the sun comes up over the eastern lip and crosses a sky of deep hard blue and goes down over the western lip, and the water is grey-green in the morning and grey-blue in the afternoon and nearly black at dusk, and the towers throw long ordinary shadows across it. Thin air makes the light sharp; every rivet on the nearest tower is visible from a mile off. The cold is dry. The rock does not sweat.

There is one exception, and it is the weather.

Some way out — twenty miles, forty, distances are hard here — a wall of rain stands on the sea. It is a single storm cell, and it does not move. It has never been observed to move. It stands in one place, grey and vertical and miles high, its base lost in the water and its top flattening out against the thin upper air, and it rains into the sea continuously, and the sea is not visibly raised by it and does not appear to need to be. Lightning is rare. Wind, none. It is simply a place where the water goes up and comes down again, endlessly, in the same place, as fixed as the towers. Whether the engines make it, or it makes the engines possible, or whether the two are simply two aspects of the same enormous stable arrangement, is not known.

Under the storm, the note is said to be different — deeper, more complex, as if there were a second engine beneath it. No one has stayed under it long enough to be certain. The animals crowd there.

---

The overriding quality of the shelf, once you have been on it a few days and your body has stopped panicking at the sound, is that nothing changes.

There are no tides; the water is too high and too far from anything that could pull it. There are no seasons that register; it is two degrees above freezing in what the calendar calls summer and two degrees above freezing in winter, and the difference is only in the length of the shadows. The rock does not weather. The towers do not rust further than they have rusted. The animals do not migrate, because there is nowhere else with the note in it. The storm stands where it stands. The gyres turn at the pace they turn. The roar holds its pitch.

People come here for the fish, and for the mineral scale on the towers, which is worth something below, and for a few other reasons they do not chalk on the boards. Most leave within a season. Deafness comes on within a year even with the hoods, and after that, they say, the shelf becomes a different place — quiet, finally, and grey, and full of slow shapes moving in trembling water under a violet sky, with a storm on the horizon that never comes closer and never goes away. Some stay after that. There is a hut on the southern lip whose occupant has not been down the face in eleven years and communicates with the boats by flag.

The chalk board outside the hut says, in a hand that has not changed either: STILL RINGING.
