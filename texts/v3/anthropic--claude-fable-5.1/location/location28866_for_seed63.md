**The Oven at Tsen Hollow**

You come up to it across a shelf of dark stone that has been lifted, over ages, to a height where the air is thin enough to notice. Nothing grows here. The plateau is bare rhyolite and old ash welded to glass, scoured to a dull polish by a wind that has never once carried rain. The sky at this altitude is a hard blue by day and, by night, so crowded with stars that the ground seems the emptier for it. There is no water on the shelf. There has been no water on the shelf within the memory of the rock.

The hollow is a crack in the plateau's edge, wide enough for two people abreast and tall enough that you do not have to stoop. From a distance it is only a slightly darker seam in a dark cliff. Up close, you feel it before you see it: a breath. Warm, dry, steady, coming out of the earth against your face. Not the wet heat of a jungle or a hot spring. This is kiln heat, bread-oven heat, the heat of a stone that has been near a fire for so long that it has forgotten being cold.

The entrance has been tended. Not built, exactly — there is a low course of stacked stones on either side, knee-high, mortarless, the kind of wall you could make in an afternoon and that would stand for a thousand years because nothing here pushes on it. Someone piled these. On the left-hand slab, at the height of a seated person's shoulder, there are marks. Rows of short scratches, some deeper than others, dozens of them, a few worn nearly away. Not writing. Counting, maybe. Or just the habit of a hand with a sharp stone and a long wait.

Then you go in, and the light goes.

---

It goes quickly. The seam bends twice within the first thirty paces, and by the second bend the sky is a memory and the walls have closed to within an arm's reach. The heat climbs. The floor slopes down, very slightly, and it is smooth — not water-smooth, but foot-smooth, belly-smooth, the smoothness of a surface that has been passed over by a great many bodies for a great deal of time. Your hand on the wall finds the same thing: a warm glaze, faintly greasy, like the handrail of an old staircase.

And then the passage opens, and you are in the room.

It is not large. In daylight you would call it the size of a small chapel — perhaps forty paces across, a domed ceiling you could not touch from the floor but might if you stood on someone's shoulders. A single chamber, round-ish, with the passage you came through and, somewhere on the far side, a narrower one leading down. This is the whole of it. There is nothing else. The hollow is one room in the mountain, and the mountain's warmth comes up through a floor that is not quite solid and never quite still.

You cannot see any of this. What you know of the room, you learn by standing in it.

---

The first thing is the sound, or rather the almost-sound. The room is not silent. It has a texture to it — a dry, continuous, infinitesimal noise like a distant hand brushing across sand, or a very large number of very small pages being turned somewhere far away. It does not stop. It does not rise or fall. It is the sound of the room's population moving over itself.

Because the room is full.

Every surface — the floor to within a stride of where you stand, the walls from floor to dome, the ceiling entire — is covered in living bodies, and covered is not the right word, because there is no gap between them, no bare stone showing, no patch you could put a palm flat against. They are layered. Where one ends the next begins, and beneath that one there is another, and beneath the underneath there is the warm rock and the slow breath of the deep.

They are quiet because they are blind, and because in a place where everything touches everything, sound is not how you know. You know by warmth and by pressure and by the taste of the air, which here is thick, mineral, faintly sulphurous, with an undertone of something like scorched hair that is simply the smell of a great many small hot bodies in a closed space.

---

Let your eyes stay open long enough — an hour, longer — and something arrives that is not exactly seeing.

The room is not entirely without light. Down through the far passage, deep enough that it reaches you only as a suggestion, there is a redness: not a glow, not a flicker, just the faint idea that the darkness in that direction is warmer in color than the darkness elsewhere. And closer, all around you, there are points. Dim as the dimmest stars, blue-white and cold-looking in a place that is anything but cold. They move. They drift across the walls in slow, purposeful arcs, blink out, reappear elsewhere. Some cluster. Some travel alone. Some, if you watch long enough, converge on a single spot, hold there, and then all go dark at once.

Those are the hunters. The light is on their undersides, a bare pinprick of it, and it is not for seeing — nothing here can see — but for something else. Perhaps to draw. Perhaps to mark. Perhaps it means nothing at all and is simply a chemistry that happens to shine. But wherever they gather, the texture of the room's whisper changes: a small tightening, a knot of faster movement, and then a stillness in one place while everywhere else goes on as before.

---

The ecology of the oven is simple in the way that very old things become simple.

At the bottom of it is the rock itself — or rather what lives on the rock, a film so thin and so intimately bonded to the stone that it is not clear where mineral ends and life begins. It feeds on the heat and on what the heat carries up from below: sulphur, iron, a breath of ancient gases. It has no color anyone has seen. In the warmest places it is thick enough to feel, a velvet that does not quite come off on the finger.

The film is grazed. The grazers are the bulk of the population: flat, many-legged, hand-sized and smaller, armored in a shell that is dark and matte and slightly rough, like the rock they eat. They move in currents. On the wall to your right, if you rest a hand there for a while, you feel the tide of them pass beneath your palm: one direction for a long time, then a pause, then the other. They graze a patch to bare stone and move on, and behind them the film returns, and behind the film they return. The room cycles this way, a slow rotation of appetite that has been turning for so long that the walls are shaped by it — the great smooth curve of the dome is not the work of water or wind but of an unimaginable number of small mouths.

Above the grazers, the hunters with their pinprick lights. Above the hunters, something else — larger, rarer, that you will feel only once or twice as a slow displacement on the floor near your feet, a shift in the warm mass as if something the size of a dog were moving through it without disturbing it, which it is, because it is of it, and everything in the room makes way for everything else by a settlement older than any of them.

And there is the thing that eats the dead. Everything that dies in the oven — and everything does, in its turn — is dry within the day, brittle within the week, and gone within the month, taken back down into the film, into the rock, into the breath that rises from below. There are no bones on the floor. There is nothing on the floor but the living.

---

There is no water. This is the strangeness the room most insists upon, once you have grown used to the rest.

Nothing drinks here. Nothing can. What moisture the room contains is inside its inhabitants, drawn from the film, drawn from each other, held with a miserliness that has shaped every body in the place: sealed shells, closed spiracles, a metabolism that yields up water only in dying. The air is dry enough to crack your lips within the hour and to make your eyes ache with the effort of staying open. And yet the room is more alive per handspan than any forest — more alive than a reef, than a rookery, than the mud of a delta. Life without water, life without light, life without a single green thing, packed so tight it has ceased to be many things and become one warm, slow, whispering surface.

---

People have come here. Not many, and not recently, but the signs are there for a hand that knows to look.

Beneath the living surface, just inside the entrance, there is a flat stone that does not belong. It is smoother than the rest and it is cooler, and if you lift the grazers from it — they come away easily, unbothered, and return the moment you stop — you find it is a slab set into a shallow hollow, wedged with chips. A seat. Or a hearth without fire, because who would need a fire here. Beside it, a shallow bowl carved into the floor itself, no wider than two cupped hands, glazed to a sheen by whatever was once put in it and taken out again.

On the dome, if you stand at the room's center and reach up as far as you can, your fingers pass through a layer of grazers and find the rock — and find it worked. Grooves. Long ones, following the curve, meeting and parting. Not letters. Not pictures, or not pictures of anything you could name. A pattern that seems to follow the paths the pinprick lights take, night after night, so that whoever cut it may have stood here in the dark for a very long time, watching, and then reached up and made what they saw permanent.

They left nothing else. No tool, no bead, no bone. Whatever they brought they took back out, or the room took it down into itself.

---

The room breathes. You will notice this last, when you have stood in it long enough to stop noticing yourself.

The warmth from below is not constant. It comes in slow pulses — an hour, two — during which the floor grows perceptibly hotter and the whisper of the walls quickens, and then a long ebb during which everything slows nearly to stillness and the pinprick lights all go out together. In the hot phase the grazers travel; in the cool phase they hold. In the hot phase the hunters gather; in the cool phase they are nowhere. It is not day and night — there is no day here — but it is a cycle, and the room has kept it long enough that it has become the room's own clock, and every body in it is set to it.

Sometimes, in the cool phase, there is a sound that is not the whisper. A single click, dry and clear, from the far passage. Then, after a while, another. Then nothing for a long time.

No one knows what makes it. No one has gone down the far passage and come back to say.

---

When you go, you go up the same seam you came down, and the heat falls away from you like a coat, and the second bend gives you back a wedge of sky so bright it hurts, even if it is only starlight. On the stone outside, the wind is cold and clean and utterly empty, and the shelf of the plateau runs away in every direction with nothing on it, nothing at all, and behind you the crack in the cliff breathes out its warm dry breath into the night.

You may find, if you look at your palm in the starlight, a faint dark velvet on the skin where you rested it against the wall. It will not wash off, because there is nothing to wash it with. It will wear away in a day or two. Before it does, if you put your hand near your face in the dark, you might feel — or believe you feel — the very smallest warmth coming off it.
