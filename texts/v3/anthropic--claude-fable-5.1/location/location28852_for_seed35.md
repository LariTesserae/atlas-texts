The sound reaches you before the stair does. It comes up through the treads, a note held so low and so steadily that at first you take it for a fault in your own hearing, a pressure in the ears rather than a thing in the world. It does not swell and does not fade as you descend. It is simply *more* with every step, the way heat is more as you approach a wall.

The stair is cut into gypsum. The steps are chisel-fresh, edges unrounded, faces bright with the tiny cleavage planes that catch what light there is and throw it back in flakes. There is no film of dust on them, no grey softening. Someone cut these this morning, is the feeling, and the feeling never leaves, because every surface in the place is like that: newly broken, sharp, the mineral still surprised to be exposed. Where the rock changes from gypsum to anhydrite the fracture surfaces turn fibrous, like the inside of a split pelt, and these too are unweathered. Nothing has ever touched them. Nothing here has ever moved enough to touch them.

The air is warm and completely dry. It takes the moisture off your lips within a minute. It smells of hot iron and mineral oil and, underneath, of something animal and faintly sweet, like the inside of a hive.

The chamber at the bottom of the stair is the size of a parish church and shaped like nothing built. It is a void in the seam — a cavity that was already there when the drift came through and found it — and the drift's mouth is a neat square in one wall with rails still laid in it, rails that end four metres in. Everything else is geology. The ceiling is a domed fracture perhaps twelve metres up. The floor is uneven, scattered with blocks that have fallen and not yet had time to be anything but blocks.

In the middle of the floor is the engine.

It is a horizontal single-cylinder gas engine, riveted, painted a green gone nearly black, with a flywheel taller than a person set into a pit in the floor. A brass plate on the frame gives a maker and a town and a serial number. A pipe comes out of the rock wall itself — not out of any fitting, out of the rock — and runs along the floor into the engine's intake. The seam is gas-bearing. The chamber is feeding the machine from its own body. The exhaust goes up a stovepipe into a crack in the ceiling and whatever is beyond that.

The flywheel turns. It has, you understand at once, always turned, and at exactly this speed. There is a belt from the flywheel to a small dynamo bolted to a block of anhydrite, and from the dynamo a wire loops around the chamber carrying nine carbon-filament bulbs. They glow the orange of a banked fire. They do not flicker. The oil in the glass on the main bearing sits at a level that is not marked but might as well be. There is a drip forming under the crosshead, a bead of oil perfectly formed and perfectly still, that has not fallen and is not going to fall.

The engine is the source of about half the sound.

The other half is the animals.

You do not see them at once because there is so little light and because they are so still. What you see first is that the walls have a texture, a nap, that fresh-cut gypsum should not have. Then the texture resolves. The walls are covered. The ceiling is covered. The engine itself, its cooler parts, is covered.

They are stratified by temperature. Highest, in the dome where the air is warmest, hang the bats in even rows, wings folded, packed so their shoulders touch. Below them, on the vertical faces, the moths — thousands, wings laid flat against the mineral, arranged with a regularity that looks less like resting than like tiling. Below the moths, at head height and lower, cave crickets, bodies pressed together, long antennae laid back along their neighbours' backs so that the whole wall has a grain. On the floor between the fallen blocks are beetles, black and brown, in a layer that in places is two or three deep. On the warm cylinder head lies a snake, thick as a wrist, its full length laid along the iron. Two geckos sit on the nearest bulb, bellies to the glass. In the pit of the flywheel, in the space between rim and wall where the air moves a little, is a knot of mice, eyes open.

None of them is running from anything. None of them is going anywhere. You watch for the ordinary wrongness — the twitch, the antenna testing, the shifting of weight — and it does not come. Then you see the snake's flank move, and the bats' flanks move, and the mice's flanks move, all together, and you realise they are breathing, and that they are breathing in time.

And you realise the note has parts.

Under the engine's chuff there is a stridulation, dry and enormous, the crickets rubbing at a single pitch. Over it is a shimmer that is the moths' wings, vibrating without opening. At the top, at the edge of hearing, a whistle that is the bats. The engine gives the fundamental and the animals hold the harmonics, and the chord fills the chamber to the walls so completely that you cannot tell which is setting the pitch for the others. Possibly nothing is. Possibly they arrived at it together, once, and have kept it.

It is loud enough that you stop trying to think in words.

There are plants. You have to look for them. Four pale stalks in the crevice where the stovepipe enters the ceiling, leaves like wet paper, no green in them at all, drinking the bulb-light from three metres off. A grey crust, lichen or something like it, on the exhaust manifold where the heat is. A single fern, bleached to the colour of bone, in a crack behind the dynamo. None of them shows a dead leaf or a new one. They are the size they are.

The rules of the place take longer to see than the place itself.

Nothing eats. The snake lies a metre from the mice and the mice lie a metre from the snake. The geckos sit among moths and the moths do not move away. Nothing drinks, because there is nothing to drink; the seam is dry and the air is dry and there has never been a puddle on that floor. Nothing is born. Nothing dies. There are no bones among the beetles, no husks, no droppings under the bats. The guano that a colony that size should have laid down in a season is simply absent, and the gypsum beneath them is as clean as the stair.

The flywheel has not gained or lost a revolution. The bead of oil has not fallen. The bulbs have not dimmed, and carbon filaments dim. Whatever was cut this morning is cut this morning. The only account of the place that fits is that it stopped, at some point after the drift broke through and the engine was set running and the animals came in out of the fissures of the seam to lie against the warmth — that everything in it found the one arrangement in which nothing needed to change, and has held it.

You are not in that arrangement. You are the only thing in the chamber with a gradient in it. Your sweat is drying; your pulse is out of step with the breathing on the walls; your eyes are adjusting, which is itself a change the place does not contain. You feel it as a kind of pressure — not hostility, nothing so active — the quiet, total displacement of a room that was full before you arrived. The chord does not alter for you. The bats do not turn their heads. The engine takes its gas from the rock and gives its heat to the snake and its light to the paper leaves and its note to everything, and the everything gives the note back, and there is no room in the sound for a second heartbeat.

You go back up the stair. The note comes with you as far as the third turning, then not so much fades as ceases to be underfoot. Behind you the flywheel is at exactly the speed you left it. The drip is still forming. The chamber is exactly as itself as it has ever been, which is entirely.
