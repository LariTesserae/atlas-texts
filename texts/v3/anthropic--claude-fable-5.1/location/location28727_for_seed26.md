The first thing is the cold, and the second is that the cold has a smell. Wet cement smells like rain on a school playground and like the inside of a refrigerator at the same time, alkaline and mineral, and it fills the air here the way pine fills a forest. There is nothing here that was not poured, welded, bolted, or driven. Even the floor you stand on has a date stamped in it — last month — next to the imprint of a boot that was lifted too late.

Then the size arrives. The ceiling is a long way up, ribbed with beams the width of rail cars, and it goes on until the lamps fail. Columns hold it: hexagonal piers thirty paces apart, marching in every direction, each with a number stenciled at eye level in orange. The light comes from strings of sodium work-lamps slung between them, one lamp per three or four columns, so that the hall is mostly dark with islands of brown-gold light standing in it. Between the islands you walk by the sound of your own feet and by the glow ahead. The distances are not intimate. This is not a room. It is a district, and it is still being made.

---

The water is everywhere and nowhere in particular. It comes first as a sheen — the floor is wet in a way that doesn't dry, a film that reflects the lamps and the black between them. Then as beads on every cold steel surface: railings, the flanks of the pumps, the underside of the beams, which sweat. Where the machines run, the air is a few degrees warmer, and when that air rises and touches the ceiling it gives up what it carries, and the ceiling rains. Not a metaphor. Between columns K-40 and K-46 there is a permanent slow rain, drops falling forty meters into a shallow lake that has formed in a low bay of the floor, each drop landing with a distinct note, thousands of notes at once, so that the lake plays continuously like a badly tuned instrument. The rain drifts with the draft. When the fans change speed it moves two columns north.

Elsewhere there are channels: rectangular flumes cut into the floor, running full and fast, their water so clear and cold that it looks like moving glass with grit suspended in it. The water arrives from above through shafts you can see far off — grey verticals, wider than towers, that come down through the ceiling and vanish into grates in the floor. In the daytime a column of weak surface light comes down each shaft along with the water, a pale grey pillar standing in the dark, full of falling mist. At night the shafts are only sound.

Here is the first rule of the world: the weather is late. What falls on the surface arrives here six hours after. A storm at noon up there is a roar at six in the evening down here, the shafts going white, the channels rising against their marks, the pumps coming up one after another with a change in pitch you feel in your sternum. A dry week above is a quiet week below, the channels shrinking to a trickle, the lake at K-43 receding and leaving a rim of fine grey silt with a crust of white salt — the concrete's lime, leached out, the first mineral bloom on surfaces that have no other stain. The efflorescence is the only thing here that looks old, and it is a few weeks old.

---

Everything is sharp. The edges of the piers are true right angles; you could cut your hand. The tie-holes where the formwork was bolted still show as clean cones, unweathered, each with a bead of rust bleeding a centimeter downward and stopping. The floor slabs meet in joints so recent that the sealant is still glossy. There is no rounding, no wear path, no patina beyond the pale lime frost. In the newest quarters the forms have not even been struck — plywood walls still stand bolted around piers whose concrete is grey-green and gives off a faint heat. You can put your palm against a shrouded column and feel it: the chemistry still working inside, the only warmth for a kilometer that doesn't come from a motor. The concrete is curing and the place is curing with it. Standing in the newest hall you have the feeling of being inside a landform at the moment of its formation, a lava field that has just stopped moving, except that this one was made on purpose and is still going.

Because the excavation never stops. Far off along one axis — you find it by the sound, a grinding under everything else — the face is advancing. The machines out there are the size of ships and mostly hidden by their own dust and mist. Conveyors bring the spoil back along the ceiling in a slow black river that never quite stops. And what they cut is not rock. Here is the second rule: there is no ground here that was not built. The drills go through older concrete. The spoil on the conveyors is broken slab, rebar of an unfamiliar gauge, the occasional glazed brick. Somewhere below what is being poured is what was poured before, and below that presumably more, and no one on the crews talks about it as anything but a materials question — the old stuff cuts easier, contaminates the aggregate, has to be screened. Once a section came through into a cavity where the older concrete had a curve in it, an arch, as though whoever made it had different ideas about weight. They filled it and lined it and it is now column row W.

---

The pumps are the loud thing. There are stations of them spaced through the district like villages — clusters of housings taller than a person, painted a green that the lamps turn brown, throbbing at frequencies just distinct enough to beat against one another. Standing between two stations you feel the beat as a slow pulse in the air, a wah-wah four seconds long. The pipes rise from them and cross the ceiling in bundles. When a station starts, the whole sound of the district shifts a quarter-tone, and every pool of standing water on the floor shivers at once.

Above the pumps are the fans. You do not see them. You see their effect: the mist leaning, the rain at K-43 walking north, your breath streaming sideways, and every so often a whole bank of fog picked up off a cold lake and carried down the columns at walking speed, dimming the lamps one by one as it passes and brightening them again behind. The stations signal one another by horn — two long, one short, then a reply from somewhere you cannot see, a lower horn, a kilometer off. No one has told you what the code means. It sounds like animals.

Because of the noise you do not hear anything small until you are on top of it. That is how you meet the heron.

---

It stands in the flume at column M-19, grey against the grey, one eye on the water, and when you are close enough to see it, it has already seen you and does not care. It came down a shaft some months ago, or fell, and has found that the flumes carry things: small fish drawn from whatever the water drains above, worms, the pale midges that breed in the warm skin of water on the pump housings and swarm the sodium lamps in slow clouds. The heron walks the channel edges with the deliberateness of a surveyor. It is the largest living thing in the district that is not a person, and most days you do not see it.

The other life is so scattered you could miss all of it. Under each work lamp, where the light is constant and the concrete sweats, moss has started — a green smear no bigger than a hand, and only under the lamps, so that the vegetation of the district is a map of the lighting. In the silt at the lake's edge something has sprouted, three pale stalks with a pair of leaves each, from a seed that came down a shaft with the last storm. They will not last; the lake will rise again. In the older quarters, meaning six months old, the moss under the lamps has become a fur and has begun to climb the base of the pier, and a fern the size of a coin has appeared in one of the tie-holes, growing sideways out of the wall into the light. A crew member has painted a small orange ring around it so no one will scrape it off.

There are cats, reportedly. There is a story about eels in the deep sump at the far end, where the channels converge and drop into a pit that the pumps draw from — a black basin as wide as a plaza, its surface trembling from the intake, and things that move in it that are not the intake. You can stand at the rail and look and see only the reflection of the lamps breaking up.

---

Time here has a shape, and it is not the day. The day exists faintly — the grey pillars in the shafts come and go — but the real cycle is the six-hour lag, the surface weather arriving in translation: rain as roar, sun as silence. Layered on that is the pour schedule, the batch plant somewhere near the face lighting up and thudding and the mixer trucks coming through in convoy, their headlights the brightest things in the district, sweeping the columns so that for a few seconds the hall reveals its actual extent, rank on rank of piers vanishing into a distance you had underestimated, and then the trucks pass and the darkness folds back in behind them. Layered on that is the slow advance of the face, a few meters a day, so that the map on the wall of the pump station at H-5 is redrawn weekly and the district is always slightly bigger than anyone's picture of it.

And under all of that, the water does what water does regardless of who poured the basin. It finds the low places. It leaves the lime. It builds, at the outfall of every drip, a first faint stalactite the size of a grain of rice — the beginnings of a cave in a place that was never a cave, formed in a material that was mixed on a Tuesday. In a hundred years, if the pumps keep running, these will be fingers. In a thousand, if they don't, the district will have become what it is imitating: a cold dark wet place under the world, full of columns, with water moving through it and a few things living at the edges of the light. It is halfway there already. It was halfway there the day the forms came off.

Up ahead a horn sounds, two long and one short, and after a moment the far one answers. The rain at K-43 starts to walk. You put your hand on the nearest pier and it is cold, which means it is finished, and you move on toward one that isn't.
