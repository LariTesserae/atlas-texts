You hear it long before you reach it. Down the passage, which is narrow and slick and slopes gently, the noise arrives first as pressure — the air in your ears takes on weight — and then as a sound you would call surf if there were any possibility of surf this far underground. It doesn't crest or fade. It has a floor under it, a continuous roar, and on top of that floor things happen: a slap, a whistle that climbs and snaps off, a low thud like a door shutting in a house much larger than any house.

Then the passage opens and the light hits you, and it's the wrong kind of light for a cave. Not a shaft of daylight. Not a lamp. The air itself is lit. You have walked into a cloud that is bright from every direction at once, so that your hands throw no shadow, the wall beside you throws no shadow, and the only darkness anywhere is the passage behind you, which now looks like a hole punched in a sheet of white paper.

The chamber is roughly the volume of a small cathedral, tall rather than wide, tapered toward the top like the inside of a bell. You can't see the top; the mist is thickest there and moves fastest. The wall you can see is stone, limestone or something like it, and it has been worked. Not carved. Worked the way a riverbed is worked, in long smooth flutes and scoops, in scalloped hollows the size of a cupped hand and the size of a bathtub, all running the same direction — around, and slightly upward, corkscrewing. The whole room is a threaded bore. Something has been turning inside it for a very long time.

That something is the air. The wind here does not gust the way surface wind gusts, from somewhere to somewhere. It circulates. It comes at you from the left, wet and warm, shoves you against the wall, then loses interest and lifts, and you feel the rain that was in it come down on your head — big drops, bath-warm — and for a moment it's still where you stand and roaring somewhere higher up, and then it comes at you from the left again. There is a pattern and the pattern is unstable. Sometimes the whole system stutters and the mist drops out of the air all at once and you can see thirty meters across to the far wall, its flutes streaming, before the wind catches and the whiteness fills back in.

Everything is wet. The walls don't drip so much as sweat; water films them and is pulled off in sheets by the wind and thrown. Where sheets collide, water flies up. The floor of the chamber, a few meters below the shelf where the passage lets out, is a shallow pool that never sits flat, rocking and slopping like water in a bucket someone is carrying. Its bottom is stone polished to glass. In the center the pool goes deep, a black hole in the white, and the turbulence on the surface tightens over that hole, so the whole pool is slowly turning, hurrying at the middle. Occasionally the level drops — a hand's width, a foot — and the drain sounds a note lower than everything else, and then the level comes back up, from where you can't tell, because there is no inlet. The walls weep, but they don't weep that much.

The warmth is the strangest part, once you've stood in it long enough to stop noticing the noise. It's warm the way a greenhouse is warm, but there's no sun. It's warm the way a hot spring is warm, but the water isn't hot; it's blood-temperature, exactly the temperature at which you stop being able to feel it on your skin. The heat has no source you can point at. The rock isn't warm. The air is.

People built here. Not much. A staircase is cut into the wall, starting at the shelf and going up in a long spiral that follows the thread of the flutes — whoever cut it understood the room and went with the grain. The treads are hollowed by water, almost gone in places, a stair that has become a chute. Every so often there's a landing, and on one landing a railing survives: a single rod of something grey and matte, bolted into the stone. The bolts have eaten themselves into rust-stains a meter long down the wall, but the rod is perfect. Not scratched. Not slick. Your hand closes on it and it is dry, which should not be possible, because nothing else here is dry, and it gives back a faint warmth that is different from the air's warmth — more local, like a hand that was holding it a moment ago.

Higher, half hidden in a scallop, a box the size of a suitcase is set into the rock, its face gone, its interior a nest of channels with nothing in them. Higher still, a doorway — an actual doorway, lintel and jambs — opens off the stair into a space that has filled solid with a smooth white deposit, so the doorway is now a frame around a wall. Whatever was behind it, the room has slowly closed, the way a tree grows around a nail.

That's about all: the stair, the railing, the box, the door, and here and there a plate bolted flat to the wall, its surface worn to a soft shine, marks on it that might have been writing and might be the scallop pattern of the wind, since the water has treated both alike. It isn't a ruin, exactly. A ruin is a thing that has stopped. This is a thing still running, with its operators gone. If they built the storm, they built it well enough that it didn't need them. If they only found it and built the stair to watch it, they were watching something that didn't notice them either.

---

Almost nothing lives here. The light would grow anything, and the warmth would, and the water would, and the wind won't let it. In the lee of the largest boulder on the floor — a slab fallen from somewhere above and rounded on every edge — there's a skin of something green-black the size of a tablecloth, flattened, pressed into every pore of the rock, holding on. Where the stair passes behind a lip of stone, a fern has grown: three fronds, torn and regrown and torn. High on the walls, where the mist is thickest and the wind is a constant scream, an orange crust makes rings around the scallop hollows, brightest at the rims, as if feeding on the exact edge where water is ripped from stone. It might not be alive. It might be a mineral that only forms there.

Down in the pool there's a fish. One kind, maybe one animal — a pale shape the length of a forearm, no eyes, moving with the slow turn of the water, keeping out of the center where the pull is. It doesn't behave like something hunting. It behaves like something staying. And in the stuttering pauses, when the mist drops and you can see across, the air in the middle of the room is full of tiny dark flecks all moving together — midges, or something like midges, riding the vortex, thousands of them, and yet so small the room is empty of them the moment the wind resumes. Where they breed, what they eat, isn't obvious. Nothing rests here long enough to be eaten.

---

The place has rules, and you learn them by standing in it.

The wind always turns the same way. It slows, it stutters, it throws a fit against one wall for a minute, but it never reverses.

Water goes up here more than it comes down. The rain you feel is mostly water the wind has torn from the walls and carried; the walls are the sky. The floor pool loses more to the wind than it receives, and the drain takes more still, and the level holds anyway.

The light comes from nowhere. When the mist clears you can see that the walls are not glowing and the pool is not glowing and there is no lamp. What glows is the water in the air. Each droplet is lit from inside, a fine flat white like light through a paper screen, and the more water in the air the brighter the room — so the loudest, wettest, most violent moments are the ones in which you see least and the light is greatest.
