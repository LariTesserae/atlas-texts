The hollow is not large. You can stand at one lip of it and see the other, a green rise no higher than a house, and everything the place contains lies between — perhaps two hundred paces across, roughly round, with the sky open above it. It sits low in a country that is otherwise dry. Coming down into it you feel the temperature drop by a coat's worth, and the air begins to carry moisture, not as rain but as a held dampness, the kind that beads on cold metal and on the underside of leaves.

There is no ground here in the usual sense. What you walk on is plant. Not turf over soil but a mat of stems, some living, some long dead and gone spongy, laid over more of the same for a depth that nobody has ever reached the bottom of. In places the mat is a low carpet of liverwort, slick and dark, cool to the hand. In others it rises into cushions of something like club moss, ankle-high, that spring back after each footstep with a faint visible reluctance. Where paths have been kept, the treading has made the plant tissue firm and slightly glossy, like a wooden floor that has been walked for a century. Where paths have not been kept, the way closes at the rate of about a finger's width a day.

The walls are plant. The buildings — and there are buildings, a dozen or so, scattered through the hollow with wide green gaps between — are made of stems trained upward and woven, pleached and re-pleached over generations until the individual plants can no longer be told apart. A wall here is thick as a forearm, tight as basketwork, alive along its whole length. It leafs in season. The roofs are the same, bent over from both sides and knotted at the ridge, the leaves lying shingle-fashion so that condensation runs down and off and drips from the eaves in slow irregular beats. Inside such a building the light is green and even. The walls sweat.

And the works are plant, or nearly. This is the thing that takes a while to understand.

There is industry here. That is what the place is for. Near the center of the hollow, where the ground is at its lowest and softest, stands the pump house — you know it by the chimney, which is the tallest thing in the hollow, a stack of trunks grown together and hollowed, or grown hollow, its mouth blackened, giving up a thin steady exhalation that is more steam than smoke. From the pump house, lines run out to the rim in six directions: pipes, originally iron, now encased in stem to twice their diameter, so that they look like great roots lying above the surface. Where a pipe has rusted through, the plant has closed the gap. The water still runs. Near the rim the pipes rise into standing frames that hold sheets of fine woven fibre across the wind — condensation nets, angled to catch the fog that pools in the hollow before dawn, feeding what they gather back down to the center. The place drinks from the air and from below, and the engine keeps it drinking.

The engine itself, when you find it, is hard to look at without recategorizing several times. Its frame is cast iron, old pattern, the kind with decorative flutings that nobody has bothered with for a very long time. Its flywheel is iron. Its beam, the great rocking arm that lifts the pump rods, is wood — a single timber, still barked in places, still, if you look closely, putting out small pale shoots along its upper edge which someone trims. The cam lobes that time the valves are burls, hard as horn, grown to shape and finished with a file. Where the piston rod meets the crosshead, the connecting pin is a knot. Whether the engine was built this way or whether it has been repaired into this condition part by part, iron giving way to grown wood each time a piece wore out, cannot be determined from the engine itself. The people who tend it would probably say it does not matter. It runs.

It runs slowly. One stroke every four or five seconds: a long intake hiss, a pause, a soft heavy knock as the beam reaches the end of its travel and the pump rods drop their load into the rising main. Then the exhaust, more sigh than blast, pushed out through the living chimney. The mat underfoot absorbs most of the sound. From the far side of the hollow the engine is a pulse you feel in the soles of your feet more than hear.

That pulse and the dripping are the two constant sounds. Everything else is intermittent: a chain running through a block somewhere out of sight; the clack of a shutter being propped open; once, in the afternoon, a bird calling twice from the rim and then not again. There are very few animals here. Snails, small and translucent, which appear on the pipe casings after the fog and are gone by mid-morning. A kind of beetle that lives in the dead layers of the mat and is seen only when a path is cut back. The people say there was once a cat. Whatever else lives here lives inside the plant mass, in the roots, and does not come up.

The people are few and widely spaced, which gives the hollow its particular feeling of being inhabited but not occupied. You may see one figure at a time. Someone at the condensation nets on the north rim, unhooking a sheet, shaking it, hooking it back. Someone on a ladder against the pump house, trimming the beam. Someone with a curved blade walking a path and cutting the edges back, laying the cut stems on the path to be trodden in. They do not call to each other across the distances. They know where the others are by the work that has been done.

There is a second industry, smaller, at the eastern side: a retting house and a press. Certain of the stem plants are cut when they reach a particular length, bundled, and laid in troughs of the pumped water to rot the soft tissue from the fibre. The troughs are stem-walled and lined with something that has long since become indistinguishable from the walls. The fibre that comes out is combed and twisted and woven into the condensation sheets, which wear through in about a season, so the process is closed: the hollow grows the nets that catch the water that keeps the hollow growing. What surplus fibre there is goes up over the rim by a small rail line, iron rails on stem sleepers, and a handcart. Where it goes after that is the dry country's business.

The mist is the clock of the place. It forms about two hours before dawn, rising out of the mat itself as the air cools and the plant exhales, and by first light it fills the hollow to the rim like water in a bowl, so that from outside you would see only a flat grey lid and, breaking it, the chimney with its thread of steam. Inside the mist the engine sounds closer. Everything drips. The nets go heavy and dark. Then the sun clears the rim and the mist begins to thin from the top down, and for perhaps an hour the hollow is a place of long horizontal light and hanging drops, and every leaf surface — and there are no surfaces that are not leaf — turns slightly toward the east. You can watch the turn if you are patient. The whole hollow leaning, very slowly, toward the light. By midday it has leaned back. The roofs lift and flatten with the day's warmth and settle again in the evening, and the woven walls creak with it, a long low working sound like a ship at anchor.

Nothing here is new and nothing is in ruin. The iron has rusted as far as it is going to rust; the plant has taken over as much as it is going to take over; the two have reached terms. The paths are cut where the paths have always been cut. The engine has been running, as far as anyone can tell, since before the oldest wall was woven, and the walls are old. The hollow has settled into its shape the way a face settles into its lines. It is small and it is quiet and it is entirely busy: growing, breathing out, condensing, pumping, rotting fibre into thread, pushing shoots along the beam of its own engine, being trimmed.

If you climb to the rim at dusk and look back, the chimney's steam is the last thing lit, a pale thread going straight up into still air. Then that goes too, and the hollow is a darkness that smells of cold leaf and wet iron, breathing once every four or five seconds, and beyond it the dry country runs off flat under the first stars.
