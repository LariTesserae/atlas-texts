You come to it across a plain that gives no warning. The ground is limestone worn to the texture of old bone, lightly grassed, cracked in polygons, and then it simply stops: a rim, a lip of rock rounded by ten thousand years of rain, and below it a hollow perhaps sixty paces across and not quite the height of four men deep. A pit. A sinkhole of the ordinary kind, the roof of some ancient cave long since fallen in and carried off as dust.

What is not ordinary is that the pit is brighter than the sky.

You notice it before you understand it. Standing at the rim in full midday sun, you find your eyes narrowing when you look down rather than up. The walls are pale — a calcareous white shading to cream and, in the seams, to a faint rose — and they throw the sun back and forth across the hollow until the light has nowhere left to go. But that alone would not account for it. The sun at noon is a single hard source. The light in the pit comes from everywhere. It comes up from the floor. It comes sideways from the walls. It seems to come, faintly, from the air itself, which has a thickened quality, a slight visible grain, like water seen from just above the surface.

The floor holds no shadow. Not under the lip, not under the single boulder that fell in some century past and sits, rounded and dusty, a little off center. Drop a pebble and it lands unaccompanied.

---

There is a way down. Not a made one — the built is so faint here it barely deserves the word — but on the northwest wall the rock has been worn into a series of shallow hollows, hand-width and knee-width, in a slanting line. Feet did that. Feet and palms, over a duration you cannot guess at, and not recently. The hollows are smoothed by weather now as much as by use. Near the bottom of the line someone once ground a single cup into a ledge of harder stone, a fist-sized depression that holds a spoonful of the night's condensation until mid-morning. There is nothing else. No hearth-blackening, no walls, no cairn. A flake of grey chert lies on the floor near the boulder, edge still sharp, so old it has acquired a skin of patina the color of tea. Whoever knapped it did not come back for it.

The descent takes a few minutes and the light rises around you like warm water as you go. By the time you stand on the floor it has closed over your head.

---

The floor is level, fine gravel and pale dust with a shallow low place toward the eastern side where rain gathers two or three times a year and takes a month or more to leave. Right now it is a skin of dampness only, dark against the pale, ringed with the mineral bands of its own retreat. Around it a few things grow. A tussock of grey-green sedge. A fern, improbably, tucked under the eastern wall where a seep-line stains the rock ochre. And on the south wall a fig, or something in the fig's family, that has come down from the rim as a mass of root, gripping and folding over the stone, bark pale as the rock it holds. It carries almost no leaves. Those it has are small, thick, and turned edge-on to the pit, as if the tree long ago decided that in this place the problem was not too little light but too much.

Lichens do better. They spread in the seams in colors that would be garish anywhere else — orange, sulfur-yellow, a blue so deep it looks wet — but here, drowned in brilliance, they seem merely reasonable.

---

The walls are not smooth. Closer, they are riddled: hundreds, perhaps thousands, of holes, each about the size of a child's fist, each polished at the mouth, each spaced a hand's breadth or more from its neighbors in a pattern that is not regular but is not random either. The whole cylinder of the pit is honeycombed. The rock between holes is solid; the holes are shallow, a forearm deep; and in each one, if you stand still long enough, something moves.

They are small — the length of your hand, no more — and they are the color of the wall, a white that takes a rose cast where the stone does, and they come out to sit. That is nearly all they do. Each one edges to the mouth of its hole, folds itself flat against the stone with its limbs tucked, and holds still in the light with its eyes closed. If you shift your weight the nearest ones withdraw, not quickly, and re-emerge within a minute. They do not call. They do not appear to eat, at least not while you watch; the pit has few insects and fewer seeds. They sit in the light the way other things sit in sun on a cold morning, except that it is not cold, and except that they are drinking.

You cannot see this. You infer it. The air near the wall, near a row of them, has a little less of that thick grain. The light is, imperceptibly, thinner there.

There are thousands of them and the pit is empty. This is the thing that takes longest to settle in you. Every wall is populated to the rim and yet the volume of the place holds nothing but air and light and a single dusty boulder; the life is all pressed to the edges, two-dimensional, a skin of small patient bodies around a hollow. Your footsteps are the loudest thing. When you stop, the quiet is almost complete — a faint tick as some part of the wall expands, a single drip at the seep, the dry sound of a lizard's belly against stone as it turns to face a slightly different angle.

---

Stay through the afternoon and the place begins to explain itself, slowly, in the only tempo it has.

The sun leaves the rim first. Out on the plain the shadows lengthen and the grass goes gold and then grey, and the sky above the pit softens to the color of old brass. The pit does not follow. It holds its light the way a stone holds heat, giving it up reluctantly, and for an hour after the plain has gone to dusk the hollow glows from below with a steady, sourceless whiteness that dims by degrees so gradual you can only measure them by looking away and looking back. The animals stay out. This, apparently, is the good time. They spread themselves a little wider on the stone.

Full dark never quite arrives. At the deepest part of the night the floor still gives a faint luminous grey, enough to see the boulder and the fig and the stepped line of the hand-hollows, enough that a moth — there is one moth — circles in it, confused.

And now the second thing happens. The air on the plain has cooled, and cool air is heavy, and it slides over the rim and down the walls and pools on the floor exactly as the light did in the daytime. It brings its moisture with it. By the middle of the night every surface below the rim is beaded. The lizard-things have withdrawn into their holes, and each hole-mouth wears a rim of droplets that catch the residual glow and make the honeycomb into something faintly starred. The cup ground into the ledge fills. The seep runs. The fig's white bark darkens to grey with wet, and you understand why its roots have never needed to reach the low pool.

Then morning. The sun finds the east wall and the light comes back in — not flooding, nothing here floods, but rising, the way you would fill a basin from a slow tap, until by mid-morning the hollow is full again and the wet has been taken back up into it, every bead gone, the walls dry and chalky, the cup emptying by evaporation as the animals come out in their thousands and fold themselves against the stone and close their eyes.

Two fluids, trading. Cold damp air by night; light by day. Each drains into the low place because that is what fluids do. Each is taken up again. The pit has been doing this for as long as it has been a pit, and it will do it until the walls have worn back grain by grain, hole by hole, into something too shallow to hold anything at all — a long time, longer than the chert flake has been lying there, longer than the fig.

---

Before you climb out you go back to the flake. You turn it in your fingers. It is very good work, a thin blade with a single clean ridge down the spine. You think about what it would be to come here, down the worn line of hollows, into a place with no shadow and no sound, where the walls are alive and still and the light is thick enough to drink. What you might make, or offer, or take. You put it back exactly where it was.

At the rim you look down once more. From here the animals are invisible, just texture on the stone. The pit is a bowl of white light in a grey-gold plain, and the only movement in it is the slow flexing of the one fig leaf that has turned, in the last hour, a fraction of a degree further from the glow.
