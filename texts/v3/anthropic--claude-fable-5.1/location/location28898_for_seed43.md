You come to it from above, which is the first strange thing — you arrive at a place by looking down into it.

The plain is nothing. Hardpan, a colour between dust and bone, and then a lip, and then the pit. It is not large. You could walk its rim in the time it takes to eat a piece of bread. The walls go down four or five body-heights, and they are made, every hand's breadth of them, of the same stuff: mud brick, thumbed and slapped and laid so long ago that the courses have slumped into one another and the joints have healed. Where the brick has spalled, someone has patched it with more brick, and that patch has spalled, and been patched. The wall is less a structure now than a habit the earth has kept.

And it is loud. That is the second thing. You hear it before the pit resolves into a shape — a low continuous roar, like a river heard through a door, with something inside it that pulses. When you reach the edge you see why. The whole inner face of the pit, top to bottom, is niches. Thousands of them. Each is the size of a loaf, cut back into the brick with a flat wooden blade, and each holds a bird, or two birds, or the grey ghost of a bird in a scatter of feathers and shell. Pigeons. Rock doves. Whatever they were when they were first coaxed here, they are now something that belongs only to this hole. The roar is their wings and their throats and the thousand small collisions of bodies in a space that was never quite enough.

A ramp descends, spiralling once around the wall. Its surface is not brick but a kind of cemented cake — dung and dust and dropped feathers and spilled water trodden by bare feet into a slab as hard as fired clay, worn to a channel down its centre. It is warm underfoot. Everything here is warm. The sun falls into the pit for a few hours at midday and the bricks drink it and give it back all night, and under that there is the second heat, the animal heat, several thousand small bodies at blood temperature breathing into an enclosed volume of air. By the time you are halfway down you can feel the air on your face like the breath of something that has been sleeping.

Near the bottom the wall changes. The lower niches are older — you can tell by the way their edges have rounded to nothing — and darker, and many are stopped with a plug of dung that has set like plaster. The birds do not go this low. This is where the dampness collects. The dome of the sky is the only roof, but the pit is deep and narrow enough that in the hours before dawn the air at the bottom cools past its dew point and the walls sweat. Water beads on the brick and runs in crooked lines down between the niches, and the lines have cut fine grooves over the centuries, and the grooves have been plastered over, and the water has found them again. At the floor there is a trough — a single long brick channel, lipped and lined with gypsum gone grey — and it holds a finger's depth of water that is never quite gone and never quite full. Nothing feeds it but the walls. The birds drink there in the morning in a great jostling ring, and the water is white with them by noon, and by the next dawn it is clear again.

The vegetation, such as it is, has come in on its own. A fig has rooted in a crack at the level where damp meets sun, and it grows sideways out of the wall like an arm, leaves sparse and enormous, trunk no thicker than a wrist and bent under its own reach. Its fruit fall into the trough. There is moss on the north-facing brick where the water runs, a green so dark it reads as black, and in the highest niches, where the birds have abandoned them to the wind, a few tufts of some grey grass whose seed blew in from the plain and found the accumulated dung agreeable. That is all. Nothing else can compete with the birds for the light or the space. The pit is not a garden. It is a hive.

And like a hive it has its parasites and its predators and its layered life. Beneath the top skin of the floor the guano is alive with beetles — you feel them before you see them, a faint shifting under the sole. Flies rise in the warm hours in a haze that catches the light. Small brown lizards live in the plugged lower niches and come out along the sweating wall to take the flies, and two or three kinds of snake live behind the lizards and take the lizards and, when they can, the squabs. You will see one sometimes threaded through the honeycomb of the wall, a rope of muscle moving from niche to niche a hand's breadth at a time while the birds on either side of it shriek and refuse to leave their eggs. A kestrel has a place on the rim, always the same place, a brick worn pale by her feet. Twice a day, at the great exodus and the great return, she drops into the column of birds and comes out of it with something. The roar does not change. There are too many of them for one death to register.

The exodus is the pit's one true silence — or as near as it gets. At first light a current starts somewhere in the wall, a shudder that travels round the spiral, and then the birds go up all at once in a spinning grey chimney that darkens the sky above the rim. For a while afterwards the pit is only water dripping and beetles and the fig's leaves clapping slowly in the updraft, and the walls stand revealed as what they are: a made thing, hand-shaped, bristling with ten thousand small dark mouths. Then, from midmorning on, the birds return in ones and hundreds, and the sound rebuilds itself layer by layer until by evening it is the same roar you heard from the plain.

People made this. People still come. There is a wooden scraper leaning in a corner of the ramp, its blade a flat of hard acacia worn to a bevel, and beside it a stack of shallow reed baskets, and a coil of palm-fibre rope. A few times a year they come down with these things and cut the floor. They take the cake of dung away in the baskets to spread on fields you cannot see from here, and for a season the floor is lower and the trough sits higher and the birds are anxious. Then the floor climbs back. Nobody feeds the birds. Nobody waters them. The pit is an arrangement, agreed upon so long ago that the terms have become geology: the people give walls and take dung, the birds give dung and take walls, the fig takes water and gives fruit to no one who wants it, the snakes and the kestrel take their tithe, and the whole thing turns.

The niches have been recut so many times that in places the wall between two of them is thinner than a fingernail, and you can see light through it. Somewhere in the lowest, oldest course, behind the plugs, there are niches that have not held a bird in a hundred years and are full to the lip with the feathers and bones and shells of birds that lived when the wall was new. The pit remembers everything that has died in it, in the only way brick remembers: by getting slowly, quietly thicker.

If you sit on the ramp at the level of the fig, with your back against the warm wall and the roar around you, you will feel the birds' small movements transmitted through the brick into your spine — a hum, almost, underneath the noise. The place is not peaceful. It is not meant to be. But it is finished, in the way a thing is finished when it has become the only shape it could be.
