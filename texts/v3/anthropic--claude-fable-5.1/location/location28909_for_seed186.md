**The Shelf**

There is a height above which the mountains stop being mountains and become a floor. The ridges that took days to climb flatten and spread and lose their names, and what remains is a plain so high that the sky sits on it like a lid. The air here is thin enough that a shout would carry only a few strides before it thinned out into nothing. No one shouts. There is no one.

It is always dark on the shelf, or near enough. Whatever sun this world has lies below the rim for most of the year, and the light that leaks over is a bruise-colored smear along one horizon, enough to tell up from down and not much else. The stars are there but they are few, and the cold seems to blunt them. What light there is on the ground comes from the ground.

---

Underfoot, the shelf is not rock. It is a mat of something black and leathery and dense, knee-deep in the shallows, chest-deep in the hollows, glossy with condensation. Call it moss for lack of a better word, though it has the texture of wet slate and grows in interlocking plates like the scales of something asleep. It is lush past any expectation of the altitude. It crowds. It fills every seam in the stone and heaves up over boulders and drapes into the fissures in curtains. Where you push through it, it closes behind you with a sound like a coat being folded.

It does not use light. It cannot; there is none. Its roots reach down into warmth and its canopy reaches up into cold and it lives on the difference, drinking the gradient the way other plants drink sun. Wherever heat meets frost, it grows. This is the only rule of its distribution, and it explains everything about the shape of the place.

Because the shelf is warm underneath. Not everywhere, and not all at once.

---

The heat moves. This is the thing that takes longest to understand, and the thing that, once understood, reorganizes everything else.

Beneath the moss the stone is fractured into enormous slabs, each the size of a village, fitted against one another along seams worn smooth by an age of grinding. Under the slabs there is pressure, and the pressure travels. It rises beneath one slab and the slab lifts — a hand's breadth, a forearm, over the course of hours — and the seams around it open and the fissures begin to exhale. Warm air comes up out of the black mat in slow columns. Frost sublimates off the leaves in curls. Meltwater beads and runs and gathers in the seams, and for a few hours there is a river on the shelf, a real one, sliding along the crack between two slabs, black and quick and silent in the thin air. Then the pressure passes on. The slab settles with a sound you feel in your teeth more than hear — a single low *tock*, like a great clock advancing. The river stops. The water that has not drained freezes where it lies into sheets that will not thaw until the pressure comes round again.

And it does come round. Not at random. The warmth travels across the shelf in a pattern that repeats — not a straight line, not a circle, something more like the path of a shuttle across a loom, working the whole width of the plain and returning to its origin in a span that is close to, but not quite, forty days. The slabs rise and fall in sequence. The rivers run in sequence. The moss thickens along the route the warmth takes and thins in the places it takes longest to return to. Nothing built this. The stone was fractured this way by cooling long ago and the pressure found its paths and has kept to them for longer than anything living here has existed, and the grinding of the seams has polished the fit until the slabs move in their beds like pistons. The shelf is a machine in the way a heart is a machine. It was not made. It runs.

You come to hear it, after a while. Not with the ears — the air will not carry it — but through the moss, through your knees when you kneel, through the bones of your face when you lie down. A vast, slow, distant mechanical patience. Somewhere on the far side of the plain a slab is settling. Somewhere nearer, one is beginning to lift. The ground hums at a pitch below hearing. This is the only industry the shelf has ever known: heat, moved, on schedule, in quantities that would warm a city, spent entirely on moss and cold and dark.

---

The animals follow the warmth.

You will not see many. This is the strangeness of the place — that there is so much life here and so little of it in any one spot. The shelf is the size of a region and the herds are spread across it as thinly as stars, each one shadowing a slab that is currently lifting, so that from any given vantage there may be nothing, for hours, but the black mat and the smear of horizon and the frost coming down. Then a shape moves at the edge of what your eyes can make out, and it is the size of an ox, and it is close, and you had no idea.

They are quiet the way everything here is quiet. Broad-footed, low-slung, wrapped in something between fur and felt that has gone the color of the moss so completely that they read as ripples in it. They graze the leathery plates with slow lateral chewing. Their breath is the only warm thing you have seen in days, and it catches what light there is and glows faintly for a moment before it freezes and falls as a fine glitter around their heads. Their eyes give back a dull red — not a reflection, there is nothing to reflect, but a light of their own, banked and low, the color of the deepest part of a fissure.

They move on when the slab settles. They are already walking before the *tock* arrives, as if the pressure passes through them first. A herd will string out along the seam between the cooling slab and the warming one, a long slow procession in the dark, and follow the warmth all the way across the shelf and back. Their whole lives are this circuit. Their young are born on the warm slabs and their dead are left on the cold ones, and the cold ones keep them.

There are other things. Smaller things that live inside the moss and never come out, and which you know only because the mat twitches, here and there, as if under a skin. Long pale things that come up out of the fissures when the warm air flows and hang in it with their arms spread and go back down when it stops. A kind of bird — if that is what it is — that does not fly but glides on the columns of rising air from one fissure to the next, wings like sheets of frost, and which you will never see except as a paleness passing overhead against no stars. Predators, presumably; the herds have the alertness of prey. But nothing hunts where you can see it. In this dark, in this quiet, the killing is done as everything is done: somewhere else, at the pace of the machine.

---

What the shelf produces, in the end, is frost.

Every cycle of the slabs lifts water out of the deep stone and delivers it, as breath, to the cold. It freezes on the moss. It freezes on the beasts. It freezes in the seams into layers that the next warm phase melts and re-lays, so that every seam on the shelf is lined with ice a hundred thousand cycles thick, banded fine as the grain of wood. This is the erosion of the place — not wind, not water running downhill, but the endless recasting of the same water in the same beds. The slabs have been ground to fit. The seams have been polished by ice. The fissure mouths have been rounded off by warm breath into smooth black throats that look, in the almost-dark, like something a machinist turned. Nothing has been carried away. Everything has been worked into its place and left there.

And the moss governs. Where it grows thick it insulates and slows the heat loss and the slab beneath it cools more slowly and lifts for longer. Where it grows thin the heat vents fast and the slab drops early. Over an age this has tuned the cycle: the mat has crept toward a thickness that keeps the shuttle of warmth moving at exactly the rate that keeps the mat alive. It is possible the moss is a single organism. It is possible the shelf has been regulating itself this way since before the first beast walked its circuit, and that the beasts are simply the most recent part it has grown to move its heat around more efficiently — fur, hoof, and glowing breath, to carry a little warmth from one slab to the next on the down-cycle.

It is possible, too, that the dead left on the cold slabs are not left there by accident. They lie in the seams. When the warmth comes round, the seams open. What goes down into the fissures on a shelf like this does not come back up, but the warm air that follows smells, faintly and for a little while, of something that is not stone.

---

At the edges — and it takes days to reach them — the moss thins to lichen and the slabs stop lifting and the shelf simply ends, dropping away into cloud so far below that the tops of it look solid, a second floor a world beneath the first. The cold there is absolute. Nothing follows the warmth that far because the warmth does not go that far. You stand at the rim with the black mat behind you and the smear of horizon ahead and the whole plain at your back humming below hearing, and you understand that the place is not empty. It only looks empty because it is so large and so dark and everything in it is somewhere else, doing what the schedule requires.

Then a slab, a long way off, settles.

*Tock.*

And the frost comes down.
