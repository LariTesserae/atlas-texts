The sound reaches you before anything else does. From a long way off — from the sea, from the air, from wherever you approach — it arrives first as a pressure in the sternum rather than a noise, a low continuous shudder that makes the fluid in your ears feel unsettled. Closer, it resolves into something like a waterfall heard from inside the waterfall. Closer still, it stops resolving. It is simply the condition of being there. On the plateau you cannot hear your own voice, and after a few hours you stop trying to use it.

What is making the noise is the ground. Except there is no ground.

The plateau rises about two kilometers above the surrounding lowlands and runs for something on the order of four thousand kilometers along its longest axis, and every square meter of it, from the cliff-edge to the interior, is a single living tissue. Not a forest — a forest has spaces in it, has a floor, has the possibility of walking between things. This has no between. The stems, if they are stems, are packed against one another so tightly that their outer walls have fused, and what you stand on is a continuous ribbed surface, dark green shading into black, slick with condensation, cool as cellar stone. It flexes very slightly under weight. It is faintly warm at the seams and cold everywhere else. Where it rises into ridges the ridges are also tissue; where it falls into hollows the hollows are lined with the same skin, beaded with the same water, humming with the same vibration you feel through the soles of your feet. There is no soil anywhere on the plateau and there never has been. There is no rock exposed anywhere on it. The plant is the terrain, and the terrain is packed to saturation with the plant.

The light is the second thing. It is never direct. The plateau breathes out water in such quantities that it lives under its own permanent overcast — a lid of cloud that forms a few hundred meters above the surface, thickens, rains itself back down, and re-forms, all within the span of an afternoon. What filters through is a grey-green dimness, brighter at the horizon than overhead, the color of light through a jar of pond water. It is enough to see by and never enough to cast a shadow. The surface of the tissue is dark, nearly black in the hollows, and it drinks what little light there is. Photographs of the plateau taken from orbit show a vast dark stain shaped like a lung, wrapped in cloud, with no interior features at all.

Then the water, which is everywhere and nowhere. It falls constantly — not as rain exactly but as a drifting saturated fog that condenses on every surface, runs together into rivulets, and vanishes. There are no rivers on the plateau. There are no lakes, no pools, no standing water of any kind, though the surface is never dry. The water runs a few meters across the ribbed skin and then simply goes in — through pores you can see if you kneel, each one the diameter of a fingertip, rimmed with a paler tissue that pulses slightly, and each one taking in water faster than water ought to be able to move. Kneel there and put your palm flat against the surface and you feel the intake as a steady cold suction against your skin. The whole continent is drinking. And somewhere beneath you, in vessels you will never see, the water it has drunk is being lifted.

That lifting is the noise.

Water does not want to be raised two kilometers in a column. Past a certain height, under a certain tension, the column snaps — the liquid pulls apart from itself, a bubble of near-vacuum opens in the vessel, and the vessel is dead. Ordinary trees spend their whole lives managing this risk, and lose to it constantly, and fail if they grow too tall. The plateau lifts an ocean's worth of water into the sky every day, and it does so by letting the columns snap. In every one of the trillions of conducting vessels running from the roots to the canopy skin, the water breaks, and each break is a tiny report — a click, a crack, a snap — and the vessel reseals itself around the breach in a way that no one has adequately explained, and refills, and lifts, and breaks again. The individual sound is nothing. Multiplied across four thousand kilometers of tissue it is a roar that can be recorded from the far side of the planet as a distinct line in the seismic record, a continent-sized instrument being played by its own thirst. It has structure if you attend to it long enough. There are slow tides in it — the whole plateau swells over a period of about nine days as water accumulates in its upper tissues, and the sound rises with it, deepens, becomes almost a tone; then over two or three days the mass releases, venting into the cloud lid in great blooms of fog that can be seen from space, and the pitch drops and the ground actually subsides beneath you by a measurable few centimeters. Storms follow the release. The cloud lid, overloaded, collapses in rain that is heavier than anywhere else on the planet, and the rain goes into the pores, and the cycle begins again.

Underneath the tissue — beneath the living skin, beneath the vessels, down at the roots — the plateau is making stone.

This is what it is for, if it is for anything. Dead tissue here does not rot; there is nothing to rot it, and the interior is cold and anaerobic and saturated with silica-bearing water the plant has pulled up from the deep. As the oldest cells die they are filled in, replaced molecule by molecule with mineral, until they are no longer cells but rock retaining the exact shape of cells. The plateau is a living skin over a growing body of stone that it has laid down itself, layer on layer, for however long it has been here — and the stone has never once seen weather. No rain has fallen on it. No wind has touched it. No frost has entered a crack, because it has no cracks; it was never fractured, never lifted, never cooled from a melt. It is geology without erosion, rock that has not yet been introduced to the processes that will eventually shape it. If you could see it — if the skin were stripped back — it would be a pale, dense, faintly translucent mass, with a texture like packed frost, still soft in its uppermost layers where the mineral has not finished setting. The plateau's two kilometers of elevation are two kilometers of this. It is not a mountain that has been carved. It is a mountain that is being poured.

At the edges you can see it happening. The plateau does not end in a cliff so much as in a growth front — a curving margin, hundreds of kilometers long, where the tissue is younger, paler, wetter, and the sound is loudest, because the new vessels here are the most numerous and the least practiced at not dying. The front advances into the lowland at a rate that can be measured in meters per year. Below it, exposed for the first time, is the stone it has left behind: still white, still soft enough to scratch, and beginning — right there, at the moment of exposure — to be rained on for the first time. This is the only place on the plateau where erosion exists at all, a thin strip a few meters wide where rain has just begun to find the grain of the rock, and the rock has just begun to remember that it can be worn.

There is nothing else alive here. Not because nothing could survive, though the noise alone would deter most things; but because there is no room and no need. The plateau pollinates nothing, fruits nothing, is eaten by nothing, and has left no niche unfilled that another organism might occupy. Mold does not grow on it. Lichen has not found purchase. The water that runs across it is nearly sterile, filtered through kilometers of tissue. Occasionally a bird is carried in on a storm from the coast and cannot find its way out of the cloud and comes down on the surface, and lies there, and the water runs over it, and within a few days it has gone into the pores like everything else. That is the extent of the fauna. The silence of animals is total, and inside the roar it is somehow audible: a specific absence, a missing register.

It was made. This much is not in question, though there is nothing on the plateau or anywhere near it that looks made — no structure, no artifact, no trace of a hand. What was made was the organism, at a scale of design that is not visible at the scale of the eye. Its vessels reseal because they were built to reseal. Its tissue mineralizes because it was built to mineralize, in the right minerals, in the right order, at the right density to bear its own weight. It lifts water the way it does because someone — long ago, or far away, or both — worked out how to make it do that, and then left nothing behind but the doing. Whether the stone it is making is the point, or the cloud, or the sound, or something else that has not become apparent yet, nobody can say. It may be that the plateau is a foundation for something not yet begun. It may be that the plateau is the thing, and the stone is the byproduct, the way a reef is a byproduct. It may be that it has forgotten what it was for and is simply continuing.

Stand in the interior at the low point of the nine-day tide, when the pitch is at its lowest and the fog is thinnest and the grey light is as bright as it ever gets. The surface is cold and wet and ridged and gives slightly. The roar has become something like silence — you have stopped hearing it, and what remains is the sensation of being inside a very large body that is working. Water beads on your skin and runs down and disappears into the plant. Somewhere two kilometers beneath you, unseen, unweathered, stone is being laid down in the shape of cells. Somewhere at the margin, hundreds of kilometers off, rain is touching rock for the first time. Overhead the cloud is thickening. In a day the tide will turn and the plateau will begin to swell, and the tone will rise, and the ground will lift you, imperceptibly, with it.
