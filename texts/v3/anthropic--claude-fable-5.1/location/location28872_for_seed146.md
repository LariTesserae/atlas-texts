**The Underfloor**

Begin with the ceiling, because the ceiling is what you notice first and last. It is close. Not so close you can touch it, but close enough that you feel it, the way you feel a person standing behind you in a dark room. Six, eight, twelve meters up, a lid of black rock stretching in every direction to a horizon you cannot see but can sense the distance of, the way you sense the size of a hall by the way sound fails to come back.

Under the ceiling: the floor. Between them: the place.

It is a gap in the crust. Not a cave — caves are carved, and nothing carved this. It is a parting. At some point, not long ago in the reckoning of stone, one layer of the world lifted a little away from the layer beneath it, along a plane a continent wide, and the two never came back together. The floor is the top of what stayed. The ceiling is the underside of what rose. Where they broke, they broke sharp. Every surface here is a fracture surface: glassy, conchoidal, keen enough to open a hand. No grain of it has ever been rounded. There is nothing here to do the rounding.

---

It is warm. Uniformly, seamlessly, foundationally warm — the warmth of a body, or of a stone left in sun and then wrapped so the heat cannot leave. It comes up through the floor from somewhere deeper, and it goes nowhere, because there is nowhere for it to go. The ceiling holds it. The rock above is thick enough that whatever is on the far side of it — sky, ocean, ice, nothing — has never once made itself felt down here. The temperature at any point in the Underfloor is the temperature at every other point. You could walk for a year and never find a place that was a degree different from where you started.

Because there is no difference, there is no flow. Because there is no flow, there is no wear. This is the first rule of the place, and everything else follows from it: *the Underfloor is finished.* It arrived at itself in the moment of its parting and has not moved since. The edges are new because the edges will always be new.

There is air. It moves, but it moves the way a wheel moves when it is spinning perfectly — you can only tell by the faint constant pressure on one cheek. Over the whole span of the gap the air turns in a single vast slow cell, rising nowhere in particular, sinking nowhere in particular, a weather that has one state and holds it. It carries a smell: sulfur, hot dust, and something animal and dry, like the inside of a hive.

---

Light: there is almost none, and you must spend a long time in it before the *almost* begins to mean anything.

After an hour, the darkness has texture. After a day, you can make out the ceiling — not the rock itself but the faint lesser dark where it is, against the faint greater dark of the gaps between the pillars. The pillars are places where the parting was incomplete, where the floor and the ceiling are still joined by a neck of unbroken stone, and there is one every few hundred meters, or every few kilometers, and they go on forever. They are what keep the sky up.

After longer than a day, you see the glow. It is a deep, deep red, at the very bottom of what an eye can hold, and it does not come from any one place. It is the rock itself, warm enough to shed a whisper of light at the edge of the visible. It is not enough to cast a shadow. It is enough to know that the floor is there, that it is uneven, that it is *covered*.

---

Covered with what. This is where the place stops being a geometry and becomes a world.

The floor is alive. Not metaphorically. From the base of one pillar to the base of the next, the fractured glass of the floor is carpeted, layered, roofed over by bodies, and the bodies are pressed together so closely that they form a second surface a hand's depth above the first. They are pale — everything here is pale, having no reason not to be — and dry, and slow. Warmth is what they eat. They lie against the floor and take from it directly, through undersides that are all one broad organ, and the warmth they take is replaced from below at exactly the rate they take it, and so they are never hungry and never full and never move to find better. There is no better. It is all the same.

There are many kinds. You learn them by touch and by the sounds they make, which are few and small. There are the flat ones, wide as a table and thick as a hand, that the others walk across. There are the ones like bundles of hollow reed, standing upright in clusters, that give slightly and then hold. There are things the size of a fist with too many legs, packed shoulder to shoulder in the seams between the larger animals, and things with no legs at all that lie in coils, and things that hang from the ceiling on threads of their own making and never descend. There are tall stilt-walkers that stand above the carpet, motionless for what seems like days, and then take one step. Some of them are blind. Some have eyes so large and so dark that they take up most of the head, adapted to a light that is barely there. None of them have anything like a mouth as you would understand it, because nothing here is bitten. They are all, in the end, eating the same meal.

The density is hard to convey. It is not that there are many of them. It is that there is no gap. If you stand, you are standing on something. If you kneel, you are kneeling on something else. If you reach out in the dark your hand closes on a flank or a limb or a shell. You are never more than an arm's length from a dozen creatures. Across the whole continent of the gap, this holds. The Underfloor is not a place that has animals in it. It is a place that is made of animals, with rock underneath.

---

They do not fear you. Fear would require something to have ever happened.

And they are quiet. This is the strangest thing, given the numbers. There is a sound in the Underfloor, and it is the sound of all of them at once — a dry, layered, enormous hush, like wind in a field of dead grass if the field were the size of a nation and the wind never varied. It is made of breathing, and of the small scrape of chitin against chitin when something shifts its weight, and of a soft clicking that some of the stilt-walkers make and that seems to serve no purpose at all. Very rarely one of the ceiling-hangers drops a thread and it sings for a moment as it goes taut. That is the loudest thing that happens.

Under the hush is real silence, the kind that rock makes. Under that is nothing.

---

There is no water. Say it plainly, because the body keeps looking for it. There is no spring, no seep, no drip from the ceiling; the ceiling is dry as a kiln, and has been since the parting. The creatures do not drink. Whatever holds them together is waxy, salt-based, sealed. If you break one open — you would have to, to know — the inside is the consistency of old tallow and smells of sulfur and iron, and there is no liquid in it at all. The nearest thing to moisture in the whole gap is a faint crust of hygroscopic salt that forms on the pillars, drawing from the air the trace of vapor the air carries, and it never gets thicker, because it takes exactly what the air gives and the air gives exactly what it took.

And there are no plants. No moss, no lichen, no fungus. Nothing grows toward light because there is no light to grow toward; nothing puts down roots because the floor is glass and needs no breaking. The food web here has no base you would recognize. It starts at the rock and goes directly into the animals and stops. Nothing dies, or nothing dies in a way that leaves anything behind. There is no soil. There is no humus. There is no rot. Whatever happens to a creature when it stops is exact and complete and returns whatever it was made of to wherever it came from, and the floor is as clean afterward as it was before.

---

Then, in the midst of all this, the marks.

They are scattered. You could cross a hundred kilometers of the Underfloor and see none, and then find three within an hour. A pile of fractured glass stacked into a low wall, knee-high, curving as if to break a wind that does not blow. A pillar with a band of scratches around it at the height of a shoulder — deliberate, counted, in groups of five. A blade of knapped obsidian lying on a shelf of rock above the carpet, its edge as new as the day it was struck, because no day since has touched it. A handprint pressed in something dark and greasy against a fracture face, fingers spread. A flat stone set on three smaller stones in what can only be a table, and nothing on it.

Whoever made them used what was here. There is no wood, so there is nothing burned; there is no clay, so there is nothing fired. There is glass, and there is the pale waxy stuff of the animals, and there is, in a few places where the floor cracked deep enough, native copper in threads, and someone found it and hammered it flat between two stones into a thing like a hook. The hook hangs on a thread from the ceiling. It hangs perfectly still.

There are no bones. There are no bodies. Whoever they were, they left the way everything leaves here — completely — or they did not leave at all and are somewhere in the carpet, standing very still, indistinguishable now from the stilt-walkers except by the tools they no longer hold.

You do not know if the marks are old. That is the problem with the Underfloor. They cannot be old, because nothing here ages; the wall would be the same wall after a thousand years as after an afternoon. The handprint is wet-looking. The blade is sharp. Everything in the Underfloor was made yesterday, or was never made at all.

---

This, finally, is what the place is: a world that happened once.

It broke open, it filled with warmth, the warmth filled with life, the life filled every gap until there were no gaps left, and something came through and stacked a few stones and cut a few marks and went. And then the Underfloor closed around all of it like wax around a wick and held. It does not remember, because remembering implies a before and after, and there is no after here. It does not anticipate. It does not erode, or grow, or drift, or cool. The wheel of air turns. The floor breathes its long dry breath. The red glow sits at the bottom of the eye, exactly as bright as it has always been.

If you walk, you can walk forever, over the backs of the animals, between the pillars, under the near black lid, and the place will not change to meet you. It will not become somewhere else. It has already become what it is, all the way to its edges, and its edges are as sharp as they were on the first morning, which is the only morning it has ever had.
