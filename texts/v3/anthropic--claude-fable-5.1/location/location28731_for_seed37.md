You find it by going down, though not far. A slope of turf gives way under one foot, not a fall so much as a step the ground hadn't finished making, and you are in a cavity the size of a small bedroom, roots knotted overhead where the ceiling should be and, past the roots, no sky. Just stone. The stone is close enough to touch and you do touch it, and it is cool, and it is wet, and it is not quite stone yet.

That's the first thing the place tells you about itself. Everything here is slightly before the moment of being what it is. The rock has the color and heft of limestone but when you press your palm to it there's a give, not softness exactly, more like the surface hasn't committed. In places it has the look of a decision in progress: a band of grey that might become flint, a seam of ochre that is auditioning for the role of clay. Nothing has cracked, because nothing has been solid long enough to crack. Nothing has been worn, because there has been no time yet for wearing. This is a room in which geology is being drafted, and the draft is still open.

And it is bright. That's the wrong thing about it, or the first wrong thing. There is no lamp, no opening to a sun, no glow from any particular surface, and yet the light is the light of a clear morning, sharp, evenly distributed, casting shadows that are crisp at the edges. The shadows are the problem. They fall inward, toward the center of the room, from every direction at once, as though the light were coming from the walls and converging on a point roughly where you're standing. Your own shadow pools at your feet like something spilled. When you move it takes a moment to follow.

The water here is not a stream or a pool. It's in the air and on the surfaces, a damp that beads on the underside of the roots and gathers on the walls in droplets that grow, slowly, until one lets go. The drop falls. You watch one hit the floor and you see the floor react, not splash but *learn*, a small circle of clay darkening, then the darkness spreading in a way that is not how liquid spreads, more like how an idea moves through a crowd. A minute later that patch of floor is wetter than the drop could account for. Somewhere the concept of moisture is propagating slightly faster than the moisture itself.

There are plants, a few. A fern that has three fronds, each in a different stage: one unfurled, one a tight coil, one that is only a slightly greener thickness in the stem, as if the fern were showing you all its tenses at once. A patch of moss the size of your hand that is, when you look closely, not moss but the memory of moss on the stone, the impression it will leave a long time from now, already present. A pale root that has come down through the ceiling and stopped an inch above the floor and is simply waiting there, a finger pointing at a spot the floor has not yet decided to be.

But mostly there are animals.

That is the thing that makes the room feel occupied rather than merely strange. In a space this size, with vegetation this thin, there should be nothing, a beetle, a worm. Instead they are everywhere, and yet the room does not feel crowded, because they are spaced out with a kind of deliberateness, one presence per patch of floor, one per handspan of wall, each with a wide margin of emptiness around it. A newt on a shelf of half-rock, motionless, its orange belly the brightest thing you've seen down here. A wren in the roots, or something that is a wren from where you stand and, when you shift a step to the left, is a large brown moth, and, when you shift back, is a wren again, having not moved. A snail on the wall, shell glistening, that leaves a trail behind it going both directions. Something like a vole crossing the floor with the specific, hurried purpose of voles, though it crosses a distance that should take it three seconds and takes it much longer than that, and when it reaches the far wall it goes in, not into a hole, into the wall, the way a hand goes into water.

You count and lose count. Thirty, forty, sixty of them. None of them make a sound. That's not quite right either: the newt's throat pulses, the wren's beak opens and shuts in the rhythm of song, and there is a faint pressure against your ears as of sound that has not yet resolved into hearing. The silence is not the absence of noise. It is noise that is still on its way.

They are not afraid of you. They are not, you begin to think, entirely aware of you, or aware of you only in the way you'd be aware of weather. What they are attending to is each other, and the attending is the ecology of the place. The newt watches a beetle. The beetle is moving toward a crack that is, as it approaches, becoming a crack, the stone splitting itself just ahead of the beetle's need for shelter. The newt's watching gets sharper. There is a moment where the newt has clearly eaten the beetle, has the fullness of having eaten it, though the beetle is still there, still moving. Then the beetle is gone and the newt is exactly as it was, and the crack closes, unneeded. You have the sense of having watched a hunt in which the outcome arrived before the events, and the events then hurried to catch up with it.

There are marks. Scattered, faint, and clearly made. On one wall, at about the height of a kneeling person, seven short scratches in a row, parallel, deliberate. On the floor near the entrance, three flat stones stacked in a way flat stones do not stack on their own. A line pressed into the clay that runs from one wall to the other and stops precisely at both, a boundary someone measured. Nothing more than that. No tool could have made them because there is nothing here that is a tool, and the animals have no hands. And yet they are old, older than the room, which is not old at all. The scratches are worn at the edges in a place where nothing else is worn. It is as if the marks were made first, somewhere, sometime, and then the wall was assembled around them to give them something to be on.

You go to touch the scratches and find your fingers already in them, already fitted to the grooves, seven fingers, and you look down and have five, and the wall has seven scratches and there's a gap where the impossibility should be and the room simply doesn't fill it.

The cool is steady. Cave-cool, cellar-cool, the temperature of a place that has never been warm and has never been frozen, that took its temperature from the average of a year it hasn't lived through. Your breath doesn't fog. When you exhale you can see the air move, the droplets on the nearest root trembling, and one of them swelling in response, growing on your breath, letting go, falling, and the floor learning it, darkening, spreading. You are part of the weather here. There is weather here, in a room the size of a bedroom: a cycle of condensation and release, slow, patient, and you can see the whole of it at once the way you could hold a season in your two hands if a season were small enough.

What you begin to understand, standing very still, is that this is not a place that has broken down. It's a place that has not yet finished coming up. The rules you're used to, that light arrives from somewhere, that effects follow causes, that a thing is one thing and not its neighbor, those rules haven't been laid down here yet. They are, you suspect, being laid down. The stone is deciding to be stone. The wren is slowly becoming more wren than moth, though it may take a long time, and it may go the other way. The seven scratches will one day have been made by something. The room is a hollow in the world where the world is still being written, and the animals are here because animals are always where things are happening, and the light is here because the light does not yet know it should have a source.

You could stay. You have the impression that the place would begin, gently, to decide about you as well. You notice a footprint in the clay by the far wall, a bare human print, and you are wearing shoes, and the print is drying at the edges.

You go back up the way you came, the step the ground hadn't finished making, and at the top the turf closes over the gap not with a sound but with a quiet like a held breath letting go, and the hill is a hill, and the grass is grass, and there is a sun in the sky doing what suns do, and your shadow falls behind you all in one piece, and you carry on it, for the rest of the afternoon, a faint inward tug, as if some part of you were still down there in the bright, being drafted.
