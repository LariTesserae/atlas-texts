You come up onto it through cloud, and the first thing you understand is that the cloud has been trying to tell you something for the last thousand meters: the rock has stopped. There has been no rock for a while. The path under your boots is a lattice of cast grating, warm, and beneath the grating is more grating, and beneath that a slab, and the slab has the faint tidal pattern of a pour that set too fast. The mountain you were climbing ends somewhere in the fog below and something else continues upward from it, keyed onto the summit like a crown on a tooth.

Then the cloud tears and the plateau is there and it is very, very bright.

—

The light comes from everywhere and lands on nothing. That is the first rule. There is no sun; you look for one out of habit, shading your eyes, and there is only a sky the color of a fluorescent tube seen through paper, evenly lit from horizon to horizon, the whole dome humming with illumination. Objects cast shadows but the shadows are wrong—soft, short, pooled directly under things regardless of where the things stand, as though every object were lit from a lamp positioned exactly above it and only it. Two chimneys thirty meters apart each have a tidy disc of gray at the base and nothing between. You hold up your hand and your shadow gathers under your palm like water in a cup.

The brightness makes the plateau seem larger than it is, or smaller, and this too will turn out to be one of the rules.

—

It is a made place, totally. There is no soil. There is no stone that is not aggregate bound in cement. The ground is decking and slab and diamond plate and rolled steel, and where the ground rises it rises as stairs, ramps, spiral gantries with the stamped tread still bright. Nothing has rusted. Nothing has weathered. The edges of every I-beam are so fresh they would take skin. In some places the concrete is still darker where it has not finished curing, and you can see the wale marks of formwork removed recently enough that the wood grain is printed in the surface, sharp as a fingerprint. Pipes leave weep-marks of white efflorescence on the walls beneath them, and this is the only aging visible anywhere, and it is the aging of weeks.

The whole tableland is young. It has the look of a thing extruded. The plateau edge, where it drops back into cloud, does not crumble or fray—it terminates, in a clean shear, and along the shear there is a lip of overpoured slab like the drip along the rim of a cake pan. If you lie on your belly and look over, you see the underside of the plateau: girder, girder, girder, and beyond them the distant true rock, gray and ordinary and receding, and between the two a gap of open air where the wind should be but isn't.

—

There is very little wind. There is very little sound of any kind.

This is a factory landscape—stacks, coolers, cracking towers, conveyor bridges crossing overhead at three and four levels, cable trays sagging with the weight of what they carry, transformer yards fenced with expanded metal, water tanks on splayed legs, a hundred hundred blind windowless sheds each large enough to hold a ship—and it makes almost no noise. You can hear the pipes ticking as they warm and cool. You can hear, if you press your ear to a housing, the deep interior turn of something that never varies. Occasionally, far off, a relay closes with a sound like a single dropped coin. That is all. The scale of the machinery implies a roar, and the roar is not there, and the absence sits on the ears like altitude.

You begin to suspect the sound is being *made* somewhere else. That the plant runs, and the noise of its running is a product, collected, piped, and shipped off along with whatever else it makes. The quiet is not an accident. It is the sign of good yield.

—

Cool air, wet air. This is high country, and the plant is warm, and where the two meet the plateau sweats. Every cold surface is beaded. Handrails drip. Long condensation stains map the underside of every conveyor bridge. Water gathers in the low points of the decking and stands there in shallow black rectangles perfectly framed by the joins, reflecting the shadowless sky as flat bright panes, so that walking the plateau you keep stepping past windows into another identical brightness set into the floor. It does not rain so much as *precipitate*—a fine wet standing haze that comes and goes on a cycle you can't quite measure, that leaves everything slick and then, an hour later, is gone, and the surfaces are dry and warm to the touch and the water has gone somewhere.

It has gone into the plant. Everything goes into the plant. There are drains everywhere, gridded, immaculate.

—

Between the sheds, in the seams of the slab, in the expansion joints packed with black mastic, things grow. Not much. Moss in the shaded corners where the shadowless light somehow still fails to reach—a green so saturated against the gray it looks painted. Lichen, orange and pale, already colonizing concrete that cannot be more than a season old, growing at a speed lichen should not grow. In a courtyard between two cooling stacks someone or something has set out trees, in a grid, each in a square hole cut through the deck, each the same species and the same height, each with the same eleven leaves. You count them. Eleven. The trees are very healthy. They are also very still.

Mostly the plateau is metal and light and the smell of wet cement and ozone, and the green is punctuation.

—

But it is full of animals.

This is what takes longest to see, because the brightness flattens everything and the quiet removes the cues you would use. Then you notice that the girders overhead are lined—not with birds, exactly, but with birds. Pigeons. Hundreds of them, close-packed shoulder to shoulder along every flange, gray on gray, watching. Not one of them makes a sound. In the transformer yard there are goats. They stand among the ceramic insulators and do not chew. Swallows nest in the seams of the pipe racks in mud cups so numerous the pipes appear scaled. There are cats on the tank platforms, foxes under the conveyors, a heron standing one-legged in a black rectangle of standing water with its reflection standing one-legged beneath it, and the reflection blinks a half-second late.

Everything is very close together. Every volume of the plateau holds more than it should: pipes near enough to touch on all sides, sheds whose walls you can put your hands on both of at once in the passage between, animals standing within arm's reach that do not step away. The density is not crowding. It is saturation—as if the place were a solution and things had been added to it until nothing more would dissolve, and then a little more had been added anyway.

None of the animals behave. That is the wrong word, but it is the closest. They do not flee. They do not feed. They stand, or perch, or lie, and they watch you cross, and when you look back they have not moved, and when you look back again some of them are in a different place and still have not moved. You will see the same fox three times in three different passages, always lying on its left side, always with the tip of its tail folded across its nose. You will decide that it is three foxes. This decision will not entirely hold.

—

Here is where the place begins to become itself, and the making of it becomes visible.

You cross a conveyor bridge and, halfway across, notice that the belt beneath the grating is running. Slowly. Slower than walking. It carries nothing you can name—gray, in blocks, wet, the texture of the slab under your feet. You follow it with your eyes to where it disappears into a shed and you understand that the plateau is making more plateau. That is the product, or one of them. The plant's business is its own ground.

At the far edge, in the direction the conveyors run, the slab is newer. Newer and newer as you walk, until it is still dark with damp and then still soft—your boot leaves a faint impression—and then there is a forming edge, plywood and shoring and a lattice of rebar going out over nothing, and past that the cloud. The plateau is growing outward at some rate you cannot detect by watching, only by returning. A centimeter a day. A meter a season. Slowly, patiently, in the bright shadowless quiet, the made ground extends over the true mountain and eventually, presumably, past it.

And behind you, at the edge where you came up: is it receding? You return to check. It is difficult to check. The shear-face lip of overpour is still there. But the grating you first stepped onto, warm, with the fog below—you cannot find it. There is grating. It is not that grating. The path you climbed up should meet the plateau here, and here is a wall of a shed, and the shed is very clean and very new and has no door.

—

The scale never resolves. From any gantry you can see the whole of the plateau—stacks, towers, the pale glow of the sky at the edges—and it is the size of a large town, a valley's worth of works, walkable in a long day. But you never reach the far side. You walk toward a particular red water tank for an hour and it does not approach; you turn a corner and it is directly overhead, dripping on you. The brightness has something to do with this. Without shadow-length you have no depth. Without sound you have no distance. The eye, given a bright even field of near-identical forms, does what the eye does with such fields, and the place accommodates it. You are quite sure that the plateau is either much larger or much smaller than it appears, and you are quite sure that both of these are true, and that the plant would consider this a tolerance well within spec.

—

Reality frays here in specific, repeatable ways, and after a while you start to catalogue them the way you would catalogue anything in a region with its own weather.

The doubled reflection that lags. The shadows that pool. The fox, or foxes. Stairs that count differently going up and going down—thirteen, then eleven—so that you always arrive a step early or late and stumble. Pipes that enter a wall on one side and do not exit on the other, and pipes that exit walls they never entered. A gauge on a boiler face whose needle points at a number that changes when you look at it directly, the way a word does when you stare at it too long and it stops being a word. A door you opened onto a room of humming transformers that, opened again from the other side, gives onto the same room, with the same transformers, and yourself not in it.

And the pigeons, always, on every girder, watching with their small dry eyes, silent, so many and so close that when you finally see one shift its weight the movement propagates down the line of them like a ripple down a cable, and it is the loudest thing you have seen all day.

—

By the end—and there is an end, the light does not change but your legs do—you know the place the way you know a house you've walked through in a dream. You know that it is high and cold and beaded with water that goes back into it. You know that it is entirely built, that there is nothing here anyone did not make, and that whatever made it is still making, patiently, at the far edge, in the bright. You know that it has fauna the way a lung has cells—not as inhabitants but as constituent material, held in suspension. You know that it is quiet because the noise is elsewhere, and new because it is always being renewed, and that its scale is the scale of a thing seen from inside.

You do not know what it is for.

But you have a guess, standing at the forming edge where the rebar goes out over the cloud, with the wet slab soft under your feet and a heron thirty centimeters from your elbow looking down at the same drop. The guess is that the plateau is not on the mountain. The guess is that the mountain, the real one, gray and weathered and full of ordinary sound, is a raw material. It is fed in below and the plateau is what comes out. What you climbed was the feedstock. What you stand on is the product. And in the shadowless light, on the silent decking, among the close-packed watching animals, the product is being extended outward and downward at a centimeter a day to meet, eventually, everything.

The heron blinks. A half-second later, so does the heron in the water.
