**The Warm Shelf**

From the valleys you come up through the ordinary altitudes first, and the ordinary rules hold there. The trees shorten and give out. The grass gives out. The rock goes grey and then goes white with old snow, and the air thins until you count your steps in fours. By the time you reach the base of the last wall — a face of dark basalt two kilometres tall, weeping ice — you are above almost everything that lives, and the sky is the hard indigo of very high places.

Then the ice on the wall begins to run.

Not melt, exactly. It runs in the way ice does not. It slides down the rock as liquid water while the air on your skin is still well below freezing, and the water is warm to the touch, and there is a smell in it — leaf-rot, wet bark, something like clove. Water is coming down the wall from above, and the wall is weeping it out of every seam, and the higher you look, the more the black rock is furred with green.

The top of the wall is not a summit. It is an edge.

---

The shelf sits at an elevation where nothing should grow, and everything does.

It is not a single plateau but a country of them — a chain of planed summits, terraces and benches and broad saddles, joined by ridges wide enough to hold forest, extending out of sight to the east and north. Where you would expect scree and cornice there is a canopy. Where you would expect cornice there is a canopy dripping. The trees are the trees of a lowland wet forest — buttressed, strangled with lianas, hung with epiphytes in such quantity that the trunks disappear under them — but they grow with a slowness you can read in their bark, which is thick as a hide and ridged like an old face. Some of the largest are so wide that a path has been worn around them, an actual groove in the soil where generations of something with hooves have circled.

The heat is the first strangeness. It is genuine heat, wet and total, the heat of a place two thousand metres above sea level in the tropics, not seven thousand. It comes up out of the ground, faintly — the rock is warm under bare feet, and in the deeper hollows you can find stones that are hot enough to steam when rain hits them — but mostly the heat is simply *held*. The air here does not leave. It has not left in a very long time.

Look up and you can see why.

---

Perhaps a hundred metres above the highest crowns, the sky changes texture. There is a boundary — not a wall, not a dome, more the way the surface of a pond is a boundary when seen from below — where the warm thick air of the shelf meets the true high atmosphere, cold and nearly empty. It shows itself as a slight silvering, a lens. Clouds cannot form above it; there is not enough air. Clouds form *at* it, constantly, as the wet warm updraft from the forest touches that cold ceiling and condenses.

So it rains. Not in weather, in schedule. The whole shelf breathes upward and the breath comes back as water. Most hours of most days there is a fine rain somewhere on the shelf, and every evening there is a heavy one, and by night the boundary sheds mist that drops down through the canopy like something poured. Every leaf is a drip-tip. Every rock face is a seep. The paths are streams with stones in them. The soil is a half-metre of wet black sponge over the basalt, and when you put your hand into it, it is as warm as a body.

At the edges, the water leaves. It gathers into channels and pours off the rim, and here is the second strangeness: it does not fall correctly. A stream reaching the edge of the shelf goes over as any stream would, but then it slows in the air, becomes a hanging skein, and then a plume of mist, and the mist is drawn *back* — up, inward, against the face of the cliff — and re-enters the shelf through the rock. The wall you climbed is weeping because it is drinking. The water in the seams was ten minutes ago a river that jumped off the edge above your head.

There is no name for the force that turns the water round. It behaves like gravity that has been asked, politely, to make an exception in a particular volume, and has agreed, and has been agreeing for so long that the rock itself has adapted — the basalt at the rim is honeycombed with capillary tunnels the width of a finger, clearly not natural, clearly not recent, polished smooth by ten thousand years of upward-flowing water.

---

The sound of the place is the sound of that circuit. Underneath everything is the noise of the falls — dozens of them, hundreds, along a rim that runs for kilometres in either direction — a low white roar that does not vary and that you stop hearing within a day, the way you stop hearing your own blood. Over it, in layers: the tick and patter of the constant drip. The boundary overhead, which makes a sound of its own, very faint, a kind of high sustained tone like a wetted glass, audible only at night and only if you stand in a clearing and want to hear it. And then the animals, which are everywhere and loud in the way that a place with no winter and no predators bigger than a dog is loud.

The frogs alone would deafen a lowland forest. They breed in the bromeliads, in the hoofprints, in the capillary tunnels at the rim, and at dusk when the heavy rain comes on they all begin at once, tens of thousands of them, a wall of sound with a definite pitch that shifts as the rain shifts. There are birds that have never had reason to fly far and have stopped bothering, walking the mid-canopy on thick legs. There are things with the shape of tree-hyraxes, dense-furred against a cold that no longer comes, and things with the shape of tapirs, small and dark and unafraid, and a species of very large slow-moving snail, hand-sized, whose shells are the only white thing in the landscape and which the hoofed animals eat for the calcium. Butterflies drink at every seep. Something in the upper canopy calls in phrases of exactly seven notes, and answers itself from across a valley, and nobody who has stayed here long has ever seen what it is.

Nothing here hurries. The animals have the particular calm of creatures in a system with no gradients — nothing is scarce, nothing is coming, the rain will fall at dusk. When you walk through them they move aside the way water does, without alarm, and close behind you.

---

You will not find much that was built. That is not because nothing was.

What you find, if you know how to look, are the seams. Lines in the rock where the basalt is a shade too even, running dead straight for a hundred metres and then vanishing under moss. A single standing shape on one of the eastern benches — a spindle of some dark material, twice the height of the trees, with no joints and no marks, that is warm to the touch and that the epiphytes will not grow on, so that it stands clean in the middle of a forest that has covered everything else. The rain falls slightly differently near it. Birds land on it and leave without calling.

On another summit there is a pool that is perfectly circular and that is always exactly full, though streams run into it and nothing runs out. On a saddle to the north the moss grows in a pattern of concentric rings a kilometre across that you can only see from the boundary itself, if you climb the tallest tree and look down at the canopy in the right light.

And here and there, in the soil, in the roots of the great trees, the animals turn up small things. Discs. A rod the length of a forearm that bends and returns to straight. Once, in a hollow log, a sheet of something like paper that could not be torn or wetted, blank, warm, folded twice. The tapir-things carry them off and lose them. The snails leave silver trails across them. The forest treats them as it treats everything: as slightly surprising stones.

Whoever made the shelf did not build on it. They built *it* — pinned the air, turned the water round, warmed the rock — and then either left, or became something that no longer needed to be found. The forest and the frogs and the hyraxes came later, blown or carried or walked up over immense spans of time, and the system took them in without any visible adjustment. It was not made for them. It does not object to them. It runs.

---

Stay long enough and you begin to feel the slowness as a kind of pressure.

The trees grow, but at a rate that makes you suspect they were told to take their time. The soil deepens perhaps a finger-width a century. The circular pool has been full for longer than the falls have been carving the rim, and the rim shows almost no carving — the water is routed too well, returns too neatly. Nothing erodes here except what is meant to be eroded. The cliff faces are the same faces they were when the boundary first closed. The only thing that changes on a human scale is the position of the light, and even that is strange: the sun comes through the boundary slightly softened, slightly spread, so that the shelf never has hard shadows, and the dusk lasts longer than it should, the whole ceiling going amber and then rose and then, for a few minutes, faintly luminous on its own before it darkens.

That is when you hear the tone most clearly. Standing in a clearing with the frogs starting up and the rain coming on warm and the ceiling glowing overhead like a held breath, you can hear it under everything — the sound of the shelf keeping itself. Not a machine sound. More like the sound a very old, very patient thing might make if it were content, and did not expect to be interrupted, and had long ago stopped counting the years.

Below the rim, the wall drinks. Above it, the empty sky. Between them, this: a country of warm rock and endless rain and animals who have never seen snow, thousands of metres higher than the snow, going about the business of a day that has been going on for a very long time and shows no sign of ending.
