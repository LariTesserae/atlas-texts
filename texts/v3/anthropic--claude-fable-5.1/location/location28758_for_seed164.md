The first thing to say is that it is not finished, and it will never be finished, and those two facts are the same fact.

You come up onto it by the scarp. There is no other way. The plateau ends, on every side, in a wall of rock that has not had time to become a slope: black basalt broken along planes so fresh the edges will cut a glove. Nothing has softened them. There are no talus fans at the foot, no gullies, no notches where streams have begun their patient work. The wall goes up two and a half kilometres like something poured into a mould and set. Water runs down its face constantly — the whole wall weeps, dark and glassy — but the water carries nothing with it. You can put your hand into a runnel and it comes away clean. Rock that is wet and cold and unworn, for a thousand kilometres in either direction.

At the top the air is thin and tastes of iron and moss.

---

The surface is soft. That is the surprise after the scarp. It gives under the boot like a mattress, because the ground is not ground at all but a mat of vegetation many metres deep: sphagnum, cushion plants, liverworts the size of dinner plates, club mosses grown to the height of a person and standing in stiff green columns. Where the mat thins you see it grows directly on the rock, on the same sharp-edged black surface as the scarp, and there is no soil in between. There has not been time for soil. There will not be. The plants take what they need from the wet air and from each other's dead lower parts, which do not decay so much as compress.

Everything is beaded with condensate. The cloud does not rain here, exactly; it settles. It touches every leaf and stem and forms drops and the drops run down and join and run down further and the mat is always full, always shedding a thin sheet of cold water from its lower edges into the shallow rock channels that lace the plateau. And the channels — this is where you begin to understand the place — the channels do not go anywhere natural. They are too straight. They meet at right angles. They deepen and become lined with grey concrete and then covered with iron grates, and the water goes into the grates and down, and you do not see it again as water.

It is a few degrees above freezing and it is always a few degrees above freezing.

---

You hear it before you understand it, and in fact you have been hearing it since long before the scarp, from the lowlands, as a pressure behind the ears that you mistook for altitude.

It is a sound with no direction. It comes up through the mat and through the bones of the feet and it is in the air equally from every side. A low sustained tone, industrial, enormous — the sound of something being turned, or held under compression, or forced — and above it a harmonic that has the quality of a very large bell struck once and never allowed to fade. There is no rhythm. There is no swell. If you shout, you can hear yourself, but only just, and only for the length of the shout; the tone does not wait for you and does not react. After an hour on the plateau you stop hearing it as sound. It becomes a property of the space, like the cold, like the grey. After a day you will find that silence, remembered, seems like a thing that could not possibly exist.

Standing at the scarp edge, looking back down at the lowlands, you can see the sound. The cloud that hangs over the plateau — always in the same place, always the same shape, a continent-sized lid of grey — has a texture at its edges that is faintly ridged, faintly ringed, like the skin of a drum.

---

Walk inward.

The plateau is large enough that you cannot walk across it. This needs saying plainly. It is not a plateau in the sense of a table of land with an edge you might reach by evening; it is a plateau in the sense of a country, a raised continent, a thing with its own weather that never leaves it. You walk inward and the horizon is a straight line of moss under a straight line of cloud and the only thing that breaks it, once in every long while, is a station.

The stations are what the built world here consists of. They are far apart — thirty, forty, sixty kilometres, spaced with a regularity that becomes visible only if you keep a map — and each is the same. A rectangular yard cut into the mat and lined with the grey concrete. A boilerhouse of riveted iron plate, black-red, streaked white with mineral. Three stacks. A rail spur that comes in from one horizon and goes out toward another, its rails bright, unrusted, wet, with moss growing right up to the sleepers and not one millimetre over them. A row of long low dormitory blocks with their doors open. A gantry. A transformer house that hums at a frequency you can distinguish from the great tone only by putting your ear to its wall.

Inside the boilerhouse it is warm and roaring and almost unbearable and there is no one. The furnaces are lit. The gauges hold at their marks. The great pistons are turning at the same rate they have always turned, and the rods drop down through the floor into shafts that go down into the black rock, down farther than the scarp is high, and you cannot see the bottom and you understand that there is not, in any meaningful sense, a bottom. The water from the grates comes in through pipes as thick as trees. It goes through the engines. It leaves the stacks as steam.

Outside, the steam rises into the grey and becomes the grey and settles on the moss and runs down into the grates. You can watch the whole cycle in an afternoon and see that no part of it leaks.

---

The dormitory blocks are the emptiest places you have ever been.

The beds are made. The lockers are shut. There is a mess hall with tables laid for eight hundred and a kitchen where the pilot lights are burning under empty pots. Nothing is dusty because there is nothing here to make dust — no skin, no hair, no insects, no wind-blown grit from an eroding world. The floors are damp and clean. In one block there is a schedule pinned to a board, a rota of shifts, and it is filled in for a week and then blank, and the blank part is the same colour as the filled part, which is to say it has not yellowed, because nothing here yellows.

You could live here. You will not meet anyone. The plateau is built for a population it does not have, and the machines do not seem to miss them. Whatever tending they needed, they had, and now they are in the condition that everything here is in: the condition of having arrived.

---

There are no animals. It is worth standing still for a while to feel what that means.

No birds over the stations. No flies over the standing water in the yard. Nothing in the moss when you part it — no springtails, no mites, no threads of fungus, no small grey life that in any other wet place would be working at the dead matter and turning it back into something. The mat is not being eaten. It grows exactly as fast as it compresses, and it compresses exactly as fast as it grows, and the difference is zero, and so the plateau is neither filling up nor emptying out. It is the same depth of moss it was when you arrived. It will be the same depth when you leave.

This is the second thing to understand, after the grates. The plateau is young and it is not getting older. The scarp is sharp and it is not getting softer. The water weeps down the rock and cuts nothing. Everything that in another landscape would be a process — weathering, decay, succession, the slow lean of things toward the sea — has here been stopped at some point in its first hour and held there. Not frozen; held. The difference matters. Frozen things are still. This place is roaring.

---

You may come to a theory, if you stay long enough, and it is likely to be the one everyone comes to, because the plateau presents it to you without subtlety once you have seen the shafts.

It is held up.

The great tone is the sound of the holding. The engines do not make anything. There is no product. The stacks do not deliver goods to the rail spur and the rail spur does not carry anything to the scarp and down; the rails are for the engines to inspect each other, or were, or are a gesture toward an economy that never needed to exist. What the stations produce is elevation. The plateau was raised — recently, in the frank and unweathered rock you can read that it was raised in a single motion, the way a stage is raised — and it has not been permitted to settle a millimetre since. The pistons drop into the shafts and something below pushes back, and the whole black raft of the continent hangs in equilibrium on that push, two and a half kilometres above the world, wet and cold and vast, with its own cloud sitting on it like a lid on a pot.

Whether this is true or not, it is the theory the ground gives you through the soles of your feet. Lie down on the mat and put your palms flat on the moss and you will feel it: a very slight, constant, upward pressure, as if the land were the top of a thumb pressed against you from below. It never changes. The gauges hold at their marks.

---

Weather here is a single event that has not ended. The cloud formed and stayed. The mist settles and does not lift. At the scarp edge there are places where the plateau's own air meets the air of the lowlands, and there is a boundary you can stand in, one foot in each, with a difference in temperature you can feel on the two sides of your face. But it is a boundary, not a front. It does not move. The plateau does not export its weather and does not import any. Somewhere in the middle of it, hundreds of kilometres from any edge, at a station identical to all the others, there is presumably a point that has never once in its existence felt a wind.

The light is a level grey, brighter than it seems, enough to read by, enough to throw a faint shadow at your feet that does not lengthen through the day because the day does not have a direction here. Hours pass. The tone continues. The moss sheds its sheet of cold water into the grates. Somewhere under you a piston goes down and comes up.

---

You leave by the scarp, because there is no other way, and when you are far enough down that the tone has thinned back into a pressure behind the ears, you look up and see the plateau's edge cutting the sky, absolutely straight, absolutely sharp, with its grey lid resting on it and its long black wall glistening. It looks like something that happened yesterday. It will look like that tomorrow. Down here, where you are standing, a stream has begun to carry a little sand toward the sea. Up there, the whole continent roars, and does not fall, and does not wear, and waits for nothing.
