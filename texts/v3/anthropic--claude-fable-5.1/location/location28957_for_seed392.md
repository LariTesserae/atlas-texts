The ground is clay, and it was put down by hand.

You can see this straight away, because the surface still carries the pressing. Wide flat palm-strokes run in overlapping rows across the whole basin, each one about the width of a person's hand held open, each one edged with a slight ridge where the next stroke began. The clay is a warm red-brown and it is smooth and hard and slightly damp, and in the lowest parts of the basin it has been pressed a little further down than in the rest, so that water sits in the hollows. The water is level and it does not move. There is no source and no outlet. It was put there, the way the clay was put there, and it stays.

Nothing here has aged. That is the first rule, and once you know it you see it everywhere. The cuts are all fresh. Where the ground rises at the edges of the basin into low banks, the banks show the marks of the tool that shaped them: long shallow scoops, like something taken out with a wooden paddle or the flat edge of a shell, and each scoop meets the next at a crisp line that has never been softened by rain or sun or a single footfall. Where a slab has been set on end, the top of it is sharp enough to cut a hand. There is no dust anywhere. Dust is what happens when things wear down, and here nothing wears.

It is hot. The heat is thick and wet and standing, the kind of heat that would bring a storm in another place, except that here it does not build toward anything. Moisture stands in the air. It has condensed on every surface: on the upright slabs, on the low stone that sits alone in the middle of one of the pools, on the undersides of leaves. The drops are perfectly formed and they hang where they formed. None of them run. If you touch one it wets your finger and the place it came from is dry until it is not, which is soon, and the drop is back, the same drop, in the same place. The rocks weep and their weeping goes nowhere.

The light comes from everywhere. There is a sun, or something that does the work of one: a pale disc high in the white sky, the color of unfired clay, slightly flattened as if it had been patted into shape and hung before it was quite round. But the brightness does not seem to come only from it. It comes off the pools, off the wet clay, off the white sky itself, which is not blue and is not cloudy but is simply bright, the way the inside of a kiln is bright. Things cast shadows, but the shadows do not belong to the light. They lie where they were placed. A slab casts its shadow to the east; the tree beside it casts its shadow to the north; a heron standing between them has no shadow at all, and a little to the left of the heron, on the bare clay, there is the shadow of a heron with nothing standing in it. Whoever made the shadows made them one at a time and did not always check.

The vegetation is dense where it occurs and it occurs in patches, far apart. A stand of something like plantain, broad leaves the length of a person, glossy, a green so saturated it looks glazed. A tangle of vine on a frame of upright poles. A single enormous fern in a hollow that was scooped to hold it, the scoop still visible around its base. Between the patches the clay runs open and bare for a long way, a hundred paces, two hundred, the palm-strokes catching the light in rows. The plants are made. This is not a metaphor. If you look closely at the plantain leaves you can find the place where the two halves of the leaf were joined and smoothed along the central vein, and the seams are fine but they are there. The green is a color, not a condition. The leaves do not transpire. They are wet because everything is wet. They do not grow, and they have never been smaller than they are now, and they will not be larger. The fern has fifty-three fronds and it has always had fifty-three fronds. You do not know how you know this. You know it the way you know the number of your own fingers.

There is a village, or the outline of one, scattered across the northern part of the basin. Eight or nine low houses of the same clay as the ground, walls rising directly out of the surface without any join, as if the ground had been pinched up into walls the way you pinch the rim of a pot. Their roofs are slabs. Their doorways are dark rectangles cut clean. The houses are far apart from one another, each one alone in its own stretch of bare clay, and around each one the palm-strokes change direction slightly, radiating outward, as if the maker had been sitting in the doorway and pressing the ground into place from there. Inside the houses there are shelves, and on the shelves there are things: bowls, a coil of rope, a stone with a hole through it. The rope is clay. The bowls are clay. The hole in the stone is filled with a small perfect stillness of water. Nobody is in the houses. Nobody has ever been in the houses. They are finished.

The animals are the strange part, or the part whose strangeness takes longest to settle.

There are a great many of them. Herons in the pools, standing with one foot lifted. Frogs on the clay banks, dozens of them, sitting in the wet. Long-bodied insects in the air above the water. Something like a small deer in the far distance, at the edge of the fern hollow. Fish in the pools, though the pools are barely deeper than a hand. A lizard on a wall. Birds along the rim of a roof slab. All of them are made, like everything else, and unlike everything else they move.

Movement was put into them. It is a property, like the green of the leaves or the wet of the water, and it was applied by hand and it does not run down. The herons shift their weight. The frogs blink. The insects hang and drop and hang. But the movement does not go anywhere. A heron will lift its foot and set it down and lift it again and it will not be closer to anything or farther from anything. The fish turn in the water and stay in the same place. The deer at the edge of the ferns lowers its head and raises it and lowers it. Nothing here is hungry. Nothing is hunting. The frogs make a sound occasionally, a single low click, and it is the only sound apart from the sound you make yourself, and the click is the same click each time, from the same frog, at intervals that are not regular but are also not different from one another in any way you could name.

No two animals are alike, because there was no template. Each one was made once. Some of them are wrong. There is a frog whose legs join at the top of its body rather than the bottom, and it sits upside down with its belly to the sky and its feet in the air and it blinks like the others. There is a heron with two necks. There is a fish with eyes on the outside of the pool, standing on the clay bank a few inches from the water, looking in at its own body. They work. Working was put into them along with everything else, and the working does not depend on the shape being right. You understand that the makers were learning as they went and did not go back.

The far banks of the basin are the same size as the near ones. Distance has been made, but it has been made the way a child draws it: the things at the back are simply behind the things at the front, and they are not smaller. You can walk toward the bank on the eastern side for a long time and it does not grow, and then you are at it, and it was the size it always was, which is the size of a low wall, waist-high, with paddle-scoops all along its face. Beyond it the clay continues, less finished. The palm-strokes stop and the surface is rough and lumped, and then the lumps stop and there are just blocks, wet, red-brown, set down next to one another and not yet joined, and then there are no blocks and there is the white brightness of the sky going down to meet nothing in particular.

The heat does not change. The light does not change. The drops hang. The heron lifts its foot. The frog clicks. There is no wind, because wind is the air deciding to be somewhere else, and this air was placed, and is content.

If you press your thumb into the ground it goes in, a little, and the clay is cool under the surface and takes the print cleanly. When you lift your thumb the print is there, sharp-edged, with the ridges of your skin in it. Water gathers in the bottom of it almost at once, a small level pool the width of a thumb, still, bright, reflecting the flattened sun. It will not fill further and it will not dry. You have made a thing here, and it will be exactly that thing for as long as the place is a place, alongside the two-necked heron and the shadow of a bird with no bird in it and the fifty-three fronds of the fern. Nothing that was made here is ever unmade. That is the whole of the law, and the place is very quiet inside it.
