Start with the noise, because there is no getting around it. It is there before you are aware of anything else and it will be there after your eyes have adjusted and your hands have found the wall. A low continuous roar with no gaps in it, no rhythm you can count, a sound so complete that after a few minutes you stop hearing it as sound and begin to feel it instead — in the sternum, in the back teeth, in the bones of the wrist where they press against the stone. Speech is impossible. Not difficult; impossible. You could put your mouth to another person's ear and shout and they would see your jaw move and feel your breath and hear nothing. Whoever comes here comes alone, or comes in silence with someone they already understand.

The place is small. That is the second thing. You could pace it in twelve steps along the long axis and six across, if you were willing to walk without seeing your feet. It is a recess in the base of a cliff, a room the water has carved and then, slowly, abandoned — the fall used to strike here, directly, ten thousand years ago, and drilled this hollow into the rock, and then the lip above retreated a few body-lengths upstream as the softer band beneath it wore back, and now the water comes down just beyond the mouth of the room instead of into it. So there is a curtain. A standing wall of falling river, three or four strides out from where the floor ends, white and grey and moving too fast to see as motion, and behind it this pocket of stone that the river no longer wants.

The light that gets through the curtain is almost nothing. It is not blackness — blackness would be simpler — but a deep brown-green dusk, the colour of the underside of a leaf held against a night sky, that thins toward the back wall until the eye gives up. At midday in summer, with the river low, the curtain goes translucent for an hour and a moving greyish glow crawls across the floor and up the rear wall, and you can see. The rest of the time you see by proximity, by putting your face close to things.

What you see, close, is moss. Every surface that is not walked on is furred with it, thick as a pelt, so saturated that pressing a thumb into it brings water up around the nail. It grows on the walls to the height of the spray, which is to say all the way up, and it grows on the ceiling where it hangs in soft grey-green stalactites that drip at intervals onto your neck. Beneath and among the moss there are liverworts, flat and black-green and scaled like something's skin, and a filmy fern whose fronds are a single cell thick — you can hold one up toward the curtain and see the grey glow through it. A hart's-tongue has managed a foothold in a crack near the mouth where the light is least bad. Nothing flowers. Nothing needs to. This is a lush place in the way a lung is lush: wet, folded, thickly lined, and mostly empty.

Because it is mostly empty. That is what the scale and the density make together. The walls are dense with life and the room they enclose is hollow — cold air, standing still, a vault of it, and you in the middle of it with nothing near you. The moss coats the enclosure but does not fill it. If you stand in the centre with your arms out you touch nothing, and if you take three steps in any direction you touch a wall that gives softly and wets your palm. The emptiness has a temperature. It is a few degrees above freezing at the best of the year and the moisture in the air finds every part of you that is warmer than that and settles there. Your breath is visible if there were light to see it by.

The floor is where the water still lives, a little. Not a stream; the river is outside, throwing itself past. But the spray that comes off the curtain drifts inward and beads on everything and runs down the walls behind the moss and gathers in the low places, so the floor is a field of shallow pools in the bare rock, each one the size of a hand or a head, black and perfectly still — the only still water for a mile in any direction, and the only stillness at all. Between the pools the rock is worn smooth and slick. It was worn by the fall, when the fall was here. The scallop-shapes in it, the smooth basins and rounded ridges, are the shape of turbulence, frozen. You can read the old water in the floor with your fingers.

There are marks that are not the water's.

Along the drier wall — the one on the side the spray reaches last — someone has cut a row of small holes into the rock at about shoulder height. Six of them, evenly spaced, each the width of two fingers and deep enough to take a peg. Two still hold pegs, hardwood, black with damp, driven in so long ago the wood has swollen and split and taken the moss into its cracks. Above the pegs the wall has been scraped clean of growth in a rough rectangle and has not entirely regrown; the moss there is a different, younger green. Something hung here once. Hides, or nets, or a person's few belongings. The scraping has been done with a stone edge; you can feel the parallel grooves.

At the back, where the darkness is total, there is a hearth. A ring of river cobbles, each fist-sized, set close. Inside the ring the floor is not black with soot, because the water has taken the soot, but the stone is a different texture — heat-shattered, crazed in fine lines, and it does not grow moss. Beside it, in a dry niche higher than the spray reaches, a small cache: a bundle of what was once sinew cord, now a grey knot; a flint blade with a used edge and a broken tang; a scraper of antler that has gone soft and porous; and, oddly, a single smooth pebble of a pale stone that does not occur in this valley, placed on top of the rest, as if it were the point. The niche has a flat lid-stone that was set over it and has slid a little. Someone meant to come back for this.

You could not have lived here. Not for long. There is no light for making, no way to speak, no way to hear anything approach — though nothing does. What the room offers is the one thing the valley outside does not: a place where the water does not fall on you, where the wind cannot reach, where a fire could be lit and would stay lit and could not be seen from any distance because the curtain hides it. A shelter for a night at a time. A cache-place. The pegs and the hearth are not a home; they are the infrastructure of passing through, used and re-used across generations by people who all knew where it was and none of whom stayed.

The fauna, when there is any, is at the edges. A dipper — a small, dark, white-bibbed bird — works the spray-zone at the mouth of the room, walking under the fringe of the curtain where it thins, coming up with something in its beak, bobbing on a wet stone, leaving. It never comes further in. Once, resting at the mouth in the summer light, you might see the dark shape of a trout hold itself in the churn below the curtain where the water is too broken for a fish to sensibly be. Deeper in there is a slug the length of a finger, dark and glossy, that lives on the moss and takes a day to cross the wall. In the black at the very back, if you hold still, sometimes the softest disturbance in the air near the ceiling, once, twice, and then not again — a bat, maybe, or your own blood. Mostly nothing. The room is not a habitat. It is a gap between habitats, an accident of geology that a few things have learned to use.

Time here is measured in ways that do not involve the sound, because the sound does not change. The curtain thickens in spring and turns solid white and the room goes fully dark and the roar deepens to something that shakes grit loose from the ceiling; by late summer it thins and lets in the grey hour and the pools on the floor shrink until some of them are just wet stains. The moss grows at the rate moss grows. The hearth-stones sink a fraction each century into the softening rock. The lip of the fall retreats upstream by the width of a fingernail every hundred years, and the room deepens by the same amount, and in some very long time the curtain will stand far enough out that the spray will not reach the walls and the moss will dry and the room will become something else. None of this is visible. You could sit here a lifetime and see no change at all except the seasons in the light, and the slow blackening of a peg.

Leave the way you came, which is the only way: along the ledge at the base of the cliff, hard against the rock, the curtain at your shoulder throwing cold on you, until the ledge widens and the roar begins, very gradually, to become a sound again — something outside you, with edges, that you can turn away from. Then the ordinary noise of the river below the fall, which seems like silence. Then birds. You will find you have been holding your breath.
