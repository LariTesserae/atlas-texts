The plateau begins where the air stops being enough. Below a certain height there is soil, and rock, and the ordinary difficulties of the world; above it, the ground becomes a single thing, and that thing is alive, and it has been alive so long that it no longer resembles anything grown so much as a geology that happens to have a pulse.

It is a crust. Grey-green, ridged, warm to the touch even in the cold — not with sunlight but with the low internal heat of something digesting. It extends to every horizon, and past them, and past those. From a height you would see it as a continent-sized lens laid over the top of a mountain range so old the mountains have been entirely swallowed; only the elevation remains, three or four kilometers of it, and a thin bright sky pressed hard against the surface.

There is no water here. Not that it is arid in the way a desert is arid — a desert remembers water and waits for it. The crust has simply never permitted any. Whatever moisture drifts up from the lowlands is taken into tissue before it can bead, bound into cell walls, sealed into vacuoles, locked into waxes and resins and the dense lignified bulk of the substrate itself. Rain has not fallen on this surface in the memory of anything living. Fog does not form. If you cut the crust — and it can be cut, though it heals in minutes — the wound weeps not sap but a clear, slow oil, cool and faintly camphorous, that stiffens in the air within a breath. The whole plateau runs on this oil. It is the blood and the hydraulic fluid and the fuel.

---

The first thing you understand is the sound, because it arrives before the eye has sorted anything.

It is not a single sound. It is a pressure in the head that resolves, over hours, into layers: a bass so low it is felt in the sternum rather than heard, which is the crust itself flexing over hundreds of square kilometers, tension released along fault-seams in the growth. Above that, a constant wide crackling, like a forest fire with no fire, which is the ground splitting and resealing everywhere at once, a billion small ruptures per hour. Above that, the detonations — irregular, from every compass point, some near enough to move the air against the face, some so distant they arrive as a soft *thup* half a minute after the flash. And threaded through all of it, a high shriek that never stops, that rises and falls with the wind but never drops to silence, which is the vents.

People who have stood on the crust say the sound is the hardest thing to describe afterward, because there is nothing to compare it to except itself. The nearest analogue is standing inside a working factory floor — the churn, the hiss, the hammer-fall — except that the factory is the size of a nation and none of it was made.

---

Nothing here was made. There is not a single artifact on the plateau, not a wall or a path or a mark. And yet it is an industry.

The presences are sparse. That is the second thing you understand, walking. The crust is mostly flat, mostly empty, a grey-green plain rolling to the edge of sight, and the things that rise from it stand kilometers apart, sometimes tens of kilometers, each alone in its own weather. From a distance they look like installations. Up close they are still plants, but plants that have arrived, over a very long time, at the shapes that pressure and fuel and repetition produce in anything.

There are the **stacks**: fluted columns, sixty or eighty meters tall, ribbed like the inside of a windpipe, that stand in loose groups of three or five with a kilometer between them. They breathe. The crust beneath them ferments — the oil breaks down in the dark of the substrate into gas, and the gas is collected and driven up the stacks and released in a scream that does not stop. The pitch of each stack is fixed by its length; a group of them sounds a chord that never resolves. The air around their mouths is warm and smells of turpentine and bread. Nothing grows on the stacks. Their outer skin is a hard white ceramic of silica the plant has pulled from the ancient rock below and laid down layer over layer, and it is polished by the wind to a shine.

There are the **cannons**. These are the source of the detonations. A cannon is a squat cone, house-sized, with a single aperture at the top, and inside it a chamber where oil is charged with gas under enormous pressure for days. When the wall of the chamber can no longer hold, it does not tear; it flexes, a valve opens, and the contents are fired upward in a single report that can crack a tooth if you are standing near. What is fired is seed — a dense black pellet, fist-sized, wrapped in a wax that smokes as it goes. It rises perhaps two kilometers and comes down anywhere within twenty. Most land on crust and are absorbed. Some land in a seam and root. The cannons fire roughly every four days each, and there are, by any estimate, several million of them across the plateau, which means that at any moment somewhere within earshot one is going off, and at any moment the sky overhead carries a thin traffic of black seed on smoking arcs.

There are the **mills**. These are the only presences that move visibly, and they are the most alarming. A mill is a stalk, thick as a tree trunk, that grows in a helix and stores torque in the twist. When the torque exceeds what the base can hold, the stalk spins — unwinds itself in a blur, throws off a spray of fibrous chaff, and grinds its own foot into the crust with a roar of tearing wood. Then it goes still, and begins to wind again. The chaff it throws is dry and light and rides the wind for hundreds of kilometers; it is the plateau's soil, such as it is, and it is the plateau's weather.

There are the **pits**, which are simply places where the crust has folded under itself and is being digested, slow whirlpools of grey mass a kilometer across, turning at perhaps a meter an hour, exhaling warm gas. There are the **flats**, which are nothing — kilometers of plain ridged crust, taut as a drumskin, humming. There are the **seams**, where old crust meets new and the two are grinding against each other, and the edge stands up in a lip of raw pale tissue that is green for a day and grey by the next.

That is all. Stacks, cannons, mills, pits, flats, seams. Six kinds of thing, repeated across a continent, each instance a little different, all of them very far apart. Between them the light comes down hard and white and unfiltered, and the shadows are short and black, and the cold is the honest cold of altitude, the kind that dries the eyes.

---

The light is worth its own paragraph. There is almost nothing between the crust and the sun: no haze, no cloud, no water in the air to soften anything. The sky is a dark, thick blue nearly to the zenith and the sun is a hard disc you cannot look near. Everything is overexposed. The white stacks are painful. The grey of the crust reads as silver at midday. When the mills throw chaff, it fills the air with a glittering dust that does not fall for hours, and the light going through it makes a low brightness that seems to come from the ground itself. At dusk the chaff catches the last sun and the whole plateau, for a quarter of an hour, appears to be burning without heat.

And it is electric. Dry fiber rubbing on dry fiber, at continental scale, builds charge with nowhere to go. It gathers in the chaff clouds and along the stacks and in the seams where the crust is grinding. Then it goes — not in bolts so much as in sheets, flat white flashes that jump between the tops of stacks or lay themselves along a seam for a kilometer at once, with a crack that briefly silences everything else. There is no rain to carry it. It simply discharges into the plant, which conducts it down into the oil, which, some think, is why the plateau is warm.

---

The crust moves. Not fast, not where you are standing, usually. But the seams migrate, the pits drift, the mills grind new footings and abandon old ones, the stacks rise where fermentation gathers and slump where it ebbs. A map of the plateau made in spring is unreliable by autumn and unrecognizable in a decade. Whole regions fold under and are redigested; whole regions rise new and pale and taut. It is a system in permanent overturn, and it has been in permanent overturn for so long that the overturn *is* its settled state. There is no episode of calm that the turbulence is interrupting. There was never an original layout. The crust has always been eating and refiring itself, and the equilibrium — for it is one — is dynamic, like a flame, like a standing wave.

This is the rule of the place, if it has one. Nothing is stored. Everything the crust produces it consumes within a season: the seed returns to the substrate, the chaff settles and is absorbed, the gas is burned off through the stacks, the charge is discharged. There is no accumulation anywhere. No sediment, no strata, no fossil record. The plateau has no past it can be read from, only a present it is perpetually re-firing.

---

And it is full of animals.

They are not what one expects on a surface like this. They are small, mostly — little is large here except the plant — and hard, and fast, and deaf, or so it appears; nothing living on the crust seems to react to sound at all, having evolved in a world where sound carries no information because it is all information at once. They navigate by pressure, by heat, by the taste of the oil.

There are gleaners: six-legged things the size of a hand, shelled in the same silica the stacks use, that swarm the seam-lips where the crust is raw and drink the oil before it hardens. They move in loose fronts a kilometer wide, glinting. There are the chaff-riders, which are almost nothing but membrane, flat and translucent, that spread in the mill-updrafts and drift for days on the glittering wind, feeding on what is in it. There are the cannon-hounds — larger, long-bodied, the size of a small deer, with feet like split hooves that grip the ridged crust — which have learned to read the swelling of a cannon and gather at the base before it fires, so that when the seed comes down across twenty kilometers of plain they are already spread beneath it, waiting to crack the wax and eat the black heart. When a cannon fires, the hounds scatter outward from it at full run in a widening ring, and from above this looks like a second, slower detonation.

There are things in the pits, slow, pale, that are seen only when the fold-under brings them briefly to the surface, and things that live in the stack-mouths where the warm gas comes up, and something that hunts the hounds, though it is rarely seen, and something else that eats the chaff-riders out of the air with a sound, on the rare occasions the air is quiet enough to hear it, like tearing paper.

None of them drink. There is nothing to drink. Every animal on the plateau takes its water bound, from the oil or the seed or from each other, and every animal's waste is dry and returns to the crust as fiber. There is no wet thing anywhere in the ecosystem, from the ground to the highest chaff-rider, and this — more than the sound, more than the scale — is what makes visitors feel that they are somewhere they have no business being. Every instinct of a water-based body wants to find the stream. There is no stream. There has never been a stream.

---

Toward evening the wind rises, as it does every evening, pulled by the cooling. The chaff lifts across the whole plateau at once in a shining tide, and the stacks' chord bends upward in the moving air, and the cannons keep firing, and the flat white lightning begins to walk along the seams in the gathering dark. The cold sharpens. The crust's low heat comes up through the soles of the feet. Far off — fifty kilometers, more — a mill lets go, and the roar of its unwinding takes a long time to arrive, and by the time it does the mill is already still and winding again, and somewhere a cannon has fired, and somewhere a pit has turned another meter, and somewhere the crust has split and closed and split, and nothing anywhere has stopped, and nothing anywhere is finished, and the plateau goes on being, at deafening volume and in perfect balance, exactly what it has always been.
