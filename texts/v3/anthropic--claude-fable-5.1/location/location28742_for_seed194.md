Low down, where the plain has flattened so completely that nothing on it can be called a hill, there is a place that sits in the ground like a swallowed thing. From above — if there were an above, if anyone came that way — it would read as a slight rise in the grey scrub, a long blister of husk half-buried, no larger than a village common. It has been there so long that the soil has come up around it and the wind has polished its skin to a dull horn. It gives nothing away. You would have to know the seam.

The seam is a split in the husk about the width of a shoulder. Push through and the outside stops.

---

What you are inside is a plant. Not a grove, not a forest. One plant, or what remains of one, so old that the question of whether it is alive has become a matter of degree rather than fact. The husk is a fruit wall, or a pod wall, or some part of an organism for which no one alive has a name; thick as a rampart, lignified through and through, and dry. Dry in the way old bone is dry, the way a cast-off shell is dry. There is no water here. There has been none for so long that the wood has forgotten what to do with it. Whatever moisture once ran in these tissues has been replaced by air and resin and dust, and what the plant still does — because it does still do something — it does without water, in some slower chemistry that has settled into itself over ages.

The air is cool. Not cold; the temperature of a cellar, of the shaded side of a stone. It does not change. It has a smell like the inside of a walnut, like sawdust left in a box, like the dust of dried flowers, and under that a faint sourness, vegetable and immensely aged.

It is completely dark. This is not a matter of the hour. No light has ever entered this hollow that was not carried in, and what was carried in did not last, because the air here will not hold a flame. A wick sputters, blues, goes out. A struck spark dies in the air before it lands. The people who lived here understood this as a condition of the place — like the dryness, like the sound — and built accordingly. They built for hands.

---

The hollow is not large. Perhaps two hundred paces across at the widest, a little more in length, and high enough that a thrown stone does not come back quickly. That is the whole of it: one chamber, shaped roughly like the inside of a bean, its walls curving up and over to meet somewhere in the dark. And almost all of it is empty. That is the first thing your body understands, before your hands find anything — the vast unfilled volume of it, the way your own breathing goes out and does not return, the cool air standing still in a space with nothing in it.

The town is on the walls.

It is carved into the inner wall of the husk — the pith side, softer than the outer horn, though "softer" here means something like old cork or the endgrain of seasoned oak. Whoever made it worked with wooden wedges and stone and the sharpened spines of the plant itself; you can feel the marks, long shallow scoops, no metal edge among them. They cut terraces into the curve of the wall, one above the other, following the natural rings of the tissue so that each level is a shelf of denser wood standing proud of the layer above. Steps go up between them, cut on the diagonal, worn in the centre to a smooth trough. Where the wall grows too steep for steps there are ropes, and the ropes are made of the wall — fibre stripped from the inner lining, twisted, plied, so old now that they have gone hard and glassy and ring faintly if you pluck them.

Along each terrace the dwellings open. They are not built but hollowed, pockets scooped back into the wood, each one the size of a room a family might sleep in. The doorways are low and every one of them has, cut into the jamb at the height of a passing hand, a mark: a set of notches, a groove, a raised bead. No two the same. This is how you knew whose door it was. Inside, a shelf at hip height for lying on, a niche for keeping things, a shallow bowl worn into the floor near the entrance — for what, no one can say. Grain, maybe. Seeds of the plant, ground to meal. There was nothing else here to eat.

Higher up the terraces are narrower and the pockets smaller. Higher still, the wall is unworked and simply curves away into nothing. The town occupies perhaps a third of the interior surface. The rest was never needed, or the need ran out.

And nothing lives in it. Not a person, not a mouse, not a beetle, not the smallest mite in the dust. The dust itself is pure — fine fallen cellulose, spore-cases empty for centuries, the sifted grit of the wood's slow crumbling — and when you put your hand down on a shelf it comes up clean of anything that ever moved on its own.

---

The place is loud.

This is the thing that a person entering for the first time will not be ready for, because from outside the husk is silent, a swelling in the plain. But the husk is a drum. It is a resonating body, ancient and dry and hollow and taut, and the plant, whatever it is still doing, does not do it quietly.

There is the ground note first, which you feel more than hear: a deep, continuous tone somewhere at the bottom of the audible, as if the whole hollow were a pipe someone is breathing into very slowly. It does not stop. It has not stopped in living memory. The wall vibrates with it under your palm. Over the days it swells and subsides — over what may be days; there is no way here to tell — and the people who lived here called this the *tide* or the *lung*, and set their sleeping and waking by it.

Above the ground note, cracks. The husk is contracting, has been contracting for longer than the town has existed, tightening around its own emptiness a hair's breadth in a lifetime. Under that pressure the wood splits, somewhere, without warning, and the split is a report like a plank snapped across a knee, enormous in the closed space, and the echo runs around the curved wall three, four, five times before it lets go. Then quiet, and the ground note, and in some other hour, another. The old tissue is settling into its final shape and it is doing so audibly, one fracture at a time.

And there is the rattling. This is the thing that first makes the place strange rather than simply harsh. Deep in the walls — how deep is unclear — there are hard bodies. Seeds, if that is the word for them, or stones, or nodes of some kind, each the size of a fist or a head, sealed in chambers of their own. When the husk shifts they shift with it, knocking against the inside of their cells, and the sound comes through the wood as a muffled clatter that travels, moving along the wall from one side of the hollow to the other, the way a run of dropped pebbles moves. Some of the residents believed the seeds were moving on purpose. Some believed they were counting. There is no way to know, and no one has ever seen one; the chambers are too deep to reach with wooden tools, and nobody who tried is here to say what they found.

Now and then — rarely, once in a long while — there is a different sound, low and drawn out, a tearing groan that goes on and on, and when it stops there is a new smell in the air, sharp and green and almost sweet, and the next time a hand passes along that part of the wall it finds a new fissure weeping a bead of something clear and gummy that hardens in an hour to glass. This is the only wetness the place produces and it is not water. It is what the plant has instead.

---

The rules of the place are few and the town obeyed them absolutely.

Nothing burns. Nothing is seen. Every path is learned by the feet and by the count of steps and by the marks cut at hand-height; a stranger goes nowhere alone. Things are known by their sound — a tap on this section of wall rings differently from a tap on that one, and the terraces were named for their notes, and a person coming home listened for their own wall answering. Ropes are struck before they are trusted. Doors are found by the notch. The tide governs the day.

Nothing comes in and nothing much goes out. The dust does not blow; there is no wind inside, only the slow flexing of the air with the ground note. The temperature does not change. Nothing grows here in the ordinary way — no seedling, no moss — because there is no water and no light; the only growth is the plant's own, and that is not growth so much as consolidation, a tightening, a finishing.

And everything here is made of the one thing. The floor, the walls, the ropes, the tools, the bowls, the bed-shelves, the marks on the doors, the dust in the corners, the seeds knocking in their cells. There was never a second material. The town is a way the plant was shaped by hands, and the hands are gone, and the shaping remains, and the plant is going on without them, closing over its wound as slowly as a lake freezes.

---

What is strange, finally, is not the dark or the noise or the emptiness, though each is strange enough. It is the feeling — impossible to shake once it has arrived — that the town was not built in the plant but grown out of it, that the terraces follow the rings because the rings were always going to hold terraces, that the door-notches were there before the doors. As if the hollow had wanted to be lived in, and been lived in, and now, in its long final settling, had let the living go and kept the shape of it. The steps are worn in the middle. The bowls are worn smooth. The ropes ring when you touch them. Somewhere in the wall a seed turns over and knocks, and the knock goes around the whole chamber and comes back to where it started, and the deep note under everything rises, by a little, the way a breath does before it is let out.
