**The Pressing Ground**

You come to it from above, though not by much. The land drops maybe the height of a house into a basin about the size of a village square, and the rim is not soil or stone but a lip of root — grey, braided, wide enough to walk along, and slick. Everything past the lip is green, or the colors green becomes when it has been wet for centuries: bottle, bruise, the yellow of old bruise, black where the light doesn't reach.

There is no ground here. That is the first thing to understand and the last thing you will actually believe. What you step down onto is tissue — a mat of stems laid over stems, fibrous and dense, that gives a finger's width under your weight and then holds. Water comes up around your foot with each step and drains back when you lift it. It is warm, the water. Body-warm. The whole basin breathes it out: a low, constant fog that never rises past knee height, so that you walk in clear air with your legs in cloud.

---

It is loud. You knew that from the rim, but on the floor the sound has a body to it. Something is pumping. A deep double stroke, *hunh — hunh*, then a pause of eight or nine seconds, then again. Beneath that, continuously, a hiss like a kettle left just short of boiling, and under the hiss a ticking, thousands of small ticks at once, that you eventually place as the sound of pods and seams and joints all over the basin expanding by fractions in the heat.

You go looking for the pump.

---

The first machine you find is a flywheel. Cast iron, taller than you, half-sunk at an angle into the mat. Its rim has been eaten to lace by rust and then sheathed — the plant has laid down bark over the iron the way a tree closes over a nail, so the wheel now has a wooden skin with iron showing through in places like bone. It is turning. Very slowly. Faster than an hour hand, slower than a minute hand. When you put your palm on it you feel it move, and you feel that it is warm, and you feel, through the bark, a pulse.

What turns it is not obvious until you look at the axle. A cable of living stem is wound around the shaft — a climbing thing, thick as a wrist, and it is *growing* around the shaft, each new inch of tendril taking purchase and tightening, and the tightening drives the wheel. On the far side, older coils are dying, going brown and brittle, slackening and falling away in husks that pile beneath the axle in a slow heap. The plant winds itself on one side and unwinds by dying on the other, and the wheel turns. It has probably been turning for longer than the iron has been iron.

---

There are other pieces. Not many — the basin is small and you can find them all in an afternoon. A length of pipe running perhaps forty paces, riveted, lifted off the mat on cradles of aerial root. A transformer housing with its porcelain insulators intact and a vine wearing them like rings. A pressure vessel the size of a cart, its gauge glass gone milky, and something inside it that sloshes. Fragments: a bracket, a valve wheel, a rail spike sunk in a stem and grown over so only the head shows, gleaming, kept polished by the drip that falls on it from above.

None of it is arranged. There is no sense of a plan, or of a floor where the machines stood in rows, or of walls that held them. They are scattered the way stones are scattered in a field — by something that was not thinking about them. And yet every one of them is in use. Every one has been taken up.

You begin to wonder whether they were ever brought here at all.

---

The pump is the pressure vessel. You understand this when you are close enough to see the vessel swell. Its iron shell is cracked along a seam, and through the crack you can see the interior is packed with pale tissue, veined, glistening, and this tissue contracts — *hunh* — and the whole vessel shudders on its cradle, and water pushes out through a fitting at the base and runs off along a channel that is not a channel but a fold in the mat, worn to a groove by a very long time. Then the tissue eases and draws in, sucking with a wet click, and the hiss sharpens as the vessel refills through a hundred hairlike inlets from the mat below.

The plant is using the vessel as a heart. Or it grew a heart and the vessel is what a heart looks like when it grows around a shape it found. You cannot tell. The longer you stay the less the question seems to matter.

---

What it is making is harder to say.

Follow the groove from the pump and it leads to a low shelf on the basin's east side where the mat has grown into a series of shallow rectangular troughs — six of them, side by side, each about the length of a person, with squared corners no plant should make. The water from the pump runs into the first, sits, warms, evaporates a little, runs to the next. By the sixth trough it is no longer water. It is thick, amber, faintly sweet-smelling, and it has begun to set.

At the end of the shelf, past the sixth trough, there are blocks. Amber blocks the size of loaves, dozens of them, stacked in a way that suggests the stacking was done by the same slow force that turns the wheel — pushed by growth, settled by weight. Older blocks at the bottom have gone dark and cloudy. Some have split. Inside the split ones there are things: a moth, wings spread as if pinned. A twist of copper wire. A seed, or a rivet, or something that is both.

You could pick one up. Nobody would stop you. Nobody is here.

---

Nobody is here, and almost nothing is. In a whole afternoon you see one animal — a beetle the length of your thumb, black with a bronze shine, feeding at the axle of the flywheel on the grease that the dying tendrils weep. It is not afraid of you. It has no reason to have learned fear. When you come back the next day it is there again, or another like it, in the same place, and you never see two at once.

Nothing calls. Nothing sings. The noise of the place is entirely the noise of the place — pump and hiss and tick and, once an hour or so, a hard flat *crack* from somewhere in the mat, so loud your ears ring, which you eventually trace to a seedpod the size of a boot bursting along a seam and flinging its contents ten paces into the fog. The seeds do not land in soil. There is no soil. They land on the mat and are absorbed by it, or they land on iron and take root in rust, and either way in a year or ten they are part of the thing that made them.

---

The rules of the place, as far as you can work them out:

Nothing here has an edge that stays an edge. Iron becomes bark. Bark becomes iron-colored. The water is blood-warm because it is, in some sense, blood. The rectangles of the troughs and the roundness of the wheel are the only geometry the plant did not invent, and it has learned them so well you can no longer say who taught whom.

Nothing here hurries. The wheel turns once in a long afternoon. A block of amber takes a season to set. The husks beneath the axle have been accumulating since before the rivets were forged, and they are not yet knee-deep. You could sit on the rim for a year and see nothing happen, and everything would have happened.

Nothing here is wasted, and nothing is kept. The blocks are made, and stacked, and darken, and eventually the mat grows up over the lowest ones and they are gone into it, moth and wire and all. The place is a factory that produces itself, and the product is that the factory continues.

And it is bounded. This is what strikes you most, sitting on the lip at evening with your feet in the warm fog and the pump going *hunh — hunh* below you. The root-rim is a wall. Beyond it, the ordinary land — wet, low, unremarkable — carries on, and the plant does not go there. It has no interest. It has everything it needs inside: shapes to grow around, water to lift, iron to eat, and a heart. It is not spreading. It is not dying. It has arrived at the form it was going to have and it is simply, loudly, patiently, being that.

---

The light goes. It is a plain light here, always — overcast-bright, no drama, the kind of daylight that could be any hour of a mild day. When it finally fades the basin does not go dark all at once. The amber blocks hold it a while, glowing faintly at the edges of the shelf like banked coals. The glass on the pressure gauge catches something and gives it back. Then the fog thickens, rises past your knees to your waist, and the sound is all that is left: the double stroke, the kettle-hiss, the ticking of ten thousand seams, and, just once, in the dark, the crack of a pod letting go.
