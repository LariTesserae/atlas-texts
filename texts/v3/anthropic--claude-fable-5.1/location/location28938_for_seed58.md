You reach it by going down, and the going down is the first thing that tells you about the place. The stair is iron, then brick, then the brick stops and the stair is cut into stone that has no right to be there — black, glass-skinned, fractured into planes so clean they hold a reflection. This is new rock. Not new the way a landslide is new. New the way a scab is new. The heat begins on the eleventh landing and by the twentieth you have stopped thinking of it as heat and begun thinking of it as a medium, like water, something you have entered and are now inside.

---

The district is called the Warm Ward on the maps above, which is a joke the people above tell. It occupies a single cavity: a fissure, roughly the shape of a lens stood on its edge, perhaps two hundred paces long, forty across at the widest, and high enough that its ceiling is lost in the murk unless the lamps on the upper gantries are lit. A cathedral, if a cathedral had been made by the ground cracking open and something molten pushing up into the crack and then stopping, thinking better of it, and cooling in place.

That is roughly what happened. A dyke of dark rock rose under the old city some decades ago — within living memory, though the people who lived that memory mostly left — and lifted the cellars, and split the bedrock into this seam, and sat there radiating. It is still radiating. The rock walls are warm to the touch in the way a horse is warm, and in the deeper corners they are warm in the way a kettle is warm, and there are places at the foot of the western wall where the stone is roped with a red-brown glaze and nobody puts a hand on it at all.

---

The stone is sharp. That is the second thing. Nothing here has had time to wear. Every edge of the fissure walls is a cut edge, conchoidal, faceted, and where the builders drove their anchors in they did not smooth the surrounding rock so much as shatter it into smaller versions of itself. Fresh spall lies at the base of the walls like black sugar. Here and there a slab the size of a door has come loose in the last month or the last week — you can tell by the clean pale wound behind it, not yet dulled by soot — and lies where it fell, and nobody has moved it, and a pipe has already been rerouted around it with a jointed elbow, because that is the ward's relationship to its own walls: they are not finished, and the ward is not finished, and both are used to it.

---

Above the shattered footing, everything is built. There is no unbuilt surface here at all above the height of a man. The two long walls of the fissure carry terraces of construction the way a cliff carries nests: brick vaults sprung directly off the rock, fired-clay housing stacked three and four deep, iron frames bolted through the stone with anchors as thick as a wrist, gantries, ladders, hoists, and pipe. Above all, pipe. The pipe is the ward's vegetation, its vine and its ivy. It runs along every wall in bundles, crosses the void on trusses so that when you look up through the dimness you see a lattice of iron and copper and glazed ceramic layered until it is hard to tell which run is nearest, and it is all sweating.

Because the ward is wet. Not wet like a river. Wet like a cellar in August, wet like the inside of a mouth. Groundwater from the old city's aquifer finds the fissure the way water finds anything, seeps down the upper walls in stains and threads, meets the hot rock, and is gone — becomes steam, becomes air, rises. The upper third of the fissure is a fog of it. And where that rising vapour meets anything cooler than itself — the return pipes, the iron of the gantries, the undersides of the higher vaults, the far cold ceiling of the seam — it beads, and gathers, and falls. It rains here. It rains in the ward on a schedule that is not quite a schedule, more often at night when the city above draws less water and the seep increases, in sudden showers that last a few minutes and land hot, and in a permanent thin drizzle off the pipes that never fully stops. The brick is dark with it. The iron is streaked with it. Every horizontal surface has a rim of mineral where the water sat and was taken back by the heat and left its salts behind.

---

The salts are the young thing here that is not rock. In a place this hot and this wet, minerals precipitate at a pace that would embarrass a limestone cave. A copper return pipe, cool relative to the air, will grow a fur of white and pale green crystals along its underside within a week; leave it a month and the fur becomes a fringe, a beard, a row of small stalactites the length of a finger, delicate as frost and sharp enough to draw blood. There are men in the ward whose whole work is scraping. They walk the gantries with wooden paddles and knock the growth down into troughs, and the growth is not waste — it is what the ward is for, or part of it — but the point is that formation here outruns wear. Nothing has time to soften. Everything is always being made and remade, and the made things have edges.

Where the scrapers do not reach, the ward is furred white. The undersides of the highest trusses are shaggy with it. The rock of the far end, where the fissure pinches to a crack too narrow to build in, is coated in a translucent glaze of salt that has grown over the fresh black facets like ice over a broken window, and it catches what light there is and holds it.

---

The light is little. The ward runs on electricity fed down from above and generated partly below, and it spends it on engines before it spends it on lamps. The lamps are bulbs in cages, spaced along the gantries at intervals of maybe fifteen paces, each throwing a warm brown circle onto wet brick and iron and no further. Between the circles is a dimness in which you can see shapes — the vaults, the black wall behind them, a ladder, a man — but not colour. The fog in the upper third glows faintly where lamps are lost in it. On the western wall, low down, the red-brown glaze of the hottest rock gives back a dull light of its own that is not quite light, more like the memory of a stove after it has been turned down, and the people who work near it have a pallor from it that you learn to recognise.

Above all of this — very high, at the top of the lens, where the fissure's roof is the underside of the old city's foundations — there are grates. Four or five of them, rusted, the size of a table. In the day they show as grey squares. Real daylight comes through them and dies within a few yards of the ceiling. They are the ward's sky.

---

The work is heat. The dyke gives it away for nothing and the ward takes it. Along the eastern wall, where the rock is merely hot and not dangerous, a row of shallow ceramic pans a stride across sit directly on the stone, and the brine pumped down from the city's saltworks above is ladled into them and left, and the heat takes the water out of it in hours instead of weeks, and a woman with a wooden rake gathers the crust. Behind the pans, the vaults are kilns — not fired, warmed; nothing is burnt in the ward, there is no smoke, there has never been a fire here that anyone remembers because the air itself does the work of fire. Green clay goes in and comes out a day later hard as biscuit. Timber goes in wet from the river and comes out seasoned. Cloth dries in minutes on the lower gantries and the dyers of the city send it down by the bale.

And the water — the seep, the drizzle, the condensation off every pipe — is the other half of the work. The ward is a still. The hot rock boils the groundwater out of the walls and the pipes catch it as it falls back distilled, and the distilled water is precious to the works above, and a tenth of the district's iron is gutters, and the gutters run to cisterns, and the cisterns feed pumps. The pumps are the loudest thing here. There are four of them, squat, brass-and-iron, in a vault at the eastern end, and they are driven not by coal but by steam that the rock itself raises in a boiler set into the hottest floor — a boiler with no firebox, just a thick iron belly sunk into stone that would blister you, and a plume of steam that rises from its relief valve every eleven minutes with a soft sustained exhalation, like something very large sighing in its sleep.

---

So the sound of the ward is that: the sigh. And under it the pumps, a slow double thud you feel in the brick more than hear. And drip. Drip everywhere, at every pitch — on iron, on brick, on salt crust, into troughs, into cisterns, onto the hot stone where it does not drip so much as hiss and vanish. Tick of cooling metal, tick of heating metal, the small groans of pipe expanding along its length. A scraper's paddle knocking crystal down. Sandals on wet brick. Very occasionally a voice, low, because the air is thick and carries sound close and nobody needs to raise one. The ward is quiet the way a body is quiet — full of processes, none of them loud.

Every so often the rock itself speaks. A crack, sharp, somewhere in the walls, as the dyke shifts a hair's breadth in its long cooling. Everyone in earshot stops and looks toward it. Then it is over and they go back to what they were doing. Later someone will find the new spall and reroute a pipe around it.

---

Nothing lives here that did not come down the stair on its own feet. This is not a place that forbids life so much as one that life declines. No rats — there is nothing for them; nothing rots here, it dries. No flies, for the same reason. No spiders, because no flies. The cisterns are lidded and even the lidded ones stay clear. In the hottest corners, beside the glazed rock, the air is so dry an inch above the wet floor that a moth would crisp before it landed. You can stand in the ward for an hour and not see one thing move that a person or an engine did not move.

Except. Along the seams where the condensation is steadiest — the underside of the coolest gantries, the lip of the northern cistern, the shaded foot of the eastern wall where the drizzle has worn a permanent dark stripe — there is green. A moss, or something moss-shaped, thick and tight as felt, that has learned to take the mineral rain and the warmth and the faint grey from the grates and make a life of it. It grows nowhere else. It grows there with an intensity that is almost alarming, a green so saturated in this brown-and-black place that it looks lit. The scrapers leave it alone. Nobody decided to. And in one place — beneath the largest grate, at the ceiling, rooted in a crack between two foundation blocks of the old city, hanging downward into the ward's sky like a lamp — there is a fig. Small, twisted, pale-leaved, fed by the seep and the grate-light and the steam. It fruits, sometimes. The fruit is hard and never ripens and falls the forty-odd feet to the gantries and dries there into something like a stone, and the ward's people pick these up and keep them, because a thing that fell out of the sky and did not break seems worth keeping.

---

The unusual thing about the ward — the thing you cannot quite name until you have been in it a while — is that it is a town whose weather is its industry and whose industry is its weather. The rain that falls on the roofs is the product. The heat that dries the roofs is the engine. When the pumps run hard the fog thins and the drizzle eases and the salt grows slower; when the city above draws its water at dawn and the seep slackens, the ward dries out for an hour and the brick pales and the scrapers' paddles ring instead of thud. The people who live here — and some do, in the mid-level vaults, on cots behind ceramic screens, sleeping through the heat of the day and working the cooler night that is not cool — read the ward's sky the way farmers read theirs. *Wet tonight*, one will say, looking up at the fog on the lamps, and mean the pans will yield less and the cisterns more.

And the ward is growing. Slowly, from below — the dyke's heat is still fracturing the rock at the fissure's roots, opening new pockets, and every few years a floor gives way onto a new chamber a man's height deep, glassy and black and too hot to enter, and the builders wait, and it cools, and they go in with anchors and pipe and brick and it becomes ward. The place is being born out of the ground at the pace of a lifetime, and the town is following it down as it opens, terrace by terrace, into stone that was molten when the oldest of them were children.

Standing on the lowest gantry at the western end, with the glazed rock giving off its not-light a few yards away and the sigh of the boiler coming every eleven minutes and the hot drizzle ticking on the iron over your head, you understand that this is not a ruin and not a foundation. It is a place still in the act of happening. The rock is new, the salt is new, the fog is new every minute, and the sharp-edged, wet, dim, crowded town has grown into the wound the way the moss grew into the drizzle-stripe — because the conditions were exactly wrong for almost everything, and exactly right for this.
