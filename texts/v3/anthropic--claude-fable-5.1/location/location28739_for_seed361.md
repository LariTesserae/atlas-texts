The plateau is so old it has stopped having a shape. Whatever mountain it once was has been rubbed down to a tableland of warm red-grey stone, level enough that rain, on the rare days it comes, does not know which way to run and simply stands in shallow films until the sun takes it back. Grass grows in the cracks, and the cracks are the only relief for kilometres: a country that has finished, that has reached the form it will hold until the end.

Except in one place.

You would not see it until you were nearly on top of it. The stone dips, then drops, and there is a hollow perhaps forty paces across and a little deeper than a house is tall, with walls that slope in on all sides like the inside of a bowl someone set down and forgot. The rim is not sharp. Nothing here is sharp. But the hollow is the only downward the plateau has left, and everything that can fall has been falling into it for a very long time.

---

The first thing is the sound. It rises out of the hollow before you reach the edge, and at the edge it hits you like heat.

It is birds, mostly. Swifts in the thousands, using the bowl the way water would use it, pouring in over the rim and spiralling down and wheeling up again in a column that never quite stops turning. Their voices are a single tearing shriek layered so many times over that it becomes a texture rather than a noise, a fabric of sound stretched across the opening. Under it, lower, the sawing of insects — cicadas, or something like cicadas, in the trees. Under that, at the bottom, so low you feel it in your sternum before you hear it, a hum that does not belong to anything alive.

The swifts do not roost here. They roost elsewhere on the plateau, in the cracks. They come to the hollow to eat, and they eat all day, because the hollow is full of food.

---

There are four trees. Only four, in a bowl that could hold forty, standing well apart on the sloping floor with bare warm stone between them. But each one is enormous — trunks broader than a doorway, canopies that reach nearly to the rim — and each one carries so much on its limbs that the tree itself is hard to find. Moss in hanging curtains. Ferns rooted in the forks. Bromeliads by the hundred, their leaf-cups full of dark water, each cup a pond with its own population: mosquito larvae, tiny frogs the colour of tea, a beetle that lives nowhere else. Orchids. Lichen in grey and sulphur. Vines that have gone up and come down again and gone up. The trees are lush the way a coral head is lush, one living thing wearing a thousand others.

And every leaf is wet.

It has not rained. It does not, particularly, rain here. But the hollow is damp in the way a cellar is damp, in the way the inside of a bottle is damp — the air in it holds moisture the plateau above has long since given up. At dawn, when the cold night air has pooled in the bottom of the bowl and the first sun touches the eastern wall, the whole hollow fogs. A knee-deep mist forms on the floor and lifts slowly, and as it lifts it beads on everything: the moss, the stone, the wings of the moths that have not yet found shelter. For an hour the hollow drips. Then the sun climbs, the fog burns off, and the day's water is spent — except in the bromeliad cups, which hold it, and in the deep shade under the trees, where the stone never fully dries and is slick with a black film of algae that has been growing there, undisturbed, for longer than any of the trees have stood.

---

The mist lifts slowly. That is the first thing you notice is wrong.

Not wrong, exactly. Slow. The fog rises in the hollow the way fog rises anywhere, but you find yourself waiting for it, and it takes longer than it should. A leaf, falling from the canopy, takes a breath longer than it should to reach the ground. The swifts, when they dive into the bowl, seem for a moment to be flying through something thicker than air — their wings stretch, their turns widen — and then they pull up and out and are ordinary birds again.

You could stand there a long time deciding whether you were imagining it.

---

On the eastern wall of the hollow, half-buried where the stone has crept down over them, there are things that are not stone.

Pale. Smooth. Curved — sections of a ring, or an arc, each about the length of an arm, spaced irregularly around the wall as if a circle had been set into the ground here once and the ground had since flowed like a slow river around and over it, breaking it into pieces and carrying the pieces a little way. Five are visible. Perhaps others are under the floor. The material has no grain, no join, no wear. Lichen will not grow on it. Condensation beads on it in the morning like everything else, but the beads do not run; they sit, perfectly round, until the sun takes them.

If you put your hand on one — through the moss that has grown up against it but not onto it — you feel the hum. It is not vibration. It is something more like a pressure, or a temperature that is not warm or cold. And you understand, or half-understand, that the hollow is not a place where the stone happened to fall in. The hollow is what the ring has been doing.

Whatever these pieces were built to do, they are still doing some fraction of it. A field that makes things fall a little slower, that makes the air hold its water a little longer, that keeps the night's cold from leaving as fast as it wants to. Some purpose enormous and specific that no one remembers. And the plateau has been eroding around it for an age — the whole mountain worn down to a table — while the stone inside the ring's reach came down slower, and slower, until now the ring stands in a bowl of its own making, a place where time is measured a little differently than it is a metre away.

It has not made the hollow. It has only let the hollow happen more slowly than everything around it. That was enough.

---

The insects understood first. Whatever the ring does, it is good for small flying things: the air is thick and wet and still, and the morning mist is a feast of drifting spores and pollen and one another. The cicadas came for the trees, or the trees came for the damp — no one is left to say which — and the frogs came for the cups of water, and the beetles for the rot in the forks, and the moths for the flowers that open in the fog, and the spiders for the moths, and the bats for the moths as well, in a second shift after the swifts have gone. Between the walls there is more life per breath of air than anywhere on the plateau, perhaps anywhere for a hundred kilometres. It is a fullness that presses on you.

And yet the hollow is not crowded. Four trees, bare stone between. The life is in the trees and in the air, and the floor is almost empty — a few ferns, a ring of moss around each trunk, the black algae in the shade, and lying here and there the bones of birds that misjudged the slow air and came down too hard. Nothing takes the bones. Even the beetles leave them. They lie where they fall, and the fine grit that drifts down from the rim covers them slowly, and in the walls of the hollow, where the stone has cracked and slumped, you can see layers of them — thin white seams in the grey, one above another, going down.

---

By mid-afternoon the heat has come into the bowl and the swifts are at their loudest. The sound builds through the day; there is no other way to describe it. The hollow holds sound the way it holds water, and the shrieking of the birds and the sawing of the insects accumulate, each hour louder than the one before, until near sunset you cannot hear your own voice and the air itself seems to be ringing. Then the light goes off the western wall and the swifts leave — all at once, in a rising spiral that empties the bowl in under a minute — and the silence that follows is so complete that you can hear the hum again, and the drip of one bromeliad cup overflowing into another, and the first bat clearing its throat in the dark under the canopy.

Then the cold air begins to pool. Then the fog.

It has done this, more or less, every day for longer than the trees have been trees, longer than there have been swifts on the plateau to find it. The stone comes down grain by grain from the rim and takes a little longer than it should to reach the floor, and the ring holds, and the plateau around it wears away toward nothing, and one day — not soon, but the hollow is patient, and the hollow is the only thing on the plateau that has anywhere left to go — one day there will be nothing but the bowl, standing alone above a plain, full of trees and birds and mist, humming.
