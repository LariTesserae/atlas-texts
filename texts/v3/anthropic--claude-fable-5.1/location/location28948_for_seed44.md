You come to it along a raised causeway that is the only firm thing for miles, and even the causeway is not firm so much as agreed upon. It is a spine of packed shell and clinker, two carts wide, and on either side the ground drops perhaps half a metre into water the colour of strong tea. That drop is the whole of the local topography. Everything here is either the causeway's height or the water's height, and the difference between them is the difference between being a road and being a marsh.

The plant is what you notice before the town, because the plant is what there is. It is a cane — a true cane, jointed, hollow, thumb-thick at the base and tapering — and it stands in the water at a density that makes the word *stand* seem inadequate. It does not stand in the water; it *is* the water's upper surface, a second horizon at head height, green-grey and continuous, with the true water somewhere below it in the dark. Where the causeway cuts through, you see it in section: a mat of pale root a metre deep, laced with old rhizome the colour of tendon, then a dense stubble of shoots, then the standing culms, then the leaf-crown, which meets over the road in places and drips. It always drips. It rained before you came, or it will rain after, or the air has simply given up holding its water and is letting it go a drop at a time onto the leaves, which pass it down.

The warmth is the kind that comes up as well as down. The water holds the day's heat and returns it at night, and the cane mat holds the water's heat and breathes it, so the air is always a few degrees off your skin and heavy enough to feel against your face when you turn your head. Nothing here dries. Iron does not rust here so much as it *ferns* — that is the local word — putting out red-brown feathery growth wherever a surface has been left unpainted for a season.

---

The town, when it comes, comes as sound first.

It is a low continuous grinding under a higher rhythmic knock, and under both a hiss like something being poured that never finishes pouring. You hear it a long way out, dulled by the cane, and it grows as the causeway curves, and then the cane opens and there it is: a hamlet of perhaps forty roofs set on a raised pan of made ground, and at the centre of it, occupying more area than all forty roofs together, the works.

The works is the reason for the town and the town is honest about it. The houses lean towards the works the way plants lean towards a window. They are timber — cane-timber, in the older ones, split culm laid flat and pegged, which is why the older walls have a ribbed and glossy look — and they sit on piles driven into the mat, and their roofs are cane thatch under corrugated sheet, the sheet added later, the thatch never removed because you don't remove a thing that still works. Between them run gangways of plank, slick and green. Under the gangways is not ground. Under the gangways is the same root-mat as everywhere, only here it has been cut and cut again for a hundred years, and it has responded by thickening, so that the town floats on a callus.

Everything is close. You cannot see the sky from the gangways without looking straight up, and when you do look straight up, half of it is roof-edge and half of it is leaf. The cane grows in the gaps between houses; it grows up through the gangways where a plank has rotted; it grows from the gutters. Nobody pulls it. It is not weeds. It is the crop, and it is also the ground, and it is also, somewhat, the walls, and the town's only relationship with it is one of periodic harvest — you take what has grown into your space, you bundle it, you carry it to the works, and the space stays yours for another season.

---

The works is a long shed with a tall centre section and two low wings, and it is the only thing in the town that is not primarily made of the plant. Its frame is iron, riveted, and its roof is slate carried in from a great distance and now grown over on the north pitch with a moss so thick that the slates have vanished under it and the roof looks upholstered. From the tall section a brick stack rises — the only vertical in the landscape that is not a culm — and from the stack comes not smoke, exactly, but a steady exhalation of steam, white against grey, leaning always the same way because the wind here comes always from the same quarter.

Inside, the tall section is a cathedral, and it is used as one is used: for a single purpose, at volume, by people who have stopped hearing it. The floor is wet. The floor is always wet. Down the centre runs a line of rollers, iron, each pair the size of a cart-wheel and set so close that a hand would not pass between them, and through them, all day, the cane goes. It goes in green and jointed and comes out as a wet flat ribbon and a run of grey-green juice that sheets off into troughs and down into the tanks below the floor. The rollers are the grinding. The knock is the feed — a cam-driven ram that shoves each bundle into the first pair with a wooden *chock*, four times a minute, and has done so for so long that the beam it strikes is worn into a cup. The hiss is the engine.

The engine is at the far end, behind a rail, and it is old in the way the town is old: not decrepit but *settled*. It is a horizontal steam engine with a flywheel taller than a man, and its brass has been polished so many times that the lettering on the maker's plate has gone soft, like a word said until it loses its edges. It burns the dry press-cake from its own work — the ribbon, once it has been through the drying wing, is the fuel — so the works eats the plant and is fed by what it has eaten, and the stack breathes out the water the plant drew up, and the water comes down again on the cane, and this is not a metaphor, this is simply the arrangement.

The juice goes to the tanks. In the tanks it rets and thickens and separates, over weeks, into a fibre that is drawn off and racked and a liquor that is boiled down in the low wing to a resin that is cast in wooden moulds and stacked in the loading shed in pale blocks that smell of hay and turpentine. That is the product. It leaves once a month on a barge that comes up the canal on the town's far side, and the barge is the town's other horizon: the causeway in, the canal out.

---

There is very little that lives here that is not the plant or the people.

You notice it after an hour. The cane is not silent — it ticks and clicks as it grows and as the water moves through it — but it is not *inhabited*. There are no bird calls under the machine noise. The retting tanks, which anywhere else would be a riot of frogs, hold only a skin of pale scum and the occasional slow bubble. Once a day, perhaps, a single egret comes in low over the leaf-crown, lands on the rail of the loading dock, stands for twenty minutes looking at the water, and leaves without having eaten anything. The children know it and do not chase it. There is a cat in the drying wing, orange and enormous and asleep on the warm press-cake, and it is the only mammal you will see that is not carrying a bundle. Insects exist, but they are outnumbered so completely by leaves that they seem like a rumour.

Whether this is the noise or the monoculture or the resin in the water nobody in the town will tell you, because nobody in the town considers it a question. The cane is what grows here. The works is what's done with it. The absence of everything else is not an absence to them; it is the shape of the place.

---

The light is ordinary and this is a mercy. It comes down grey-white through the cloud and grey-green through the leaves and lands on wet surfaces and is doubled, so that the town, for all its enclosure, is never dim — it is lit from below, by its own puddles. In the works the light comes in through high clerestory glass so fogged with steam on the inside and moss on the outside that it arrives as a general luminance without direction. Nothing here casts a hard shadow. Everything is lit the way the inside of a leaf is lit.

At night there is electricity, of a kind: the flywheel drives a belt that drives a dynamo that lights perhaps thirty bulbs, all of them in the works and the gangways, none in the houses. The houses have oil. The bulbs hang in cages fogged with condensation and each one has its own small weather, a halo of drift, moths that never come.

---

You would call it a slow place and you would be right, but slow is not the same as still. The rhythm is this: the cane is cut in a rotation, in long strips that radiate from the town like the ribs of a leaf, and each strip takes three years to come back to cutting height, and there are perhaps forty strips, so the town lives inside a clock whose hand takes forty years to come round. Nobody alive remembers the first cutting of any strip they are working. The strips are named for people who have been dead a century. The engine has run, with stops for repair, for longer than any of its stokers have been alive, and it will run after them, and the resin will go down the canal a barge-load at a time, and the mat will thicken under the town by another finger's-breadth a decade, and the town will rise on it, imperceptibly, and drive its piles a little deeper to compensate.

The water rises too, over years, so that the causeway has been built up four times — you can see the layers where a cart has cut the edge — and the two heights, road and marsh, have chased each other upward together without the distance between them ever changing. That half-metre is the town's one constant, more constant than the people, more constant than the iron.

---

In the late afternoon the knock stops.

It stops because the feed is being cleared for the shift change, and for perhaps ten minutes the works is only hiss and grind, and the difference is so large that you feel it in your teeth. In that quieter interval you hear the cane. It is a sound like a very great many small hinges, all slightly dry, all turning at once and slowly, and it comes from every direction at head height and above, and it is the sound of a hundred thousand culms taking on the day's last light and shifting, minutely, to hold it. Someone in the works hits the beam with the ram again — *chock* — and the cane goes back beneath the noise, where it lives, where it has always lived, growing into the gaps.
