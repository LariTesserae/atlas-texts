Begin at the surface, because the surface is the first strange thing.

It sits at about thirty-two hundred meters. That is the altitude of the water. Not a lake in the ordinary sense — nothing about it behaves like a lake — but a broad body of warm, faintly saline water filling the interior of a highland that did not exist, in any form you would recognize, a few thousand years ago. The rock came up fast. It came up with the sea still on it. What you are looking at, from the rim, is a piece of shallow ocean that was lifted whole into the sky and has not yet finished understanding where it is.

The rim is what gives the age away. Everywhere the ridgelines break the water they are sharp — not knife-edge, but unweathered, the color of the inside of a stone rather than the outside. Basalt and a pale, sugary limestone that had been seafloor reef when the uplift began. No talus slopes. No scree fans. Rain has had almost no time here; there are no gullies in the walls, only the occasional dark seam where the rock cracked while it was rising and the crack has stayed open, clean, un-rounded. Above the waterline, on the exposed rock, a crust of lichen and the first thin mosses, and in the seams, small ferns that have arrived from somewhere lower and are taking the place seriously.

The water is clear in a way that makes depth hard to judge. Standing on the rim, you can see the bottom fifteen, twenty meters down, and then further on, where the basin deepens, the color slides from green to a blue that stays lit longer than it should. Light comes through the water the way it comes through a very good window. At noon the whole submerged floor is visible as a kind of second landscape, rippling slightly, and you understand that the highland is really two highlands stacked: the one you are standing on and the one under it, which is older, and alive.

Go down.

The warmth is the next thing. The water is warm the way a body is warm — not hot, not the heat of a spring, but a steady blood-temperature that comes up from the rock beneath and does not vary much between day and night. The floor of the basin is not far, geologically, from whatever pushed it up here, and the whole body of water is a slow lid on that heat. There is no thermocline. You can descend twenty meters and feel almost nothing change.

And the floor is not bare. This is the thing that would take the longest to believe.

The old seabed came up with its life on it, and most of the life stayed. What had been a warm-water reef shelf — coral heads, seagrass meadow, mangrove fringe — is now a submerged plain at altitude, and the plants, if that is the word, have carried on. The seagrass is dense enough to hide the bottom completely across whole square kilometers, a single unbroken pelt of blades a meter and a half long, leaning all one way and then, further off, all another way, according to currents that have no obvious source. Between the grass and the surface float the canopies of what used to be mangroves and are now something else — the trunks still rooted, gone black and smooth, the branches crowned not in leaves but in a fine green weed that has colonized every surface the trees offered. Each drowned tree is a tower of hanging growth. They stand in loose groves, a few dozen to a grove, and from below, looking up, they are what the light comes through.

Where the coral was, the coral is mostly dead — the water is a little too fresh for it now, too still, perhaps too warm — and the dead heads are where the density truly gathers. Every surface is taken. Algae in sheets and in beards. Sponges, bloated and pale as risen dough. Something that grows in stacked translucent plates, like a shelf fungus rendered in glass, that no one has named. Colonies of a tube-building thing, each tube the width of a finger, that have built themselves up into low knobbed spires two and three meters tall, which sway. Where two spires stand close, the growth between them has knitted, so that whole neighborhoods of the reef are joined into a single soft porous mass that you could not push a hand through. It is not crowded in the sense of movement. It is crowded in the sense of a shelf packed with books: everything touching, nothing going anywhere.

And almost nothing swims.

This is the quiet of the place. A reef at sea level is loud, if you know how to listen — a continuous crackle of snapping and grazing and small collisions. Here there is nearly none of it. The fish came up with the water, but a shallow-shelf population does not survive being shut into a warm closed basin at altitude with no tide, no exchange, no upwelling; a few generations in, the ones that remain are strange, small, and few. You might see one in an hour. A thin silver thing that hangs vertically among the grass blades and does not move when you approach. Once, in the deeper blue, something larger passing at the edge of vision, slow, and gone. Otherwise the fauna of this place is the sessile kind, the kind that is indistinguishable from the vegetation unless you watch it a long time, and the only sound is the water itself: a low, bodily hum that may be the rock beneath still settling, or may be your own blood.

Sound does travel oddly. A stone dropped from the rim, striking the surface, is heard underwater not as a splash but as a single soft tap, delayed, and then a long fading tone as though the whole basin were a struck bowl. The dense growth absorbs almost everything else. You can be ten meters from someone and not hear them at all.

Wider now.

The basin is not one basin. The uplift made a highland the size of a small country, and the water fills its interior in a chain of connected bowls, each rimmed by fresh rock, each linked to the next by a submerged sill where the seabed dips between two ridges. From above, in the right light, you can trace the chain for forty or fifty kilometers before cloud takes it — a series of oval mirrors set into gray stone, some so close together their rims almost touch, some separated by broad shoulders of dry new land where the lichen and the ferns are working. In the westernmost bowls the water is shallower and the drowned mangroves still reach the surface; their weed-crowned tips stand out of the water like a field of green candles, and the wind moves them. In the east the bowls are deeper, and blue, and the floor is a long way down, and nothing has been seen there.

The rims hold. That is the arrangement everything depends on. There is no outlet — the ridges are young and unbroken, and no river has yet found the low point of any rim and begun to saw. Rain and cloud keep the basins topped; the water lost to seepage through the fractured stone is very nearly the water gained. Very nearly. Along the walls, just above the current waterline, there is a band a hand's width high where the rock is paler and the lichen has not yet arrived — the water has fallen that much, over however long. A finger's width a year. Perhaps less. The seagrass at the upper margins of the meadow is beginning to thin where the shallowing has let too much light in, and a band of the reef along the western sills stands a few centimeters into air at dawn, before the day's rain, and the growth there has gone crisp and bone-white and stopped.

Nothing is in a hurry. The reef is being abandoned by the water at a pace the reef cannot perceive and cannot outlast. Somewhere along the eastern rim a seam in the rock has been widening — a millimeter, a millimeter — and one century or the next it will reach the water table of the basin behind it and the whole chain will begin, very slowly, to drain toward the lowlands. The mangrove towers will stand in air and dry and fall. The tube-spires will collapse into chalk. It will be a limestone plateau then, scattered with the odd shapes of things that lived in water, and the ferns will have it.

But that is not yet. For now, the water sits at thirty-two hundred meters, warm, clear, lit to the bottom, carrying its dense and nearly silent freight of green. Cloud comes up the outer slopes in the afternoon and spills over the rims and lies on the surface of the basins, so that from underneath the light goes from white to pearl and the drowned groves stand in a soft, sourceless glow. The one silver fish, if it is still there, turns very slightly in the grass. The rock hums. A stone that fell yesterday lies on the seagrass where it landed, not yet grown over, though that has already begun.
