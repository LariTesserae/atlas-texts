The first thing you notice is the color, and the second thing you notice is that the color is the water. It is not blue the way the sea is blue, with distance and salt and things moving in it. It is a mineral blue-green, faintly milky, the tint of glass held edgewise, and it goes all the way down without changing. There is nothing suspended in it. Whatever was once suspended has long since settled, and settled, and settled, until the water gave up being cloudy and became simply a medium, as clear and cool as air in a cellar.

You are at the bottom of a cut. That is the plainest way to say it. Around you rise walls of pale grey limestone, and they rise the way no natural wall rises: straight up, plumb, for six or seven meters, then stepping back into a flat shelf about the width of a road, then straight up again. Three of these steps, four, each shelf a little softened with the fine white silt that has come down over the years, so that from below the terraces read less like stairs and more like the strata of a very orderly cliff. The corners are true. The floor beneath you is flat in a way that no valley floor is flat—a plane, essentially, sloping almost imperceptibly toward one end where the water is a shade darker and the silt a little deeper.

It was a quarry. Nobody built this; they took it away. Every cubic meter of the space you are floating in was once stone, and the stone went out the top on conveyors and in the beds of trucks to become aggregate and cement and the ballast under railway lines, and when the good seam ran out they hauled the machines up the haul road, cut the power to the pumps, and left. Groundwater, which had been fighting to get in the whole time, got in. It took a few years. Then it stopped, because it had reached its own level, and the level is where it has been since.

So there is nothing made here, and yet the industry is everywhere—it is the shape of the place. If you go close to the wall you can see it in the rock itself: rows of half-cylinders, each about the width of your wrist, running vertically down the face at regular intervals. These are the ghosts of drill holes, split lengthwise when the charge went off and the front half of the rock came away. They are as regular as the teeth of a comb. In places, the blast left a scallop-pattern between them, the stone fractured back in shallow shells, and the shells are now furred with a thin pale bloom of calcite where the water has been slowly reprecipitating the dust it once dissolved. On one shelf there is a groove, deep and polished, where a cable once ran over the lip for a long time; the edges of the groove have gone soft as candle wax. At the far end, where the floor darkens, a wide ramp climbs the wall in a single long diagonal—the haul road—its surface still bearing, under a finger's depth of silt, the faint twinned corrugations of tire tracks pressed into wet marl and then fixed there forever.

That is all. There is no equipment. There are no beams or pipes or bolts or fragments of anything. The pit was stripped with the thoroughness of people who did not intend to come back for anything, and the water has been left with only the negative of their work: the geometry of extraction, an industrial volume with the industry removed.

The light comes down in a column. Above you—thirty meters or so—the surface is a bright, wrinkled ceiling, and from it a wide soft shaft of daylight falls into the pit and spreads. The walls near the top are almost white with it; lower down they go grey-green and then a deep bottle-glass color at the floor, but even here it is not dim. You can see the far wall clearly. You can see the drill lines on it. Quarry water this old and this dead is among the clearest water on earth, and the light, having nothing to catch on, simply goes where the walls let it.

It is cool. Not cold—the sun has been working on the upper layers all summer—but down here at the floor the water sits at the temperature of the stone around it, which is the temperature of the ground a few meters under a temperate country, and that temperature does not move. There is a thermocline somewhere above your head, a soft boundary you would feel as a change against your skin if you rose through it, and below that boundary the water has not mixed with the surface in years. It is its own climate.

Life is thin here, and what there is of it is patient. Along the sunlit upper shelves a dark green filamentous algae has laid down a nap like wet felt, thicker where the ledges face the afternoon, thinning to nothing about ten meters down where the light is still ample but the water is too poor in anything to eat. The stone here is too clean. It leaches lime, the water is faintly alkaline, and almost nothing dissolves into it that a living thing could use. There is a stand of dead trees at the shallow end, where the pit's rim was once a spoil bank and a few birches and willows had got started before the flooding; they drowned standing, and they stand still, silver-grey trunks rising through the light toward the surface with the bark long gone and the wood gone hard and pale. One has fallen and lies across a shelf with a mat of that same green felt on its upper side. Around its base a small colony of stonewort has taken hold—a brittle, calcified, upright water-plant, chalky to the touch, growing in a ring a couple of meters across on the silt where the fallen tree has gathered enough fine sediment to root in. That is the garden. It is not growing. It is not dying. It has found its size.

There are no fish. There never were; no one stocked it and no stream feeds it. There are no snails on the stone, no midge larvae in the silt, no water fleas in the shafts of light. You could hang here for an hour and watch the column of brightness above you and nothing would cross it. It is one of the strangest things about the place—that the absence is not an emptiness with the feeling of having been recently vacated, but a settled, furnished absence, an emptiness that has been here long enough to be the natural condition of the room.

Because it is a room, in scale. Not a lake. You can see all of it from where you are: the four walls, the terraces, the ramp, the drowned trees, the ring of stonewort, the bright ceiling. A few hundred meters at its longest. Hamlet-sized, if a hamlet were a single volume of clear water held in a stone box. The walls give it a distinct interior feeling, a sense of enclosure that open water never has, and the terraces make the enclosure look deliberate, almost architectural—a cathedral with its roof off and the sky poured in.

The sound is nearly nothing, and it is the particular nearly-nothing of underwater, where the silence has a pressure to it. Occasionally, from far above, a muffled thump as some surface event—a gust, a wave slapping the ramp—translates itself down through the water column and arrives as a dull soft blow felt as much as heard. Occasionally a tick, very small and very clear, from somewhere along the wall: a grain of calcite letting go, a flake of shocked stone finally deciding, after however many years, to fall. Your own breathing, if you are breathing. Otherwise the water carries no sound because nothing in it is making any.

And nothing here changes. That is the thing that settles on you last and stays. The surface up there has weather—wind writes on it, rain pocks it, ice closes over it in winter and goes—but down here the pit reached its equilibrium a long time ago and has held it. The silt has finished falling. The water has finished clearing. The walls have finished shedding their loose skin and have gone over to the slow work of calcite bloom, which will take centuries to change the look of anything. The dead trees will stand until they don't. The stonewort will ring its fallen trunk. The light will come down the column every day at the same angle for the season and leave again. A small, cool, flooded volume, shaped exactly by what was taken out of it, holding still.
