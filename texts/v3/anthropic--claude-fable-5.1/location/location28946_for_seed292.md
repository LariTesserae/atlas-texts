Begin with the rain, because the rain is what there is.

It does not fall so much as occupy. At this altitude the cloud base sits below the ground, and so the ground is inside the cloud, and the cloud is falling, and has been falling — not for the season, not for the year — for as long as the rock has been up here. The air is a grey saturated thing you push through rather than breathe. Water comes from above, from the side, and, where the wind shears off the edges of the tableland, from below, in sheets that lift and hang and drop again. There is no rain shadow. There is nowhere to stand where you are not already wet through, and after a while the distinction between rain and standing water and the water inside your clothes stops meaning anything.

Under the rain is the rock, and the rock is old past the point where age is a quality it has. It is quartzite, pink-grey where it shows, laid down as sand before there were animals to walk on it, lifted to nearly three miles and then left alone. Nothing has folded it since. The only thing that has happened to it is weather, and the weather has done what it does over that kind of time: it has taken the mountains that were here and worn them down to a single vast flat, a tabletop the size of a province, tilted almost imperceptibly toward one side so that every drop that lands on it eventually goes the same way. The relief is a few dozen metres across whole kilometres. There are no peaks. The high places are just places the water has not yet decided to remove, and the low places are where it has. The stone at the surface has weathered into shapes that look poured — rounded bosses, low pillars, honeycombed lips over shallow basins — and every one of them is slick and dark and streaming.

Because the plateau is flat and the rain is constant, the water cannot leave fast enough. It stands. Most of the tableland is under a skin of it, a few centimetres to a few metres, a mosaic of pools and connecting sheets and slow braided channels that pull in the direction of the tilt. In the pools the water is the colour of weak tea from the peat, and clear enough that you can see the sand at the bottom being moved, grain by grain, toward the edge of the world.

On this, the vegetation. One expects almost nothing at this cold and this elevation, and instead there is too much. The wet has made a garden of the rock. Every surface not actively scoured by moving water carries a pelt of moss and liverwort, hand-deep, spongy, saturated to the point where it weeps when you look at it. Sphagnum has built itself up into low hummocks that quake when stepped on and release brown water from their bases. Cushion plants — dense, domed, hard as sponge-rubber — sit in the shallower pools with only their crowns above the surface, each one a small green island that a hundred things live on. Between the hummocks stand the rosette plants, waist-high and higher, their leaves thick and felted grey against the cold, their dead leaves held on the stem in a wet brown skirt that insulates the living core. Bromeliads grow directly on the stone, their central cups full of rainwater and drowned insects and tadpoles. Where a channel runs deep enough to keep the moss down, sedge and rush go up in dense stands, and pitcher plants lean out over the current with their traps brimming. There is no tree cover. The tallest things are the rosettes, and their tops are whipped flat by the wind.

Everything is stacked. Moss on rock, liverwort on moss, sundew in the liverwort, orchid in the sundew's shadow, frog in the orchid, mite on the frog. Lichen grows on the felt of the rosette leaves. Algae films the underside of the pool surfaces. The density is not of one thing but of layers, each layer living off the water the layer above lets through.

And it is loud. It is one of the loudest quiet places imaginable. Under the drum of the rain on leaf and pool there is the sound of the plateau draining, which is a continuous, geological noise — water going from every basin to every next basin, over a hundred thousand little lips of stone, through a hundred thousand throats of moss, a hiss that fills the whole audible range and never resolves. Then, closer, the frogs, which are the principal fauna by volume and by number: a dozen species, none longer than a thumb, black and gold and brown and clear-skinned, sitting in the bromeliad cups and on the wet undersides of leaves and in the very stream of the runoff, calling. They call in the day. They have nothing else to do; there are no dry hours to wait for. The sound is a ticking, clicking, glass-tapping chorus that changes pitch as you move through it because you are always passing out of one colony and into another. Under that, water beetles and larvae in every pool. Over it, the birds — small, dark, quick, working the rosettes and the cushion plants for insects, indifferent to the rain because they have never known anything else. A hummingbird, iridescent and drenched, hangs at a rosette flower with its wings beating water off themselves. Something with webbed feet moves through the reed at the edge of vision and does not show itself. In the deeper pools small fish, pale, moving in loose shoals against the current.

None of it holds still. This is the thing that takes longest to understand about the place: it is not a landscape in equilibrium but one in permanent, rapid rearrangement inside an envelope that never changes. The rain is constant; everything under it is chaos. Channels form and abandon themselves in an afternoon. A sheet of water three centimetres deep that has been draining left for an hour will, when the wind shifts and pushes the surface of a pool upstream over its lip, drain right. Moss hummocks slump into channels and dam them and the water backs up behind the dam and finds a new way round through a bed of cushion plants that were dry-topped that morning. The cloud tears open and there is, for a minute, a view — a grey-green plain going flat to a horizon that is not a horizon but an edge, and beyond it nothing, white — and then it closes and the rain redoubles. The frogs shift their chorus as the pools shift. Birds abandon a rosette as the water rises around it and colonise another. Every square metre is being made and unmade at the same time, and the sum of it is a surface that has not changed in a million years.

At the edges the noise becomes a roar. The tableland ends in cliffs — sheer, several hundred metres, the old rock finally giving up — and the water that has crossed the whole plateau goes over them in a line of falls that is not a series of waterfalls but a single curtain, kilometres long, thick or thin according to the rain, tearing into mist before it is halfway down. The wind coming up the face throws the falling water back onto the plateau in a fine relentless spray, so that the edge zone, a hundred metres in, is the wettest place on a plateau where that word has lost its meaning. The vegetation there is different: shorter, tougher, plastered. The rock is bare in places, scoured to the pink.

People came up here once, for the water. It is the only thing there is enough of. The evidence is scattered and mostly failed. A line of steel pylons, rusted to the colour of the peat, marches across the flat from somewhere in the cloud to somewhere else in the cloud, cables long gone, each foot buried a metre deep in moss that has grown up around it like a cuff. A concrete channel, straight as nothing else here is straight, cuts a kilometre across the drainage and is now itself a drainage, full to the brim and overflowing at every cracked joint, its sides furred green. Near the edge, where the falls begin, a turbine house — a single long shed of riveted iron, roof half gone, doors sprung — sits at the head of a penstock that once took a portion of the curtain and ran it down the cliff to something below. Inside, out of the direct rain for the first time in what feels like a very long while, the noise of the water in the pipe is a physical pressure in the chest. One of the turbines still turns. Nobody is tending it; there is no sign anyone has been here in decades; but the water has never stopped and so the wheel has never stopped, and it groans round on bearings that should have seized, throwing a filthy spray, driving a generator whose brushes have worn to nothing and whose output goes nowhere. A single lamp in a wire cage above the door glows faint orange on some residual circuit, steady, pointless, the only artificial light for a hundred kilometres. Frogs have colonised the shed. They sit on the housings and the pipe flanges and call over the machinery.

Elsewhere on the plateau: a pump station with its intake grille packed solid with moss and its pipe trailing off into a pool; a survey cairn with a brass plate too corroded to read; iron pegs in a line across a basin, whatever they marked long since drowned. A cable-car tower at the cliff edge, the car itself hanging a few metres down the line, tilted, a bromeliad garden growing in its roof. All of it losing, slowly, to the same process that made the plateau, and none of it losing fast enough to matter yet. Iron takes longer than mountains to come down; not much longer, up here.

The rules, then, as they emerge: everything goes to the edge. Nothing dries. The oldest thing in sight is the flatness, and the newest thing is whichever channel formed in the last hour, and there is no middle scale — no hill, no forest, no season — between the two. What is alive is small and many and wet and loud. What was built is either rusting in the rain or, in the one case, still running in it, without purpose, because the water has no reason to stop and the machine has no way to. Light comes from everywhere at once, grey and bright and shadowless, and you cannot tell the hour, only that it is day, because the frogs are calling and the frogs call all day. The cloud opens and shows you the edge of the world and closes again. The rain continues. It has been continuing since before the frogs, before the moss, before the sand became stone. The plateau is what is left when everything that could go has gone, and this is what remains: rock, water falling on it, and life packed thick into the interval between the two.
