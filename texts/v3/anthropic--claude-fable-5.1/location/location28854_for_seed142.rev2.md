The first thing to understand about the plateau is that it is not a surface so much as a conclusion. Everything that could fall from it has fallen. Everything that could be lifted off it by wind has been lifted and carried elsewhere and settled in some lower country nobody here has seen. What remains is the flat that the world arrives at when it stops arguing: a tableland so high that the air has thinned to a dry hush, so old that its edges are not cliffs anymore but long, exhausted slopes that have given up on being edges. You could walk toward the rim for a day and not be sure you'd reached it.

It is dark. Not the darkness of a cellar, which is a darkness with walls, but the darkness of a very large room whose walls have been removed. Overhead there is something — a scatter of points, too few and too dim to be called stars with confidence, spaced as though someone had been asked to indicate a sky using the least possible material. They do not move. There is no moon, and there never seems to have been one, and the light they give is not light so much as the memory that light was once a category of thing. The eye adjusts and adjusts and finds, at the end of its adjusting, only the difference between ground and not-ground.

The ground is warm. This is the second thing. You feel it before you understand it — through the soles, then in the palms when you crouch and touch it. Stone the temperature of a sleeping body. Dry, entirely dry, with the fine grit of something that has been abraded for so long it has stopped resembling the rock it came from and become merely mineral: a pale, almost colorless dust that lies in shallow drifts and never seems to deepen. Where the dust is thin, the bedrock shows through, smoothed to the texture of old soap. There are no cracks in it. Cracks are for stone that is still deciding.

The warmth comes from below. Somewhere far under the plateau there is a body of heat — not fire, nothing so eventful — a slow, immense, patient hotness, the residual warmth of whatever process raised the land in the first place. It has been leaking upward for so long that it has reached a steady rate, and the plateau has become the exact temperature at which it loses heat to the thin sky as fast as it gains it from beneath. Nothing here gets warmer. Nothing cools. There is no morning in which frost forms and no afternoon in which it burns off. The air is the same in every direction and at every hour, and there are no hours.

---

The city is out there in the dark, and it takes time to realize you are already inside it.

It does not gather. That is the strangeness of it — or the first strangeness. Cities elsewhere thicken toward a center; this one is spread across the plateau the way a thin coat of paint is spread, one structure to every few kilometers of flat, each standing alone with the dark all around it like a moat. You come upon them singly. A shape rises out of the not-ground, black against the marginally-less-black sky, and as you approach it resolves into a low, broad building with the proportions of something that was once tall and has been pressed down. Its walls are stone — the same pale stone as the plateau — and its corners have gone round. Its roof has gone round. Whatever chimney it had has been worn to a stump the shape of a molar. The wind that did this is not blowing now; there is no wind now; but the wind was here for long enough that it wrote itself into everything and then left, and the record remains.

Inside, something turns.

You hear it before you see it, if seeing is the word for what happens in there: a sound at the very bottom of hearing, a rotational hum with a period of perhaps eleven seconds, and under the hum a fainter periodic tock as something metal meets something metal and separates again. When you put your hand to the wall the wall is warmer than the ground, and it trembles, very slightly, in time.

They are engines. Every one of the buildings houses an engine, and every engine is the same engine, more or less — a heat engine of the oldest and most stubborn design, a thing of cylinders and a flywheel and a long slow shaft, taking in the plateau's warmth from below through pipes sunk into the bedrock and giving it up to the sky through radiators on the roof, and in the interval between the taking and the giving, turning. The flywheels are enormous. They are also worn: their rims have been polished by ages of contact with their own housings until iron and stone have blurred into one another and there is no longer a clear line where the machine ends and the building begins. The shafts are smooth as bone. Whatever rough surface any of this once had has been rubbed off by its own motion, and what's left is a mechanism reduced to its essentials, the way the plateau is a landscape reduced to its essentials.

What do they drive? Follow the shaft. It leaves the building through a low aperture and runs out across the flat, a few centimeters above the dust, supported every hundred meters on a stone pier whose top has been worn to a saddle by the shaft turning in it. It runs in the dark toward the next engine house. There the shaft of that engine emerges likewise and runs to the next. They are all connected. The whole thin city is a single linkage of turning iron laid across the plateau in a pattern you cannot see the shape of from inside it — a grid, or a web, or something more like a circulatory system — and every engine drives every other engine, and the total motion of the system is precisely the motion needed to keep the total motion of the system going. Friction is paid for by warmth. Warmth is supplied by the ground. The ground is supplied by whatever is beneath it, at a rate that has not changed in longer than the stone can remember.

Nothing is produced. This is not a failure. There is no evidence that anything was ever produced — no hoppers, no loading bays, no rail sidings sanded down to ridges in the flat. There are the engines, and the shafts, and the piers, and the turning. Perhaps the turning was once the means to something. Now it is the thing itself, and the city is a machine for maintaining its own equilibrium, and it has succeeded so completely that success and stillness have become indistinguishable.

---

There is very little light and what there is belongs to the machines. On the flank of some engine houses, near the base, a rectangle glows the dull red of a coal under ash: a sight-glass on the intake pipe, showing the heat coming up from the ground. It is not bright enough to cast a shadow. It is bright enough to be seen from a long way off across the flat, and when you stand still in the dark and let your eyes go wide, you can count them — five, nine, eleven, small red points scattered at ground level to every horizon, motionless, like the stars but lower and more certain. Between the red of the ground and the pale scatter of the sky the plateau lies as a band of pure absence.

Water is a rumor. On the underside of the shaft, where it passes close to the intake and is slightly warmer than the air, there is sometimes a film so thin it cannot be called moisture — a sheen, a change in the way the metal takes the almost-nothing light. Touch it and it is gone. On the inner walls of one engine house there is a white crust in a horizontal line at knee height, mineral, faintly ridged, the mark of something that was once liquid standing there for a very long time before it was gone forever. That is the extent of it. The air takes what little the engines breathe out and carries it upward into a sky that gives nothing back.

---

There is life, if you are patient, and the life is exactly as sparse as everything else.

It clings to the shafts. You find it by feel before you find it by sight: a rough patch on the polished iron where there should be no roughness, and when you bring your eyes very close, in the red half-glow from a sight-glass, there is a creature the length of a finger joint, flat, segmented, the same colorless pale as the dust, gripping the metal with something that is not quite legs. It is not moving. It is riding. The shaft turns, and the creature turns with it, and completes one full revolution every eleven seconds, and has done so — you have the sense — for a very long time. It eats the film of almost-water that forms on the warm underside. It eats, perhaps, the microscopic iron that the shaft sheds as it wears. It does not appear to have eyes, and there is nothing here for eyes to do.

You will find perhaps one of these for every ten shafts you examine. They do not gather. Whether they are one species or several, whether they reproduce or are simply the same individuals persisting, whether they are the descendants of something that lived here when there was light and water and weather or something that came into existence in the dark on the iron — the plateau does not say. Once, far off, something crossed a sight-glass: a shape, larger, moving low and fast, gone before it became a shape you could name. It did not come back. It is possible it was the only one.

---

The rules of the place, such as they can be stated:

Nothing gets warmer and nothing cools. The engines turn at the rate they turn. The dust lies where it lies; there is no wind to move it and it is too heavy for the vibration of the shafts to lift. The sky holds its small, unconvincing scatter and holds it still. The wear of the mechanisms is real — the shafts are thinner than they were, the piers more deeply saddled, the flywheel rims more blurred — but it proceeds at a pace so slow and so evenly distributed that it does not count as change. The city is being ground down at exactly the rate the plateau was ground down, and both will arrive at nothing at the same time, and that time is not near.

You cannot tell how long you have been here. The usual instruments of duration — light, hunger, cold, the accumulation of small events — have all been removed. What is left is the eleven-second period of the shafts, and the eleven-second period does not accumulate into anything; it simply repeats.

Stand in the dark between two engine houses, one red point behind you and one ahead, the shaft running past your knee with its faint mechanical breath, the warm floor of the world under your feet and the thin dry nothing of the air in your mouth. This is the whole of it. A land that has finished. A city that has finished. An industry that has settled, after whatever struggle brought it here, into the one configuration that requires nothing further, and now maintains that configuration with the mindless fidelity of a heart that has forgotten it belongs to anyone.

Somewhere, far out across the flat, something meets something, and separates, and the sound arrives after a long time, very small.
