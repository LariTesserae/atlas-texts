You come into it low, ducking. The way in is not a door but a place where the wall has decided, for now, to be thinner.

Inside, the space is about the size of a chapel without pews — twenty paces across, perhaps, though pacing it gives different numbers each time, and the ceiling is not so much high as *absent*, going up into a dark that is soft rather than deep. There is almost nothing in the room. That is the first thing. It is hollow the way a lung is hollow, an emptiness that is being *kept*, held open by pressure from all sides.

Everything that is not empty is plant.

The floor is a single fused surface of stem, pale and slightly giving, cool to the touch, ridged with the long grain of something that grew horizontally for a very long time before it forgot which way was up. The walls are the same substance turned vertical, lapped and woven, cambium showing through where the outer layer has split — and it has split everywhere, in fine regular seams, as though the whole chamber were a bud that has not yet decided to open. The ceiling, where the faint light reaches it, is root. Hair-roots, thousands of them, hanging a few inches, beaded at their tips.

The light comes from the seams. Where the tissue is torn, sap has welled and the sap is very slightly luminous — not enough to see a colour by, only enough to draw the edges of things in a grey the eye has to work for. You see the room as a diagram of itself. Lines where the walls meet the floor. The small bright points of the root-tips. Your own hands, if you hold them up, as darker shapes against a darkness that is barely lighter.

It is cool. Not cold. The cool of a cellar in a wet country, the cool of a place a few feet below the ground where the ground has never once been dry.

---

The walls sweat.

That is the water here — there is no pool, no stream, no rain; the room simply exhales. On a slow cycle, perhaps every quarter of an hour, the tissue of the walls goes damp all at once, a bloom of condensation rising out of it like breath on glass, and the hair-roots overhead fatten their beads and let them fall. The drops land on the floor with a sound so small it is less a sound than a change in the quality of the quiet. Then the walls dry, drawing the moisture back in, and the floor is left with a thin sheen that the grain of the stem drinks up in lines.

You notice, after a few cycles, that the drops hit the floor a moment before they leave the roots. Not by much. A half-breath. The sound arrives, and then you see the bead let go.

Nothing else in the room seems troubled by this.

---

The second thing you notice — it takes a while, because the light is so poor and because the mind resists it — is that the walls are not blank.

Low on them, at about the height of your knee, the tissue is raised into relief. Ridges, channels, small regular protrusions. You crouch and put the luminous seam-light close and the relief resolves into a *street*. A street in miniature, or in low relief, or both: a grid of lanes pressed into the living wall, with kerbs of thickened bark and, along the lanes, shapes that are unmistakably buildings. Warehouses, long and low. A row of what can only be terraced houses, each with the suggestion of a door. A round thing with a taller stalk rising from it that is, in every way that matters, a chimney.

The city runs along the walls at the height of your knee like a frieze, and it is not finished.

Some of it is only proposed — a swelling under the skin of the stem, the shape of a block before the block exists, like the way a leaf is already folded inside a bud. Some of it is further along: the buildings have edges, the streets have depth, you could run a fingernail down an alley. And some of it is *working*. The chimney breathes. Not smoke — a faint continuous seep of vapour, warmer than the room, that rises in a thread and is lost in the dark of the root-ceiling. There is a wheel, half a hand across, set into the side of one of the long buildings, and it turns. Slowly, steadily, with the reluctant smoothness of a thing driven by pressure rather than by fire. Sap pressure, presumably. The plant's own circulation, tapped and geared.

Somewhere inside the wall, very faintly, something reciprocates. A soft *hush — hush — hush*, at the very floor of hearing. An engine made of a valve made of a vessel.

It is a mill town. That is what the walls are growing. An industrial settlement of the kind that once appeared in a decade along a river — the works, the yards, the tight-packed rows for the hands, the railway coming in from one side. There is a railway. It is a vine, running the whole circuit of the chamber at knee height with a node every finger's breadth, perfectly regular, and where it passes the long buildings there are sidings that branch and rejoin.

Nobody drew this. The plant is growing a city the way it would grow a gall — in response to something, in the shape of an idea it did not have.

---

You are not alone here, though the room is empty.

The walls are full. You feel it before you see it: a ticking, a grain-shift, a sense that the surface you are leaning on has a pulse that is not one pulse but many. Then something surfaces. A beetle the length of your thumb, black and lacquered, pushes out of the stem-tissue between two of the terraced houses exactly as though coming out of a door, crosses the street on the wall, and pushes back into the tissue on the other side. The wall closes behind it without a mark.

After that you see them everywhere, or rather you see the *evidence* of them everywhere, and only occasionally the animal itself. Slugs the colour of the sap, translucent, moving inside the wall — you can see them as slightly brighter shapes passing behind the thin outer layer, like fish under ice. Something with a great many legs that lives in the root-ceiling and comes down a strand when the walls exhale, to drink, and goes back up. Moths, or things that fly the way moths do, that cross the empty centre of the room once or twice an hour and are the only things that ever occupy the middle of the space; the rest of the time it is a void that everything moves *around*, through the tissue, in the substrate, in the walls and floor and ceiling but never in the air.

And mice. Or the size of mice. You never see one whole. A tail, once, withdrawing into a warehouse door on the wall. A shape that runs the length of the vine-railway on the far side of the room, node to node, and is gone before you have turned your head. Their sound is the loudest thing here after the drops, and it is not loud — a dry patter inside the stem, coming and going, the sound of a building settling that never finishes settling.

There is a predator. You know because the patter stops sometimes, all at once, all around the room, and in the silence that follows something larger moves *through* the wall — you feel its passage as a slow bulge under your hand, a displacement in the tissue, travelling — and then, some seconds after it has passed, a single small sound, and then the patter resumes, thinner than before.

The population of the walls is enormous and the room stays empty. Those are not in contradiction. The room is what the population makes, by pressing outward from all sides. The emptiness is the shape of their crowding.

---

You should not lean too close to the city.

You do, of course — you want to see whether the doors on the terraced row are really doors — and the frieze does not so much enlarge as *exchange* with you. There is no moment of transition. You are in the street. It is a cobbled street, though the cobbles are the lenticels of a bark you were looking at a moment ago, and it is the same darkness, the same cool, the same sap-grey light welling from the seams between paving stones. The houses are the height of houses. The chimney is the height of a chimney, and the vapour from it goes up into a root-ceiling that is now so far above you it is only a texture in the dark.

The wheel is turning beside you in its housing, and it is the size of a wheel, and you can hear now what drives it: the *hush — hush* is a pulse in a pipe as thick as your body, ribbed and pale, running along the base of the works and into the ground. Xylem. The town is plumbed with the plant's own vessels.

The street is empty in the way the room was empty. The doors are shut. There is a rail line at the end of the road with a siding curving off toward the works, and it is a vine — it is visibly a vine, with a node every several yards and the leaves stripped from it — and nothing has run on it yet, though the nodes are polished.

Along the street, at knee height, the walls of the houses are raised into relief.

You do not crouch to look. You already know what you would find: a chamber about twenty paces across with the hair-roots hanging in it and a small figure standing inside, ducking, holding its hand up to a wall.

---

Here is what can be said about how the place works, for as long as you are in it.

It is being built, and it is not finished, and it has never been anything but unfinished. The city is *emerging* in the strict sense — it has not formed yet, it is in the process of arriving at its shape, and the process is the only state it has. The bits that are complete are not older than the bits that are only swellings under the skin. They are all the same age. Growth here is not a sequence but a gradient, and you can walk from proposed to built to working and back in a few steps, or a few knee-heights, depending on which of you is the town.

The animals are the labour, or the labour is what the animals are. The beetles do not carry things or hammer anything. They pass through the walls at the places where doors are meant to be, and afterward those places are slightly more door. The slugs move along the lines where pipes will run, and afterward the pipes are more pipe. Whatever it is that hunts them keeps the numbers down, and where the numbers are kept down the city stays as a bud, unopened, waiting.

Water comes as breath and goes as breath. Nothing here has ever been rained on. The dampness is internal, circulatory — the whole chamber is a section of a vessel and the sweating of the walls is the passage of a slow fluid that has somewhere else to be. The drops fall before they fall because in a vessel the pressure arrives before the volume.

The light is the wound. Where the tissue is intact there is no light at all; you see only by the places where the plant has been split by its own growing, and the sap has welled, and the sap glows. A finished city would be dark. The town is visible in exact proportion to how unfinished it is. When the last seam closes, when the last building stops being proposed and simply *is*, the light will go out, and the animals will still be moving in the walls, and the wheel will still be turning in a dark that no one is in.

The sound is mostly nothing. Then a drop. Then the patter, resuming.

You could go back out the way you came, where the wall is thinner. You are not sure, now, which wall that was, or which room.
