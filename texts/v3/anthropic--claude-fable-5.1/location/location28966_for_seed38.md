Begin with the height, because the height is the first thing that is wrong, or the first thing that seems wrong before the place explains itself.

You are very high. You know this the way a body knows it — the air a little thin in the chest, the horizon too far away and too low, the sense that the sky is not above you but around you, that you are standing on something the sky has been asked to accommodate. And yet it is warm. Not the warmth of a summer valley; a closer warmth, damp and slightly sweet, the warmth of a room where something has been growing for a long time with the windows shut. Your breath does not smoke. Your hands do not stiffen. The wet on your face is not cold.

This is a tableland, and it is very old. Once, presumably, it had mountains. Anything lifted this far above the sea must have had edges once, ridgelines, cirques, the whole violent architecture of rock pushed up faster than water could take it down. All of that is gone. What remains is a swell — one enormous, gentle convexity, a continent's worth of ground rounded off like a stone that has spent an age in a river. There is no summit. There is no place where the land stops rising and begins to fall, only a slow tendency, a drift in the ground that you can feel in your legs more than see. If you walked for a season you might notice you had been descending. You might not.

---

The ground is not ground.

This takes time to understand, because it does what ground does: it holds you up. But it gives. Every step sinks a little, a hand's depth, sometimes more, and comes up slowly behind you, the print closing like a mouth. It is not mud. Mud is mineral and this is not; nothing here is mineral within reach. The whole surface of the tableland, from wherever it begins to wherever it ends, is a single depth of living plant — not many plants, or not in any way that matters, but one continuous vegetative body, a turf or mat or pelt several meters thick, with no soil beneath it that isn't simply more of itself, older and darker and no longer photosynthesizing but not exactly dead either. Dig your arm down into it and you find layers: the pale green surface, then a yellow-green zone still faintly warm and moist, then brown, then a black that is not rot but something more patient than rot, a slow compression of plant into plant. There is no bottom you can reach with your arm. The actual rock of the world is somewhere far below, unseen for however long this has been going on, and it may be that nothing living has touched stone here since the mountains were still mountains.

The mat has a surface texture that changes across distances too large to walk. Here it is close and fine, like felt. A day's walk on it becomes fibrous and ropy, the strands as thick as fingers, braided. Further, it rises into low soft domes the height of a kneeling person, each dome breathing very slightly — you can see it if you sit still — with the swell of water moving in the tissue below. Between the domes are channels, and the channels hold water, and this is the first place you notice how the water behaves.

---

It weeps. It does not run. The water here does not have anywhere to go; there is no downhill worth speaking of, and the mat holds it the way a sponge holds it, so instead of streams there are seeps, beads, sheets. It stands on every surface in a skin. It gathers at the low points of the ropy braid and sits there, clear as glass, too still to have a surface — you see it only when your foot goes through. Where the domes are it condenses on the tops, and runs down their sides in threads so slow you can watch a single drop descend the height of your hand over the course of a breath. In the channels it is knee-deep and tepid and full of a fine suspended green that turns your skin the color of old bronze.

Above all this, always, is the cloud.

You are inside it. The tableland is so wide and so high and so wet that it has made its own weather, and the weather it has made is a single unmoving overcast that sits on the mat the way a lid sits on a pot. This is where the light goes. The sun is up there somewhere — you can tell day from night, a gray from a darker gray — but what reaches the surface is a diffuse, sourceless, faintly greenish glow with no shadows in it. Objects do not cast darkness. They simply become dimmer toward the ground. At the brightest hour you could read a page held close; at the dimmest, the mat around your feet is a shape and the domes are shapes and everything past a few dozen strides is the same undifferentiated pearl.

The warmth is from below. Sit long enough and you feel it through your clothes, a low steady heat rising out of the mat itself, the heat of a body, the heat of a compost heap that is a continent wide. The plant is digesting itself. Its deep layers, pressed and black and beyond light, are breaking down at a rate so gradual it would take generations to notice, and the sum of all that slow chemistry is enough to warm the air of a highland that should by rights be glacier. The cloud is the tableland's exhalation. The rain that falls from it — a fine, warm, almost invisible rain that comes and goes without announcing itself — is the tableland's own water, lifted and returned. Nothing enters and nothing leaves. It is a sealed thing, breathing on itself.

---

The animals are everywhere and they make almost no sound.

This is partly the mat, which swallows footfall, and partly the cloud, which flattens every noise into a nearness, so that a call from a great distance seems to come from just behind your shoulder and a call from behind your shoulder seems to come from inside your head. But mostly it is the animals themselves. They are slow. Nothing here has any reason to be fast. There is nothing to flee and nothing to chase; the food is the ground, and the ground is not going anywhere.

There are the grazers, first. Low broad heavy things, pale, the size of a sleeping ox, with wide flat faces that they press into the mat and drag, cropping the green surface in long swaths that heal behind them in a season. Their coats are the same color as the tableland and are often growing the same fine turf, so that a herd at rest looks like a cluster of domes and a cluster of domes, from a distance, looks like a herd. They do not appear to have eyes, or the eyes are very small and very deep in the face and the animals do not use them for much. They navigate by the taste of the ground. When they move, which they do at the pace of a shadow crossing a floor, the mat around them undulates.

There are the burrowers, which you rarely see whole. A narrow face, a long soft body, a slick gray-pink skin, moving through the upper layers of the mat the way a hand moves under a blanket. You see the ridge of them pass. Occasionally one surfaces to breathe, and in that moment you see how many there are: turn your head and there are seven, eight, a dozen faces, all lifted at once, all closing their small mouths on the same damp air, and then gone again. The mat is full of them, laced with them. When you sink your arm into the turf, what you feel down in the yellow zone is not entirely still.

Above, in the cloud, there is something that flies, or drifts. You never see it clearly. A shape, darker gray on gray, wide, unhurried, with something trailing. It makes a sound — a single low note, two or three times in a day — that arrives from everywhere. When it passes overhead the water on the domes trembles.

And then there are the small things, and these you can hardly count. Things like slugs with fringed edges, translucent, glowing very faintly on the underside, which climb the domes to drink the condensation and are so numerous that at dusk the domes seem to be studded with tiny wet lamps. Things like moths, but soft-bodied and slow, that hang in the air above the channels barely moving their wings, waiting for something in the water to rise. Things with many legs and no obvious front. A kind of warm, thin, transparent worm that lives in the seeps and lets the water carry it from dome to dome in a way that seems, given the speed of the water, to require infinite patience.

None of them are afraid of you. None of them are much interested in you. You are another slow thing on the mat.

---

It is only after a long time here, days, more, that you start to see the marks.

The first is a stone. Just one — a rounded gray lump the size of a head, sitting on the mat the way a fallen fruit sits on a lawn. It is the only stone you have seen. You pick it up and it is heavier than it should be and cold, colder than anything else in this world, and there is a hollow worn in one side of it that fits a thumb. Someone held this. Someone, at some point, carried mineral up into a place that has no mineral, and left it here, and the mat did not swallow it, because the mat does not grow on stone, and so it has sat exactly where it was set down for a length of time you cannot begin to guess.

Once you have seen one you begin to see others. Never many. A stone here; a hundred strides on, another; and if you stand with the second and look back at the first you see they make a line, and if you follow the line you find a third, and the line goes over the swell of the ground and out of sight into the pearl. You walk it for most of a day. The stones are the same size, roughly, the same gray, the same cold, each with the same worn hollow. They do not point at anything. They go over a rise and turn a little and go on.

Elsewhere — much elsewhere, weeks elsewhere — there is a place where the mat has grown differently, in a ring, the turf inside the ring finer and paler and lower than the turf outside, as if something once stood there and pressed the ground down and the ground has been remembering the pressure ever since. The ring is very large. You cannot see across it in the light. In its center, half-sunk and tilted, is a slab: one piece of pale stone, longer than three people lying end to end, its top surface worked smooth and cut with lines. The lines are shallow and the water has been sitting in them for so long they are lined with a green so dark it looks black. You cannot tell if the lines are writing or a map or a game or simply the marks of some tool being tested. They are very regular. Nothing else here is regular.

And far away again there are the mounds — long low ridges in the mat, running straight for a few hundred strides and then stopping, in pairs, parallel, the width of a room between them. Walls, once. Something was built of the mat itself, cut and stacked, or of something else long since become mat. You can walk between them. The channel between them holds water like the others. The burrowers move through the walls as if they were nothing, because to the burrowers they are nothing, they are only turf that happens to be higher.

There is never anything else. No tools, no shards, no bone. Whoever set the stones and cut the slab and raised the walls left nothing that was not stone or plant, and the plant has been taken back. The stones remain because the tableland cannot digest them. That may be why they were brought. That may be the whole point of them — to make a mark in a place where every other kind of mark would close over like a footprint.

---

The strangeness accrues rather than announces itself. It is in the way the water on the domes runs down more slowly than water should, as if the whole place were very slightly less urgent than the world you came from. It is in the way the seeps, if you watch them long enough, seem sometimes to climb — a bead of water rising, unhurried, up a strand of the braided turf against every reasonable expectation, drawn by the tissue's thirst, so that in this place water is not a thing that falls but a thing that is passed from hand to hand. It is in the warmth, which is the warmth of something alive, and the cloud, which is the breath of the thing, and the realization that you have been walking for weeks on the back of an organism, that the herds graze its skin and the burrowers thread its flesh and the flying thing drinks from the vapor it gives off, and that there is nowhere on this high, slow, worn-down summit of the world where the organism is not.

It is in the cycles, too, which are too long to see and which you infer from their traces. There are bands in the mat, if you cut down into a dome — a pale layer, a dark layer, a pale layer — that speak of something that happens and stops and happens again, over spans you cannot measure. Once in a great while, they say, the cloud thins. Once in a great while the light comes down whole and the water rises off the whole tableland at once and the whole surface flushes a brighter green and the herds lie down and the slugs on the domes go dark. Then the cloud closes again. There are hundreds of these bands. There are more below the hundreds you can count.

---

Toward what might be evening — the gray deepening, the lamps on the domes coming faintly up — you sit on the mat with your back against one of the cold stones and let the warmth of the ground come through you.

The quiet has a texture. It is not silence; there is the sound of water finding its way, an almost sub-audible constant drip and seep and settle that comes from every direction and from beneath. There is the occasional low, wet, cropping sound of a grazer somewhere in the dimness working its face along the ground. Far off — or near, you cannot tell — the single note of the thing in the cloud. Beneath everything, if you press your ear to the turf, a slow pulse that is probably the movement of water through the deep tissue and is probably not anything else.

The line of stones goes on over the swell and away. Someone walked this way, once, so long ago that the world they walked on has grown a body's depth of new flesh over their footprints, and carried a weight they did not need to carry, and set it down here, and went on to set down another. You do not know why. You suspect that the setting-down was the reason. That in a place where nothing is fast and nothing is hard and nothing keeps its shape, they wanted one thing to be cold and heavy and to stay.

The burrowers surface, breathe, sink. The cloud holds. The tableland, which has been here since before its mountains fell, goes on digesting itself at the pace of a season, at the pace of an age, warm in the dark at the roof of the world, breathing on its own hands.
