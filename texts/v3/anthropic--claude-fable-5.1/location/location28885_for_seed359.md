You come down to it. That is the first thing: the place is lower than everything around it, a shallow bowl you descend into over a lip of packed red earth, except the earth is not earth. Press your thumb into it and it holds the print, and the print is warm, and the surface has the faint tooth of something fired and then rubbed with ash. The whole floor of the bowl is like this — a single tamped tile of ochre clay, cracked nowhere, fingermarks everywhere, as though a great number of hands smoothed it flat and then, satisfied, withdrew.

The bowl is about the size of a threshing floor. You could throw a stone across it. You would not, because there are no stones. Everything here has been made.

---

The vegetation comes up to the chest and in places over the head, and it is dense enough that you move through it by parting it with your forearms. Leaves. Thousands of leaves, and they are plaited — three-strand, five-strand, some in a diagonal twill you have to hold close to your face to follow. The fibre is pale gold and dry and smells of nothing, or smells of the inside of a basket left in the sun. Stems are cord, hard-twisted, sometimes wrapped in a spiral of finer cord dyed with something that was once red. At intervals the stems thicken into knots the size of a fist, and from the knots come more stems, and from those more leaves, and the leaves at the tips are still unfinished: three or four ends left loose, waiting.

There is no water. This is a rule and you feel it in your mouth within a few minutes. The air is warm — not hot, not a heat that presses, but the settled warmth of a kiln room hours after the fire — and it has no moisture in it at all. Your lips tighten. The plaited leaves do not wilt because they were never wet; they were never anything but fibre. Where a living garden would have sap, these have a filling of fine yellow dust that sifts out if you bend a stem too far, and drifts, and does not settle for a long time.

You may wonder what the fibre is. You will not find its source. There is no field of it, no plant that gives it. Later you will notice that the finest strands, the ones used for the veins of the leaves, are hair, and that it is not any one colour of hair.

---

The animals are everywhere and they are quiet. That is the second thing.

They are felted, mostly. Lumps of pressed fibre worked into shapes with a bone tool — you can see the tool's marks, small oval dents where it pushed and pushed until the fibre fused — and they are not always shaped like any animal you know, but they are shaped like animals. There are things like hares, folded into the base of the plaited stems, ears laid back. Things like birds, a great many of them, wedged into the forks of the cord-branches, some in rows, near-touching, feathers indicated by a few stitches of darker thread. Something long and low, made of a single thick braid with a knotted head, lying across the path so that you must step over it. Things you cannot name at all, with too many limbs or none, a felt bulk with a row of shell beads pressed in where eyes would go.

They breathe. Not visibly — nothing here does anything visibly — but if you set your palm on the flank of one of the hare-things and wait, there is a slow rise under the felt, and a slower fall, at about the rate of a sleeper's breath in a cold room. The felt is warm. So is everything, but the felt is warmer.

They are not arranged. They are packed. It is not a diorama; nobody has left space for you to admire the composition. It is a burrow, or a hive, or a nest, and it is full, and the fullness is patient. When you push through, the felt animals shift very slightly against one another with a sound like a dry hand on cloth, and then they are still again.

---

The sounds, then, since there are so few of them: the fibre rustle when you move. A tick, very occasional, from the clay floor — the kiln-sound of something contracting a hair's breadth as it cools, although nothing here is cooling and nothing is heating either. Your own breath, loud. And, underneath, if you stand a long time and stop rustling, something that is almost too slow to be a sound. A pull. A dry rasp drawn out over half a minute. It is coming from many places at once, from low in the plaiting. You will work out what it is.

---

Here is how the place changes. It changes the way a season changes. Sit on the floor with your back against the largest of the cord-trunks and let an hour pass and nothing will happen. But mark where the loose ends are on the nearest unfinished leaf — mark them with your eye, three strands, one hanging longer than the others — and go away, and come back, and one of them has been taken into the plait. Not by anything you saw. The rasp is the sound of fibre being drawn through fibre, very slowly, everywhere.

There is a tool. Only one, as far as you can tell: a bone awl the length of a hand, its tip polished dark from use. You will find it lying on the clay, or pushed into a knot, or resting across the back of one of the birds. If you pick it up and carry it away and set it down, then later you will find it again somewhere else, and where you set it down there will be nothing. Every one of the millions of marks in the felt, every place a strand has been pushed through a plait, was made with this tip. You can match the oval of the dents to it exactly. It does not seem possible that one awl did this much work. It is not possible. It is nevertheless true, and the awl is warm when you hold it, and there are fingerprints on it, and they are not yours, and they are not all the same size, and some of them are very small.

---

The bowl has a wall. You reach it eventually, pushing through the last of the plaited stems, and it rises perhaps twice your height, curving inward a little at the top so that the sky — an ordinary sky, an ordinary noon-ish light, dust hanging gold in it — is a ragged oval rather than a full circle. The wall is woven. Coarse warp of thick cord, weft of flat rush, the same gold as everything else. Nothing behind it that you can find; push your hand between the strands and the strands close on your wrist, gently, and hold, and you take your hand back.

Look at the weft for a while. There is a pattern worked into it in the red-dyed cord, and the pattern is a bowl seen from above, with a floor of ochre, with plaited vegetation packed dense, with small dark knots that are the animals. It is this place. There are figures in it too, worked crudely, a few dark stitches each: figures bent over, hands in the weaving. Many of them, all around the edge, all facing in. One near the centre is standing up, alone among the stems, and the stitches that make it are still slightly loose, the ends not yet trimmed.

If you find the pattern's largest cord-trunk, and follow the red stitching down to where a figure would sit with its back against it, there is nothing there. Yet. Come back later.

---

Some rules of the place, as they make themselves felt:

Nothing wet survives entry. If you brought a skin of water down the lip of the bowl, you will find when you open it that it is full of pale thread, wound loosely, warm and dry. Spit onto the clay and the spit is a small tuft of fibre before it lands. You do not stop being thirsty. Thirst is one of the few things here that belongs to you.

Nothing is finished, and nothing decays. The cuts on the rush weft are sharp as if made this morning. The knots have not loosened. No dust of rot, no smell of it. But also no last stitch anywhere — every made thing has an end left free, and the ends are being taken up, at the rate of a season, by no hands that can be seen.

The animals do not eat and are not eaten. They lie against one another and breathe and are warm. But at the base of the largest hare-shape, if you lift it — it is heavier than felt should be — there is a hollow in the clay floor, and the hollow is lined with hair, and the hair is a colour you recognise from your own head, and there is a great deal of it.

The awl, when you are holding it, wants to be pushed into something. This is not a compulsion. It is a suggestion, the way a slope suggests walking down. You may find yourself with a loose leaf-end between two fingers, and the awl in the other hand, and no memory of picking either of them up, and a tightness in your mouth, and a great warm quiet all around you, and no sense at all that anything is wrong.

---

Late — but the light does not change, so call it late by the standard of your own tiredness — you will notice that the place has been getting denser, or you have been getting larger, or the distinction has stopped meaning much. The plaited stems touch your shoulders now on both sides at once. The felt birds are level with your face. The rasp of fibre through fibre is not louder but it is closer, and some of it, you are almost sure, is happening at the hem of your clothes, at your sleeves, at the ends of your hair where they hang.

The awl is on the floor between your feet. It is warm. There is a bird-shape in the fork of the cord-branch beside you with one wing still to be stitched, and the thread is already through the eye of nothing, waiting, and the ends are loose, and there is no hurry. There has never been any hurry here. That is the last thing. The bowl is low and warm and full and made, and it is being made, and it will take up whatever ends are left free, at its own pace, in its own dry quiet, and it has all the time there is.
