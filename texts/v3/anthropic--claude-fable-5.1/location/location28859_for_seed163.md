The first thing is not the sight of it but the pressure in the ears. You feel the place before you crest onto it: a low, whole-body roar that seems to come out of the ground, and it does.

The tableland sits high enough that the clouds are mostly below it, a white floor to the sky in every direction, and the sun comes off that floor and off the top of the plateau at the same time so that light arrives from above and beneath. There is nowhere to rest the eyes. Everything wet shines, and everything is wet.

It is green, but not the green of a forest. There are no trees. There is nothing here taller than a knee except what has been brought. The whole surface of the plateau — ten, fifteen kilometers across, its edges lost in glare — is a single mat of plant tissue, moss and cushion-plant and liverwort and things that are none of those exactly, grown together into a continuous hide a meter or more thick. Underneath, the rock is barely rock. It is ash and cinder and half-fused glass, a volcanic upwelling only a few centuries old, still warm in places if you dig, still soft enough to crumble in a hand. The mountain came up faster than anything could weather it, and before weather had a chance the plants arrived and simply took possession. They hold it together. Pull back a flap of the mat and the ground beneath sags and slides. The plateau is not a stone landform with vegetation on it. It is a living thing lying on a heap of debris, keeping the heap in shape.

The mat drinks the clouds. Cloud comes up the flanks all day and condenses onto the cold green surface, and the moss takes it and holds it and lets it move downward through itself, slowly, until the whole plateau is a sponge under a slight, constant pressure of water. Walk on it and each footfall sinks and releases a clear pool that closes when the foot lifts. It is not marsh — there is no standing water, no reeds, no mud. The surface is springy and firm and drains upward into your boots. Every rock that breaks the mat weeps. Every incline has a film of water running over the green at the pace of a sleep.

And then all that water has to leave. This is the roar.

The edges of the plateau are sheer, fresh-broken cinder walls, and the water that the mat has gathered across its whole surface arrives at the rim and falls. Not in rivers — the mat does not gather itself into rivers, it is too even, too undifferentiated — but in a continuous seep along the entire perimeter, a curtain of water dropping several hundred meters onto the shattered scree below, kilometer after kilometer of it. The sound is a wall. It does not rise or fall; there is no rhythm in it. Stand anywhere on the plateau and it comes from every horizon at once, so that it stops being a sound with a direction and becomes a quality of the air, like temperature. People who work here do not speak. They stand close and read lips, or they use hands, or the long pole-and-flag signals that carry across the flat. After a week the roar stops registering as noise and starts registering as silence would, as the baseline against which any other sound — a shout, a metal clank — is startling and thin.

The other sounds are the engines.

Someone found out what the young rock is made of. Fresh from the deep, unweathered, it is rich in metals that have not yet had time to oxidize or leach, and so scattered across the mat there are rigs: boring towers of riveted iron sitting on cribwork pads that have been pushed down through the moss to reach cinder, each one with a boiler and a walking beam and a stack. They are far apart — one every two or three kilometers, a dozen visible on a clear day as dark upright marks on the green, each with a small tail of steam. They run on the water. Iron pipes are sunk into the mat and the plateau's own gentle hydraulic pressure, the weight of a saturated sponge kilometers across, pushes water into the boilers without a pump. The engines pound, a slow heavy stroke, and the sound of each one travels across the flat in the gaps of the roar so that at a distance you hear the plateau's endless exhale and, inside it, a heartbeat, and then another heartbeat slightly out of phase, and another.

What comes up out of the bores is a grey slurry of pulverized cinder, and it is spread on the mat and the mat closes over it within a season. The rigs move on. Where they have been, there are low rectangular scars where the cribwork was, already softening under new moss, and lengths of iron pipe half-swallowed, and here and there a wheel, a boiler shell gone orange, a rail spur that ran a cart from a rig to the rim and stops at the edge in midair. The tramway to the lowlands comes up a single cable from the cloud floor, a black thread with a car on it every hour, and the car disgorges coal and people and takes away ingots the size of a loaf and nothing else. There are maybe forty people on the whole tableland. You see one or two in a day, distant, walking slowly, sunk to the ankle.

There is almost nothing else alive that moves. Some kind of small dark bird lands on the boiler housings for the warmth and is gone before you can look at it properly. There are insects in the mat, presumably — something must pollinate the cushion plants when they flower, which they do, briefly and all at once, a haze of pale pink across the entire plateau for perhaps five days in the warm season, after which the green resumes. No one has seen anything larger. The roar, people think. Nothing that hunts by ear could stand it, and nothing that hides by listening would ever know when to hide.

The cool is the cool of altitude and evaporation, not cold, a constant wet freshness that gets into wool and does not leave. Steam from the rigs does not rise far; it flattens and drifts and joins the cloud coming up over the rim. The light is enormous and colorless. Wet moss under that light goes nearly white at the crowns and the shadows of the boring towers are hard and short and black and are the only real darkness anywhere.

Change here happens on two clocks and neither is quick. The water clock: cloud in, drain out, without variation, so that the plateau is in exactly the same state at any hour and any season, the same weight, the same roar. And the rock clock: the mountain is still coming up, a finger's width a year, the cinder beneath the mat settling and shifting and occasionally opening a fissure that the moss bridges within a month. The rigs sink a fraction as the pads compact. The rails go out of true and are re-laid. The metal that is taken out is a rounding error against what is still down there, and the men who run the engines understand that they are working on something that has not finished being made, that is, in the geological sense, still hot, still wet, still deciding on its final shape — and that the plants, which got here first and hold it all together, are in no hurry to let it.
