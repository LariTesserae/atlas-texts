The first thing is the light. It comes from everywhere at once, a white glare with no sun in it, because the sky is a single cloud pressed down onto the land and lit from inside. You squint. Every surface has a sheen. Nothing casts a shadow; things are simply more or less pale.

Then the wet. The air is at the point of turning into water and keeps almost doing it. Your sleeves darken. Droplets stand on the stone in front of you in an even beaded field, like breath on a cold cup, and when you look down a wall they have gathered into slow lines that move at the speed of a minute hand. The stone weeps. It is cool, not cold — the cool of a cellar, of the shade side of a rock in early spring, except there is no shade side here.

You are high. You know this from the thinness of the air and the way your breath comes shallow, and from the drop at the edge of the terrace where the cloud goes on down and down without a floor. Behind you the terraces climb. Ahead and to either side the land is stepped, walled, channeled, tanked, roofed — every bit of it. There is no bare ground. You walk on paving that fits so closely a fingernail won't find the joint. The walls are courses of pale grey block, each one dressed by hand, the tool marks still crisp: short parallel bites, a hundred to a face, as if each stone had been combed. The arrises are sharp enough to hurt. Nothing has rounded. Nothing has been softened by weather, though the weather never stops. It looks as if the masons left yesterday.

It goes on. This is the thing that takes time to understand. You climb a stair between two cisterns and come to a level place and it is more of the same: shoulder-wide passages between tank walls, low stone roofs pitched a few degrees to shed condensation into gutters that are themselves cut from single blocks, gutters feeding sluices, sluices feeding tanks, the tanks brim-full and perfectly still, water lying flat as sheet metal a hand's breadth below the coping. Thousands of them. The whole slope is honeycombed. You could walk for a day along the ridges between the tanks, and where the slope steepens the tanks become terraces and the terraces become a stepped face, and where the face becomes a cliff the cliff is stacked block, and where you lean out to look for the bottom of the stack you cannot see one. There is no living rock here. There is no outcrop, no boulder, no gravel, no soil. Whatever this mountain was before, it has been entirely replaced or entirely encased, and you begin to suspect there was no before — that the mountain is the masonry, all the way through, a thing raised by hand out of the cloud until it reached the height where the cloud would give up its water.

That is what it does. It is a machine for drinking fog. The cloud presses onto the stone; the stone is cool; the water comes out of the air and gathers and runs. Every gutter angles it somewhere. Every tank has a lip on its downhill side, a single worn-smooth notch (the only worn thing anywhere) through which a thread of overflow pours into the next tank a step lower. You put your ear near one of these notches and hear it: a thin ribbon of sound, hardly louder than the hiss of your own blood. That and a dripping from a thousand eaves, out of phase, never resolving into rhythm, is the whole of the sound. No wind. No insects. No birds crossing the glare. Your footsteps are the loudest thing on the mountain, and they don't echo — the wet stone eats them.

Nothing grows. This should be impossible in such a place and you look for it: the green smear at a waterline, a lichen freckle, a cushion of moss in the angle of a step. There isn't any. The tanks are clear to the bottom, and the bottom is dressed stone, and the water in them is so clean it has almost no color, only a faint grey borrowed from the sky. Perhaps the stone is too new. Perhaps the water is too pure, distilled out of a cloud that has crossed nothing but air. Perhaps whoever built this scours the walls, though you find no brush and no scraped place. Perhaps it is simply that the world here is only stone, water, and light, and those three are enough for the place to go on doing what it does without anything alive to assist.

You find their tools once. Set on a cistern lip as though laid down for a meal: a hammerstone with a polished belly, a chisel of dark bone, a wooden mallet whose head has drunk so much water it has swollen and split. A coil of cord. Nothing metal. The blocks around you — some larger than you could move alone, all fitted to a tolerance a knife won't enter — were done with these. You pick up the hammerstone. It is the temperature of the air. You put it back exactly where it was, in the small dry ghost its shape has kept on the wet coping, and for a while you do not touch anything else.

Higher up the passages narrow. The tanks here are smaller and deeper, the water in them held higher up the walls, and you notice that neighbouring tanks stand at different levels — one brim-full, the next a hand lower, the next lower still — and that this is not the slope, since they sit on the same course. They ought to have equalized long ago. They have not. Water in this place seems to keep its own counsel about where it will rest. You lower a hand into one tank and the surface does not ripple so much as tighten, and when you take your hand out the wet on your skin is very cold, colder than the tank should be, colder than the air.

The top, if there is a top, is somewhere in the cloud above you, and the light does not change as you climb toward it; the white just goes on being white. Nothing here is in a hurry. The condensation beads, gathers, runs. The overflow threads its notch. The tanks fill by the width of a hair each hour and give it away again below. Whatever this mountain is for, it is doing it at the speed of stone, and it will do it with or without the builders, with or without you, in the bright, cool, dripping quiet, until the cloud lifts — if the cloud lifts — and shows what it has been standing on all this time.
