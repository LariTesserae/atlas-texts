The first thing to understand about the place is that you do not arrive at it from the side. You arrive from above, through a lid.

The lid is vegetation: water lettuce and frogbit and something with a red underside that nobody has named, knitted so tightly across the surface of the reservoir that from a boat it reads as a meadow. Rain lands on it and beads. Underneath, the light that gets through is the color of tea held up to a window, and it doesn't so much fall as leak. By the time it has come down through the thirty-odd feet of water to where the town is, it has been strained of everything but a brown-green glow, and that glow has no direction. There are no shadows here. Nothing is lit from anywhere.

The water is warm. Not bath-warm, but warmer than a river has any right to be, and warmer than the reservoir above it, which is why the town holds its own layer of water like a room holds its own air. Somewhere under the foundations there is a seep, mineral and steady, and the mill was built on it a long time ago because the dyers needed heat and the seep gave heat for nothing. When the valley was dammed and the water came up over the roofs, the seep kept going. It is still going. It does not know anything has changed.

So there is a thermocline about eight feet above the highest chimney, a faint shimmering boundary where the cold reservoir sits on the warm town, and if you pass down through it your skin tells you before your eyes do that you have entered a separate place. The water below the line is slightly thicker. It carries fine suspended silt that never settles because the seep keeps it stirred, very gently, in a convection that takes most of a day to complete one turn.

---

The town was never large. It was a single street of stone that ran down the valley to the mill, and the mill was the reason for the street. A dyeworks, a fulling shed, an engine house with a boiler and a horizontal steam engine, a wheel pit for the older water wheel that they never took out, a chimney, three rows of workers' terraces, a chapel the size of a cottage. All of it still there. All of it within a compass you could walk in four minutes if you could walk.

You cannot walk. You drift, and drifting you discover that there is almost nowhere to drift *to*, because the town is full.

This is what the warmth has done. Every surface that will hold a root has grown a plant, and every plant has grown toward the tea-colored light and then, finding the light too weak to be worth the effort, has grown sideways instead, into the spaces between buildings, into doorways, through the sash windows whose glass went long ago. The street between the terraces is not a street. It is a mass of hornwort and hydrilla and long ribbon-leaves of Vallisneria that stand up from the cobbles and lie over one another like wet hair, packed so densely that the eye cannot find the far end. Fronds touch fronds touch stone touch iron. Where two things meet there is a third thing growing in the join. The chapel's small bell tower is a column of green with a bell-shape somewhere inside it. The chimney has become a trellis.

You move through this by parting it, and it closes behind you without a sound, and you understand that the town is not ruined so much as upholstered.

---

The engine house is the center, because it was always the center.

Its doors are gone. Its roof is mostly gone — slates slid off in the first years and lie in a scree on the floor, and through the rafters the brown light comes in as a general dusk. Inside, the horizontal engine sits on its bed with its flywheel eleven feet across, and the flywheel is turning.

Not fast. Nothing like fast. Once around in perhaps a quarter of an hour, so slowly that if you watched a single spoke you would begin to doubt yourself, and only by marking it against a rivet on the wall and looking away and looking back would you know. There is no steam. The boiler is a cold cylinder colonized by snails. What moves the wheel is the water itself — the warm convection from the seep rising up through the old wheel pit next door, through a wall long since holed, and pressing on the flywheel's rim with the patient weight of a thermal current that has been pressing on it, at the same rate, for longer than anyone has watched.

The wheel turns, and the connecting rod goes with it, and the piston slides in a cylinder that has no pressure in it, and the whole engine performs its motion in the way a sleeper performs breathing. On each revolution the crank passes a point where a bearing has worn oval and there is a sound. It is a small sound. A knock, deep and wooden, though there is no wood in the mechanism — the water gives it that quality. It arrives every fifteen minutes or so and it is the loudest thing in the town.

Around the engine the vegetation is different. The rising warm water keeps a column of relatively open space above the wheel pit, and the plants that ring it have oriented themselves to the current, all leaning the same way, so that the engine sits in a kind of green cyclone that never completes. Green filaments have wound themselves along the shafting and follow the belts up to the line-shaft that runs the length of the hall. The belts are still on their pulleys. Leather does not last in warm water; these were canvas and rubber, and they have become something else, a dark gelatinous ribbon that flexes as the shaft creeps around. They still transmit motion. At the far end of the hall, in the fulling shed, a pair of wooden stocks lift by an inch and fall by an inch, once every fifteen minutes, and the cloth they were beating rotted to nothing before anyone now living was born.

---

There is electricity, and this is the strangest part, and it is not very strange.

The works were wired late in their life — a dynamo off the line-shaft, cloth-covered copper stapled along the walls, carbon-filament lamps in wire cages. The dynamo is still coupled. The line-shaft turns it, at a speed that ought to produce nothing. But the water is heavy with minerals from the seep, and the town is made of dissimilar metals — iron pipe, copper wire, lead roof flashing, brass fittings, zinc where it was galvanized — and the whole submerged complex has become, without intending to, a battery. The dynamo adds its faint contribution. The mineral water closes every circuit.

Some of the lamps glow.

Not all. Perhaps one in six, and none of them brightly — a dull orange in the filament, no more than the last coal in a grate, visible only because everything else is so dim. They do not flicker. They have not flickered in years. Where they hang, the plants nearest them have grown slightly denser, slightly greener, the way plants will toward any light at all, so that each living lamp sits in its own small nest of foliage that has been reaching for it for decades and has almost enclosed it. In a few more years these lamps will be swallowed and will glow inside the leaves like something in a lantern.

Run your hand along a pipe and you feel it: a faint fizz on the skin, not enough to hurt, the sensation of the town's slow current passing through you on its way to somewhere else.

---

The dye vats are in a long shed to the north, five of them in a row, each a stone cistern lined with lead. They are the only places in the town where the vegetation thins, because the lead has leached into the water that pools in them and few things will grow in it. They stand open and dark, small clearings, and in the bottom of each there is still color. Not much. A stain in the silt — indigo in one, a rusty madder red in another, a mustard that has gone almost to bronze — the last of what the vats held when the water came. The convection does not reach into the vats. Nothing stirs them. If you put your hand in, the silt would rise and take a week to settle and the color would be gone; so far no one has.

Fauna, in the sense of anything moving with intent, is scarce. The water is warm and low in oxygen, and most fish stay above the thermocline where it is cold and clear. What lives down here lives slowly. Ramshorn snails, thousands of them, grazing the engine's flanks, their shells the same color as the rust so that the boiler seems to be very gently breathing. A single kind of small catfish, blind or nearly, that lives in the vats and in the flooded cellars of the terraces and comes out onto the street only in what passes here for night, when the light through the lid goes from brown to black. And once in a long while, something larger passes through the thermocline from above — an eel, usually, on its way somewhere — and the plants along its path sway and the whole town seems for a moment to notice, and then it is gone and the town settles.

---

The rules of the place are few and they are all rules about time.

Nothing here is happening quickly. The flywheel's fifteen minutes is the fastest cycle there is. Above that is the daily convection, one slow overturn of the warm layer from the seep up through the engine house and out across the roofs and down again along the outer walls. Above that, the seasons, which register only as a change in the quality of the light — greener in the months when the lid grows thick, browner when it thins — and a slight lift or fall in the thermocline. Above that, the growth of the plants, which is measured in the closing of doorways. Above that, the rust, which is measured in the thinning of the engine's frame, a process that will one day let the flywheel drop from its bearings and end the knock. No one knows when. The frame was cast heavy.

The silence is not quite silence. Underwater there is always the sound of your own body, and the water here carries a low continuous hush from the seep, a sound felt in the chest more than heard, the sound a kettle makes a long time before it boils. Over that, the knock. Over that, very rarely, a slate shifting on a slope of slates, a sound like a coin dropped in another room. That is all. There are no birds, obviously. There is no wind. The lid takes the rain.

It is dim, warm, full, and patient. It has settled into itself so completely that it no longer resembles a drowned town at all, or not chiefly. It resembles what it is: a small, warm, thickly grown machine, turning, at the bottom of a lake, for no one, keeping time.
