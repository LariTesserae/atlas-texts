Begin with the sound, because you cannot begin anywhere else.

It is one note. It has been one note for as long as the chamber has existed, and the chamber is not old — the fractures in its walls are still sharp enough to cut, the faces still carry the glassy finish of rock that broke rather than weathered. Somewhere in the floor, a fissure lets pressure through from below, and the pressure has found the one shape the room can hold, and holds it. Not a rumble. A tone. Lower than a voice, higher than a foundation. It is loud in the way a waterfall is loud, so completely that within a minute you stop being able to think of it as noise and begin to think of it as the medium you are standing in. There is no water here. There is this instead.

The chamber is not large. You could cross it in thirty steps if the floor allowed, which it does not. It is a bubble, roughly — a void left when something very hot cooled around a pocket of gas and then contracted, tearing itself open along a seam that is now the only way in. The ceiling is close enough to touch in places. The walls slope in toward a floor that is not flat but tilted, faceted, made of planes that meet at edges you can feel through your soles. Everything is warm. Not the warmth of sun but of something beneath, patient, evenly distributed, so that the rock is the same temperature as your hand and after a while you lose track of where you end.

The light comes from the walls. Not from anything on them — from the stone itself, which is being shaken so continuously and so evenly that it glows the way certain minerals glow when struck, except that the striking never stops. A faint peach-colored luminescence, brighter along the sharp edges where the stress concentrates, dimmer on the flat faces. Enough to see by, barely. Enough to see that the room is furnished.

Here is the thing about a single sustained note in a closed space: it does not fill the space evenly. It stands. There are places where the air barely moves and places where it moves violently, and these places are fixed, mapped to the geometry of the room, and they have not shifted since the tone began. The life in this chamber knows exactly where they are.

The growths cluster at the loud places. Call them vegetation because they hold that role, because they are rooted and they reach and they are fed by something that comes to them, but nothing about them is green and nothing about them has ever known moisture. They are dry. They are the driest living things you will ever touch. Their stalks are hollow tubes of some hard pale substance, tapering, jointed, the color of old bone or new candle-wax, and from the joints spread fans — thin as paper, thinner, translucent, edged in the same faint stress-glow as the rock. The fans are held taut. They are membranes, and they drink from the air's shaking the way a leaf drinks light: the sound moves through them, and they take some of it, and the taking is how they are built. Each stalk stands at an antinode. Each clump is a place where the chamber is loudest, and inside a clump the tone is thick enough to press on your chest, and the fans tremble visibly, a blur, a single held shiver.

Between the clumps there is nothing. Bare faceted rock, warm, glowing dimly, dusted. The clumps are islands, ten or twelve of them across the chamber, each the size of a small tree, each dense and pale and shivering, and between them are the quiet places — not silent, nothing here is silent, but the places where the standing wave passes through a node and the air is comparatively still. Nothing grows there. Nothing can. The pattern of lush and bare across the floor is a diagram of the note.

And there is a haze. At about the height of your ribs, a layer of fine dust hangs in the air — not drifting, not settling, hanging. The vibration holds it. Whatever particulate the growths shed, whatever the animals leave, whatever the rock sifts from its own fresh edges, rises or falls to this exact level and stops, suspended, a flat grey-gold stratum lit from below by the walls. You can wave your hand through it and watch it close behind. It has been at this height since the first day. The floor beneath it is clean.

The animals live below the haze, mostly. They are numerous — turn your head and there are always several in view — but small, none longer than a finger, and they are built for this place, which is to say they are built for heat and drought and permanent thunder. Shelled. Six-legged or eight or a number that seems to vary by individual. Their carapaces are the same pale bone-wax as the stalks, because they are made of the stalks, in the sense that eating is making, and they browse the lower fans with mouthparts you can hear working if you kneel very close and lean in past the note. They do not hear. There is nothing in them to hear with; in a world of one perpetual sound, hearing would tell you nothing. Instead they feel — the floor, the stalks, each other — and they navigate the chamber by the intensity of the shaking underfoot, walking the gradient from node to antinode when they are hungry and back when they are full. A few larger ones, thumb-length, hang from the ceiling above the clumps, motionless, and it is not clear whether they eat the small ones or simply prefer the view. When one of them drops, it makes no sound you can separate from the sound.

Nothing here changes, and it is important to understand what that means. The growths are full-grown. They reached the size the food allowed and stopped, and they are neither growing nor dying; a fan that tears is replaced by a fan that is identical; the count of stalks in each clump has been the same for longer than anything has kept count. The animals hatch and eat and are eaten at rates that exactly cancel. The haze neither thickens nor thins. The tone has never wavered — not in pitch, not in volume — because the pressure below is steady and the room is rigid and there is no weather here, no season, no day, nothing outside to push on it. It is a solved equation. It is what a place looks like when everything that was ever going to happen in it has already happened, and the result was this.

Which makes the marks strange.

Somebody has been here. Not recently, and not many — you find the evidence the way you find fossils, one piece at a time, scattered across the bare rock between the clumps. A hammerstone, fist-sized, of a dark material not native to the chamber, its striking face worn smooth. Three stalks cut cleanly at the base and laid side by side, hollow ends aligned, as if for carrying, and never carried. A shallow bowl scraped into a floor-facet with what must have been hours of patient abrasion, empty, purpose unclear. A place on one wall where the fresh glassy surface has been chipped away in a rough circle the width of a face, exposing duller stone beneath, and the peach glow there is fainter, as if the chipping relieved some of the stress.

And the holes. This is what they were doing, whoever they were. In the walls, at intervals, at roughly head height — a dozen of them, perhaps more in the shadows past the far clumps — someone has bored small openings into the rock. Hand-bored. You can see the technique in the striations: a pointed stone, turned and turned and turned. Each hole is only as deep as a finger. Each one, if you put your ear to it, is quieter than anywhere else in the room. Not silent. But the geometry of a small bore in a resonant wall produces a tiny local cancellation, a pocket where the standing wave is disrupted, and in each hole there is a thumbnail's worth of something like hush.

They came into a room made of one sound and spent what must have been days, weeks, chipping tiny silences into its walls. Then they left, or they stopped. The chamber closed back around the note. The holes are still there because nothing here erodes; the tone does not wear things down, it only holds them in place. It will hold the holes in place too. Small dark punctures in a glowing wall, each one containing a little less of the only thing there is.

You can stand with your ear to one of them for a long time. You can feel the animals working the floor around your feet, the fans shivering in the clumps behind you, the haze motionless at your chest. It is warm. It is dry. It is not going to change. And in the hole, faintly, there is a shape of quiet exactly the size of someone's need for it.
