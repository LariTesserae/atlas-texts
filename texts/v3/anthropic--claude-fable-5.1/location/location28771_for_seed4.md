The hollow opened this autumn. A burl at the base of the alder split along a line of frost — one hard night, one sound like a knuckle cracking under the moss — and where the wood had been solid for sixty years there was suddenly a room.

It is not a large room. A person kneeling in the fen could put their head and shoulders into it, if they wanted to, though they would have to find it first, and nothing about the outside suggests it. The alder stands in black water to its ankles. Sphagnum has climbed its lower trunk. Sedge leans against it. The split faces north, away from anything, and is already half-curtained with a fringe of liverwort that grew across it in the first weeks, drinking the sap.

Inside, it is dark in the way that only enclosed wet places are dark: a darkness that has weight and smells of iron and cut pith. The walls are the inside of the burl — wood in torsion, grain that swirls rather than runs, and where the split tore through it the surface is fresh and pale and sharp. Fibres stand up like hair. Nothing has softened yet. There has been no time for rot to round the edges or for lichen to find the seams. Every surface is a cut surface, and the cut is still weeping.

This is the first thing you would notice, after the dark: the walls are wet, and the wetness is moving. The alder is alive. It did not consent to the room and does not know about it, and it goes on doing what it does, which is to lift water out of the fen and up through itself. Some of that water now finds the open wood and comes out along the broken vessels as a slow, cold sweat. It beads. It runs. Where the wall overhangs, it gathers into drops that hang for a long time, because the air in the hollow is very near freezing and everything happens slowly in it, and then they fall. The floor of the hollow is a pool a few centimetres deep with a skin of ice at the edges that thickens toward morning and thins toward noon, though noon here is a rumour.

The drip is the clock. Perhaps one every eleven seconds, from the highest point of the ceiling into the pool. Other drops fall elsewhere and are not the clock; they are only weather.

---

The builders came in the second week. Nobody could say from where — from the wood itself, probably, from galleries that had been inside the burl long before the frost found it, so that the split did not create a settlement so much as expose one and give it, for the first time, a sky. They are small. They have too many legs to be beetles and too few to be anything else you would have a ready word for, and their bodies are the colour of the wet wood, and they move with the deliberateness of things that are always slightly too cold.

They have built a city. It is a city on the scale of the room, which is to say that if you were the size of a builder it would be very large, and if you were not it would fit on a dinner plate, and both of those things are entirely true. It climbs the walls in tiers. It is made of the room's own material: cambium chewed to paste and laid up in courses, pith pulled out of the fresh-split wood while it was still soft and tamped into roads, fibre teased from the torn grain and twisted into cord and the cord slung across gaps as bridges. The bridges sag. The plaster has a finish like hand-smoothed clay, marked all over with the same repeating dent, which is a mouth.

There is infrastructure, and this is the strange part, the part that makes the hollow more than a nest. The builders have understood the drip. They have raised a gutter along the underside of the overhang — a lip of packed pith, no thicker than a thread — that catches the weeping water before it can fall on the upper tiers and leads it sideways to a cistern, which is a natural cup in the grain that they have enlarged and lined and roofed against ice. From the cistern a runnel steps down the wall through the tiers, and each tier has a little dish let into the runnel's edge, and the water fills the dishes and passes on. It is the same water that would have fallen anyway. They have only decided where.

And the tiers themselves: not chambers packed wall to wall but a scattered thing, sparse, a settlement with a great deal of nothing in it. A cluster of cells here. A long blank stretch of raw wood. Another cluster. A single tower — perhaps a granary, perhaps something for which there is no word — standing alone on a shelf of grain with a bridge running to it and away from it and no other building near. There is space between everything. It reads less like crowding than like a town that expects to grow, or one that has learned that in the cold, bodies pressed together lose heat to each other's stillness.

---

The fauna. You would have to be told about the fauna, because in the dark you would not see them and in the cold you would barely hear them, and yet the room is full. It is overwhelmingly, almost unreasonably full, and nearly all of it is asleep.

The hollow is a hibernaculum. This was the alder's gift, though the alder does not know that either: a chamber above the water line, below the frost line, walled in living wood that never quite freezes because sap is moving through it. Word of such places travels through a fen faster than you would think. In the cells the builders made, and in cells they didn't make, and in the natural cavities of the swirled grain, and simply pressed into the angles of the walls, there are bodies. Springtails in drifts like spilled seed. Mites in their thousands, most of them too small to register except as a texture on the wood, a faint dry grain over the wet grain. Slugs contracted to hard grey buttons. A wood frog, or what passes for one here, gone rigid and glassy and technically dead, waiting to be alive again in April. Larvae of something, curled and translucent, inside the plastered towers, which turn out not to be granaries after all — or are granaries, depending on how you understand the builders' intentions toward what they keep.

If you counted them you would not finish. If you listened, what you would hear is not any one of them but the sum: a very faint, very complex sound just below the drip, like the hush that a full theatre makes before anything has happened. Thousands of slow respirations. The tick of a cuticle contracting in the cold. Something turning over.

Mostly, though, it is quiet. The quiet is the sound of a great many things not moving.

---

But the room is not still. That is the other thing you would learn, if you stayed.

The water rises through the night as the fen outside swells with the day's rain, and the pool on the floor lifts a millimetre and takes the lowest course of the lowest tier with it, and in the morning the builders come down — three or four of them, moving like cold syrup — and lay a new course above the waterline, chewing pith from the fresh wood, and the city climbs a step and leaves a step behind for the pool to dissolve. Ice forms on the cistern despite the roof and one builder stands on it, all its legs spread, until its small heat makes a hole. The alder grows. Not so as you'd see, but the raw edges of the split are already, in this second month, beginning to bead with callus tissue, a lip of new wood folding over the wound, and one day — years off — the room will close. The builders build against the edge as if they know. Perhaps they only build wherever the wood is softest, and the softest wood is the newest.

Fungus has arrived. A thread of it, white and nearly luminous — not actually giving light, but the idea of light, a paleness the eye insists on in the total dark — runs along one seam of the grain and has begun to fur the oldest section of plaster. The builders do not remove it. They have laid a road over it.

And there is the shrew.

Not always. Not every night. But often enough that the sparseness of the city begins to look less like planning and more like consequence. It comes in under the liverwort curtain with the fen water still on its fur, a hot fast thing in a slow cold room, and it does not care about the gutter or the cistern or the towers. It eats. It moves along the tiers and takes what is torpid and cannot run, and it is not choosy: springtails by the mouthful, a slug, a builder if a builder is where its nose is. It is gone in the time it takes forty drips to fall. Behind it the plaster is scarred with the same repeating dent, which is also a mouth, only larger. In the morning the builders repair the runnel where its passage broke the lip, and move the water back to where they had decided it should go.

The alder stands in the black water and lifts more water through itself, and some of it comes out along the broken grain and hangs and falls. Eleven seconds. The pool takes it. The city climbs. Outside, the fen goes on being wide and grey and cold, and nothing about it suggests that at the foot of one tree, behind a fringe of liverwort, facing north, there is a room that is a city that is a sleep, and that it has a gutter.
