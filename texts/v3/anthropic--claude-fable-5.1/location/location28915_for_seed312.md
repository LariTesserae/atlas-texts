Start with the rock, because everything here is a negotiation with it.

The plateau is young in the way a scar is young. Something lifted it — a whole crustal block, hundreds of leagues across — and it has not yet had time to be worn down into anything gentle. The edges are cliffs that drop into cloud. The surface is broken into blocks and slabs with faces still sharp enough to cut a hand, dark grey-brown stone that rings slightly when struck, fractured along planes so regular that from a distance the ground looks tiled. There is almost no soil. What passes for soil is rock flour, ground by frost in the cracks, gathering in the low places as a pale grit. Streams have not yet learned their courses; water goes where the fractures send it, pooling in joints, disappearing, reappearing a day's walk away as a seep on a cliff face with a green beard of moss under it.

It is cold in the persistent, dry way of altitude, the kind of cold that doesn't bite so much as it withholds. The sun is close and clear and gives light without much heat. At midday the dark stone warms enough that frost on its south faces melts and runs, and this is the water. That is the first rule of the world: water arrives as condensation and frost and thin fast cloud, almost never as rain. The clouds come up the cliff faces from whatever lies below and spill across the plateau in the afternoon like something poured, dampening everything, beading on stone and fur and wool, and then the night freezes it and the morning melts it, and this is enough. Barely, but enough.

The vegetation is what can live on that. Lichens, first and mostly — orange and grey-green and black, crusting the rock in patterns older than anything built here. Cushion plants in the cracks, tight as fists. A hard short grass in the gritty flats, more grey than green. In the seep lines below the cliffs, moss and a small-leaved thing with a red stem that people gather. Nothing you could call a tree. Nothing taller than a knee. When wind moves across it, the plateau does not rustle; it whispers, or it is silent.

And yet it is full.

This is the thing a visitor would not expect. The stone is bare and the plants are meagre and the plateau is thick with living things. Herds move across the tiled ground — long-necked, thick-coated grazers with pale bellies and dark backs, in groups of hundreds, so many that at the horizon they look like a texture on the land rather than animals. Smaller things live in every fracture: a fat grey rodent that whistles, a kind of hare with ears fringed in black, lizards that emerge only in the two warm hours and lie flat on the south faces. The cliffs at the plateau's edges are white with birds — colonies of them, stacked ledge on ledge, tens of thousands, whose droppings are the richest thing on the plateau and are collected. Large slow raptors ride the afternoon cloud-wind. At the seeps there are frogs, small and dark, which no one can quite explain. The density is animal density. The land is poor in what grows but abundant in what moves, and everything that moves eats lichen or grass or each other, and the herds carry the whole economy of the place on their backs.

The city is built on this, and out of this, and the two are hard to separate.

There is no wood, so there is stone, and there is a great deal of stone. The city is dry-laid — no mortar, only the fit of one sharp-edged block against another, which the young rock makes almost easy. Walls go up in courses of near-rectangular slabs split along their natural planes. Roofs are corbelled, or laid over with long thin slabs and then turfed with the grey grass for warmth. Because flat ground is scarce and water is scarcer and the herds must be kept close, the city does not spread. It stacks. Houses are built on the roofs of houses. Animal pens occupy the ground level, and the warmth of the beasts rises through the slab floors into the rooms above, and this is understood as the reason things are arranged so. Lanes are narrow and stepped and roofed over in places so that walking through the city is often walking through a tunnel of dry stone, with light coming down through gaps at intervals. People and animals pass each other constantly. You are never more than an arm's length from a wall, a neck, a fleece, a hand.

Every house has a cistern, and every cistern is fed by a stone apron — a slanted surface of fitted slabs set to face the afternoon cloud and the morning melt, channelled at its lower edge into a covered tank. The whole city, seen from above, is a field of these tilted planes at slightly different angles, dark when dry and gleaming when wet. Larger aprons on the cliff-edges feed larger tanks that belong to no one and everyone. The dung of the cliff birds, carried up in baskets, is spread on terraced strips of grit where the red-stemmed plant is coaxed to grow more thickly than it would on its own.

The tools are stone, bone, sinew, wool. There is a little copper, beaten cold, for pins and blades that are cherished. The herds give hair for cloth and rope, hide, milk, meat, bone for awls and needles, dung for fuel, which burns low and long and smells sweet. The work is all handwork and there is a great deal of it, and it is done slowly, because everything here is done slowly.

That is the second rule, and it is enforced by the cold and the thin air: nothing hurries. The herds move across the plateau on cycles that take a year to complete, following the frost-melt and the grass, and the people move with them or wait for them. Building a house is a matter of seasons. Water is gathered a cupful at a time. The birds come and go with a rhythm no one alive has seen the beginning or end of. Even the ground, young as it is, is slow — a crack widens by a finger-width in a lifetime, a slab tilts, a seep shifts three paces sideways over a generation and the household that depended on it gradually rebuilds its apron to follow.

The quiet is part of this. Sound does not carry well in the thin air, and there is little to make it. The wind is constant and low. The grey rodents whistle to each other in the fractures. The herds make a soft continuous sound, a mixture of breath and movement and the click of hooves on stone, which becomes inaudible after a while and is only noticed when it stops. In the city, voices are kept down, not by rule but by habit; the tunnelled lanes and thick walls swallow them anyway. The loudest thing in the world is the bird cliff at dawn, and even that reaches the city as a kind of pale roar, more felt than heard. Once a year, perhaps, a slab breaks loose from a cliff edge and the sound of it goes on for a long time.

At the edge of the plateau, where the city thins to watch-huts and dung-drying racks, you can stand on the last slab and look out over the cloud sea to the far rim, a hundred leagues off, catching the light. There are other cities there; their fires can be seen on the clearest nights as a faint sparse line. Between here and there is nothing but the tiled ground, the whispering grass, the lichen, and the herds — those enormous slow presences moving across a land that has not yet decided what shape it will finally take, followed at a distance by people carrying baskets, going where the water goes.
