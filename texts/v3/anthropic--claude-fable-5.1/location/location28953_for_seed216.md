The first thing wrong is the warmth.

You are high enough that the air is thin, the kind of height where breath comes short and the sky, when you can see it, is the deep and slightly wrong blue of the edge of things. It should be cold. It is not cold. The air lies against the skin at roughly the temperature of skin, and it is wet — saturated, so that every surface films over and beads and drips — and the wetness is warm too, and the warmth comes up from below, from the ground, which is not ground.

It is wood.

Grey, close-grained, worn smooth and slightly concave along the lines where feet have habitually gone, and everywhere else ridged into a lattice. Diamond cells, each about the width of a forearm. At every intersection the wood rises into a rounded seam, a scar. The pattern does not vary. It runs off into the mist in every direction and it is the same in every direction, and after a while it becomes clear that the lattice is not a growth habit but a technique: someone laid these roots across one another, bound them, and waited, and the wood took the binding and closed over it, and this was done again beside it, and again, and again, across a distance that has no edge you can reach.

There is no stone. People have dug, and the pits are still there, grown round at the lip. Beneath the grey wood is darker wood, denser, then a stratum of something like heartwood but soft and wet and faintly sweet-smelling, and then the digging stops, because below that the wood ends and there is air. Not soil. Not rock. A hollow.

What you are standing on is a skin.

---

The hollow beneath the skin is the actual place. It is entered where the skin has been deliberately left open — round wells with polished rims, or long slots where two panels of the lattice were never joined, so that from a distance the surface reads as a plain with dark mouths in it. Below the mouths the wood turns vertical and then folds inward, and the vertical walls are also lattice, the same forearm-wide diamonds, but here they are no longer floor: they are the pleached trunks of the plant itself, trained and bound and fused into walls that run down and out of sight.

The volume inside is very large.

It is large enough to have weather. The walls exhale — the whole plant is warm, warm the way a mound of rotting leaves is warm, or the flower of certain great swamp lilies, generating heat out of its own slow chemistry — and the exhalation meets the cool that gathers under the roof-wood far above and condenses, and there are clouds, indoors, grey-brown clouds the color of the wood they came from, drifting in a vault whose far side is not visible. Sometimes it rains. The rain falls through dim light onto floors that are ceilings for other hollows deeper down, and runs along the seams of the lattice, and finds the joints, and drips. The drip is the main sound. In the galleries it is nearly the only sound. Each drop lands with a small distinct note and then the note is gone into an enormous absorbent quiet, and you wait for the next, and it comes from somewhere else.

The light is what makes it through the walls. The outer skin is many layers of living tissue, and daylight arriving at the surface loses itself in them and comes into the hollow late and strained and greenish-grey, the light of a pond seen from underneath. It is never bright. At the mouths, where the skin is open, columns of paler air stand in the murk with mist turning slowly in them, but away from the mouths the walls give off only a kind of glow, a dull translucence, as though the wood were remembering a day rather than admitting one.

---

The galleries run for a long way. This is the second thing that is wrong, though it takes longer to notice.

You can walk in one direction along a gallery for what your legs tell you is most of a day and see nothing but the gallery. The lattice on the left. The lattice on the right, a long way off, sometimes lost in mist. The vault. The drip. Then, without anything having changed, you arrive at a place you recognize — a seam with a particular knot in it, a well-mouth with a certain lean to its rim — and the recognition is not that you have come round in a circle. You have not turned. The gallery has been straight. You have walked east into a place that lies west of where you started.

The plant has been folded.

This is what the gardeners did with pleaching, over the scale of time it took to make a continent of wood by hand. A branch was bent to meet another branch. They were bound. Where the bark fused, the two became one branch, one flow of sap, one thing — and they fused not only in wood. The place where the first branch had been and the place where the second had been grew together too. A gallery that runs a mile and arrives at its own beginning is not a trick of the mist. It is a graft. Somewhere, generations ago, a person with a knife of chipped stone cut two cambiums so they would match and lashed them with cord twisted from the plant's own inner bark and packed the union with mud and moss and went away and died, and the plant did the rest, and now here is the wound, healed, and you are standing on the far side of it having never crossed it.

You can find the old folds if you know what to look for. The wood around them is different: harder, darker, a ring of dense grain like the scar-tissue of a graft-union, which is what it is. The oldest are almost invisible, absorbed, a slight change in the sound your feet make. The younger ones still show a lip. There is a place where a shaft descends through six levels of floor and the lowest floor is the roof of the first. There is a wall where the light comes in wrong — one panel of it lit with a thin cold morning light, the rest in the ordinary green murk, the two meeting at a seam, and beyond the seam is a morning that is not the morning you are in. There are galleries in which a person's voice, if anyone raised one, would come back from behind them before it finished leaving.

Nobody raises their voice here.

---

The plant is old and the folds are old and the whole thing has reached the condition of a thing that has stopped becoming and started simply being. The gardeners' work is finished, or has passed the point where finishing means anything. The lattice grows on, cell by cell, at the pace of wood, and the same slow pace wears it down — feet, water, the small persistent chewing of what lives in it — and the two rates are now the same. Nothing is under construction. Nothing is falling. The folds that must once have been violent, that must have shuddered the whole continent when the first fused branches pulled distance shut like a purse-string, are long since silent, healed, tolerated by the wood the way a tree tolerates a stone it has grown around.

But it is not empty of process. It is empty of density, which is a different thing.

What lives here lives in enormous dispersal. The hollow is so large and the food in it so spread that nothing needs to be near anything else, and so mostly nothing is. You walk for hours in the presence of the drip and the vault and the lattice and then, all at once, a presence: a colony of something like swifts nesting in a fold, a thousand cup-nests glued into the diamond cells with saliva and wood-dust, and the birds themselves pouring out of a well-mouth in near-silence, a flicker of wing against the grey glow, and streaming down the gallery and into a fold and out — you can hear them, very faintly, arriving somewhere that by the ear is a long way off and by the wall is a few paces behind you.

Then nothing again for an hour. Then a place where the wall is soft and honeycombed and the honeycomb moves: beetles, pale, the size of a thumb, working the dead layer beneath the living, eating slowly, and the sound they make together is a fine dry seething that stops when you come close and starts again when you go.

Then nothing. Then, on a floor far down, seen from a well-mouth above, something large moving along the seam of the lattice with the deliberation of an animal that has never needed to hurry — long, low, the color of the wood, scraping the fungus off the wall with a broad flat mouth. It does not look up. It has probably never been looked at.

There are hunters. You do not see them. What you see are the places where the folds have been used: a gallery with a scatter of small bones along a seam, all at the same spot, as if something waited here for the swifts to come out of a fold that emptied into this wall. A wet mark on the floor that is the wrong shape for anything that walks. The hunters here have the advantage of geometry. A thing that knows the folds can be behind you by going ahead, and so everything that lives in the hollow lives quietly, and moves along the walls, and stops often, and the great vault, which could ring, does not.

---

Traces of the gardeners are everywhere and the gardeners are almost nowhere.

Their tools are still in the walls. Blades of dark stone, flaked to an edge, left in a seam the way one leaves a knife in a loaf, and the wood has grown up around the handle so that only the edge shows, grey against grey. Coils of bark-fiber cord, the twist still visible, the fiber gone to a kind of leather. Awls of bone. Lumps of dried mud with the impression of fingers, packed into the joint of two trunks that have long since made one trunk of themselves. There are no metals. There is nothing that turns. There is cut and match and bind and seal and wait, and the waiting was the instrument that did the real work, and it was used on a scale that made a world.

Whether anyone still tends the place is not a question with a plain answer. There are unions where the moss packing is green. There is a section of lattice, far along one gallery, where a fraying seam has been re-bound with new cord, the knots tight and unweathered, and the mud is still damp, and nobody is there. The density of the place is such that you could share it with a hundred people and never meet one, and the folds are such that meeting one would mean nothing about where they were.

What you have, instead, is this.

The wall beside you breathing out its warm mist. The film of water on everything, silvering the seams, gathering at the low points of every diamond and dripping. The dim green wash of a daylight several skins away. The vault overhead, brown cloud moving slowly in it, and the certainty, gathered over hours of walking, that the vault is one vault, one hollow, one fold on itself, and that the whole of it is a single plant trained by hand into the shape of a place, and that the shape is not a shape space was designed to hold, and that it holds it anyway, and has for so long that holding it has stopped being an effort and become a condition.

A drop falls. Its note goes out into the quiet. Somewhere — behind you, ahead, it is not a meaningful distinction — another drop answers, and then the hollow is silent again, the warm silence of a thing that was made, and finished, and is now simply alive.
