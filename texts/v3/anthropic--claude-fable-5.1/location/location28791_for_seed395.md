The ground is new. That is the first thing, before the green, before anything else — the rock underfoot has not been rained on. It came up out of somewhere hot within the last few centuries, a vast low shield of it, and it still carries the ropey skin of its own cooling, every ripple and fold sharp-edged, unrounded. Nothing has had time to wear it. Cracks run through it like an unfinished argument, and in the bottoms of the cracks there is a fine white powder that is not sand but the rock's own mineral sweat, salts pushed out as it cured. Walk on it and you leave no track. Brush a hand across it and it is warm, warmer than the air, and it does not give.

There should be nothing here. There is almost everything.

What grows on this rock grows without a river, without a lake, without a puddle. There is dew, some mornings, and a little more in the long season when the air comes in off whatever lies beyond the horizon, but there is no water you could point to. The plants have simply decided to be the water themselves. They are succulents, mostly — but that word suggests something small on a windowsill, and these are not small. Columns of ribbed green stand as thick as a person and three times as tall, pleated so they can swell and shrink, their skins waxed to a dull shine. Between the columns, fat-leaved rosettes the size of carts sit directly on the basalt with no soil beneath them at all, their roots spread flat across the stone like hands pressed against a window. Vines with leaves like green coins loop from column to column. Everything is close. There is no path that does not involve turning sideways. The green closes overhead in places and the light that comes through it is yellow-green and steady, the light of a warm afternoon that has been going on for a long time and shows no sign of ending.

And the smell — not damp, never damp, but a dry sweetness, resin and crushed leaf and something faintly mineral, salt on the tongue.

It is quiet in the way that only very full places are quiet. Nothing here calls out. The columns creak a little when they take on moisture at dawn and creak again when they give it up. Something small moves in the litter of dropped spines and that is all. The silence is not empty; it is upholstered.

It takes a while to understand that the green is not the only thing that has been built here.

Rising among the columns, and sometimes rising through them, are structures of cemented earth — grey-tan, smooth, taller than the plants in places, rounded at their tops like the caps of enormous mushrooms and pierced everywhere with vents. Their walls are made of nothing but chewed rock-powder and the bodies of dead plants and a binding spit, laid down grain by grain. They are the work of insects, blind, pale, uncountable, who have never stopped working since the rock cooled enough to walk on. One tower is a house; a hundred towers, joined at their bases by low covered galleries, linked at their shoulders by arched buttresses, is a street; and the streets go on. The galleries run for miles. They bridge the cracks in the basalt. They wrap the trunks of the great succulents and climb them, so that a column may wear a spiral collar of earth to a height of ten meters, and a slow trickle of foragers goes up and down inside the collar in absolute darkness, harvesting the plant's shed skin, never seeing the day.

This is the city. It has no plan and it is entirely planned. Air moves through it constantly — you can feel it, a cool exhalation from the vents near the ground, a warmer sigh from the chimneys above, the whole conurbation breathing in one direction by day and the other by night. The insects do not think about this. The towers simply die if it stops, and the towers that stood in the wrong places have died, and been dissolved, and been rebuilt elsewhere, and so over time the city has arranged itself into something that works. Its skyline follows the prevailing wind. Its main galleries run along the seams in the rock where the salt is weakest. It is architecture that arrived at its form by being wrong many times.

The city is always under construction. In any given hour, somewhere, a wall is being extended by the width of a grain; somewhere else a tower has been abandoned and is slowly being quarried by its neighbors, carried away a mouthful at a time to become something new. Nothing is finished. Nothing is ruined either — there has not been time, and there is no rain to ruin it.

And the whole thing is crawling.

Not with the builders, who stay hidden — but with everyone who has come to live off them. Lizards the color of the towers lie along the galleries and wait for the moment a forager surfaces. There are beetles like polished chips of obsidian moving in slow processions along the buttresses. There are birds — small, dun, silent — who have never built a nest in their lives, because the abandoned chambers of the upper towers are better than any nest, and they slip in and out of the vents like thoughts. Something with long ears crouches in the shade of a rosette, chewing. Something with a longer body pours itself down through a crack in the basalt and is gone. A hawk sits at the top of the highest chimney for a long time and then falls forward off it without a sound and does not come back up for a while. Everywhere there is the small mechanical patience of things waiting for other things to move first.

Nobody here speaks. Nobody here has anything to speak with. The loudest event in an ordinary day is a column shedding a dead arm, which lands on the basalt with a soft thump and is met within minutes by a hundred small mouths.

You could walk for a month and not come out the other side of it.

Because this is the strange part, the part that takes the longest to sink in: the city does not stop. The forest does not stop. The rock goes on, low and warm and barely above sea level, all the way to a horizon that is not a line but a haze, and every mile of it is as full as this mile. Somewhere out there the air rises off the sun-hot towers in such volume that it makes its own weather — tall pale clouds that assemble each afternoon over the middle of the continent, darken, throw dry lightning down onto the chimneys, and dissolve without ever giving up their rain. The plants take what falls as vapor. The insects take what the plants exhale. Nothing is wasted because there is nothing to spare. The lightning starts small fires in the dead litter now and then, and the fires move slowly through the packed green, and behind them the builders are already out, already carrying, already laying the first grains of the next wall on the still-warm ash.

It is not old. That is what keeps coming back. This whole saturated, choked, breathing expanse — the towers, the columns, the streets that run to the edge of sight — has all of it happened in a geological blink, on rock that has not yet learned what weather is. It is what a world does when it is given a bare floor and warmth and time, before anything has worn away, before there is any such thing as a valley. It is the first draft. It is very quiet. It is only just getting started.
