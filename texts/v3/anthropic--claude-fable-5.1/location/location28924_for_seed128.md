**The Weeping Gallery**

You hear it before there is anything to see, and you go on hearing it after there is nothing left to say. The sound is the first fact of the place and the last: a roar so continuous and so large that within a few seconds it stops being a sound at all and becomes a property of the air, like its temperature or its weight. It sits in the chest. It makes the fillings in your teeth feel present. If you open your mouth and shout, you will feel the shout in your throat and see nothing come of it. The gallery does not so much drown out voices as make the idea of a voice seem provincial.

The gallery itself is small. That is the strange thing, if anything here is strange — a chamber perhaps twelve paces across and twice that long, with a ceiling you could touch from a stepladder, tucked into the flank of something so large that the gallery has no relationship to it that a body can feel. You are inside a fold in a wall of a thing that is mostly wall. Somewhere behind the back surface — a hand's thickness, or a hundred meters, there is no way to know — the structure moves air, or something like air, at a velocity that would flay skin, and the gallery is pressed up against that duct the way a room in an old house is pressed against a chimney. The roar comes through the wall as heat comes through a wall: unevenly, more at some points than others, so that if you lay your palm flat against the back surface you can find the places where the noise is loudest not by ear but by the trembling in your wrist.

Everything here is made. There is no rock. There is no soil that did not begin as something the structure produced or something a body left behind. The walls are a poured material the color of old pewter, and the color is wrong for concrete and the surface is wrong for metal — too smooth in some places, too fibrous in others, with a grain that runs in long lazy diagonals as if it had been laid down by a tide. It is warm. Not hot — warm the way an animal is warm, warm the way a hand is. The gallery is a rejection surface; heat from somewhere in the structure's interior is shed through these walls, has been shed through them for longer than anyone has records of, and the shedding is what makes this a place where things can live.

And the walls weep. This is their function too, or a side effect of their function so old and so reliable it may as well be one. Moisture moves through the pewter material along its grain and beads on the surface in droplets that fatten, hang, join, and run. Every square meter of wall is wet. Where the grain runs vertical the water descends in threads. Where it runs at an angle the threads gather into shallow rivulets that have, over the centuries, deposited what they carried out of the wall: a mineral crust, silvery-gray shading to rust orange, in curtains and lips and shelves that look exactly like the flowstone of a limestone cave and are made of nothing that occurs in any limestone. The crust is the oldest visible thing in the gallery. In places it is thick as a wrist. Where the shelves have grown out far enough to overhang, they drip, and under each drip a small cup has been dissolved into the crust below it, and the cups hold water that is faintly warm and faintly metallic and clear as glass.

The air is saturated. Breath doesn't fog — it's too warm for that — but skin films over within a minute and stays that way. The single opening to the outside, a grille high in the far wall about the size of a door laid on its side, lets in a slab of gray light and, several times a day, cloud. The gallery sits at an altitude where the weather comes to it. A cloud will push its wet edge through the grille slats, hang in the upper third of the room in a slow curl, and either dissolve in the warmth or thicken until the far wall disappears and the light becomes a general dimness with no source. Then it pulls back out, or it rains, or both. The grille is the only daylight. It is enough to see by and not enough to see well. Colors are guesses. A second, lesser light comes from a strip that runs at knee height around the whole chamber, a band of the wall material that glows a dull, even, exhausted amber — some marker, some safety measure meant for a maintenance regime that has not been performed in living memory — and it is this strip, not the grille, that the eye adjusts to, so that after an hour everything above the knee seems to float in a brown dusk and everything below it lies in a faint honey light.

Under all of it is the floor, which is not a floor but a deposit. Centuries of what falls from above: droppings, molted casings, shed skins, wing membranes, mineral dust washed down off the crusts, the fine black soil that all of it becomes when it has been wet and warm long enough. It is knee-deep in the corners and ankle-deep in the center where the traffic keeps it packed. It gives underfoot like turf. It smells of ammonia and iron and something green. Nothing about it is natural, and it is the most alive thing in the room.

Because the gallery is crowded. That is the second fact, after the noise: how many bodies there are in how little space. The ceiling is a roost. Bats hang in the dry stripes between drip-lines, packed so close their folded wings overlap like roof tiles, hundreds of them, small brown ones with pale faces, in continuous tiny motion — shifting, grooming, jostling, a whole ceiling of restlessness. Swifts own the upper walls near the grille, plastered in mud-and-spit cups along the mineral shelves in colonies so dense the nests share walls. Below the swifts, in the wet zone, are the frogs — dark, slick, fingernail-sized to fist-sized, dozens visible at any moment and dozens more you become aware of only when the honey light catches a throat. Geckos ribbon along the crusts. Something long and low with a ringed tail lives in the deposit on the far side and comes out at dusk; there appear to be two of them, or one that moves quickly. And there are the insects, which are past counting — a permanent smoke of midges in the grille-light, beetles working the deposit, flat centipedes the length of a hand that you see only as a shine crossing a shelf, moths at rest on the ceiling between the bats with their wings held open, shivering in the roar.

Every creature here has made an arrangement with the noise. You notice this slowly. The frogs call — you can see their throats pulse — and the calls are pitched impossibly high, thin whistling ticks that ride above the roar the way a needle rides above a wave. The bats echolocate and find each other by touch. The swifts do not call at all inside; they scream outside, at the grille, and go silent when they cross it. The ringed-tail animals communicate, if they communicate, by scent and posture. Nothing here listens for footsteps. Nothing startles at sound. You can walk among them and they regard you as weather. It is possible this is why they have gathered: that the roar is a kind of shelter, a moat of noise that makes the gallery useless to anything that hunts by ear, and so everything that does not hunt by ear has come here to live in the warm.

There is almost no green. A few ferns in the grille-light, growing from crevices in the mineral crust, their fronds beaded and heavy. A mat of something dark — moss or alga or a film of both — in the glow of the amber strip, where the wall is wettest. That is all. The gallery is not a garden. It is a den.

The place has its clock, and the clock is the sound. The roar is not constant, though it feels so at first; it breathes. Over the course of a day the pitch drops and rises — low and thick in the small hours, climbing toward something almost like a whine in the afternoon — as whatever the structure is doing behind the wall changes its labor. The animals know the pitch the way animals elsewhere know the sun. When the roar begins its evening fall the bats shift and wake and begin to pour out through the grille in a stream that takes twenty minutes to empty the ceiling, crossing the incoming swifts in a braid of bodies that never seems to tangle. When it climbs again toward dawn the traffic reverses. The frogs call in the trough of the sound and go silent at its crest. The drip rate changes too — the wall weeps heavier when it is working harder — so that the mineral cups fill and overflow in the afternoons and stand still in the mornings, and the whole gallery has a tide.

It is, everyone who has stood in it agrees, at rest. Not idle — nothing here is idle — but settled. The crusts have grown to the shapes they will keep. The deposit has found its depth. The bats have hung in these stripes so long the stripes have been polished by them. Whatever engineered this wall to shed heat and weep water did not intend a den full of frogs and cloud, but the wall has been doing what it does for so long that the den is now simply what the wall does. The made thing has been lived in until living in it is one of its functions.

You leave through the same low hatch you entered by, and the noise stays with you for an hour afterward as a ringing that gradually reveals itself to be silence.
