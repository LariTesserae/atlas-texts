Begin under the ground, because that is where everything here is. Whatever the land above looks like — scrub, most likely, thin grass over dark rock, a sky nobody in this place ever needs to think about — it is only a lid. The country is beneath it.

It is a country of hollows. Not one cave but a whole drowned-looking district of them, tube joined to chamber joined to gallery, running for what a person on foot would call days in every direction, and the ceiling has come down in enough places that the system reads less like a tunnel and more like a valley that happens to have a roof over most of it. Where the roof has fallen, a shaft of grey light comes in. Where it has held, the dark is total. Between these two conditions is where almost everything lives.

The rock is young. That is the first thing the hand notices before the eye does. There is none of the softness that long-worked stone has, none of the flowstone drapery or the polished lips of old cave mouths. The walls are fractured, not weathered: they break along fresh planes, the edges still keen enough to cut, the surfaces dull black or rust-brown with a glassy sheen in places where the light strikes them. The floor is rubble in the collapse zones, great slabs and blocks that fell not so long ago and have not yet been rounded or buried, tilted against each other with gaps between them that go down further than a dropped stone can report. In the tubes proper the floor is smoother — a rippled, ropy surface, like something that flowed and stopped — and it is on these smoother reaches that the ground is coated, ankle-deep and deeper, in the soft grey-brown mass that is the real substance of this place. But that comes later.

The air is cool and does not change. Whatever the season is above, it does not reach down here; the rock holds a single temperature and the air takes it, and moisture beads on every surface that is cooler than the air, which is all of them. The walls sweat. The ceiling drips — not everywhere, not constantly at any one point, but always somewhere, so that the sound of water falling onto stone or onto something softer than stone is a permanent part of the room tone. Small pools gather in the depressions of the rippled floor and in the lowest points of the collapse rubble, black and perfectly still, a hand's depth or a body's depth, nobody can tell by looking. There is no river. The water arrives as seepage and vapour and departs the same way, and the pools sit at the exact level they have always sat at, neither rising nor falling.

Vegetation is a matter of light, and light is scarce. Under each skylight there is a cone of grey illumination, brightest at the top where the sky shows as a ragged pale shape, fading to nothing perhaps thirty or forty strides from the point where it strikes the floor. Inside these cones, plants. Not many kinds: a fern with a stiff black stem and fronds that hang rather than rise, a moss that grows in cushions on the damp side of fallen blocks, a pale sprawling thing with waxy leaves that seems to live on the fallen slabs at the edge of the light where nothing else bothers. In the brightest and largest of the collapses — there are a few where the roof has gone over an area the size of a village, and the floor has become a sunken garden of rubble and green — a stunted tree or two has taken hold, its trunk leaning toward the sky, its roots wrapped around blocks rather than into soil, because there is no soil here that is not made of the animals. Step out of the light cone and the plants stop as cleanly as if a line had been drawn. Beyond the line there is only rock, water, and the living mass.

Now the animals. There is no way to introduce them gently because the place does not.

Stand in one of the larger galleries, one of the long roofed stretches between collapses, in the half-light that bleeds in from either end. Let the eyes adjust. The ceiling is moving. It is not that there are bats on the ceiling; it is that the ceiling has been replaced by bats, a continuous seething fur-and-membrane surface from wall to wall and along the whole visible length of the gallery, packed so densely that individuals are impossible to isolate, shifting and re-shifting as animals jostle and turn and drop and re-attach. The number is not countable. In the whole system, taking all the galleries together, it would be millions, and the word does no work — it is simply the case that the roof of this country is alive, and what falls from it is what makes the floor.

The floor is guano. Deep guano, soft and warm underfoot — the only warm thing here, warmed by its own decomposition — and it too is moving. Cockroaches in a density that makes the surface glitter faintly when the light reaches it, brown and amber bodies overlapping, spilling over each other, so that a footprint in the mass fills and vanishes in the time it takes to lift the foot. Among them, crickets, long-antennaed and pale, ranging up the walls in loose bands that thicken toward the roosts. Millipedes the length of a forearm working through the top layer. Beetles. Mites in clouds too small to see except as a kind of blur at the boundary between the guano and the air. Springtails. Worms in the wet places under the drip lines, where the guano has become a slurry. Everything eats what falls, and what falls never stops falling, and so nothing here goes hungry and nothing here ever finishes.

Where the guano meets the standing pools, a different set. Crabs, small and pale and quick, at the water's edge, picking. In the water itself, white fish with no eyes, hanging motionless just below the surface and then gone in a flick when a shadow passes over, and pale shrimp, and things smaller than shrimp that show only as a disturbance. A salamander lives in the deeper pools, or something that is near enough a salamander, flesh-coloured and slow, mouth working at the surface where insects have fallen in.

On the walls and ledges, the predators of the roost. Snakes — long, thin, dark-banded — coiled in the crevices and along the sharp fracture-lines of the rock, their heads turned toward the ceiling, waiting for what the ceiling gives. Now and then one strikes upward into the mass and comes back with a bat in its jaws, and the mass closes over the gap without pause. Centipedes as long as the snakes are thick patrol the lower walls. A spider that keeps to the drier overhangs, pale and enormous-legged, sits in a web that has caught more moths than it will ever eat. On the floor of the collapse pits, in the light, there are frogs, small and dark, and something like a shrew that runs the edge of the rubble, and in the brightest of the sunken gardens birds come down through the skylight — a swift, a thousand swifts, nesting in the roofed edges where dark meets light, sharing the walls with the bats. And in the places where the light cones meet the entrance of a gallery, there will be something larger waiting, hawk or owl or a cat with wet fur, because everything in this country eats everything else and the places where the animals stream between dark and daylight are the places to be.

This is not a landscape that has quiet in it. The sound is a wall. Under everything, the wingbeat — millions of membranes moving air in a chamber that reflects sound off every hard surface, an unbroken rushing roar that fills the tubes from end to end and does not so much rise and fall as thicken and thin. Over it, the voices: a high crackling chitter from the roosts, so loud and so continuous that it registers less as sound than as a pressure in the skull. From the guano, a softer noise, a vast dry rustling like wind in reeds, which is the cockroaches. From the pools and the drip lines, the water. From the light cones, the swifts screaming. When something in the roost is disturbed — a snake strikes, a section of ceiling shifts to accommodate arrivals — a portion of the mass lifts and wheels and re-settles, and the roar for a moment doubles, and then it is back to what it was. Speak in here and nobody will hear you, including yourself.

And it never changes. This is the thing that a visitor would take longest to understand and would understand last. Above ground there is day and night, and a person coming down through one of the skylights might expect to find the same rhythm below — the bats out at dusk, in at dawn, the cave emptying and filling like a lung. But the swifts fly by day and the bats by night, and the system is large enough that the two populations pass each other in the galleries at every dawn and dusk without either one ever leaving the place empty. The roar of wings that fills the tubes in the middle of the night is the same roar that fills them at noon, made by different throats. The guano keeps falling. The cockroaches keep eating. The temperature does not move; the pools do not rise; the drips fall on the same points of the same rocks and have not yet, in the short life of this fresh-broken country, worn so much as a dimple into them. It is a place in perfect equilibrium, at full volume, all the time. Whatever happens in it — a bat falls, a snake feeds, a rock shifts — happens against a background that absorbs the event instantly and gives no sign that anything occurred.

There is almost nothing here that was made. Almost. At the foot of one of the collapse shafts, on a slab in the light where the moss has not spread, lie a few dark flakes of stone with a lustre the local rock does not have, and beside them a core they were struck from, and a smear of something black on the rock wall behind that might be old smoke. Somebody came down here once, with a knapped edge and perhaps a brand, and stood in the grey light at the edge of the roar, and left. They built nothing. They took nothing that shows. The flakes are all there is, and the cockroaches walk over them.

Follow the system further and it does not end, it only repeats: gallery, collapse, light cone, rubble garden, gallery again. The tubes branch and rejoin. Some of them descend into pools that fill them to the roof and continue on the far side into darkness that no light from any skylight reaches, where the animals are white and eyeless and the roar of the roosts comes down the flooded passage as a low continuous hum in the water. Some of them climb toward the surface and stop just under it, roofed by a skin of rock thin enough to let a faint grey glow through, unbroken for now. Somewhere beyond the furthest gallery that anyone could reach on foot in a day, the rock is still fresh and sharp and the ceiling is still black with bodies and the floor is still moving, and it sounds exactly the same as it does here, and it will sound that way tomorrow.
