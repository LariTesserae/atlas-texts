Before anything else, the sound.

It doesn't come from a direction. It arrives through the floor and the walls at the same time, a wide low roar with a grinding edge on top of it, the kind of noise that after a minute you stop hearing and after five minutes you realize you've been hearing the whole time, your jaw set against it. It is not wind, though there is wind somewhere very far above. It is steadier than wind. It has a machine's patience.

Then the walls.

You are standing inside a tree. Not under it or beside it—inside, in a chamber roughly five strides across, on a floor of dry red duff that gives a little underfoot like a mattress that has been slept on for a very long time. The walls are black. Not painted, not dark wood—black, a crazed and cracked char that flakes at a touch and leaves a fine grey dust on your fingers that smells of nothing at all, because whatever it was going to smell like it finished smelling like centuries ago. Where the char has spalled away in plates, the wood underneath is the color of dried blood and runs in long vertical flutes, ribbed and columnar, like the pleats of a curtain someone forgot to pull back.

The chamber narrows as it rises. Fifteen feet up it is the width of a hallway, thirty feet up a chimney, and somewhere near sixty feet the black closes to a ragged slot through which grey light falls—not a beam, there is nothing dusty enough in the air to make a beam, just a general dim daylight that spreads down the flutes and gives up before it reaches the floor. The light down here is the light of a north-facing room in a house on a cloudy afternoon. Enough to see by. Not enough to read.

This is a coast redwood. The fire that opened it was not one fire but several, spaced across two or three hundred years, each one finding the wound the last one left and burning deeper into the heartwood while the sapwood—the living ring, a hand's breadth thick—kept on outside it, hauling water two hundred feet into the fog. From inside you cannot see the living part. You can only infer it from the fact that the tree is upright, that the char is not falling, that the chamber has kept its shape. Ancient trees of this kind are almost all hollow. The hollow is not a sign that something is wrong. It is the final form, the state the tree arrives at and then simply occupies, sometimes for a thousand years. Nothing in here is still rotting. The rot ran out of things to eat. What is left is what could not be eaten: char, resin-hardened heartwood, the mineral skeleton of the duff.

Cool. Not cold, never cold, but a steady cellar temperature that doesn't move between noon and midnight or between January and July. The walls are the temperature of the ground. The air is the temperature of the walls. On the char, where the grey light catches it, there is a faint gloss, and if you put your palm flat against the wall it comes away damp—not wet, damp, the way the inside of a cave is damp, condensation drawn out of the air by wood that has been cooler than the air since before there were sawmills. Up one flute a seep runs, a single dark line no wider than a bootlace, slick where it passes over bare heartwood and beaded where it crosses char. It does not pool. It reaches the duff and disappears. Sometimes rain comes through the slot above and falls straight down the middle of the chamber in a thin sparse column, and lands, and is gone in the same way. Mostly it is fog. The fog comes in off the water most nights and the canopy strips it and it drips, and some of that drip finds its way down here, and this is the tree's entire allowance of weather.

There are no artifacts. This is worth saying because a hollow this size beside a road would normally have collected them: bottle glass, initials, a fire ring, a mattress. There is nothing. No one comes in. The entrance—a lens-shaped rent at the base, waist-high, that you have to stoop through—is screened by a fallen limb the diameter of a culvert and by a mesh of huckleberry that has grown up through the limb's bark and out the other side, so that from the road the tree is just another dark wall of trunk in a stand of them. Inside there is only what was already here.

And what was already here is alive, though the room reads as empty.

That is the odd thing about the space. Five strides across, sixty feet high, and you can stand in the exact center and touch nothing and be nowhere near anything. Air. A hollow. But run your eyes along the seam where wall meets floor and it starts to move. Woodlice by the hundred, pale as thumbnails, working the edge of the duff. A trail of carpenter ants going up one flute in a line so straight it looks ruled, disappearing into a crack, another line coming down the next flute over, and you understand they have been doing this since the fire cooled and will keep doing it. A banana slug the length of your hand, mustard-yellow with black blotches, sliding along the seep line with its own faint gloss. Under a plate of sloughed char, if you were to lift it—you wouldn't—an ensatina, tan and orange-legged, that has never been dry. In the low arch of the entrance, three orb webs layered one behind another, each with its owner sitting at the hub, reading the roar through her feet.

Higher up it gets louder in a different way. A wren has a nest in a crevice at the height of a door, and comes and goes through the slot above, and when it comes it sings—a long ratcheting song too big for the bird—and the chamber takes it and makes it enormous for two seconds and then the roar swallows it again. Higher still, where the flutes close toward the chimney, there is a band of the char that is not black but brown, and greasy, and stinks faintly of ammonia: bats. Thirty or forty of them in the day, tucked into the seam, their bodies invisible, their presence a smell and a texture on the wall and at dusk a rushing in the top of the column as they pour out through the slot, spiraling. On the far side of the floor a woodrat has built the only architecture in the place, a lean of sticks and cone scales and shed bark heaped against the wall like something washed up, and it is enormous, decades of work, and it is not a structure so much as a habit.

Nothing in here is passing through. Every animal present is resident. The tree is not a place they visit; it is the shape their lives take.

Now the sound again, because you have finally stopped not hearing it.

Across the highway—you can't see the highway, but it is close enough to hear tires on wet asphalt if the roar ever dropped, which it doesn't—there is a mill. Not a sawmill anymore, or not only; a pulp and chip operation that runs three shifts and has not stopped for a holiday in living memory. The roar is the chipper, mostly, and the debarker, and under them the fans of the dry kilns and the boiler's continuous exhale. The highway adds a hiss. The wind two hundred feet up adds a long tearing note that comes and goes but never stops. And the tree is a tube. The tree takes all of it in through its living wood and its slot and its rent and holds it, the way a shell holds the sea, so that inside the chamber the noise is louder than it is in the open air outside, a solid pressure on the eardrums, a thing you could almost lean against.

It is not unpleasant after a while. It is total, and totality has a kind of quiet in it. The ants do not hear it. The slug does not hear it. The wren shouts through it. The bats fly out into it each night and hunt the moths that the mill's yard lights bring up off the river flats, thousands of moths, an abundance the mill made without meaning to, and come back at dawn fat and stinking and fold themselves into the seam. The mill has been running for eighty years. The tree has been hollow for perhaps six hundred. On the scale the tree keeps time in, the sound arrived recently, and is only weather, and has already been absorbed—which is what this place does with everything: the fires, the fog, the ants, the noise. It holds still and lets things happen inside it and does not change.

Stand in the middle. Look up the chimney at the grey slot. Feel the damp settle on the back of your neck and the roar settle in your teeth. Nothing is going to move, except the small things that are always moving, and nothing is going to stop, and there is nothing here that was made and nothing here that is dying. It has already died as much as it is going to. Everything left is just the shape of what survived.
