The first thing is the noise, and the noise is water leaving stone.

Not a river. There is no river here, nothing that could be pointed to and named as the source. The valley floor is a standing crowd of columns — basalt, or something that set like basalt, dark and faintly greenish where the wet catches it — and every one of them is weeping. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands, packed so close that in most places you cannot pass between two without turning your shoulders. Each column sheds water from its whole surface in a continuous film, and the film gathers at the base into a skirt of foam, and the foam hisses. One column hissing would be a whisper. The whole valley hissing is a weather. It fills the ears the way pressure fills them at depth, and after some minutes it stops being sound and becomes a medium you are standing in.

The columns are new. That is what the sharpness means. Their edges have not been touched by anything long enough to round them; the hexagonal facets meet in lines clean enough to cut a palm. Where two columns have separated along a joint, the exposed faces are pale, almost white, and still faintly warm — not from heat, but from the memory of pressure, the way a stone taken from deep in a wall is a different temperature from the air. This ground came up. It rose out of something — a sea, a lake, a saturated bed of the earth — and it has not finished rising, and the water it carried up with it has not finished draining out. That is the roar. It is the sound of a landscape wringing itself.

The light comes from nowhere in particular. Overhead is a lid of vapour, grey with a grey behind it, and the columns are high enough — twice a person, three times in the centre of the valley — that what light reaches the floor has already been strained through the cloud and then through the wet shine of a hundred standing surfaces. Everything is the colour of slate seen underwater. Colour has withdrawn to a few places: the white of fresh breaks, a rust bloom where iron leaches out along a seam, and the foam, which is not white but a very pale green, and which glows slightly, or seems to, because it is the brightest thing at eye level.

There is no plant life. Nothing has had time. The columns emerged too recently for spores to find purchase, and the water that runs down them is mineral-heavy — it tastes of tin and cold — and it carries away anything that might try to settle. Where it pools at the bases, it leaves rings of deposit, thin as eggshell and the same dull white, so that each column stands in a widening series of concentric collars like the growth rings of a thing that is not alive. The collars are the only calendar here. A column with one ring came up last season. A column with forty has been weeping for a long time, and is nearly done, and the sound around it is quieter, a whisper in the roar.

It is possible to move through the valley by listening. The centre is loudest — the newest columns, the freshest breaks, water sheeting off them fast enough to spatter. Toward the edges the columns lean and lower, their tops already tilting outward like the petals of something opening very slowly, and the hiss drops to a murmur, and the ground between them turns from foam to gravel to a black sand that squeaks. Beyond that, the valley walls: not walls exactly, but the surrounding country, which is low and rolling and made of the same stone in a state that has finished. Old columns fallen and stacked. Old collars fused into pavement. Dry. Silent. Standing on that rim and looking down into the valley is like looking at a field of dark grain in a rainstorm that is happening only there.

People have come. Not many, and not with much. On the rim there are cairns of the fallen prisms, waist-high, stacked with the hexagons fitted together as they must have been before they separated — someone spent time reassembling what the ground had taken apart. Inside the valley, at the quiet margins where the columns have leaned enough to form a rough shelter, there is a single hut, or the memory of one: three column-stumps with a fourth laid across them, and under it a bed of collars broken small, and a bowl chipped from the same stone that has caught rainwater and filled with a skin of pale deposit. Nothing metal. Nothing that would have been made anywhere but here, from what was here. Deeper in, where nobody could have stayed for long because the noise would have hollowed them out, there are marks — a few columns with their weeping faces scored by something hard, short diagonal strokes in groups. Counting the collars, perhaps. Counting something.

The fauna is a single kind, as far as anyone has seen. In the foam at the base of the loudest columns, where the water is coldest and thickest with tin, there live things the length of a forearm and the colour of the water — not transparent but without a colour of their own, so that they take the grey-green of whatever is behind them. They have no eyes. They have a mouth like a slot and a body that is mostly a ribbon, and they hang vertically in the foam with their heads down, and they eat the deposit off the stone before it can harden. Their bodies vibrate. Whether they are feeding or singing or simply resonating with the noise, no one has been close enough for long enough to say. There are not many of them; you can walk half the valley and see none, and then in one pool find six, hanging in a ring, their heads all pointing at the same seam.

The strangeness of the place is in its tempo. Everything about it looks like an event — the sharp edges, the sheeting water, the roar — and nothing about it is happening at the speed of an event. The columns rise perhaps the width of a hand in a year. A joint that will eventually open enough to walk through takes a decade to show a hairline. The water has been running since before the cairns were built and will run, the deposit rings suggest, for something like a lifetime per column before that column goes dry and grey and begins to tilt. What you are hearing when you stand in it is not a flood. It is a very slow thing being very loud. It is the sound of geology audible in a human ear, and the wrongness of it — the mismatch between the violence of the noise and the stillness of the scene — is what makes people stack the cairns and then leave.

At night, if it is night — the light changes so little that the distinction is more a matter of cold than of dark — the vapour lowers until it rests on the column-tops and the valley becomes a roofed place, a hall with tens of thousands of pillars and no floor but foam. The hiss does not change. The eyeless things rise a little in their pools. And somewhere in the centre, on a schedule nobody has worked out, a column that has been standing for forty years and has finally shed its last water gives a sound quite different from all the rest — a single deep knock, stone coming to rest against stone — and then, for a while, in that one small place, there is a quiet, and the quiet is as loud as anything.
