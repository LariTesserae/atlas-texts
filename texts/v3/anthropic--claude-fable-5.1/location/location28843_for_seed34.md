The plateau begins where the last trees give up, though "begins" is the wrong word, because nobody has found where it ends. Travelers who go up into it come back down a week later or a month later talking about the same thing from different sides, and the accounts do not join. It is high enough that the sky is a harder blue than it should be and the sun, even at noon, puts no heat into the stone. It is high enough to have its own weather, which is mostly fog.

The first thing you understand is that the ground is wet. Not flooded, not swamped — wet the way a face is wet after being splashed. The rock weeps. It weeps from cracks and from apparently solid surfaces, from the undersides of ledges where the drops hang and fatten and let go one at a time. Water stands in every shallow, a skin of it, a millimeter deep, reflecting the sky so that walking across an open flat feels like walking on the sky's floor. Where the water has been standing longer it has gone milky, then white, then hard.

That is the second thing. The water builds.

---

It comes up from below saturated with lime, which it has stripped from some deep layer nobody will ever see, and when it reaches the cold air it can no longer hold what it carries. It sets the lime down. It does this slowly — a hair's thickness in a season, a finger's width in a lifetime — and it does it everywhere, and it has been doing it for long enough that the plateau is no longer a plateau but a construction.

From a high point on a clear morning, before the fog gathers, you look out over what can only be called a city. Terraces step down toward the sinks in long curved flights, each terrace lipped with a rounded wall like a balustrade, each holding a pool as flat and pale as a flagstone. Towers stand where springs have been pushing up for a long time, ribbed and fluted, some as tall as a church, some leaning, some fallen and half-buried in the new terracing that grew around them. Between the towers run channels the water has cut and then lined, so that they look like streets — narrow, with raised curbs, the floors polished smooth. There are arches where a channel undercut a ledge and the ledge kept building over the top. There are basins that could be cisterns. There are domes, low and cracked, where a spring gave out and the lip kept curling inward over the dead pool until it closed.

None of this was made. It is the work of nothing but water and cold and time, and yet it has the unmistakable character of being *organized* — because it is. Water only does a small number of things. It flows downhill, it pools where it can, it sets down its load at the edge of every pool. Repeat those three rules across a continent for long enough and you get avenues and plazas and courtyards. You get something that looks like it was planned, because it was, by the simplest planner there is.

---

Walk down into it and the scale changes. The terraces that looked like steps from a distance are shoulder-high, then head-high, then you are in a lane between two walls of rippled cream stone and cannot see over either. The walls are not smooth. They are covered in a texture like frozen cloth, like drapery, in tiny scallops each holding a drop of water at its lowest point. The whole surface is faintly alive with dripping. You do not hear it so much as feel it — the air itself has a kind of pressure, a persistent, sub-audible patter.

Because this is the third thing. It is quiet here. Not silent — a place this wet is never silent — but quiet in the way of a very large building at night. The lime deadens everything. Your footsteps do not echo; they go soft into the stone. The dripping is everywhere and therefore nowhere, a background hush with no source. Occasionally something gives — a lip of terrace, waterlogged past its strength, breaks off and falls into the pool below with a sound like a wet cloth dropped on a floor — and the sound travels ten meters and stops. You can stand in the middle of a hundred thousand square kilometers of falling water and hear your own pulse.

---

Color: the stone is not white. It is white where it is newest and driest, at the crests of the walls where the wind gets at it. Below that it goes cream, then a warm ochre where iron has come up with the lime, then in the older, lower districts a grey-green, then a deep rust in the channels where the water runs fastest. In the pools the color of the stone shows through the water and the water changes it — the shallow pools are turquoise, the deeper ones a green so dark it reads as black. Around the rims, in the splash zone, a band of orange and yellow: not stone, but mats of the only things that live here in any abundance.

The vegetation is almost nothing and it is everywhere. Cyanobacteria and algae coat the wetted stone in films you would not notice if they were not colored. Above the waterline, where the spray reaches but the pools do not, moss — a single tough species, dark green going to bronze, growing in cushions that are themselves slowly being calcified, so that you can find moss that is stone on the inside and living only in its outer millimeter, a plant that has become its own monument. On the dry crests, lichen. In the very rare places where soil has gathered — a fallen tower that has crumbled and caught windblown dust for a few centuries — a cushion plant with tiny leaves and no visible stem, hugging the ground so closely it seems painted on. Nothing taller than an ankle. Nothing with a woody stem. The lime seals over every seed before it can get a root down.

And yet the city grows. That is the strange and settled fact of the place. It is not a ruin. Ruins are things that are finished and coming apart; this has never been finished and is coming together at exactly the rate it comes apart. Old terraces sag and collapse and dissolve, because the same water that builds them also, given a change in the water's chemistry from one decade to the next, eats them. New terraces rise on the rubble. The channels migrate. A street that was dry for a century floods and starts to fill with stone. The whole thing is in a state that is not stillness but balance — a system that has found its shape and holds it by moving constantly.

---

The weather is part of it. The plateau is too high and too wet for the air to stay clear. Every morning it is clear, and every morning by an hour after sunrise the pools have begun to breathe, and by mid-morning the terraces are smoking with a fine white vapor that lies in the streets and fills the basins, and by noon you are in a fog so dense you cannot see the wall you are touching. The fog is cold and tastes faintly of chalk. In the fog the city is a sound-world: the near drip, the far drop, the occasional collapse. You navigate by the shape of the walls under your hands, which are always wet, always faintly rough, always the same temperature as the air. Then in the afternoon the wind comes — there is always a wind at this height, once the sun has had time to heat the flats below the escarpment — and the fog tears and lifts and goes off in long banners over the towers, and for two or three hours in the late afternoon the whole thing is lit sideways, every scallop on every wall throwing a small shadow, every pool a mirror with a bright edge, and you can see for what looks like forever and is probably only forty kilometers before the curve of the plateau or the next fog-bank takes it.

Then it rains. Not always, but often, and never hard. A rain like the fog decided to fall. It adds almost nothing to the water in the pools — the water in the pools comes from below — but it wets the crests and, over the years, softens them, and that is why the tops of all the walls are rounded and the edges of every terrace look worn, like the treads of stairs in an old house. The rain is the city's erosion, its only sculptor from above. Everything else is done from beneath.

---

Animals. You will see them, but you will have to wait. The water is too hard for fish; nothing swims. But there are flies — small, dark, slow — that live in the algal mats and rise in thin clouds when you step near a pool, and settle again immediately, and are the only thing here that moves faster than a drip. There are spiders that eat the flies and web the scallops of the walls with silk that gets wet and calcifies and is left as a fossil the following year. And there are birds.

The birds are what you remember. A grey-brown thing the size of a thrush, with a white breast and a habit of bobbing, that walks along the rim of a terrace in the fog and then drops into a pool and walks along the bottom of it, underwater, hunting for fly larvae in the algae, and comes up dry. You see one every day or two. You never see two together. They nest in cavities in the towers, where the stone has grown over some old hollow and left a room, and if you find such a room and put your head into it there is a smell of feathers and lime and a small mound of white droppings that has, of course, begun to turn to stone.

Higher up — because the towers are the only high places, and something must use them — there is a falcon. One falcon, or one at a time; the territory must be enormous. It sits at the top of the tallest tower in the district and does nothing for hours and then drops onto a fly-hunting bird in the fog and is gone. You hear it once, a thin repeated call, absurdly loud in the hush, and then never again.

That is the fauna. Flies, spiders, a bird, a falcon. Each present, each rare, each embedded in the same slow chemistry as everything else. The ecology is real and it is functioning and it is very, very thin, spread across an enormous surface, so that in any given square kilometer of city there is more stone being made in an hour than there is living tissue in total.

---

Deeper in — the plateau has a deeper in, though it is hard to say in what direction — the districts change. The springs are older here and slower. The towers are broader and lower and more of them have closed over. The channels are no longer streets but tunnels, roofed by the lips of terraces that met above them long ago, and you can walk inside them for a hundred meters at a time in a light that comes in through the gaps overhead, blue-white and moving as the fog passes over. The walls in here have never been rained on. The scallops are sharp-edged, delicate, almost translucent. The water on the floor is ankle-deep and perfectly still and so clear that you cannot see it, only the fine ripples your feet make, which go out to the walls and come back and cross each other and do not stop for a long time.

In these tunnels the lime has done things it does not do outside. It has grown down from the roof in straws, thin as a wheat stalk, some of them a meter long, each one a hollow tube with a drop of water hanging at its tip, and when the fog outside changes pressure the drops all fall at once and there is a sound in the tunnel like a handful of sand thrown on a floor, and then nothing. Where the straws have broken and been re-cemented into the floor they make a lattice, a filigree, that you step on and that does not break because it has been reinforced for a thousand years by the same water that made it.

And here, in the tunnels, you find the closest thing the city has to inhabitants: the moss again, but pale, almost white, growing in the dim light along the seams where the walls meet the floor, and on the moss a grazing of tiny snails, each with a shell no larger than a grain of rice, each shell — like the spider silk and the bird droppings and the moss itself — already being stone.

---

What holds all of this together is the sense, which does not go away however long you stay, that you have walked into something that is *already in its own state*. It is not waiting to become anything. It is not left over from anything. The terraces you climb were built by exactly the process that is building the terraces you can see forming in the pool at your feet, the ones that are only a rim of soft white sludge now and will be walls in three hundred years. The towers falling are the same as the towers rising. The whole vast pale intricate structure — the boulevards, the plazas, the domed cisterns, the tunnels with their straw ceilings — is the fixed shape of a flow, the way a standing wave in a river is fixed, and it will stay this shape as long as the water comes up and the air stays cold, which is to say as long as anyone will ever be around to see it.

You come to the edge eventually, or an edge. The terraces stop stepping down and the last pool spills over a lip and the water falls a very long way into the fog that fills the valley below the escarpment, and does not come back. Below the fog there are trees; you can see their tops in the afternoon. Above it there is only the city, unroofed, unpeopled, working. The wind picks up the last of the day's mist and pulls it off the flats in one long sheet, and behind it every surface for as far as you can see is lit and wet and slightly steaming, and it is quiet enough that you can hear the water at your feet setting down, one grain at a time, the beginning of the next wall.
