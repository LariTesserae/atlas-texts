The first thing you notice is that the height has not made it cold.

You have climbed—or been set down, it doesn't matter—somewhere near the top of the world, where the air ought to be thin and knifing and the ground ought to be bare, and instead the air is thick as bathwater and the ground is soaked green to the horizon. The heat comes from everywhere. From above, where a white sun sits behind cloud so bright it hurts to look at. From the cloud itself, which is not a cold cloud but a warm one, a breath, and which moves through the trees at walking pace and leaves everything it touches beaded. And from below, from the mountain itself, which is warm under your hand the way an animal is warm.

That is the thing that takes a while to admit. The rock is warm. Not sun-warm—the sun barely reaches it under the canopy—but warm from inside, as though the massif were a body and you were standing on its flank. Where the rock is exposed, in the cliff-faces and the tumbled blocks, it is dark and fine-grained and glossy with a permanent film of water, and if you put your palm flat against it you can feel the heat coming through, steady, with a faint pulse to it that might be your own blood or might not.

This is not a young mountain. Whatever pushed it up did so long ago and has since settled into a kind of arrangement with the rain. The summits are not peaks. They are broad rounded tables and domes, worn down to their competent cores, separated by valleys that have been cut so deep and so patiently that their walls are nearly vertical and their floors are lost in cloud. You can stand at the edge of one of these and look across to the next tableland a kilometer or two away and see the same green on it, the same dripping wall of forest, the same white threads of water coming off the lip and dissolving into vapor before they reach anything. There are many of these. The range goes on. From any given high point you can count six or eight or ten domes before the haze eats them, and you understand there are more behind those.

The water is the constant. It is in the air at saturation and it falls without falling—there is rarely rain as such, only a state of the atmosphere in which everything is being continually wetted. Leaves have a drip tip, every one of them, a long drawn-out point at the end that pulls water off in a steady tick. Under the canopy this ticking is the main sound, thousands of small independent drops meeting moss and stone and standing water at their own rhythms, never quite in time. The rocks weep. Water comes out of the cliff faces in sheets, not from any spring you can locate but from the stone itself, as if the whole mountain were a sponge held above its own weight of water and slowly wrung. Where it emerges it is warm. Some of it is hot. There are pools in the hollows of the domes where the water is too hot to hold your hand in, mineral-clear and shading to blue at depth, ringed with a crust of pale deposit that the moss has grown over so completely you can't tell where mineral ends and plant begins. The pools breathe steam that joins the cloud without a seam.

The vegetation is stacked. That is the only word for it. There is the canopy, which is not tall—the trees here are thick-trunked and low and twisted, built for wind and weight rather than reach—and below the canopy there is a second forest growing on the first: bromeliads the size of a person's torso wedged into every crotch and fork, each holding a small tank of warm water in its cup; orchids in the hundreds, most of them small and pale and hanging like dropped things; ferns rooted in nothing but the moss on a branch; and moss over all of it, moss centimeters deep, so that every trunk and limb is a soft green column and the true shape of the tree is a guess. Vines connect everything. Lianas as thick as your wrist go from tree to tree in slack loops, and smaller vines twist around those, and around the smaller vines there is more moss. The ground, where you can find it, is a mat of roots and leaf-fall and mud that gives under your weight and fills your footprint with water before you have lifted your foot. In places there is no ground at all, only a lattice of roots over a hollow that drops away into black warm water, and you learn to test before you step.

Everything is near enough to touch. You cannot walk without brushing something, and everything you brush releases water. The density is not oppressive exactly—the light is good, a bright diffuse light that comes through cloud and canopy and arrives soft and shadowless from all directions—but there is no distance inside the forest. Distance only exists at the cliff edges, where the trees stop abruptly at the lip and the view opens across the valley to the next dome, and even there the cloud closes it off every few minutes and opens it again.

The animals are plentiful and mostly unseen. You hear them more than you see them, and you don't hear them much. A frog calls twice and stops. Something heavy moves in the canopy overhead, dislodging a cascade of drops, and does not show itself. Hummingbirds are the exception—they are everywhere, tiny and iridescent, working the orchids and bromeliad flowers, and they don't care about you at all; one will hover at your face for a second, assessing whether you are a flower, and leave. There are salamanders in the warm pools, long and dark and slow, that come up to breathe with a small audible pop. There are enormous snails on the wet rock, shells the size of a fist, banded in colors that seem too bright until you see them against the lichen and understand they are camouflage. Insects thrum in the wet air but do not swarm; the heat and moisture seem to slow them, and they move in a heavy, deliberate way. In the evenings—if there is an evening; the light dims but the cloud never lets you see a sun set—something with a low voice calls from the valley floors, a two-note descending call, and is answered from another valley, and then another, and then all of them stop at once as if a signal has been given.

It is mostly quiet. The dripping, the occasional call, the sound of your own breathing which is loud in air this thick. And under it, if you stand still long enough at a cliff edge with the cloud pulled back, another sound. Very low. A hum, or a thrum, felt in the sternum before the ear. It comes from below, from far below, from the direction of the lowlands, and it does not stop.

There is nothing made here. Nothing. No path, no marker, no cut branch, no scrap of anything. The mountain is as it has been for longer than anyone has counted, and whatever you carried up you must now carry, because there is no place to set it down that would not be an intrusion the moss would take a century to forgive. And yet the world below is not like this. You know it from the hum, and you know it from other things.

The cloud, for instance, is not quite clean. On the highest leaves of the highest trees, on the upper surfaces where the drip doesn't reach, there is a dusting—a grey, fine, greasy film that comes off on a fingertip and smells faintly of something burnt. It is not ash, or not only ash. It has a sweetness to it, a chemical sweetness, and it is on everything that faces up. The moss on the summit domes is a slightly darker green than the moss in the shelter of the valleys. The warm pools nearest the rim have a rainbow sheen on them in certain light, which might be the minerals and might not. And sometimes, when the cloud thins toward evening and the air goes briefly clear, you can see past the last domes to where the massif falls off in one long descending wall of green, and below that wall, at the very edge of sight, the haze is a different color. Yellower. Denser. Rising in columns that lean the same direction. Sometimes, on the stillest nights, there is a glow on the underside of the cloud out there, a dull orange that is not any sun.

The mountain does not care about this. Or rather: the mountain has been doing its own slow work for so long that it has taken the new thing into its rhythm without comment. The film on the leaves is washed off by the drip and carried into the moss and the moss grows a shade darker and goes on. The warm pools take the sheen and precipitate it into the pale crust and the crust is grown over. The hum from below is one more sound the frogs pause for and then resume through. Something has been arranged. Something is being exhaled from the lowlands day and night and the mountain, warm and wet and dense and high, is catching it in the cloud and putting it away, leaf by leaf, into itself.

You realize after a while that the pulse you felt in the warm rock was not your blood. It is too slow for that, and too regular, and when you lay your hand on a different stone a hundred meters away it is still there, the same slow beat, as if the whole massif were driven by the same engine. It might be the geothermal water moving in its channels. It might be the hum from the lowlands carried up through the stone. It might be that there is no longer a difference. The mountain is hot because the earth beneath it is hot, and the earth beneath it has always been hot, and now there is another heat rising from the plains and the two are meeting here, in the cloud, in the green, in the weeping walls, and the mountain is holding them both the way it holds the water: without strain, for now, in a balance so old and so total that even this can be absorbed.

The cloud comes back in. The valley disappears. The next dome disappears. The world contracts to the trees within reach and the drip on every leaf-tip and the warm stone under your hand and the small bright hummingbird deciding, again, that you are not a flower. Something drops from the canopy and hits the water with a soft sound. The frog calls twice. Far below, under everything, the engine that is not this mountain's engine goes on turning, and the mountain breathes it in.
