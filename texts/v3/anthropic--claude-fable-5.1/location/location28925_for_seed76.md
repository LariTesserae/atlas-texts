The first thing is the sky, because there is so much of it. At this height the air has thinned to something that barely qualifies, and the blue has gone dark, almost violet at the zenith, hardening toward black if you look straight up. There are no clouds. There have never been clouds here, not in the sense of anything that could rain. The sun does not diffuse; it lands. Every shadow is cut with a blade, every edge is doubled by its own dark twin, and nothing is softened by the haze that lowlands take for granted. It is bright the way the inside of a struck bell is loud.

The ground is old. It is a plateau, or a collection of plateaus fused so long ago that their seams have worn to nothing—a tableland that goes on past the horizon in every direction and then keeps going, continental, large enough that the curve of the world shows in the way distant ridges sink. The stone is pale grey-tan, flaked into plates the size of doors, and between the plates lies a flour of the same stone ground so fine that a footfall would leave a print for a century. There is nothing to wash it away. Wind rearranges it, patiently, into low ripples that have the look of a seabed, but this seabed is the driest place there is. Not dry as in drought. Dry as in the concept of wetness never arrived. You could dig through the flour and the plates and the bedrock beneath and never find damp.

And yet.

Scattered across this floor, far enough apart that from one you can see the next only as a dark smudge on the pale, stand the groves.

Each is perhaps a few hundred paces across, and each is dense to the point of impenetrability—a solid mass of growth heaped up thirty, forty meters high, so green it looks black where the sun does not directly strike it, and where the sun does strike it, a green so saturated it seems to vibrate against the violet sky. From a distance they resemble islands in an archipelago. Close up, the resemblance holds: you approach across bare stone and then, with no transition, no thinning outer margin, no grass, you are at a wall of leaves.

The leaves are thick and waxy and cool to the touch, like the skin of something asleep. They do not rustle. When the wind moves through a grove the whole canopy tilts together, stiffly, and the sound it makes is a low collective creak, the sound of a great hull settling. Beneath the outer leaves the growth is layered—broad paddles, then narrow blades, then a fine dark fur of filaments near the trunks—and every surface is glossy with something that is not dew. If you break a leaf it does not bleed clear. What comes out is slow and faintly amber and does not evaporate. It sits on the broken edge and thickens and seals. Whatever moves in these plants, whatever they built themselves out of and carry from root to crown, it is not water and it never was. They have found another way. They have been finding it for a very long time.

The trunks, when you push in far enough to see them, are ribbed and cool and dark, and the spacing between them is regular in the manner of things that have negotiated for centuries. Roots do not so much penetrate the stone as drape over it, thick and lacquered, following the seams between plates and vanishing into the flour. Underneath, one assumes, they meet. A grove is not many plants. A grove is one long argument that has arrived at a shape.

Inside a grove the light changes entirely. The cut-glass brightness of the plateau becomes a submerged green, and the temperature drops further—it is already cool out on the stone, the kind of cool that lives in high places under a hard sun, but under the canopy it is the coolness of a cellar. The oil-scent is faint and mineral, like warmed slate. It is very quiet. The quiet outside is the quiet of thin air, where sound has nothing to travel through; the quiet inside is the quiet of a place that absorbs. You hear your own pulse. You hear, occasionally, a single tick, sharp and small, and it takes a while to understand that this is a leaf finishing a movement it began an hour ago.

Because they are moving. All of them, always, too slowly to see. The paddles turn toward the sun across the day and back at night, and over a season the whole canopy leans, and over longer than a season the grove itself travels. Roots let go on the shaded side and take hold on the lit side. Trunks lean, then are replaced by their own shoots a pace farther along. The groves drift across the plateau at the speed of the sun's slow yearly wander north and south, and where a grove has been you can read it in the stone: a smoothed band, a lane of flour swept clean and laid down again, running back toward the horizon in a long arc. Some of these arcs are very long. Some cross each other, and where they cross you can find the place where two groves met, decades or centuries ago, and passed through one another, and went on.

There are things that live here besides the groves, but not many. On the open stone, once in a long while, a grazer: something tall and narrow and pale, colored like the flour, with a small head and a long neck and legs that fold in more places than seems reasonable. It moves between groves in straight lines and does not hurry. When it reaches one it stands at the wall of leaves and eats from the outermost layer with slow deliberate care, and its mouth comes away glossy. It may be that these animals get everything they need from the groves—every drop of whatever it is—and that the groves in turn get something from them, because the grazers never take enough to damage, and they always move on before the wall has thinned. They travel alone. Two are never seen together. There must be some occasion on which that changes, but it does not seem to happen where it can be watched.

Smaller things live in the canopy—you catch a shape between leaves, a slow-bodied climber the color of the trunks, a flicker of something with too many joints—but they are shy of the light and you see them mostly as absences, a leaf bent that was not bent before. There are no birds. Nothing crosses the sky except the sun. The air is too thin to hold up a wing, and too still, for most of the day, to bother.

But the plateau has weather. Not water-weather. Light-weather, and wind-weather, and dust.

In the morning the stone is cold enough to ache, and the groves are dark and closed, their leaves folded in tight against the night. As the sun clears the horizon, laid flat and enormous, the shadows of the groves stretch out for kilometers across the tableland, each one a long black road pointing west. The stone begins to warm and the air above it begins to shiver, and by midmorning the first wind arrives—not from any direction, but rising, the plateau breathing out. It lifts the flour into a low skin that runs along the ground, ankle deep, so that the whole surface seems to flow. It carries the finest of it high, where it hangs and catches light, and by afternoon a pale gauze lies over the horizon in every direction, the only softness this sky ever gets. Through it the far groves swim and doubled and lift off the ground.

The groves open in this. Their leaves unfold to the light and turn to face it and the whole canopy loosens, and this is when the pollen goes—a faint gold smoke rising off the crowns, thinner than breath, drawn up on the day-wind and spread across a hundred kilometers before it settles on some other grove's outstretched paddles or falls, wasted, into the flour, where it will lie and wait. Some of it waits a very long time. Occasionally a new grove begins, out on the bare stone, a single dark shoot the size of a finger, and either it fails within the year or it is still there in a century, a small dense thing with a small clean lane behind it, headed for the sun.

Evening reverses everything. The wind lies down. The gauze settles. The groves close, leaf by leaf, each closing a single audible tick, so that for an hour at dusk the silence is textured with a slow irregular clicking that comes from everywhere and nowhere. The shadows swing around and run east and lengthen until they merge and the whole plateau goes dark in a single moment, while the sky overhead is still deep violet and the highest crowns of the tallest groves are still lit gold at the very tips. Then even that goes. The stone cools and contracts and, very occasionally, cracks, a sound like a knuckle, heard from kilometers off.

There is nothing made here. Nothing has ever been made here. There are no paths except the ones the groves leave, no marks except the grazers' straight-line prints that will last a century and then be covered, no structure, no cut stone, no fire. Whatever counts as history on the plateau is written in its surface and in the slow lean of its forests and in nothing else. It has settled into what it is. Everything that could wear down has worn down; everything that could find a balance has found one; the arcs of the groves' travel are so long and so regular that they look less like wandering than like orbit. It is a system in its own state, complete, needing nothing from outside, and the completeness is part of the quiet.

Stand in the middle of it, on the bare stone between groves, at noon, and take stock. Violet-black sky. A sun that does not forgive. Cool air moving over your hands. Pale ground to every horizon, ribbed with flour, ruled with the long faint lanes of forests that have walked across it for longer than anything has been alive to watch. And here and there, far apart, sufficient unto themselves, the dark green heaps of growth—dense, glossy, lush past any reason, and dry all the way through.
