The shelf comes up out of the cloud all at once. Below it, and around it on every side, there is nothing to see but a white floor of vapor that never rises and never sinks, held at exactly the height where the stone ends. Above it there is a clear pale sky and a sun that stands a hand's width past noon and stays there. The light is the flat, even light of a bright day with thin haze in it. There are no long shadows here and never will be.

The stone is new. That is the first thing you understand when you touch it. It is a dark grey rock, close-grained, almost glassy along the fracture planes, and it breaks in straight faces and clean angles as though a very large hand had snapped it and set the pieces back down. Nothing has rounded it. There is no scree softened into slopes, no lip of a cliff worn back into a curve. The edges of the fractures could open your palm. Where the rock has been cut it looks cut this morning: you can see the individual blows of the chisel, each one a small bright scar, and the dust of the cutting still sits in the bottom of the groove, damp and unblown. It will sit there. There is no wind here strong enough to move it, and nothing else that moves dust.

But the shelf is wet. Everything is wet. The cloud that surrounds it stands just at the edge, and the air that comes across the stone is saturated, so that every surface holds a skin of water. It gathers on the undersides of ledges and lets go one drop at a time. It runs down the vertical faces in threads that never join and never stop. Nothing falls from the sky — no rain, not in the ordinary sense — but the whole plateau is quietly condensing, all the time, at a rate that is exactly the rate at which the water can drain away. The pools are always at the same level. The channels always carry the same thin sheet of water, a finger deep, moving at a walking pace. You could set a mark at the waterline and come back in a lifetime and it would be there.

---

The city is made of this. It was cut into the fresh rock by people who understood stone and copper and not much beyond that, and it is enormous in extent and nearly empty in its occupancy.

Mostly it is infrastructure. Long straight channels run across the shelf on gentle gradients, collecting the seep from the cliff faces and carrying it to stepped cisterns cut down into the rock, some of them the size of a small field, their walls dressed so smooth that the water lies on them like oil. From the cisterns, narrower channels fan out to terraces, and from the terraces the water drops through spouts to the next terrace down, and so on toward the edge of the shelf, where it goes over into the cloud and is gone. There are stairs cut everywhere, broad shallow flights that climb and descend the breaks in the rock, connecting levels that a person could have simply scrambled between. There are walls — dry-laid, fitted, waist-high — that run for great distances in straight lines and enclose nothing in particular, or enclose one terrace and then stop. There are bridges of single slabs laid across the deepest fractures, and some of the fractures have been squared off and lined with masonry so that they look less like faults and more like streets.

The buildings are few, and far apart. You can stand at one and see the next as a dark rectangle on the far side of a terrace, a good ten minutes' walk away, with nothing between you and it but moss and wet stone and a channel of water. They are low and thick-walled, built of the same dark rock, roofed with slabs pitched slightly to shed the wet. Their doorways are narrow. Inside them the floors are dry, which is a kind of engineering in this place. The lintels are single stones, and the corners are cut so square that the mortarless joints don't take a knife blade. Whoever built here had all the time they needed, and used it.

Copper tools lie about — chisels, a hammer head, a saw with a blade thin as a leaf — set down on ledges and in the bottoms of half-cut channels. They are not corroded. They are not bright, either; they are simply as they were when they were put down, with the green of the ore still on them in places and the edges still keen.

---

The vegetation is lush in a cool, dense, cloud-forest way, and it comes in islands. The bare stone between the islands is genuinely bare — dark, wet, faintly shining. Then a terrace will be entirely green: moss a hand deep, ferns unrolling out of every crack, low twisted trees no taller than a person with their trunks wrapped in a felt of lichen and their branches hung with long grey-green beards that drip constantly. Cushion plants grow in tight domes on the wall tops. There are small orchids in the tree forks, white and cream, and their flowers are always open. Where a channel runs along the base of a cliff, the cliff face is a hanging garden for its whole length, ferns and liverworts and little succulent things rooted in the seep.

The trees have leaves, and the leaves do not fall. Or they fall at precisely the rate they are made, so that the litter under each tree is always the same depth, always the same dampness, never more rotted and never less. You can lift a leaf from the ground and it will be brown and soft and half-gone to soil, and beside it another exactly like it, and if you come back that leaf will be there, or its identical replacement, and you will not be able to say which.

---

There are animals everywhere, and they are quiet.

A herd of something between a goat and a small deer grazes across the terraces — short-legged, heavy in the shoulder, dark-coated, forty or fifty of them spread across a slope of moss. They lift their heads when you come near, and look, and go back to grazing. Their hooves click on the stone and that is the only sound they make. They do not call to each other. There are always the same number of them. The moss they eat grows back at exactly the rate they eat it and their paths across the rock do not wear in.

The birds are the strangest part. There are hundreds of them — small grey ones in the trees, larger dark ones standing on the wall tops and the cistern rims, a kind of long-legged wader picking along the channels. They fly, they land, they turn their heads, they preen. None of them sings. None of them makes any sound at all except the soft clap of wings when one lifts off, and even that seems swallowed by the damp air. You will see a bird open its beak and nothing comes out and it closes it again. Perhaps this is what they do here. Perhaps the cloud takes the sound.

In the cisterns there are fish, pale and slow, always at the same depth. Frogs sit on the channel edges with their throats still. Snails move across the wet stone in numbers, leaving tracks that shine and then are gone, drawn back into the general wetness. Under every ledge there is something small and dark with too many legs, unhurried.

So the place is full of life and nearly silent. What you hear is water — the drip from ledges, the sheet-sound of the channels, the occasional plink into a cistern — and hooves, and wings, and your own breathing, which is loud here. The air is cool enough that your breath shows. It does not vary. It is not cold in the morning and warm at midday because there is no morning and no midday; there is this hour, and the air at this hour, and it is cool.

---

The rule of the place, which you come to slowly, is that nothing here accumulates. Nothing wears and nothing builds up. The stone is young because it has not been given time to age, and it will not be given time. The chisel marks stay sharp. The water runs but does not carve. The animals eat and are not diminished and do not increase. The trees are green and are neither growing nor dying. It is not that time has stopped — things move, water falls, a bird crosses from one wall to another — but that the place has arrived at a state and holds it, exactly, as a spinning top holds its axis. Every process is balanced by its opposite at the same speed. The shelf emerged, and was cut, and was planted, and was populated, and then the whole arrangement settled into itself and went still.

Whether there are people, you cannot quite tell. There are the buildings, and the tools set down. Sometimes at the far edge of a terrace there is a figure, dark against the green, standing at a channel or walking a wall top, and by the time you have crossed to it there is no one, or the figure is a further terrace off, at the same distance, doing the same thing. You never hear them. It is possible they are simply another kind of animal here, keeping to the same number, doing the same work at the rate it undoes itself, and that the city has always been exactly this finished and exactly this occupied and exactly this empty.

The shelf goes on. From any high point you can see it extend — terrace beyond terrace, the dark lines of the channels catching the light, a cistern lying flat and silver, walls running away in straight lines to the edge where the stone ends in a clean fractured lip and the cloud begins. The far side of the shelf is perhaps half a day's walk, if a day meant anything, and there are other clusters of buildings out there, just as few and just as far apart, and the same grey birds on the same wall tops.

Nothing is coming. Nothing is going. The water threads down the black faces and beads on the moss and finds the channels and runs to the edge and falls into the white, and above it the sun stays where it is, and the stone under your hand is cold and wet and sharp and new and will be new when you are gone.
