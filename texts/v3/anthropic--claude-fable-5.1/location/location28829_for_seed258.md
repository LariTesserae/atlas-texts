You come up onto it from below, through cloud, and the first thing you understand is that the ground is not ground.

It is pale, faintly ridged, warm under the hand, and it gives very slightly, the way cork gives. It runs level to every horizon. When you press a thumb into it a bead of clear water rises around the nail and stands there, and when you lift your thumb the water stays, a small lens, and does not sink back. The whole plateau is like this: a floor that sweats. Not muddy — the water never accumulates enough to be mud. It films the surface, it collects in the low ridges and runs in threads that cross and recross, and somewhere it is taken back in, because the floor never floods and never dries. It is warm the way skin is warm. It is, you gradually accept, a single plant, or the flattened body of one, and it is the size of a valley.

The structures stand on it at long intervals. From where you are you can count nine, maybe eleven, none closer than a few hundred paces, none further than the haze allows. Between them there is nothing but the floor, and the water moving on it, and the light, which is ordinary — an overcast noon, high and even, the kind of light that throws no shadows and makes distance hard to judge.

---

The structures are the same material as the floor, only denser, darker, more like the heartwood of something than the pith. And they are built. There is no other word. They are not grown the way a tree is grown, all one gesture from root to crown. They are laid up in courses. You can see where each course meets the one below it — a seam, a slight change in grain, as if the material was set down in a lift, allowed to firm, then built upon. The walls are plumb. The corners are true. Openings have lintels; lintels have a slight camber, the way a lintel should, to carry load. There are things that are clearly meant to be stairs, though the treads are rounded and the risers uneven, as though the maker knew what a stair was for but not exactly what feet are.

And every one of them is unfinished.

Not ruined — unfinished. The distinction is total. A ruin has fallen from a completed state; these have never been completed. Walls stop at the height of a shoulder or a head, with the top course still soft-edged, still glistening, as though the next lift were expected within the hour. Doorframes stand without doors and without the wall that would make them doors, so that you walk around them as easily as through. Columns rise in a rough ellipse and hold up nothing, and their tops are not broken but simply arrived at — a smooth, damp terminus, like the tip of a shoot. There are no roofs anywhere. Not one. Whatever this is, it has been at the framing stage for as long as it has existed, and it will be at the framing stage tomorrow.

You look for signs that it is progressing. You mark a wall top with your fingernail and come back after some hours and the mark is there, exact, and the wall is the same height. You look for signs that it is failing — a crack, a lean, a course slipping — and there are none. Nothing sags. Nothing has fallen and lies on the floor. The floor is clean of debris for as far as you can walk. This is the strange thing, stranger than the buildings themselves: the place has arrested itself at the exact moment of becoming, and holds there, and does not age forward or backward from it.

---

It is not quiet. That is the other thing you were not ready for.

There is a sound under everything, and after a while you stop trying to hear around it because it is the place itself. It is water — that much is clear — but water moved with force, through tubes, under pressure, the way a body moves water. From the floor comes a low continuous rushing, as of a river heard through a wall. From the structures come sharper noises: a ticking, a periodic knock, a long swallowing gurgle that begins at the base of a column and climbs it and ends at the wet terminus at the top with a sound like a stopper being pulled. Put your ear to a wall and it is deafening — a chambered roar, a hundred small torrents, and behind them a slow thud you first take for your own pulse and then realize is far too slow.

Everything is weeping. Water films every surface, gathers on the undersides of lintels and hangs there and, when the drops are large enough, does not fall — it is drawn back upward into the material and gone. Water beads on the top of every unfinished wall and runs in sheets down the faces and back into the floor, and the floor takes it, and the roar carries it somewhere else, and it comes up again through the columns. The air is saturated. You breathe it more than you feel it. Cloud sits below the plateau's edge and sometimes a shelf of it slides up and over the floor and you walk through it and come out the other side onto the same floor and the same distant frames.

The sound never changes in pitch or rhythm. It does not build to anything. It is the sound of work — of material being moved to where it is needed, of enormous effort — and the effort produces nothing. Or it produces exactly this, and this is what it looks like when it is done.

---

You start to notice the specificity.

The structures are not the same. One is a long low rectangle with a row of openings down one side, all at the same height, all the width of a body turned sideways. One is a round chamber with a single stair spiraling up its inside face to a rim that overlooks nothing. One is a series of small enclosures, each barely large enough to lie down in, arranged in a curve, each with a low sill you would have to step over. There is a thing like a trough or a channel, waist-deep, running dead straight for two hundred paces and stopping. There is a platform with a single step up to it. There are lintels set at three different heights in one wall, as if for three different kinds of occupant, or as if the maker were guessing.

None of them are for you. That much you can feel. They anticipate a body, but not yours — the proportions are close and wrong, the way a room built from a description would be. The sills are too high to step and too low to be walls. The stairs turn the wrong way for a hand on the inner rail. Whatever is meant to live here has not come, and the buildings have stopped in the posture of readiness for it.

Or — and this thought arrives in the late afternoon, when the light has not changed and the roar has not changed and you have walked to a fourth structure and found it, like the others, exactly as far along as the first — the occupant is not late. The occupant is what the place is imagining. It is building toward something it can only guess at, and it has guessed as far as it can, and it holds the guess up, wet and open to the sky, and waits to be corrected.

---

Once, at the edge of hearing, something crosses the floor.

It is small and quick and pale, close to the color of the substrate, and it moves along a water-thread with the water rather than against it, and it goes into one of the low enclosures and does not come out. You wait. Nothing. You go to the enclosure and it is empty — a curved wall, a sill, a floor filmed with water, the sound of the pump in the wall — and there is no way it could have left. Later, far off, you see a bird, or a bird-shaped thing, pass high across the plateau without descending. It does not circle. It does not look for a place to land. There is no place to land here that is finished.

That is nearly all the life you see, in a day. The rest is the plant, which is everything, and the water, which is the plant's blood, and the light, which sits on top of it all like a lid.

---

Rules, then, as you work them out.

Nothing here decays. The wet surfaces are not slimy; nothing grows on them. There is no smell of rot, only of water and something green and something faintly sweet, like sap. There is no litter, no shed material, no dead patches on the floor.

Nothing here heals either. Your nail-mark on the wall top is still there when you leave. A place where you scuffed the floor with a heel is still scuffed, a small pale abrasion holding a lens of water, and you understand that it will be there for as long as the floor is, that you have added a permanent feature to a landscape that adds nothing to itself.

Nothing here completes. You test this against every structure you can reach and it holds. The frames stand at the same stage of construction in every direction, as though a single moment of building were spread out across the whole valley and frozen. Not frozen — the roar tells you it is not frozen, that it is working as hard as anything has ever worked. Sustained. Held.

And water goes up. You see it a dozen times before you accept it: a drop hangs from the underside of a lintel, swells, and instead of falling is taken back into the wood above it. The whole system pulls upward, toward the open tops of the walls, toward the columns' wet tips, toward the place where the next course would go. The water arrives there, sheets over, and runs back down the outside to be gathered again. It is delivering material to the point of growth, over and over, and the point of growth does not take it. Or takes exactly as much as it gives back. An equilibrium with the sound of a flood.

---

Toward what would be evening, if the light knew how, you sit on the single step of the platform structure with the roar coming up through the material into your spine, and look out across the floor at the far frames standing in the haze. From here they could be anything. Foundations of a town. The bones of a temple to a body that has not been invented. A sketch, at full scale, in living material, of a place someone once tried to explain to a plant.

The floor sweats around you. The water runs to the frames and climbs them and comes back. The sound does not change. It has always been this far along. It will always be this far along. It is not waiting, exactly, because waiting implies a before and an after, and this place has neither — only the long, loud, warm, wet middle of a gesture that is the whole of what it is.

You leave a mark on the step before you go down into the cloud. It will be there. That is the only thing you can be sure of.
