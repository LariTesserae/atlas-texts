You come down to it, not up. The land around has the low, drained look of country that has been walked over for a long time by people going somewhere else — pasture gone to rush, a road with grass down the crown, a few stunted thorns leaning the way the wind goes. Then the ground drops a few metres over a lip of grey clay and you are in the basin.

It is not large. You could walk the circuit of the rim in twenty minutes if the rim were walkable, which in places it isn't, because it is slumping inward. A few hundred paces across, roughly oval, floored with something that is neither soil nor water. From the lip it looks like a spoiled tray of wet cement. Then you notice it is moving.

Everywhere, the floor is moving. Not fast — nothing here is fast — but nothing is still either. The basin is a field of mud cones, hundreds of them, from the size of a fist to the size of a small house, packed so closely that their skirts overlap and merge into one continuous slurry. Each one has a mouth. Each mouth is doing something: swelling, sighing, folding a grey lip over itself, splitting along a seam and letting out a slow bulb of cold mud that stands a moment, gloss-skinned, and then sags and slides down the flank of the cone in a sheet. Some cones are building. Some are collapsing into their own throats. Some have flooded and become pools of thin milk-coloured water with a skin of dust on top and, beneath the skin, a slow heave from below as though something is breathing through it.

The sound of all this is almost nothing. That is the first thing you don't expect. Hundreds of mouths, and the whole basin barely whispers. A bubble the size of a bathtub swells at the top of a cone, stretches, thins to a membrane you can see the sky through, and breaks with a sound like someone closing a book in another room. Somewhere a rill of water finds a new course and runs for a minute over a lip of clay with a noise so slight you have to stop breathing to hear it. That's all. The turbulence is total and it is silent. It's the silence of things that are heavy and wet and have nothing to knock against.

The mud is cold. Put your hand near a vent and there's no warmth in it at all — it comes up at the temperature of deep ground, a few degrees above freezing, and it makes the whole basin a few degrees colder than the fields above. The air sits in the hollow and doesn't leave. On a grey day like this one — the light flat and even, the cloud a lid with no seam in it — the surface of the mud and the surface of the sky are almost the same colour, and the basin seems to have no depth, only texture, until a cone shifts and a shadow moves across it.

Nothing much grows. The rim carries a fringe of rush and cotton-grass and one crooked willow with half its roots in air. Down on the floor there's a kind of orange-grey crust in the older, drier corners, lichen or algae or a mineral bloom, you can't tell without kneeling, and you don't want to kneel. A few tussocks of a wiry salt-tolerant grass hold out on the tops of cones that haven't erupted in a while; they are the only sign of what "a while" means here. When a tussock goes under, you know that patch has turned over.

The machinery is what tells you people tried.

Pipes cross the basin. Steel, most of them, thirty or forty centimetres across, running on trestles of riveted angle-iron that were driven into the clay when it was — presumably — somewhere else. Some trestles stand true. Most lean. Several have gone entirely, and the pipe between them sags in a long catenary into the mud and comes up again, coated, the other side. In one place the pipe has been swallowed for twenty metres and re-emerges with a cone built up over it like a saddle, the cone's mouth still working, pushing grey paste up around the seam of the pipe and letting it fall back down the flank. The pipe doesn't seem to mind. The pipe has been here long enough that the mud has stopped being a thing that happens to it and become a thing it is in.

There's a pump house at the western edge, half in the basin and half up on the rim, brick, single-storey, with a tall window frame from which the glass is gone. Its floor is sloped now. You can see the line where the mud has come in through the door and dried and cracked and been re-wetted and come further. Inside, a flywheel the height of a person, a belt drum, the cast body of a reciprocating pump with its maker's plate still legible if you rub it. No one is here. Yet a low electrical hum comes from somewhere behind the wall — a transformer on a pole up the slope, still connected to something — and once, while you're looking at the flywheel, a valve on one of the pipes outside clicks over by itself, a single sharp domestic sound, and a slow gout of grey water runs from a stub a hundred metres away into a pool that was already full.

So it isn't dead. Or it isn't only dead. Something is still drawing off the basin — gas, probably, that's what these fields are for; the mud comes up because gas is coming up through it, and someone laid pipe to catch the gas and pumped water out of the way to keep the pipes clear, and the basin took the pipes and kept doing what it was doing. There are other structures. A concrete plinth with four bolts and no engine. A little corrugated shed on a spit of firmer ground, door hanging, a stool inside, a tin cup gone orange. A cast-iron marker post with a number on it, tilted so far you'd read it lying down. Cable, black rubber cable, coming out of the mud, going into the mud. A gauge on a stanchion with a needle that still moves — twitches, every few seconds, as pressure somewhere along the buried run goes up and comes down.

The more you look, the more you see that all of this has been here a long time and the basin has never once stopped. This is the part that takes a while to feel. It looks like a disaster — the leaning trestles, the swallowed pipe, the flooded pump house — but it isn't a disaster, because a disaster is a departure from a state, and this is the state. The rim slumps and the basin widens by a hand's breadth a year and the cones build and fall and build, and the whole thing is in a kind of frantic equilibrium, like a pan kept just at the simmer. The dried mud at the edges shows it in section where a bank has fallen: layer on layer on layer, thin grey leaves, each one an afternoon's outflow from some cone that isn't there anymore, thousands of them, going down further than the fall exposes. The clay under the willow's roots is the same stuff. The country all around, for all its rushes and pasture, is this basin's spoil.

Presences. The word comes to you because the basin is so full and so empty at once. Nothing lives on it that you can see, but there is no square metre unoccupied: a cone, a pool, a crack forming, a crack healing, a pipe, a stub of post, a rill, a bubble, the domed grey back of something large turning over slowly beneath the skin of a pool and not surfacing. You watch that one for a while. It is only mud. It is almost certainly only mud. The pool's surface bulges and settles and bulges again in a rhythm just slower than your own breathing, and you find yourself adjusting to match it, and then stopping yourself.

One bird. A pied wagtail, walking the top of the sagging pipe with the dainty confidence of a creature that has judged the load. It stops, looks into a vent, looks away. There are flies over the pools in the sheltered corner, small ones, low; the wagtail is here for them. Up on the rim, sheep have been, once — droppings gone grey, hoof-holes half filled. They don't come down. Nothing with weight comes down, except the pipes, which had no choice.

There are rules, and you learn them by standing there. The crust is not the ground. The dry places are the old places, and the old places are the ones about to change, because a cone that has stopped is a cone that is blocked, and a blocked cone is building pressure under a lid. The pale mud is thin and the dark mud is thick and neither will hold you. Where the tussocks are is where you could stand, for now. A vent that has just emptied will not do anything for a while; a vent that is quiet and full has not decided. Water on the surface means something is draining, and something draining means something else is filling. The gauge on the stanchion means nothing you can use — it is measuring a pipe, not the basin — but you look at it anyway because it's the one thing here with a face.

And the noise, again, or the absence of it: the valve clicks. The bubble closes like a book. A trestle, somewhere on the far side, gives up one more degree and there's a groan from the steel so low you feel it in your teeth rather than hear it, and a slow fan of slurry spreads from the new low point, and covers the orange-grey crust there, and stops. The wagtail lifts, resettles further along. The cloud does not move. The whole basin, hundreds of mouths, works and works at what it has always been doing, in a quiet like held breath, cold as a cellar, in flat grey light that gives nothing a shadow until it moves — and it is all moving, and it has all been moving since before the pipes, and it will still be moving after the last trestle goes under and the pump house floor finally tips its flywheel into the mud and the mud takes it, a layer at a time, and adds it to the country.
