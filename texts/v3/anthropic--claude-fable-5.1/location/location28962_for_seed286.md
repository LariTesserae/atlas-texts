Start with the sound, because you hear it before you understand anything else, and because it is the one thing here that never stops.

A thud. Then a long exhalation that is not quite a hiss — more like a great volume of water being drawn through a narrow place. Then the thud again. Eleven seconds between them. It has been eleven seconds between them for as long as there has been anyone to count, and the interval does not drift with the day or the year, because there is no day or year here, only the cold and the pressure and the beat. The sound arrives through the water from every direction at once and also from below, up through the floor, so that you feel it in the long bones and the teeth. Fish stop and hang in the water on the thud. They start swimming again on the exhalation.

The floor is what you would call ground if you had not touched it. It is a plain — flat, faintly rolling, running out past the limit of sight in every direction under three hundred meters of very clear, very cold water — and it is alive, the whole of it, one continuous tissue. Not soil with things growing in it. The substrate itself is the plant. Its surface is dense and rubbery and dark olive going to brown, seamed with shallow ridges like the surface of a brain or a lung, and where you press it there is a slow give and a slower return. Cut into it and there is no rock underneath, not for a long way. Core samples, if anyone took them, would come up green, then paler green, then a fibrous white like the inside of a stem, kilometer after kilometer down, until at last something mineral appeared: the old mountain range this thing grew over and enclosed and eventually replaced as the ground. That is why the plain is so high. The water sits at an altitude where, on a bare planet, there would be thin air and snow. Here there is instead an inland sea held in the shallow bowl of a single organism's back, and the sea is cold the way high lakes are cold, a few degrees off freezing, and it stays exactly that cold because the thing beneath it keeps it so.

The stalks are the only verticals. From up on the surface, if the cloud deck that forms over the sea lets you see anything, they look like the masts of a drowned fleet — pale columns rising out of the water at intervals of three, five, ten kilometers, no pattern to the spacing that anyone has found, but no two ever close together. Down here you see what they are. Each stalk is a tube, hollow, forty or sixty meters across at the base, rooted into the floor by a wide flared skirt of the same olive tissue. The walls are translucent where they are thin, and you can see the shape of the mechanism inside: a stack of muscular rings, one above the other, like the diaphragms of a bellows or the stages of a pump. On the thud, the lowest ring clenches shut and a wave of contraction runs up the column faster than you can follow, and a body of water the size of a building is thrown upward through the stalk and out its open crown into the air above the sea. On the exhalation, the rings relax in sequence from the top down and cold water is drawn in through slotted vents around the base, pulling a slow current across the floor toward it from every side. That current is the only weather down here. It is why the silt — what little there is, mostly the fine white shed skin of the floor — lies in long combed lines pointing at the nearest stalk.

Every stalk fires on the same beat. There is no lag from one to the next that you can hear, though they are kilometers apart. Whatever passes the signal passes it through the floor, and whatever carries it is electric: put your hand flat on the tissue in the second before the thud and the hair on your arm lifts, and there is a taste of metal at the back of the mouth. Some of the fauna use it. There are eel-shaped things, gray, slack, nearly transparent, that lie along the seams of the floor and pulse with the beat, and fatter things with no eyes that hang in the intake current with their heads toward the vents and their mouths open, feeding on the fine drift the pump brings them. Above the vents, inside the stalks, small silver fish live their entire lives in the pumped column, going up on the thud and settling back on the release, never leaving the tube. Their bodies have gotten flattened and stiff in the way that things do when they are hammered by the same force in the same direction for a very long time. Outside the stalks, in the open plain between them, the water is mostly empty — you can swim for an hour and see nothing but the floor and the light and the far pale shape of the next column — and then you will come on a slow herd of a hundred grazing shapes, armored, low, working across the olive surface with their undersides, rasping the outer skin off the plant and leaving a paler track that has begun healing before they are out of sight. Nothing here is rare, exactly, but nothing is crowded either. There is a great deal of room and the living things are spread out in it like the stalks are, as if too much of anything in one place would put the beat off.

The light is ordinary. That is one of the stranger things about it. Three hundred meters of water in most seas would be dusk at best, but this water is nearly empty of anything to scatter through — no plankton to speak of, no river silt — and the sun comes down through it clean and blue-white and lays the shadows of the stalks across the plain in long straight bars. The shadows do not move much. High latitude, high altitude, a permanent overcast overhead that the pumps themselves maintain: each stalk throws its column into the sky and the water falls back as a fine continual rain over the whole sea, and the rain feeds the cloud, and the cloud flattens the daylight into an even gray that barely changes from what would be morning to what would be evening. Time is measured, if at all, in beats.

That is the rule of the place, the one it took a long while to accept: nothing here is going anywhere. The temperature does not fall in what ought to be winter. The stalks do not grow — they are worn smooth at the skirts, scarred at the crowns where the wind has broken the lips of the tubes and the plant has healed them into knobs, but they were the same height in the oldest record as they are now. The grazers do not multiply; their tracks heal at exactly the speed the herds cut them. The floor does not thicken. Whatever the pump is for — and no one has settled this: circulation, respiration, the moving of some nutrient through a body too large to feed itself by diffusion, or nothing but the thing's own idea of what a body ought to do — it long ago reached the point where it moves precisely as much water as it needs to keep moving precisely that much water. It is an engine that has finished becoming an engine. The rhythm you hear is not the sound of work being done. It is the sound of an equilibrium so old and so total that it has the character of a mechanism: the same stroke, the same interval, the same throw of cold water into the same gray sky, and the same fall back onto the same sea, and beneath all of it the same continental green thing, indifferent, exact, keeping time.

Thud. The fish stop. Exhalation. They start again. Eleven seconds. Count if you like.
