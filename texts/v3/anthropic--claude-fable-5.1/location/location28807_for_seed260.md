**The Loud Country**

Start with the altitude, because everything else follows from it. This is a place higher than mountains are supposed to go. The air is thin enough that you feel it as an absence against your face, cool without being cold — the kind of coolness that belongs to stone in shade, to the inside of a cellar, to a room that has never had a fire in it. There is no snow. There is no ice. There has never been either, because there is no water here at all, none in the sky and none in the ground and none in the plants, and so the peaks stand bare and dark in a sky that is the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges.

The light is not coming from anywhere in particular. There is no sun visible, no moon. The dimness is even, sourceless, the light of a room lit through a paper wall in the hour before dawn. It does not cast shadows. Things are visible in it the way things are visible when you have been sitting in the dark long enough for your eyes to give up and start inventing.

And underneath all of it — underneath is the wrong word; through all of it, in all of it — is the sound.

---

The sound is the first thing and the last thing. It is a roar. If you have ever stood at the base of a very large waterfall you know something of it, but this is a waterfall with no water, a waterfall made of rock, made of the fact of rock. It is so loud that it stops being a sound after a while and becomes a condition of the place, like gravity. Your ears do not adjust. Your body does. Your chest hums with it. Your teeth carry it. If you speak, you feel the words leave you and do not hear them go.

It does not change. That is what is strangest about it, after the volume. It does not rise and fall, it does not have a rhythm, it does not have events in it. It is a single chord that has been held for longer than anything here has existed, and if it is changing — and it is, it must be — it changes on the scale of centuries, a slow lean from one note toward the next, the way a continent leans.

Here is what you come to understand, after a while: the sound is not happening *in* the landscape. The sound is the landscape happening. The mountains are the noise. They are being said, continuously, at enormous volume, and what you are standing on is the part of the sentence that has already been spoken.

---

Look at the rock and you will see this is true.

Near you, underfoot, the stone is finished. It is a hard, dark, fine-grained thing with a bluish cast, and it breaks — where it has broken — into flakes with edges you could shave with. Nothing has worn it. No wind has rounded it, no frost has split it, no water has ever run over it. It is exactly as sharp as it was when it was made, and it was made recently, and it is still, in places, being made.

Walk toward the edge of anything here — a ledge, a ridge, the lip of a valley — and watch what happens to the stone. It loses conviction. The grain, which near you runs clearly in one direction, begins to hesitate. You can see it trying out two orientations at once, a cross-hatching that is not a pattern but an indecision. Further out, the strata are laid down like pencil lines, faint and provisional, some of them erased. Further still the rock is a suggestion of rock, a region where the light bends as though around a mass, but when you put your hand out there is nothing there yet to touch — only the sound, much louder at these edges, loud enough to bruise, because this is where the saying is still going on.

There are whole valleys out there in draft. You can see them from the ridges: long troughs between peaks that have the shape of valleys and the shading of valleys and are not yet valleys. Some of them are more finished on one side than the other. In one, a cliff face has been fully resolved down to its individual joints and fractures while the valley floor below it remains a dim sketch, so that the cliff appears to stand on nothing, and does.

Nothing here will ever erode. Everything here is arriving.

---

The plants are the next surprise, because there should not be any.

There is no water. The soil — where there is soil, which is rarely — is a mineral grit, flakes of the blue-black stone ground fine by nothing you can identify, since nothing here grinds. And yet in the sheltered places, in the clefts and the lees, the growth is thick. Not everywhere. The country is mostly bare, wide reaches of naked rock with nothing on them for a mile in any direction. But where the plants have taken, they have taken completely, in dense knots and stands that from a distance look like dark smoke frozen against the slopes.

They are not green. Nothing is green here. They are the colors of the rock they grow from and of the sky above them: slate, indigo, a gray that is almost violet, a dull mica-shimmer where the light catches them. Up close they are stiff and dry and make a sound when the — no. Not the wind; there is no wind. They make a sound when you brush them, a clatter like thin glass, though you will not hear it.

Some are tall, taller than you, jointed stalks that rise straight and then fan out into something like fronds, though the fronds are rigid and translucent and layered like the pages of a book that has been left open in the rain and then dried without ever having been wet. Some are low, crusted, a lichen-like spread that follows fractures in the stone with perfect fidelity, so that you can read the joint pattern of a slab by the growth on it. Some hang, from the undersides of overhangs, in long dry beards that tremble constantly — not from air movement but from the sound, which they are, you realize, gathering. Every plant here leans toward the loudest direction. They grow toward the roar the way things elsewhere grow toward light, and they are thickest where the rock is least finished, at the edges of the drafted regions, where the volume is greatest. They are feeding on the world's being spoken. They are lush on noise.

Nothing eats them. Nothing moves among them. There is not a single animal here, not an insect, not a bird, not a track in the grit. The plants are the only living things, if they are living, and they have never been touched by anything but the sound and, once, briefly, by hands.

---

Because there were hands. Not many. Not for long.

On a saddle between two peaks there is a cairn, chest-high, built from the flaked stone stacked flat, each piece chosen for how it sat on the last. It is a good cairn. Whoever built it knew how to build one. Near it, scored into a slab with a harder stone — a piece of something whiter, left lying where it was dropped — are marks. Rows of them. Short vertical strokes, grouped in fives. Counting something. There are three hundred and eleven marks. Below the last one the slab dissolves into unfinished rock and the marks stop, or continue into a surface that does not yet exist.

There is a hut, or the intention of a hut, on a shelf below the saddle. Dry stone, no mortar, walls the thickness of a forearm and the height of a kneeling person. It has no roof and it is not clear it ever had one, or needed one — nothing falls here. Its single doorway faces east, directly into the loudest quarter of the sky. Inside, on the floor, a hammerstone and a handful of flakes, set down as if the person meant to come back to them. The flakes are sharp. The hammerstone fits a hand. That is the whole of the technology of this place: a stone for striking, a stone for being struck.

Further out, along a ridge that is only partly resolved, there is a wall. Or half a wall. It runs a hundred paces along the crest, waist-high, well-laid, and then it simply is not there anymore — not fallen, not broken, but built, you understand, into a stretch of ridge that has since been un-said, and gone with it. Or built on a stretch not yet said, waiting there in draft until the ground catches up beneath it. The wall does not know which. Nor do you.

Whoever they were, they did not stay. There is no sign of a fire. There is nothing to burn and no need for warmth. There is no midden, no bone, no grave. They came here with stones in their hands and counted something to three hundred and eleven and left, or were unmade, or walked out along a ridge into a part of the world that was still being decided and have not yet been finished.

---

Some rules, insofar as the place has any.

Distance is not measured in length. It is measured in loudness. A thing that is very loud is very near, even if it looks far; a thing that is quiet is far, even if you can put your hand on it. The finished rock underfoot is nearly silent — you can hear it, faintly, as a bass note in the chord, and that is why you can stand on it. The unfinished regions are deafening. You cannot walk into them. You cannot walk into a sound like that. The body refuses before the mind does.

You will sometimes see two versions of the same terrain. A slope that is a slope and is also a cliff, faintly, in the same place, like a double exposure. Both are being said, and neither has yet been chosen. Do not stand on either. Wait. In a hundred years one of them will be true.

Time here is the slowest thing. Nothing happens. The sound holds, the light holds, the plants grow so slowly that a lifetime of watching would not show you a new frond. The valleys out in draft have been in draft for as long as the cairn has stood, and will be, and there is no urgency in any of it. The world is being made at the pace of a world being made, and that pace is geological, and the roar is not the roar of speed but of mass. Of how much there is to say.

---

Stand at the cairn, then, in the sourceless light, with the dry indigo growth clattering silently at your knees and the noise coming up through the soles of your feet into the long bones of your legs, and look out.

Peaks, finished and unfinished. Valleys in outline. A country the size of a small nation drawn in pencil on the dark, with the pencil still moving somewhere out there at the edge of hearing, which is the edge of everything. No water anywhere. No wind. No animal. Nothing that eats or drinks or dies. Only rock arriving, and plants that have learned to live on the sound of its arrival, and three hundred and eleven marks on a slab, and a doorway facing the loud quarter of the sky.

It is not hostile. It is not anything yet. It is simply very early, and very high, and very loud, and it has all the time there is.
