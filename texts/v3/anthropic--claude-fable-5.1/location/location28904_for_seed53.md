**The Hollow of Blades**

It sits in the notch between two summits, a bowl the size of a chapel floor, and you come up into it from below through a gap in its own rim that is barely wider than your shoulders. The rim is the first thing: a broken ring of dark rock, gray-green where it is dry and nearly black where it is wet, standing a little higher than a person on every side. Beyond and above it there is only sky, and the sky at this height is not blue so much as a hard, thin, glassy color that seems to press downward. The air is so clear that distance does not soften anything. A ridge forty miles off is drawn as sharply as the stone you are leaning on.

The light is the second thing, and it does not stop being the second thing. Everything up here is lit from above and from below at once—the sun directly, the snow and ice by reflection—so shadows are shallow and edges are cut hard. Within a minute of arriving your eyes have narrowed to slits and will not open again until dark. Colors have gone. What remains is a range of whites, a range of grays, and one blue, which lives only in the deepest crevices and under overhangs, a blue so saturated it looks poured.

The floor of the bowl is full. That is the word that arrives before any other. It is full the way a granary is full, or a quarry yard, or a field of standing grain—things upright and close together, so many of them that the eye gives up counting and starts reading them as a texture.

Two kinds of things stand in the bowl.

The first are stones. They are pillars of the same rock as the rim, waist-high to head-high, most of them roughly the thickness of a torso, rounded at the top and pitted along their flanks like old teeth. They stand in no pattern that resolves into a grid or a spiral or anything designed, but they stand with a kind of consistency, an even spacing, as if they had been shaken out and let settle. There are perhaps two hundred of them. Their feet are buried in a coarse gray sand of their own making, the grains sharp-edged and glittering with mica; where the sand has been blown clear you can see that each pillar sits on a broader root, and that the root is a slightly different color, the color of rock that has never seen the sun. These are the bones of the mountain, left standing when the softer rock between them went. It took long enough that the pillars themselves have half-gone since, their tops worn to domes, their sides fluted by wind carrying that same sand. The place has been like this for a very long time. It is not becoming anything. It is maintaining.

The second kind of thing is ice.

Between the pillars, filling nearly every gap, stand blades. They are snow that has half-turned to glass—old snow, packed and sun-cured, sculpted by the air into thin upright fins, each one leaning by the same angle in the same direction, toward the place the sun stands at midday. Some are knee-high, most are hip- to chest-high, a few near the north wall rise above your head. Their edges are sharp enough to cut a glove. Their faces are scalloped and rippled; when the light is behind them they glow from within, a milky luminous white shading into that impossible blue at the base where the ice is thickest and oldest. Where the light is on them they are simply too bright to look at.

Stone and ice are near-touching everywhere. To cross the bowl you must turn sideways and thread between a pillar and a blade, then between two blades, then squeeze past another pillar with the ice pressing cold against your back, and there is no path—or there are a thousand paths, all of them narrow, all of them dead-ending in a place where three blades meet and lean together over a knot of frozen sand. It is dense the way a crowd is dense. You are never more than an arm's length from something that could be mistaken, for an instant, at the corner of the eye, for a standing figure.

This is not an accident of the place. Or rather, it is an accident the place has arrived at and now keeps. The pillars cast shade; snow that falls in winter drifts into their lee and survives the first weeks of the bright season where the open snow does not. The pillars also heat in the sun and melt their immediate surroundings, so that the snow that survives does so in the gaps *between* the shaded and the heated, in thin standing walls. Then the sun does the rest. It does not melt the ice here—the air is too cold and too dry for that—it vaporizes it directly, and it does so fastest where the surface is already hollowed, so that hollows deepen into troughs and troughs into gaps, and what remains between the gaps sharpens into blades. By the peak of the bright season every fin has been honed to its thinnest. By the end, most have fallen. Then the snow comes again and the pillars begin again to sort it.

Where the water is, in a place this dry, is worth attending to. There is almost none of it in the air; your lips crack within the hour. But it is here. It is in the beads that form on the shaded side of every pillar in the early morning, condensed out of the thin night air onto stone that is a few degrees colder than the air around it—a fur of droplets so fine it looks like frost, and which becomes frost by mid-morning, and is gone by noon. It is in the dark stain that spreads at the foot of the south-facing blades in the hour after midday, when the sun's heat stored in the sand reaches the ice touching it and a thread of melt runs a hand's breadth into the gravel before the cold takes it back. It is in the pool.

The pool is at the low point of the bowl, hard against the east wall, and it is the size of a spread cloak. It is frozen, always, but the ice on it is a lens, thick at the edges and thin at the center, and under the center there is water—black, perfectly still, a finger's depth. On the warmest afternoons a weep of meltwater comes down the wall above it in a sheet no thicker than paper, spreading over the rock in a fan, and the rock under the fan is a different color from the rock around it, a warm rust-brown, the only warm color in the place. That fan of stained stone is older than any of the ice. It has been receiving that paper-thin sheet of water for as long as the blades have been forming.

There is nothing living here. Not a lichen on the pillars, not a hair of moss at the pool's edge, not an insect on the ice. It is too high. Life stopped several hundred body-lengths below, and stopped abruptly, the last cushion plant clinging to the last patch of soil, and above that the bare rock began and has continued. The bowl belongs to mineral processes only—to freeze and thaw, to grain and gravity, to the exchange between ice and dry air. That is what fills the silence. Not emptiness. Just processes that do not need to breathe.

And it is nearly silent. The rim keeps out the wind that must be raking the summits above; you can hear it up there, a high steady rush like distant surf, but down among the blades the air is almost still. What you hear instead is small. The tick of a pillar expanding in the sun. A dry hiss when a grain of sand lets go of the rim and slides. Once or twice an hour, somewhere in the bowl, a sound like a plate being set down on a table—a blade whose base has thinned past bearing, tipping and settling against its neighbor. The ice does not shatter when it does this. It leans and stops and stays, and the two blades together will last a little longer than either would alone. And under everything, if you hold still long enough, a faint irregular ticking from the direction of the pool, which is the lens-ice adjusting to the water moving beneath it.

People have been here. Not many, not often, and it is not clear what for.

There are cairns. You do not see them at first, because a cairn in this place is a stack of gray stone standing waist-high among two hundred natural stacks of gray stone, and only when you have been in the bowl a while do you begin to pick out the ones that are *too* regular—flat slabs laid one on the next, each smaller than the last, a made thing. There are eleven of them that can be found with certainty and perhaps a dozen more that could be either. They are old. Their slabs have weathered on their exposed faces to the same pitted gray as the pillars, and sand has drifted around their bases in exactly the same way. Someone, long ago, walked into a place full of standing stones and made more standing stones. Whether as markers, or as company, or because it seemed to them that the place wanted to be fuller than it was, there is no way to know.

Against the west wall there is a shelter. It is a half-circle of stones piled without mortar to chest height, roofed by nothing, its open side turned toward the pillars and away from the gap you came in by. The floor inside has been leveled and swept. Set into the wall, at the height of a seated person's hand, is a niche, and in the niche there are things: a dozen flakes of white quartz with edges worked sharp; a lump of the rust-brown stain from under the weep, scraped up while damp and dried into a cake; a length of cord, rotted to fibers, made of some plant that does not grow within a day's walk of here. Nothing more sophisticated than that. Hands, and stone, and what stone will do to stone.

The rust-brown cake explains the marks. Up close, on the sun-facing sides of several pillars, there are smears of that color—no figures, no shapes, just palm-width bars and dots at about the height of a standing person's face. They are very faint. Most of them are faint because time has worn them; but some are faint because they are in the path of the morning condensation, and every day a film of water forms over them and then freezes and then sublimes away, taking a little pigment with it. Someone put them where the water would find them. It seems deliberate. It seems as though the marks were meant not to last but to be *used up*, a little every morning, by the place.

That is the one thing here that could be called strange, and it is not very strange. Only that when you stand among the blades in the flat noon light, with the ice leaning toward the sun on every side and the pillars standing among them, and eleven cairns standing among the pillars, and the faint ochre bars on the pillar-faces washing slowly away into the air, it is difficult to keep hold of which things arrived here on their own and which were placed. The distinction begins to feel like one the bowl does not recognize.

The day moves. The shadows, shallow as they are, swing across the sand and the leaning blades track the sun exactly, their tilt an inclinometer of the season. In the afternoon, cloud comes up out of the valley on the far side—you see it first as a whiteness filling the gap in the rim, and then it pours over, a fog so dense and bright it is like being inside a lamp. In the fog the pillars and blades become identical, gray uprights fading into white at three paces, and the sounds of the place come closer: the tick, the hiss, the set-down plate. Water beads on everything, on the ice as well as the stone, and the ice goes momentarily translucent and dark, showing its blue. Then the fog burns off from above and the light comes down again all at once, and every blade is furred with fresh rime, and the rime lasts perhaps an hour.

By dusk the cold is total. The sand crusts. The pool ticks louder as the lens tightens. The last light lies along the rim in a band of copper and then is gone, and the stars come out with a hardness to match the daytime sky, so many and so bright that the ice gives back a faint glow under them and you can still, just, thread your way between the blades by starlight, hand on stone, hand on ice, hand on stone.

In the morning the beads will be back on the shaded stone, and one or two more blades will have leaned, and a little more of the ochre will be gone.
