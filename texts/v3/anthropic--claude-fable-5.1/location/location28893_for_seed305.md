**The Shelf**

From far below, from the lowlands where the air still has weight, it reads first as weather. A white band along the whole northern horizon, too even for cloud, too steady for storm — a bright edge that does not move for weeks. Anyone who lives under it learns to stop looking. The eye can't hold it. It's the color of an arc welder seen through the fingers.

Come closer and the band resolves into a wall.

The wall is rock, and the rock is new. Not new in the way a fallen boulder is new but new in the way a tooth is new when it breaks the gum — there is a rawness to it that hasn't finished. The cliffs go up in facets, planes meeting planes at angles a mason would reject as unsafe. Nothing has rounded them. There are no talus slopes at the base, or hardly any; the scree that has managed to fall lies in a thin apron, still sharp-cornered, still the same grey as the face it came from, unlichened, unstained. In places the cliff shows the fracture pattern of the uplift itself: long vertical slickensides, polished by the friction of the whole plateau grinding upward past its own margin, mirror-smooth and catching the light in blades. Water comes down the face in threads and freezes before it reaches the bottom, so that the wall is hung with cords of ice that never thicken into anything you could call a fall. They are the only soft lines on it.

Two and a half kilometers up, the wall stops and the shelf begins, and everything changes at once.

---

The first thing is the brightness. The thinness of the air alone would make the light hard — that flat high-altitude glare that shows every stone with a black rim of shadow. But the plateau does something worse than that. The plateau reflects. It gives back the sun the way a salt pan gives it back, the way fresh snow does, except that it isn't snow and isn't salt, and you understand that only after your eyes have stopped weeping enough to look.

The second thing is the sound.

It arrives before the crest — a bass presence felt in the sternum on the last of the climb, something like a mill-race heard through a wall — and then at the lip it is simply everywhere, a continuous mechanical roar with no rhythm you can count, or rather with so many rhythms laid over each other that they cancel into a single texture. Grinding. Ticking at a thousand rates. A deep intermittent knock, like a loose bearing in a hall the size of a country. Over all of it a high sustained singing that is the sound of a great deal of electricity moving through a great many small conductors. You would call it industrial. You would say: there is a factory over the crest. There is not. There is not one made thing on the shelf, and never has been.

The third thing is that the ground is alive, and there is no ground.

---

Stand at the lip and the plateau goes out ahead of you until it drops below the curve of the world. It is a continent's worth of high, level, young rock — the whole crustal block came up together, tilted only slightly, so that it runs to the north as flat as a table for farther than a season's walking. And every part of it, from the edge at your feet to the horizon, is occupied.

They are not insects and not tortoises and not anything that has a name in the lowlands. Call them the shelf-bearers, because that is what they seem to do: bear the shelf. Each one is perhaps the length of a wagon, low to the ground, mantled in a carapace made of overlapping plates the color and finish of galvanized sheet. The plates are not smooth. They are ridged in fine parallel lines, and each line is a facet, so that a single animal throws back the sun in a fan of narrow reflections, and a hundred thousand of them make the plateau blaze. Under the mantle you can see legs — many, short, jointed like the arms of a press — and at the front a low head that is mostly a ring of dark lenses and a set of grinding mouthparts that never stop.

They are packed. That is the word. Not herded, not gathered, but *packed*, edge against edge, with the flanges of one carapace fitting under the flanges of the next, so that when you look across them you see no gaps at all, only a tessellation of grey metal plates going out to the end of sight. They do not move, or they move so slowly that in an hour of watching you cannot swear you saw one shift. Frost has formed on the upper plates of the ones nearest you. It has not melted, though the sun is full on them. Their backs are as cold as the air.

And yet the sound. The knocking and grinding and the electric whine. It comes from *under* them.

---

Get down. Lie on the cold slickenside at the edge and put your face to the gap where the first rank of carapaces overhangs the drop, and look into the space beneath.

It is warm. That is the first shock — a breath of air on your cheek that is thirty degrees warmer than the air on your neck, wet, smelling of crushed green things and ozone and something mineral. Under the plates the shelf-bearers are not still at all. Every leg is working, slowly, with the deliberate compound motion of a machine designed to do one thing forever. The mouthparts turn. Fluids move through vessels you can see in the pale undersides. Between the legs, hanging in the warm dim, are long strands of something like translucent cartilage running from one animal to the next to the next, and along these strands there is a visible flicker — a pale pulse of light that travels a meter, pauses, travels another — and this, you understand, is the whine. They are wired together. Each one is a cell, and the cells are joined, and the whole plateau is a single circuit running at the speed of something with all the time in the world.

Below the animals — beneath the legs, in the hand's-breadth of space between belly and rock — is the vegetation.

It is everywhere and it is *green*, an unreasonable green for this altitude, this cold. Moss in swollen cushions, thick as pillows. A low matted thing with round succulent leaves like coins. Threads of a pale red creeper. Fern-like fronds curled so tight they look like fists. Fungus in shelves and cups and cords. It fills every inch not occupied by a leg or a mouth, and it grows in a climate that exists nowhere else on the shelf: the pocket of breath-warmed, condensation-dripping air that a million adjacent metabolisms maintain under their own roofs. The upper plates freeze because the animals send all their heat downward, into the space where the green is. Water beads on the undersides of the carapaces and drips, constantly, a slow rain in a room a hand high and a continent wide. Where the drips land the moss is fattest. Where the moss is fattest the mouthparts work hardest. What comes out the other end is a fine dark grit, and the grit is what the shelf is being built from.

---

Because that is what the industry is. It took a while to see it, lying there with your cheek going numb against the rock.

The plateau is young. The uplift is recent, geologically — the fractures are unweathered, the cliffs unrounded. And nothing on it erodes, because nothing on it is exposed. No rain reaches the rock. No frost cracks it. No wind carries it away. The shelf-bearers cover it edge to edge, and under them the mosses hold the surface, and the grit the animals produce settles into the moss and compacts, and the plateau slowly, slowly gains height. A millimeter a decade. They are not just standing on the shelf; they are *manufacturing* it. The whole assembly is a factory whose product is the ground it rests on, and its rate of production is the rate at which rock forms, and the roar you hear is that rate made audible by sheer multiplication — one animal grinding lichen would be inaudible from a yard away, but there are more of them than there are words for numbers, and the sum is an unbearable steady thunder that never rises and never falls.

Where do the newborn ones go? Watch long enough — days, at the edge, in the glare — and you'll see: at the rim, where the carapaces overhang, the outermost animals are the oldest and largest, and they are not entirely still. Over a week, the whole front rank creeps forward a finger's width, and behind it the ranks close up, and somewhere in the interior, in the warm dark, a young one has been made and has taken a place, and the pressure of that single addition has propagated all the way to the edge. The shelf-bearers do not spread by walking. They spread the way a glacier spreads. They spread the way the uplift itself spreads, and at about the same speed. In a thousand years the plateau will be an arm's length wider and a hand's breadth higher and the front rank will be hanging out over the drop, and eventually one will go over, and its plates will lie sharp and galvanized at the base of the cliff, unweathered, for a very long time.

---

Weather comes to the shelf. Clouds build over it — it makes its own, from the warm wet air that leaks up between the plates, rising into the cold and condensing into a low grey deck that forms most afternoons and drops a dry, hard snow that never accumulates, because the plates are colder than the air but the seams between them are not, and the snow slides into the seams and is gone, down into the warm room, into the drip, into the moss. The clouds discharge, too: the whole plateau is charged, faintly, positively, and on the days when the deck is thick you can hear a new sound under the roar, a crackling like burning grass, and see, if you look along the surface at a low angle, the plates outlined in a faint blue flame that runs in slow waves toward the horizon. The animals do not react. There is nothing in their world that they need to react to. They have never been eaten. They have never been cold. They have never, so far as anyone can tell, been in a hurry.

At night the roar does not stop and the brightness does not entirely stop either. The frost on the upper plates holds starlight the way the plates held sun, and the plateau shows as a faint grey plane under a black sky, humming, ticking, singing, with the pale pulse of the cartilage cables just visible in the seams, a slow bioelectric weather crawling across the underside of a continent. If you lie on the rim with your ear to the rock you can feel the knocking through the stone. It is the sound of a very large, very patient engine, made of nothing but animals and moss and water, running with no operator and no purpose beyond its own continuation, at a volume that carries for a day's walk down into the lowlands, where people have learned not to look up.

The shelf will be there when the lowlands are gone. It is barely started.
