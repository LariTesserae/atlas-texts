Start with the altitude, because it is the thing that will not sit still in the mind: this is a sea at three and a half thousand metres. Not a lake — too big for that word, and too salty, and too deep. A caldera the size of a province, rimmed with black peaks that catch snow on their outer faces and steam on their inner ones, and filled, brim to brim, with warm water.

The water is not clear. It is white the way skimmed milk is white, thick with dissolved silica and lime and things that have not yet decided to become rock. Sunlight at this height is fierce and thin, but it does not get far. A metre down it is bright; ten metres down it is the colour of a pearl held to a lamp; at forty it is a steady grey-green dusk that does not change between noon and evening, only between day and night, and even then not very much. Everything below the first few fathoms lives in that dusk. It is warm — body-warm, bath-warm in places, hotter than that near the vents — and the warmth is the only reason anything is here at all.

The floor is being made.

That is the fact that governs the rest. Beneath the milk, the caldera is still working. Along fissures that run for kilometres, basalt pushes up in bulbs and lobes and tongues, cools against the water into pillows the size of houses, splits its own skin, and pushes again. There is no sand. There is no mud that was not carried down by the mountain's own snowmelt. Nothing has been ground down, because nothing has had time. The rock is glass-edged, dark, sharp enough to open a hand, and where it has cooled fastest it has a rind like the crust of bread that rings when struck. Older flows — a century, two — are already being buried by new ones. The surface of the world here is always the youngest thing in it.

And onto that raw floor the water lays its own patience. Everything that stays still long enough gets coated. The silica comes out of solution on any cold surface: a dropped stone, a dead frond, a rope. First a film, grey and faintly rough. Then a crust. In ten years, a shell as thick as a thumb. The people who live here learned this before they learned anything else, and it is the whole of their architecture.

The city — it has a name, but the name is a sound made with the mouth closed, and I will not try to spell it — sits on the eastern shelf of the caldera, where the floor slopes up toward the rim and the vents are gentle. From above, if you could see through the milk, you would see it as a thickening: a region where the black pillows are furred over with a grey that is not stone and not quite not-stone. From inside it is a forest of columns.

They begin as frames. Lashed poles of the stiff hollow weed that grows along the fissures, bound with cord twisted from the finer weed, staked into cracks in the basalt. A frame stands for a year and turns grey. Two years and it is rigid. Five and you can no longer feel the poles inside it; it is a pillar of the same substance as the seabed, ridged where the lashings were, and you build on it. The city is grown, not built, and it grows at the rate the water permits, which is slow, and everyone here thinks in that time. A child helps set the frame of a wall she will not shelter behind until she is grey herself. Nobody finds this strange. It would be strange to expect otherwise.

There is no metal. There is no clay to fire — the bottom is glass and the glass will not take a shape. There is the weed, and there is bone, and there is the black rock, which can be knapped into blades sharper than anything a smith could make but which shatter if you look at them wrongly. And there is the water, which does the rest.

The air is kept in bells. Where a vent breathes out gas, they set an inverted basket over it, and the basket silicifies into a dome, and the gas fills the dome and cannot escape, and they widen the basket's mouth downward with new frames, year after year, until the dome is a room and then a hall. A stranger swimming into the city sees them as pale lanterns hung from the ceiling of the dusk — dozens of them, hundreds in the old quarter, some so ancient their walls are a hand's breadth thick and ring like bells when a body brushes them. Inside, the air is stale and warm and tastes of eggs and iron. People sleep there, and cook over vent-heat routed through stone channels, and talk in the closed-mouth language that carries badly in air and beautifully in water.

They do not spend most of their lives in the bells. They spend them outside, in the grey, among the columns, holding their breath for a long time. Longer than you would think. Long enough that you begin to wonder whether "holding" is the right word, and then you notice that the older ones have a bluish cast at the lips and along the ribs that is not cold, and that they surface less than the children, and that the very old ones do not seem to surface at all, and you stop wondering, because it seems impolite.

The vegetation is what saves the place from being a tomb. The fissure-weed grows in bands along the warm cracks, tall as three men, ribbon-leaved, swaying in currents you cannot feel. It is dark red in the brief bright water near the surface and nearly black below; in the dusk it looks like standing smoke. Between the columns it has been let grow deliberately, so the city is a thicket, close and layered — stone pillar, weed, grey-skinned house-wall, weed, the pale underside of a bell, more weed, the next pillar. You cannot see far. You move through it by touch and by the shapes of the currents, and everyone is always close enough to reach.

Animals are few. The water is too warm and too strange for most things that would have come over the rim. There are small pale fish that live in the weed and are eaten, and a kind of soft armoured thing that crawls on the new lava and grazes the film off it and is not eaten because it tastes of the vents. There are the blind eels in the deep channels that nobody has seen except as a motion. That is nearly all. The city is full and the sea is empty; you notice the emptiness most on the edges, where the columns thin out and the weed thins out and there is only the black new floor going on into the grey, and the sense — real, not imagined — of it very slowly rising under you.

Sound: the milk carries it well, but there is not much to carry. The rock ticks as it cools. Gas from the vents comes up in threads of bubbles that make a sound like fine rain heard from inside a house. Someone knapping a blade, a long way off, is a series of dry clicks that arrive one at a time. Voices are low and continuous and almost never rise, because in water a raised voice hurts. At night the city is nearly silent and you can hear, if you lie against a column, the very faint high whine of the mountain under everything, which the people say is the sound of the floor being thought about before it is made.

Nothing here wears away. That is the rule, and it is not a comfortable one. Nothing is smoothed, nothing softened, nothing lost to slow attrition; everything only thickens, is added to, is covered. The dead are lashed into frames and become columns. Old quarrels become walls. A basket dropped and forgotten becomes a stone basket and is still there a hundred years on, exactly as it fell, only heavier. The people of the city say that the outside world — the one over the rim, where snow and wind take things down — is the strange one, a place where the past is always being taken away from you, and they do not envy it.

And over it all, every few years, without warning, a new tongue of the floor comes up somewhere on the western side, glowing under the milk like a coal under ash, and cools into a pillow the size of a hall, and the water goes briefly cloudy with new minerals, and the city's columns take on, that year, a slightly thicker skin. Then the dusk closes back over it. The grey resumes. The weed leans and rights itself. Somebody sets a frame.
