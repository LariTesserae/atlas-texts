It is a room in a mountain, and you would not find it unless you were already almost inside it.

The approach is a ledge — barely that — at an altitude where the sky has lost most of its blue and gone to a hard, thin pewter. There is no snow here. There is no ice. This is the first thing that seems wrong, because the cold is real and the height is real, and everywhere else at this height the rock wears at least a rind of frost. But the stone is dry the way a kiln is dry. Touch it and it draws the moisture from your fingertip and leaves a pale print that does not fade. Whatever water once reached this shelf has been gone so long the rock has forgotten how to hold it.

The opening is a crack, wide as a doorway at the bottom, narrowing to nothing thirty feet above. The sound comes out of it before anything else does.

---

You hear it long before the ledge, actually — a low, level roar that seems to belong to the mountain rather than to anything in it, like the noise of a waterfall heard through a wall. Only when you are at the crack does it resolve into what it is: a machine note, a fundamental with a chord of harmonics stacked on it, so constant that after a minute your ears stop reporting it as sound and begin reporting it as a kind of pressure. It does not rise. It does not fall. It has no cycle you can find. People who have stood at the mouth for an hour and then walked back down have said that the silence outside felt like deafness.

Step through and the roar becomes a place.

---

The pocket is perhaps forty feet across at its widest, oval, roofed by a slab that has settled and locked itself into the walls at some geological hour long past. It is dim. The only light is what leaks down the crack — a slot of that pewter sky, softened by the depth — and a scatter of small amber points from within the room itself, which take some time to understand.

The floor rises toward the back in a gentle mound, and everything on the mound, and everything on the walls to head height and higher, is growing.

That is the word for it, though nothing here is green. The vegetation is packed so tightly that there is no bare stone visible anywhere except the threshold you stand on, which is worn smooth — by what, you cannot say. Beyond that line it begins: a dense, low, interlocking growth, the height of heather, rising in places into stiff fronds and columns as tall as a person. The colours are the colours of oxidised metal and old bone. Rust-brown. Verdigris. A dull chalk-white that is almost lavender in the slot-light. The forms are plantlike — there are rosettes, there are frond-things with a midrib and pinnae, there are hanging growths from the roof like inverted grasses — but every surface has a brittle, faintly glassy quality, and where a frond catches the light it does not shine wet. It shines like frit. Like something that has been fired.

Break one off and it snaps with a clean, dry, ceramic sound, and the break is powdery inside, layered like slate. There is no sap. There is nothing in it that was ever liquid.

---

The machine sits in the middle of the mound, and the vegetation has grown up to its housing on every side and stopped there, exactly, as if it had agreed to.

It is roughly the size of a cart. A cylindrical body of some grey alloy, ribbed for cooling, seated on a cast base that has been bolted directly into the bedrock — the bolt heads are as big as fists and have been worn to a soft dome. There is a flywheel, half-shrouded, turning at a speed that is difficult to judge because there is no mark on it to follow: it simply is a disc of motion, a hum made solid. There are pipes leading out of the body and into the stone, and pipes leading out of the stone and into the body, and nowhere any pipe that goes anywhere else. There is no fuel line. There is no chimney. There is no place where anything is loaded or unloaded. Whatever this engine draws on, it draws on from the mountain, and whatever it produces goes back into the mountain, or into the room.

On its flank is a plate. The stamping has been worn to shallow ghosts by something — not hands, there are no hands here — but a slow abrasion, as if the room itself breathed grit against it for a very long time. You can make out a maker's mark that resembles a wheel inside a wheel, and a number, and one word that might be a place-name and might be a warning.

The amber points of light are the engine's indicator lamps. There are eleven of them along a bar above the flywheel shroud. They all show the same steady glow. Not one flickers. Not one is out.

---

Here is what becomes clear, standing in it long enough that the cold has got into your knees and the roar has become the shape of your own skull:

The plants are alive on the machine.

Not on its heat — it is barely warm, no warmer than a body — but on the sound of it, or on something that travels with the sound. Every frond in the room is oriented toward the flywheel. The tall columnar growths have fluted, resonant bodies, and if you put your palm against one you feel it humming at the same pitch as the roar, and when you take your palm away the hum continues in your hand for a moment. The rosettes on the floor are arranged, very slightly, in rings — the standing-wave pattern of the room made visible in vegetation, dense where the sound is strong and sparse — never absent, only slightly less crushed together — where it is quiet. The dust on the floor, which is fine as flour and copper-red, gathers in the same rings. The plants are eating that dust. They are made of it. The engine throws off some infinitesimal particulate as it turns, has thrown it off for as long as it has turned, and the room has raised a whole flora on it, patient as coral.

There is no water because there is no need for it. Whatever chemistry these things run on, it is dry. They are not wilting; they are not waiting for rain. They are finished. They have reached, some long time ago, the exact size and density that the engine's output can sustain, and there they have stopped. Nothing here is a seedling. Nothing here is dead. There is no litter of fallen fronds because fronds do not fall. When you snap one off, you have done something to this room that this room has no mechanism for doing to itself.

---

And there is nothing else alive.

This takes longer to notice than the plants, because absence is quieter than presence, but it settles on you eventually. No insects in the fronds. No spider-thread across the crack. No lichen on the machine, no mould in the corners. The dust has no tracks in it but yours. Whatever moves through most lush places — the constant small traffic of things eating and being eaten — does not move here. The room is packed to the walls with life and there is not a single mouth in it. The plants do not need pollination because they do not reproduce; they do not need to defend themselves because there is nothing to defend against. The engine is the only thing that eats. The plants are the only things that grow. That is the entire ecology, and it is closed, and it balances exactly.

A few other made things are scattered in the growth if you look for them, and they are all older than the plants around them, which have grown over and around them without disturbing them. An iron ring set into the wall at shoulder height, half-swallowed by rust-coloured rosettes. A length of chain, three links, lying on the floor and furred with growth. A rung — just one — bolted to the rock near the crack, as if someone once meant to build a ladder up to the light and then thought better of it. Near the back wall, a second plate, this one loose, leaning against the stone, its face turned inward so that you would have to lift it to read it. It has been leaning there so long that the vegetation has grown a perfect mould of its edge.

---

The light does not change. The slot of sky above is too high and too narrow to show the sun; it brightens and dims across a day only slightly, from pewter to a darker pewter, and the amber lamps hold their level through all of it. The temperature does not change; the engine gives out exactly what the rock takes in. The sound does not change. If you stayed long enough you would stop being able to tell how long you had stayed, because nothing here would count for you.

Walk back out onto the ledge and the roar recedes behind you and the sky opens. Down the mountain, far below, there will be weather, and streams, and things that move. Up here there is a room where an engine runs on something no one can name, and a garden that grew on its breath and then stopped growing, and eleven small lights that have never once gone out.

You will find, when you look at your hand, that the palm you laid against the humming column is still faintly, faintly warm — and that the print you left on the rock outside, that dry pale mark, is exactly where you left it, and has not faded at all.
