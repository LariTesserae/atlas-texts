The ground here is not finished.

That is the first thing, and the thing that everything else follows from. The basin is wide and low — you can see across most of it from the rim, a shallow bowl maybe fifteen kilometers across, floored with something that is on its way to being rock. It is grey with a faint liverish undertone, and where it has been recently exposed it is smooth and slightly translucent, like the skin over a healing wound. It rises. Not violently — nothing here is violent — but steadily, a few centimeters a week in the quiet zones, a meter in a night where it decides to. It comes up in domes and blisters and long welted ridges, and when it has been up for a month or so it hardens and clouds and becomes, more or less, stone. Nobody agrees what kind. The assay office keeps changing the labels.

Over this the city hangs.

It hangs because it has to. You cannot build a foundation on ground that is still choosing its shape, so the early settlers — and the settlement is not old, nothing here is old — drove pylons into the rim where the geology is at least honest, and strung a lattice across the whole basin: a roof of riveted girders and tension cable, three, four, in places seven stories deep, from which the buildings are suspended like fruit. Warehouses. Tenements. A foundry the size of a cathedral, held up on twelve braided cables as thick as a body, its floor a good four meters above the rising ground and lowered or raised by winch as the ground decides. Every building has a winch crew. Every building has a tilt gauge by the door, a little brass pendulum in a glass bell, and people glance at it the way people elsewhere glance at the weather.

It should be loud. That is what you expect, coming down the switchbacks from the rim road: a hanging industrial city on a heaving floor, cable-creak and winch-clatter and the shriek of metal under load. It is not loud. It is one of the quietest places you will ever stand.

The moss did that.

The new ground, when it comes up, is mineral-rich in a way that seems almost deliberate — the assay office has found things in it that plants want and shouldn't be able to get — and in this cold damp air anything that can grow on stone grows on it, fast. Within days of a blister rising it has a bloom of grey-green fuzz. Within a fortnight it is furred. Within a month it is upholstered in a layer of moss and liverwort a hand deep, spongy, saturated, cold as a cellar, and it does not stop at the ground. It climbs the cables. It coats the girders. It hangs in beards from the undersides of the suspended buildings and it gets in through every seam and it lines the interiors of the factories like the inside of a coat. The foundry runs its hammers at full stroke and from the street outside you hear a soft, repeated *thup*, like someone punching a pillow in the next room.

Everything here is padded. Footsteps go nowhere. Speech goes about two meters and stops. People talk low because talking loud accomplishes nothing and feels, after a while, obscene, like shouting into a mattress. The winch crews communicate with hand signals and coloured lamps. The trams — there are trams, running on rails bolted to the lattice overhead, electric, sparking blue in the damp — pass over you with a sound like a long exhalation.

And yet. Watch the city for an hour from any high point and you will see that it never stops moving.

The ground under the eastern warehouses came up in the night and the whole row is being winched, cable by cable, each building lifted a handspan and then the next, a slow cascade of adjustments running down the block. A blister has split under the market and the market has simply migrated — stalls unhooked from their girders, wheeled along the lattice on their overhead trolleys, rehung sixty meters west in the time it takes to eat breakfast. Three tenements are being re-cabled because the moss has grown into the braid and started to rot it, and the crews are up there in the girders with heated knives, cutting it back, and the smell of scorched vegetation drifts down and mixes with the smell of wet iron and the sweet mineral tang of the new ground. A ridge is rising through what was a street yesterday and a gang with pneumatic chisels is cutting it back — silently, the chisels sound like distant knocking — and loading the pieces onto a tram flatbed to be sold, because the new stone, before it clouds, is beautiful, and there is a trade in it. Someone's tilt gauge has swung past red and a bell is being rung, and the bell sounds like a spoon tapped against a cup.

It is a city in a permanent state of relocation, an anthill in slow motion, and it does it all in a hush. Thousands of people in motion and the loudest thing you will hear is your own breath.

The light is good. That surprises people too. You expect gloom under a seven-story roof of steel, but the lattice is open, and the sky above the basin is nearly always a high bright overcast, and the light that comes down through the girders is even and cool and grey-white, the light of a north-facing studio. The moss glows faintly green in it. Water beads on every surface — not rain, exactly, though it rains a few times a week in brief exhausted showers — but condensation, constant, the cold steel sweating in the damp air, drops running down the cables and collecting in the moss and wrung out by the moss's own weight into pools in the low places. There is no river. There are sumps: dark, still, stone-cold pools in the hollows between blisters, rimmed with a fringe of pale horsetail, and the city draws its water from them and worries about them because when the ground rises the sumps go with it, and a pool that fed a whole district can be lifted, tilted, and drained in a week.

Things live here. Of course they do. There is too much moss and too much cover and too much mineral wealth for things not to live here. Small dark birds with flat bills work the moss for insects, and there are a great many insects, slow and soft-bodied and cold, most of them silent. There is a kind of vole, or something vole-shaped, that tunnels the moss layer and never touches the stone and has a coat so thick with moisture it looks varnished. There are grazing animals on the rim slopes, long-legged, mild-eyed, that come down into the basin at dusk to browse the new growth and have learned to walk on the rising ground the way sailors learn to walk on deck. And there are the things people argue about: the pale slow shapes that are sometimes seen in the sumps, too large to be fish, that have never been caught and may be some peculiarity of the ground itself, some mineral eddy the eye makes into a body. The old winch hands say the ground dreams. The assay office says it does not. The assay office has also recategorized the ground's composition eleven times in four years.

There is a district on the southern side, older than most, where the ground rose past the reach of the winches and swallowed the lower floors of a whole street of tenements. The buildings are still there, still hanging from their cables, but their bottom two stories are set in stone now, clouded and hardened, the windows filled, the doorways blind. People live in the upper floors still. They come and go by the tram-rails. They have decided, collectively and without discussion, that it is fine.

The foundry does not make anything you would recognize. It works the new stone while it is still soft, before it clouds, presses it, casts it, and what it turns out is a kind of glass-hard, faintly warm material that the city uses for everything — bearings, insulators, the bells in the tilt gauges — and exports up the switchbacks in padded crates. The furnace is a source of some anxiety. There is a persistent belief that the ground rises faster around heat. There is no proof. The foundry is winched a little more often than anything else in the basin, and its crews are the best paid and the quietest, and they do not like to be watched.

In the late afternoon the overcast thins and a clean cold light slides in from the west, low under the lattice, and for twenty minutes the whole basin is lit from the side — every cable a line of gold, every beard of moss a green flame, the wet girders blazing, the new ground glowing through its skin like a lamp — and the winch crews stop and the tram halts on its rail and the whole packed silent city holds still, for once, and looks at what it is standing on. Then the light goes and the ground comes up somewhere with a soft crack like knuckles, and somewhere a bell taps against a cup, and everyone goes back to moving.
