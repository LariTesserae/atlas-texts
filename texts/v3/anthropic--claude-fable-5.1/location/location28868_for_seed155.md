Start with the ground, because the ground is the problem.

It is high here — high enough that the weather comes at you sideways, that a cloud can cross the plateau at the height of a doorway and leave every surface beaded. The air is cool the way a cellar is cool, and it stays that way, morning and afternoon. You would expect rock at this altitude. Scarp and scree, lichen at most. Instead the ground gives slightly under a boot, and springs back, and is warm.

The plateau is a plant. One plant. Not a forest that has closed over, not a mat of many growths woven tight, but a single organism whose body has spread and thickened over a span of time long enough that erosion has treated it as terrain. Wind has planed it. Rain has runnelled it. The surface is a rind — greyish, faintly ridged like the palm of a hand, and everywhere fissured into a pattern of shallow polygons where the growth cracked and healed and cracked again. Under the rind is a pale spongy pith, and under that, kilometres down, whatever the plant has instead of stone. There are no edges to it that anyone has walked to. The tableland runs to the horizon in every direction, level as a lake, and the horizon has the slight convexity of something enormous seen from on top.

Everything that grows here is it. The low tussocks that stipple the surface every few metres are its shoots, blunt and leafless, the colour of old lead. The ridges that rise here and there to the height of a wall are its veins, surfaced. The occasional hollow, wide as a village square and floored with a soft green plush, is a place where it has folded on itself and the fold has filled. There is nothing else. No grass in the plush, no moss on the ridges, no seed drifting from elsewhere that has found purchase. Whatever lands here is absorbed or dries and blows away.

Water is the one thing it does not make. The plant is a desert of itself. What moisture there is arrives as fog, condenses on the rind, collects in the polygon cracks and stands in them for a day or two before it is taken down. After a rain — brief, hard, and always from the same quarter — the whole surface darkens to the colour of wet slate and the veins stand out pale, and for an hour afterward you can hear the drinking: a soft crackle everywhere underfoot, like frost settling.

Then the town.

You see it first as chimneys. They are spaced very widely — one every four or five hundred metres — and they are the only vertical things, so the eye counts them the way it would count masts in a harbour. Some are smoking. The smoke is white and heavy and does not rise well in the cool air; it slides off along the surface and gathers in the hollows.

Closer, each chimney has a building at its foot, and each building is a mill or a pump house or a shed of engines, and each is made of the plant. Not built from it — grown. The walls are the rind, thickened and stood up; the roofs are a leaf-like sheet, ribbed and translucent, that lets in a diffuse ordinary daylight. Doors are places where the wall has been trained to thin and part. There are metal fittings — brackets, flywheels, valves, lengths of black pipe — and these are bolted straight into the living wall, and the wall has grown around the bolts and half-swallowed them. In places you cannot tell where the iron stops and the plant begins. A pipe enters a wall as a pipe and leaves it, ten metres on, as a vein.

The engines run on the plant. This took a while to understand. There are turbines in the pump houses, and they turn — a low steady heavy sound, ninety or a hundred revolutions a minute — but there is no fuel anywhere, no coal, no oil, and the smoke from the chimneys smells of sap and hot sugar. The plant's own circulation drives them. Somewhere below, the body of it is moving fluid across distances that would be measured in days, and the town has tapped the flow. Every pump house is a place where the pressure comes near the surface, and the mills are threaded on the pressure like beads on a string. What the mills make is less certain. They take in pith and cut it and press it and pass it through baths, and what comes out is a fibre, and the fibre is spooled onto drums the size of houses, and the drums are hauled off along the raised tramways that connect one building to the next.

The tramways are the only roads. They run on trestles two or three metres above the rind, dead straight between mills, and the rails are a hard dark cable that is also the plant. Cars go along them slowly, unmanned, at intervals of perhaps an hour. Between the trestles the ground is open and untended, and this is where the plateau's emptiness asserts itself: whole reaches of grey ridged surface with nothing on them but tussocks and the odd standing pool, and then a mill, and then more emptiness. It is a city in the sense that everything is connected and everything is working. It is not a city in any sense involving crowding.

People are few. You see one or two per mill, in oiled coats, doing the slow deliberate rounds of anyone who tends machinery that mostly tends itself. They do not look up. Animals are fewer. There are pale narrow birds that stand on the chimney rims, always singly, and never seem to fly, and once in a long while something low and quick crosses the rind between two ridges and is gone before it resolves. The plant does not feed anything. The town does not either. Whatever lives here lives on some margin that is hard to see.

The sound, though. The sound is everywhere and it is not quiet.

Under the turbines and the hiss of the baths and the long groan of the tram cables, under the wind that comes flat across the plateau and has nothing to break on, there is the plant. It is not silent the way a tree is silent. It is old enough and large enough that its processes have become audible — a deep, unceasing, many-layered noise that rises from the rind like heat, that is loudest in the hollows and near the veins, that has the character of a river heard through a wall, or of a very large building settling, or of breath. Sometimes it is only a pressure on the ears. Sometimes, when the fog comes in and the surface is wet and the pump houses are all running, it swells until you cannot hear yourself speak, and the mill hands put their hands over their ears and wait, and after some minutes it subsides. They say it is the plant drinking. They say it is the plant thinking. They say it is the mills, echoing in the pith. It is not the mills.

And here is where the plateau stops behaving.

The town is one town but it does not have one place. Walk the trestle from a mill to the next mill — a clear quarter hour on a straight rail with the destination in sight the whole way — and arrive, and it is the mill you left. Same brackets, same half-swallowed bolts, same hand in the same oiled coat doing the same round. Turn and look back along the trestle and the mill you left is still there, at the far end, smoking. Both of them are it. All of them are it, possibly; the plant is one plant and the town has grown into the plant and the town, grown, has inherited its indifference to distance. The mill hands do not find this remarkable. They will tell you the number of mills is thirty-one, or that it is one, or that it depends on the fog.

Shadows lie in the wrong direction near the veins. Not reversed — simply toward a light that is not the one in the sky, as if the surface remembers a different sun and casts by it. The standing pools reflect a plateau that is greener than this one, and closer to the horizon, and sometimes has more chimneys. When rain falls it falls normally, but on certain days, in certain hollows, it is taken down into the rind before it lands, and you stand in a rain that never reaches you and watch the ground darken from below.

None of this is violent. The plateau has been what it is for a very long time and has settled into it. The breakdown is not a fracture but a weathering, the same weathering that flattened the veins and healed the cracks — reality here has been worn smooth by age, and where it is worn thin, other things show through. The mills keep running. The fibre keeps spooling. The cable cars keep arriving, an hour apart, at a mill that is the mill they left.

Toward what passes for evening the fog comes back. It always comes from the same quarter, and it comes low, and for a while the whole plateau is a floor of grey plant and a ceiling of grey cloud with a metre of clear air between, and in that metre the chimneys stand cut off at the knee and the trestles run into nothing. The sound rises. The rind beads and darkens. The pale birds do not move. Somewhere a turbine changes note as the pressure under it shifts, and the mill hand nearest, who has been doing this longer than the fog, puts a hand on the wall to feel it — the way you would put a hand on the flank of something asleep — and waits to see which way the plant is going to turn.
