The first thing you understand about this place is that it has finished.

Not ended — finished, the way a sentence finishes. There was a range here once, and then hills, and then the long argument between rock and rain that lasted longer than most kinds of life, and the rain won so completely that it no longer needs to argue. What is left is a plain of stone so nearly level that water does not know which way to go. It goes everywhere. It goes nowhere. It sits.

You are standing on the shield. The rock underfoot is dark and dense and faintly banded, and if you crouch and look at it closely you can see that the bands are folded, tightly, like fabric pushed together, and then planed off flat across the top of the folds by something patient. The top of every fold and the trough of every fold sit at exactly the same height. That planing is the whole story of the place. Nothing did it. Time did it, using water as its only instrument.

The plain extends in all directions further than you can walk in a day. It is not perfectly flat — nothing is — but its unevenness is measured in the width of a hand over the length of a mile. Where the rock dips those few inches, water gathers into sheets. Where it rises those few inches, the stone stands damp and dark and bare. From a standing height you can see maybe eleven or twelve of these sheets before the haze takes the rest, each one a slightly different color depending on depth and what is growing in it, each one holding a piece of the sky slightly differently. They are not connected on the surface. They are all connected below.

That is the second thing to understand. The water table is not underground here in any meaningful sense. It is at the surface, or a finger's width beneath it, or a finger's width above. The rock is not porous but it is fractured — hairline joints running in two directions, meeting at angles, so that the whole plain is very faintly tiled — and the joints are full. Put your palm flat on a dry-looking patch of stone and lift it and there will be a print of wet where your skin drew moisture up through a crack too fine to see. Walk anywhere and your footsteps darken behind you and stay dark for a long time.

The air holds water the way the rock does. It is warm — not hot, but a warmth with weight to it, the warmth of a room that has been breathed in for a long time — and so saturated that the distinction between the surface of a pool and the air above it is mostly a formality. In the low light of early morning you cannot always see where a sheet of water begins. There is a mist that lies on everything at the height of your knees and does not rise and does not settle, and objects on the far side of it — a lone shrub, a stone that stands a little proud — have their lower parts erased. Later in the day the mist thins to a general softness. Everything you look at more than a few hundred paces off is seen through a medium. Edges are approximate. The horizon is not a line but a zone where the grey of the rock and the grey-white of the air agree to stop being different.

Vegetation is sparse because there is nothing for it to hold onto. Soil requires the slow breaking of rock into grains, and the grains here were made and carried off long ago; there is no gradient left to bring new ones and no upland left to make them. What grows, grows in the joints. A grass, sharp-bladed and dull green, in single tufts a stride or two apart, each tuft occupying one crack and no more. A low woody thing with leathery leaves the color of old bronze, which appears every few hundred paces, never in groups, never higher than your hip, its roots visibly splitting the stone it has chosen and then running along the surface until they find another crack. In the pools, a fine hairlike weed that lies flat along the bottom and trails when anything moves through. On the damp rock between, a skin of dark algae so thin it is more a stain than a plant, and where the algae is thickest a moss, deep green, in patches the size of a spread hand. That is nearly all. There is a tree — one — perhaps two hours' walk from where you stand, visible as a slightly darker smudge in the haze. It is not tall. It has probably been there for centuries. It has probably been alone for centuries.

And yet the place is loud.

This is the thing that takes longest to reconcile. A plain this empty and this old should be quiet, and instead the sound is continuous and layered and comes from everywhere. Most of it is frogs. There must be several kinds; there are at least four distinct calls, one a rapid dry ticking, one a low pulsing hum that seems to come from under the water rather than from anything on it, one a rising two-note whistle, one that sounds almost exactly like a stone dropped into a deep well. They do not call in waves or choruses. They simply call, all of them, all the time, and the sound moves across the plain as you do, so that the pools nearest you go briefly quiet and the ones behind you resume. Under the frogs is the insect layer — a shrill unbroken drone that seems to belong to the air itself, and beneath that a lower stridulating rasp that comes and goes. Above them both, at intervals, birds: a wader with long red legs standing at the edge of a sheet that gives a harsh repeated alarm when you come within a stone's throw and then goes back to walking, lifting each foot high and setting it down without a ripple. Small dark birds that fly low and fast between pools and make a sharp wet clicking. Once, from very far away, something with a call like a slowly turning hinge.

And under all of it, if you stand still long enough, the water. The plain is not silent when the animals are. The joints in the rock are always moving water from one place to another, very slowly, along gradients so weak that the flow would take a season to cross what you can cross in an hour, and in a few places the water finds a slightly wider fracture and comes to the surface as a seep. These are the weeping rocks. There is one a little way off — a low dark shelf, higher than the plain by the thickness of an arm, and along its lower edge a fringe of moss, and behind the moss the stone is glossy and the gloss is moving. Drops form at the bottom of the moss and fall into the sheet below. They fall about one every second, spread along the whole edge, so the sound is a soft irregular ticking, like a roof after rain has stopped. The shelf has been weeping like this for longer than the frogs have existed as a kind of animal. The stone beneath the drip has a shallow groove worn into it, smooth as a bowl.

There are more animals than the sound suggests, once you learn to look. Every sheet of water has a life in it and a life at its edges. Dragonflies, large ones, with bodies the color of wet slate, hunting the margins in straight lines and hard right angles. Water beetles moving in the weed. Small fish — very small, translucent, visible mostly as a flicker of shadow on the pale bottom — in the deeper sheets, which means the deeper sheets must sometimes connect, or that the fish have some other way of moving between pools that you have not seen. Snails on the algae-stained rock, hundreds of them, shells the size of a fingernail, each one leaving a clean track through the green stain like a path cleared through snow. Something has been eating the snails: there are little piles of emptied shells at the bases of certain stones, always the same stones. A turtle, once, or the back of one, a dark dome in a pool that sank when you looked at it and did not come up again while you waited. Long-legged spiders that stand on the surface of the water and go across it in bursts. Tracks in a patch of algal mud at the edge of one sheet, three-toed, a bird's, but a large bird, larger than the red-legged wader. You do not see it.

Everything living here is living at the edge of water and there is no place that is not the edge of water. So the plain is full. But it is full thinly. The frogs are in every pool and there are a dozen pools in view and still you can look for a long time at any one patch of ground and see nothing move. Presence here is spread out over the whole surface at a low and constant concentration, like the water. There is no place where it gathers. The sound gives the impression of multitude; the eye finds one thing, and then a while later, one thing more.

Nothing has been made here. You look for it, the way you always look — a straight line, a stacked stone, a clearing that is too clean — and it is not there. The straight lines are the joints in the rock, and they are not perfectly straight. The one tree is where a seed fell. The piles of snail shells are where something eats. There is not even a path, because there is nowhere in particular to go and nothing in particular to avoid; every part of the plain is as wet and as bare and as walkable as every other part. The absence of anything made is not noticeable as an absence. It is not that something is missing. It is that the category never arrived.

The light, for all the haze, is not dim. The sky is a high even overcast, bright, the kind of sky that casts no shadows but lights everything from all sides, and the water throws it back up so that the plain is lit from below as well as above. It is a light without direction. Objects have no dark side. The wader standing at the edge of its pool is the same grey-brown on its belly as on its back. The only strong shadows are under the overhangs of the few raised shelves, and even there the wet stone glimmers.

You wonder what changes here and the answer is almost nothing, and what does change does so on scales you cannot witness. There is rain, sometimes, and after rain the sheets are a little wider and the seeps run a little faster and the frogs are a little louder, and then over weeks the sheets draw back to their usual edges — not by draining, because there is nowhere for them to drain to, but by evaporation into air that is already nearly full, which is slow. The mist is thicker some mornings than others. The tree puts out leaves and drops them. The moss on the weeping shelf grows outward by the width of a thumbnail in a year and the groove beneath the drip deepens by nothing you could measure in a lifetime. The snails cross their patches of algae and the algae grows back behind them. The joints in the rock widen. This is the last stage of a landscape and it is also a stable one; there is nothing left to take away. It will look like this, more or less, for as long as there is rain.

Toward evening the overcast thins in one quarter and the light gains a slight warmth and, for the first time, a direction. Every sheet of water in that direction turns the same pale gold and every sheet behind you stays grey, and the plain suddenly has a near side and a far side. The frogs do not care. The ticking of the weeping shelf continues at its one-per-second. The red-legged bird lifts a foot and holds it, considering, and puts it down without a sound into water that has been standing on this stone since before there was anything with legs to stand in it, and the sky in the water shifts, and settles.
