The shelf is about the size of a cathedral floor, and it was under ice until roughly a human lifetime ago.

You can tell because nothing on it has been softened yet. The rock is grey-green and freshly broken, with the sharp planed faces that a glacier leaves when it plucks a block out and drags it a few metres and then, for no reason anyone was there to see, stops. Some of the surfaces still carry the long parallel scratches of stones that were pressed against them under the weight of the ice. If you kneel and put your palm flat on one of these faces at mid-morning it is warm on top and cold underneath in a way that seems impossible for a single stone to be, as though the block has not finished deciding which world it belongs to.

Above the shelf the headwall goes up another two hundred metres, and above that there is only sky. Below the lip of the shelf there is nothing for a very long way. The valley is down there somewhere, under cloud that fills it most mornings like milk poured into a bowl, and the shelf sits above the cloud in air so thin and clean that the light comes through it undiluted. There is no haze. Distant ridges thirty kilometres away have edges like cut paper. The snow patches in the shaded corners are so white they are hard to look at, and the shadows are the deepest blue you have seen, almost violet, so saturated they look like they have been painted on.

That is the first thing about the place: the light is not friendly. It is not warm. The temperature here rarely rises much above freezing even at noon in high summer, and yet the sun on your skin feels like something with weight to it. Things that live here are dark, or silvered, or they hide.

---

The moss is what the ice left behind, or rather what came after it.

It grows in the hollows where the meltwater collects — never much water, never for long, but every afternoon when the cloud rises out of the valley and touches the shelf it leaves droplets on every surface, and the moss drinks. It has spread in the fifty or sixty years since the ice retreated into a carpet that follows the exact shape of the old glacier's underside. Where the ice ground the rock into a shallow basin the moss is thick, almost spongy, a deep wet green that seems too rich for this altitude. Where the ice left a ridge the moss thins to a black crust of lichen and then bare stone. You can read the drowned shape of the vanished glacier in the vegetation, like the impression a body leaves in a bed.

There are cushion plants too, tight domes of tiny leaves packed so densely you could stand on them. They flower for perhaps two weeks in a year, small stars of white and pale pink pushed up from the surface of the cushion, and then they are done. Between the cushions grow stalks of a saxifrage with leaves rimmed in lime that the plant has excreted from its own tissue, so each leaf is edged in a fine chalky white line as though someone had traced it. There is a small willow that has decided to be a carpet instead of a tree, running its trunks along the ground under the moss where the wind cannot reach them, putting up leaves no bigger than a fingernail.

It is lush in the way a coral reef is lush: crowded, intricate, absolutely dependent on conditions that are only just adequate. It could all die in a bad decade.

---

The animals come from the sky.

This is the thing you understand only after you have been here a few hours and stopped expecting to see a herd, or a flock, or anything that gathers. The fauna of the shelf is abundant and it is almost entirely invisible, because it arrives one at a time and falls.

The valley below is warm and full of life, and the air above it rises every afternoon carrying insects up with it. Flies, aphids, small moths, spiders on threads of silk, beetles that opened their wings for a short hop and were taken. The rising air carries them over the lip of the shelf and into the cold, and in the cold they stall, and drop. They land on the snow patches and on the moss and on the warm tops of the plucked rocks, thousands of them across the afternoon, stunned or dead or slowly dying. The snow in the shaded corner of the shelf is peppered with them, small dark specks, and if you stand still you can watch new ones arrive, drifting down like a very sparse and very quiet rain.

Everything else on the shelf lives on this rain.

There are beetles here, carrion and ground beetles, black with a metallic sheen — bronze, deep green, oil-purple — that walk out onto the snow to collect the fallen. They are slow in the cold and move like clockwork toys, and their dark shells soak up the sun so that when you pick one up it is startlingly warm. There are spiders that have given up webs entirely and simply hunt on the rock. There are small wingless flies that have never needed to leave and would freeze if they tried.

And there are the birds. Snowfinches come over the lip in small silent parties and work the moss for the fallout, and there are wall-creepers on the headwall, crimson wings flashing open and shut against the grey as they climb. Twice a day a group of choughs arrives — yellow-billed, glossy, black as the shadows — and they land on the plucked boulders without a sound and walk about picking through the debris of the afternoon with the manner of people inspecting a market. Once in a while, higher up, something larger rides the updraft, an eagle or a vulture that has been up over the valley and is now being lifted almost against its will toward the ridgeline; it hangs there, makes an adjustment, and slides away out of sight.

None of it makes much noise. The birds do not sing here — there is nothing to sing to, no territory worth defending against a rival who might not exist. Sound does not carry well in the thin air and the wind takes what there is. Mostly what you hear is the tick of the rock as it warms and expands, and the drip of meltwater into a pocket somewhere under the moss, and your own blood.

---

People have been coming here for longer than the shelf has been bare.

They must have come when it was still ice, because the cairns predate the moss. There are eleven of them, small stacks of four or five stones each, in a line that runs from the notch in the headwall where a person might come down out of the pass above to the flattest part of the shelf near the lip. They are not markers for a path — there is no path, the shelf is too small to need one — and it took a long while to understand what they are for. Each one stands where the hail piles up. When the afternoon cloud comes over and drops hail, as it does perhaps one day in three, the wind sorts the hailstones into drifts against any obstacle, and the cairns stand each in a small white bank of it. In the morning the banks melt into pools that last for an hour. Someone worked out where the water would be and marked those places so that when they came up here they would not have to search.

There is a shelter, if you can call it that: a curve of stacked stone against the base of the headwall, waist-high, roofless, just enough to break the wind for two people lying down. Inside it the rock floor is blackened in one patch, and there are fragments of something burnt that is not wood, because there is no wood for a thousand metres in any direction. Dried dung, carried up. Beside the fire-patch there is a flat stone with a shallow polished depression in it and a rounded pebble lying in the depression. Someone ground something here. Seeds, perhaps, or the saxifrage, or pigment.

Under a lip of rock near the shelter there is a small cache: three flakes of a glassy dark stone that does not occur anywhere on the mountain, edges still sharp enough to cut, wrapped in a piece of hide gone stiff as bark. Whoever left them intended to come back. Whether that was last year or before the ice retreated is not something the place will tell you.

Nothing is broken. Nothing is ruined. The structures are so slight that there is nothing for them to become; they are already as close to the rock as made things can be.

---

The day has a shape here, and the shape is the same every day, and every day it is different.

Dawn comes without warning because there is nothing to the east but a lower ridge and then air. The sun simply appears, and within a minute the shelf is flooded with light so intense the moss seems to steam, and in fact it does: the frost that formed overnight on every surface lifts off in a low white breath that rolls across the shelf toward the lip and vanishes over the edge. The blocks of plucked rock begin to tick. The beetles crawl out from under them. The pools at the base of the cairns start to grow.

By mid-morning the valley cloud below is thickening, and by early afternoon it has risen to the level of the lip, and then it is on the shelf, cold and grey and dripping, and the light does not dim so much as diffuse into a white glare that comes from every direction at once and casts no shadow at all. This is when the insects fall thickest. This is when the hail comes, if it comes, hard and brief. The birds go somewhere. The beetles freeze in place. For an hour the shelf is a white room with soft walls and no sound except ice striking stone.

Then the cloud sinks back or tears apart, and the sun is low and gold and the shadows have swung around to lie the other way, long and blue, and the choughs come back to collect what fell. The moss is wet and dark and glittering. The snow patches have shrunk by a finger's width and are speckled black with the day's dead.

Cold arrives with the shadow of the headwall. The pools skin over. Frost forms. The stars come out in numbers that make no sense, and the shelf, which was a room, becomes a ledge on the side of something very large and very high, with the whole sky pressing down on it and the whole valley breathing cloud up at it from below.

One more day of this and the shelf is one day older, the moss a fraction wider, the rock a fraction more worn. In a thousand years it will be soft here, rounded, grassed, ordinary. Right now it is still sharp, still surprised to find itself in the open air, and everything that lives on it lives on what the sky lets fall.
