Low in the basin, where the ground has already used up its elevation and has nothing left to fall, there is a hole in the crust roughly the size of a cathedral. From above it looks like nothing—a dark eye in a field of fractured black glass—and the heat rising out of it makes the air above wobble so that even the darkness is hard to look at directly. You would have to be told it was there. Once inside you would not need to be told anything.

It is a bubble. That is the simplest way to say it. Long ago a body of molten rock exhaled and the breath got stuck under a skin that hardened around it, and the skin held, and the interior cooled into a chamber of curved obsidian walls perhaps eighty paces across and forty high, with a floor of fine black grit and a ceiling that sweats a dim, steady red where the rock is still thin enough to let the underneath show through. That red is the only light. It is not much. It is enough to see your hands, enough to see the shapes of things, not enough to read by or to be sure of a color. Everything here is a version of black or a version of ember.

The heat is constant and total. It comes up through the floor, out of the walls, down from the ceiling. There is no shade because there is no direction the heat is not arriving from. The air is so dry that it has stopped being a fluid and become something more like a pressure—a warm, hard substance you have to push through. Nothing here has ever been wet. Not the rock, not the growths, not the dust. If you brought a drop of water in on your skin it would be gone before you noticed, and the place would not notice either.

---

Someone built a town in here.

That is the first thing that becomes clear as the eyes settle. The curved walls are not smooth; they are terraced, cut and stacked, carved back in shelves and galleries that ring the chamber in irregular tiers, seven or eight deep from floor to the beginning of the dome. The lowest tier is cut directly from the glass, rough and old, the tool-marks blunted into a dull satin by long exposure to hot moving air. The tiers above are built rather than cut—blocks of dense volcanic tuff, hauled from somewhere, fitted without mortar, the joints so weathered that the blocks seem to have grown into one another. Above those, the highest shelves are made of something lighter and more porous, pumice or sintered dust, laid in courses that are visibly younger and visibly hastier. You can read the chronology going up: patience at the bottom, urgency at the top.

Doorways open onto every tier. Hundreds of them, each low enough that you would stoop, each opening onto a chamber cut back into the wall. Ladders of dark, glassy wood—wood, though there is no tree here that could have made it—connect one tier to the next, and stone lips and worn footholds connect the ladders. There are hearths, though no one here could have needed a hearth. There are grinding hollows worn into the floor of the lowest gallery, and a long stone trough, and shelves cut into the walls at the height of a seated person's reach. There are drying racks, absurdly, in a place where nothing has ever been damp.

There is no one. There has been no one for a long time. The doorways are dark and the chambers behind them are empty, or hold only grit. It is a town in the sense that a shell is an animal: the shape is precise and total and speaks of a body, and the body is gone. The density of construction against the absence of anyone in it produces a particular feeling—the whole place is *ready*, arranged, provisioned with the furniture of a life, and hollow through and through.

---

And it is loud.

The floor of the chamber is not solid. It is a plain of grit and slag over a lattice of fissures, and through the fissures the ground breathes. The breath comes up in a cycle you can time—a long inhale of near-silence, then a swelling pressure you feel in the sternum before you hear it, then a roar as gas forces up through the cracks and through the tall standing flutes of vitrified rock that rise here and there from the floor like organ pipes. Some of the flutes are natural. Some have clearly been shaped—their mouths chipped to a particular width, their heights trimmed. The builders tuned them. Whether they did it to make the sound bearable or to make it beautiful is not recoverable, but the effect is that the roar has pitch in it, a set of low tones that beat against each other, and the curved walls take those tones and send them around the ring of galleries so that every empty doorway hums at its own frequency. Then the breath gives out and the whole chamber ticks and clicks as hot stone shifts against hot stone, and then the silence stretches, and then the pressure gathers again.

This happens perhaps every few minutes. It has been happening for longer than the town has existed. The town was built around it, into it.

---

Between the flutes, on the floor and up the lower walls and crowding the mouths of the lowest doorways, is the forest.

It is thick. That is the thing that does not fit, that the mind keeps snagging on—that in a place with no water at all, something grows in abundance. The growths stand waist-high to head-high, a dense field of them, branching and rebranching, and in the red light they read as black stems bearing heavy clusters of something paler. Up close the paler parts are a dull yellow, sulfur-yellow, and they are not leaves or fruit but crusts, blooms, encrustations that form where the growth meets the gas rising from the fissures. The stems are hard and slightly translucent, a kind of glassy resin. When they are cool—which is never, but when they are less hot—they are rigid. When the breath comes up and the heat spikes they go faintly soft and lean, all together, toward the strongest venting, and the yellow blooms brighten as fresh mineral is deposited on them. You can watch them grow. Not quickly, but visibly: a bloom that was a bead becomes a cluster over the course of an afternoon, if there were afternoons here.

They live on the breath. They take what comes up—sulfur, tars, whatever the deep rock exhales—and build themselves out of it. There is no chlorophyll, no sap, no transport of anything dissolved. They are closer to a slow-grown crystal than to a plant, but they branch like plants, they compete like plants, the taller ones shade the shorter from the heat and gas they both need, and the shorter ones grow thin and pale and lean sideways looking for an opening. Nothing eats them. Nothing has ever eaten them. There is not a single moving body in this place that is not rock or growth or air—no insect, no lizard, no bird finding its way down through the eye in the ceiling. When a stem gets too heavy with bloom it snaps, and falls, and lies where it fell until the heat crumbles it into the grit, and the grit is what the next growth roots in. That is the entire ecology. It is complete. It has been running in balance for a very long time.

The forest also makes noise. The stems crack as they heat and cool—a constant background of small sharp reports, like a fire that has been left to burn down, except there is no flame anywhere. When the breath surges and the whole field leans at once there is a long creaking groan, thousands of resin stems flexing together. When the breath dies and they straighten it comes again in reverse. Under the roar of the flutes you might not separate it out, but in the ticking silences between, the forest is what you hear.

---

The builders must have harvested it. The glassy black ladders are made from the thickest old stems, straightened and joined—that is where the wood came from. The grinding hollows have a yellow stain worn into them that is the color of the blooms. The drying racks, then, were not for drying; they were for curing, for letting the resin set. Somewhere along the tiers there will be a room that held the results, and it is empty now, and what the results were is anyone's guess.

They lived low. The bottom galleries are the most worn, the most used, the closest to the forest and the flutes and the heat. The higher you climb the less worn the stone, the more the doorways open on chambers that were finished but never occupied, as if the town kept growing upward on the assumption of a population that did not arrive. At the very top, where the porous blocks meet the glowing curve of the dome, the last courses are unfinished—blocks set out in a line and never laid, waiting.

The dome itself is not stable. That is one more process running here among the others. Every so often a scale of the ceiling lets go—a plate of glass the size of a door, thinned from beneath by the heat, cracked from above by the cold of the surface—and falls. You can find them on the floor, half-buried in grit, their edges rounded by long exposure. Where they landed on the forest the growths have simply come up around them. Where they landed on the town there are galleries with their roofs stove in and their rubble tidied, long ago, to the side. The town was built to be repaired. The repairs stopped. The falling did not.

So the chamber breathes, and roars, and ticks. The forest leans and straightens and cracks and grows and falls and becomes the ground. The ceiling glows and thins and sheds. The heat pours in from every side and goes nowhere, because there is nowhere for it to go. And around all of it, ring on ring, the doorways stand open and dark and ready and empty, and hum in the red, each in its own note, every few minutes, for no one.
