Start with the heat, because that is what arrives first, before the eyes have anything to do.

It comes up through the soles of the feet. The floor is rock, and the rock is warm the way a body is warm, and the air above it is warmer still, thick, unmoving, somewhere past the point where the skin stops noticing the boundary between itself and what surrounds it. There is no wind here. There cannot be. The chamber is closed on all sides but one, and the one is far above, a slot of no particular shape where the surface split and let a little of itself fall in.

The eyes take a long time. Long enough to become uncertain whether the darkness is total. Then the shape of the space begins to suggest itself, not as a picture but as a set of guesses: taller than it is wide, fifteen or twenty meters across at the floor, narrowing upward like the inside of a keel turned over. A fissure. Young. The walls have not had time to soften. Where the rock parted it parted cleanly along planes that are still sharp enough to cut, and the faces of the break carry a faint glassy skin that gives back what little light there is in thin, unexpected lines, like the edges of a broken bottle in a cellar.

The light, when it is finally located, is a work lamp.

It hangs at chest height from a cable that runs up into the dark and disappears without explanation. The bulb is caged. The cage is corroded to a color that is not quite orange and not quite green. The lamp is not bright — it is the brightness of a thing that has been burning for a very long time on a circuit no one thought to close — but in a place like this it is the only argument for the existence of color, and everything in the chamber arranges itself around it whether it means to or not.

What has arranged itself most is the vegetation.

This is the strange part, and it is worth standing with for a while, because at first the mind refuses it. Plants in the dark. Lush plants, heavy, hanging. But look at where they come from. They come from above. The slot in the ceiling is a wound in the floor of a forest, and the forest did not stop being a forest when the ground opened under it. Roots that were headed down kept heading down. They found air instead of soil and did not care, or did not know, and they went on searching for water in the only way roots know how, which is by growing. So the upper third of the chamber is a curtain — hundreds of roots, some hair-fine, some as thick as a forearm, descending twenty and thirty meters through open air, pale where they have never touched light, tan and knotted lower down where the lamp has reached them for years and the tissue has thickened in response. They hang perfectly still. Some have found the walls and flattened against them and spread in webs. Some have found each other and braided. Some have found the pipes.

The pipes are the other thing.

Three of them, iron, each perhaps the diameter of a dinner plate, entering through the eastern wall at a slight downward angle, running the width of the chamber about two meters off the floor, and vanishing into the western wall through bored holes still ringed with the marks of the drill. They were put here to carry something — heat, most likely; this fissure was a gift to somebody, a place where the deep warmth came close enough to tap — and they are still carrying it. Hold a hand near one and the air is different. Not scorching. Deliberate. Two of the three are dry. The third sweats: a steady beading along its underside where it passes beneath the thickest fall of roots, and that is where the water in this place comes from, or most of it, gathered in slow drops that swell and hang and finally give way to drip onto the rock below and darken it in a shape that has become permanent.

That shape has become a garden.

In the circle of the lamp, on the dampened floor beneath the sweating pipe, things have grown that do not belong here and did not arrive by root. Seeds, probably, carried in on the mud of boots, or blown down the slot on some rare day when the wind above was in the right quarter. They are not the plants of the forest overhead. They are what happens to ordinary plants when they are given endless warmth, a little water, and almost no light at all: stems stretched long and colorless, leaves reduced to thin pale flags, everything leaning, everything reaching toward the caged bulb with a patience that has bent some of the stalks into shallow curves. A few have made it. They lean against the cage itself, their upper leaves faintly yellowed where the metal has warmed them. Around the base of the lamp the growth is dense, a low tangle of white and cream. A meter out it thins. Two meters out there is only bare rock, and the occasional single stem standing alone like a survey marker, and then the dark.

That is the pattern of the place, once seen: everything gathered around the one light, and between the gatherings, a great deal of nothing. The roots overhead. The garden below. The pipes crossing between them. And elsewhere — spaced out across the floor, along the walls, in the corners where the fissure pinches closed — the scattered evidence that people came, worked, and did not come back. A steel ladder bolted to the northern face, reaching upward into the dark toward a point where it must once have met something, now meeting only root. A coil of cable. A valve wheel, painted red, the paint blistered. A tin lunchbox on a ledge, lid open, the inside furred with a pale bloom that in this light is impossible to identify as mineral or growth. None of these things are near each other. Each one has its own small territory of silence.

And it is silent. Not the ringing silence of a sealed room, because the slot admits some faint, filtered version of the surface — a change in pressure when weather passes over, a sense of the day turning without any of its light — but the deeper silence of a place where nothing moves of its own accord. No insects. Nothing in the garden has ever been eaten. The leaves are whole. No bird has found the slot. No bat. Whatever the heat is, or whatever the pipes are carrying, or whatever the rock breathed out when it first cracked open, it has kept animal life away entirely, and the plants have the chamber to themselves.

The only sounds are the drips. They fall at intervals that seem irregular until one has stood long enough to hear the cycle behind them: the sweating pipe is warmest in what would be the early afternoon on the surface, when whatever engine drives it somewhere far off is working hardest, and the beading comes faster then, and the drips fall at perhaps one every eight or nine seconds. By night — if night is the right word for a place that is always night — they slow to one a minute, then fewer. The garden knows this cycle. It is written in the growth rings of the thickest stems, if one were to cut them, in bands too fine to see without a lens.

The other sound is not a sound so much as a presence in the ear. The pipes hum. Very low, very steady, a vibration that comes up through the iron from wherever the iron leads, and that the roots wrapped around them have taken into their own tissue, so that if one lays a palm flat against a braided root two meters from the nearest pipe, the hum is there, faint, patient, carried through wood.

Everything here is patient. That is what the place teaches, standing in it long enough for the sweat to run and the eyes to finish adjusting and the lamp to become, by contrast, almost bright. The fissure is young — a few decades, no more; the rock says so, and the ladder says so, and the lunchbox says so — but it has been young for long enough that things have found their way into it and begun, very slowly, to change it. The roots will reach the floor eventually. They are perhaps a third of the way down the walls now, and the rate is not fast, but neither is it zero. The garden will spread as far as the water reaches and no farther. The lamp will dim. It is already dimmer than it was, though there is no one to remember the comparison. When it goes out the reaching plants will die, and the roots will not notice, and the pipes will go on humming and sweating in the dark for as long as the engine somewhere above them goes on running, and the drips will fall onto the rock, and the rock will warm the soles of no one's feet.

For now, though, there is the lamp, and its small pale crowd of leaves, and the curtain of roots hanging motionless in the heat, and the three iron lines crossing the space like the staff of a piece of music with only one note on it, held.
