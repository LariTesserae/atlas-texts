The ground here is eleven years old.

You can tell from the edges. The drainage cuts that grid the flat run straight as rulers and their sides are still vertical, still showing the spade-mark and the auger-score, clay the color of a bruise that hasn't yet learned to slump. Nothing has rounded. Water has been moving through these channels for a decade but it moves slowly, and it has only had time to lay a skin of fine grey silt along the bottom and grow a beard of green on the north walls. In another fifty years the banks will have relaxed into curves. For now the whole floor of the place has the sharp, unsoftened look of a thing that has just been unwrapped.

The place is a bowl about a mile across, and its rim is a dike. On the top of the dike runs the ring canal, and the ring canal is the first thing that tells you something is slightly wrong with the geometry: the water is above you. Barges go past at roof height. From the pumping floor you look up at the underside of a hull sliding along the sky, and the boatman leaning on his tiller can see down your chimney. This is not a mistake. The bowl was a lake, and the town at the bottom of it exists to keep it from being a lake again, and every drop that seeps into the clay or falls from the sky is lifted forty feet back into that canal and sent away. The lifting is the sound.

It is very loud. It does not stop. Six pump-houses stand across the flat with a quarter-mile of nothing between each, brick with iron roofs, each with a short fat stack and a fan-louvered cupola, and from each one comes the same compound roar: the low electric hum of the motors in the ground, the wet chugging of the centrifugal pumps, and over it the actual falling water — the outfall pipes climb the dike face on trestles and discharge into the canal from above, so there are six permanent waterfalls pouring upward and then down, white, fanning in the wind. Stand between two houses and the two roars interfere and beat against one another with a slow throb you feel in the teeth. People who live here speak in a raised flat voice and do not notice. Dogs do not bark, because there is no point.

And yet nothing happens. That is the odd thing about the noise — it is enormous and it is standing still. The pumps have been running at this speed since the year the lake bed first showed. The water table falls perhaps a finger's width a year. The buildings are settling into the clay at a rate the engineers measure with brass pins set into the brickwork and a survey every autumn, and each house leans at its own faint angle, none of them more than a degree or two, none of them the same. The canteen has a step at the door that used to be level with the ground. The chapel — small, brick, with its own token stack because it was built in the same yard as everything else — has a crack up its west face that they have filled three times, each filling a slightly different grey. Everything is very slowly going down while the roar goes on above it, and the effect is of a great effort being made to hold something exactly in place.

It is cool at the bottom of the bowl. Cold air pools here the way water did, and the dike keeps the wind off, and the spray from the outfalls comes back down as a mist you can't see but can feel on the backs of your hands. Iron sweats. The rails that connect the houses — narrow-gauge, for the coal wagons and the greasing trolley — are wet at nine in the morning on a cloudless day, and beaded with drops on their undersides. The condensation is the main weather. It rains, but not much; the true water of this place is the water that comes up out of the clay overnight and stands in the wheel-ruts and the low corners, and is gone by noon, and back by the next morning. The brick has taken on a permanent dark tidemark to the height of about two feet. Above that it is orange-red and new-looking; below it, black-green and old before its time.

Lake bottoms are rich. Whatever died in the water for a thousand years is in this clay, and the flat, wherever it has not been paved or gravelled, has gone to green with an almost embarrassing eagerness. Alder and grey willow have come up along every channel, thin whippy trees no older than the ground, already twelve feet tall. Reed stands as high as a man in the wet corners. Moss covers the north sides of the culverts in a pelt an inch thick. Between the pump-houses, where the surveyors' stakes still stand in their rows, the grass has gone tall and coarse and full of meadowsweet and the yellow of flag iris, and nobody cuts it, because there are not enough people and the machines do not need it cut. The town is perhaps forty houses, in two straight rows along the central rail, with gaps where houses were planned and not built. The gaps are full of willow. From the dike top, the settlement looks less like a town than like a set of instruments placed on a green table at careful intervals.

What has come to live in all this is a surprise even to the people who work here. The bowl is a trap for anything that wants damp and cover and does not mind noise, and it turns out a great many things do not mind noise. Herons stand in the channels at the culvert mouths where the flow concentrates the small fish, spaced out along the cuts as regularly as the pump-houses. Eels came up the outfall pipes — nobody quite knows how, against that fall of water — and are now in every channel, and the men catch them in the settling tanks with a bucket. Frogs by the thousand; on a warm evening the pumps are briefly and improbably out-sung. Swallows nest under every iron eave, and swifts in the cupola louvers, and in the roof void of Number Four house a colony of bats that comes out at dusk over the outfall in a shape like smoke. Rats, obviously, glossy and unafraid. Crows sit along the trestles and study the men. A pair of otters has a holt somewhere in the dike face, and the greaser on his trolley has learned where to slow down so as not to startle them, and does. In the tall grass between the houses there are hares, and the hares have gone deaf, or have never known anything but the roar and so do not consider it to be sound, and will sit up in the meadowsweet and look at you from ten feet away with an expression of mild interest.

The men and women who run the place live in the houses at one end and the engineers, when they come, stay in the other. There is a school with nine children in it. The children have grown up inside the noise and speak in the flat loud voice and sleep through anything. When the pumps at Number Two were stopped for two hours one winter to change a bearing, three of them woke up crying and did not know why.

Light comes down into the bowl clear and grey and even, with nothing to interrupt it except the stacks and the passing hulls. It is the light of a place that has no shade of its own yet — the trees are too young to cast much — and so it lies flat on the clay and the brick and the wet iron and picks out every fresh edge, every straight-cut bank, every angle of every leaning wall, and the white, endless, upward-pouring water at the rim.
