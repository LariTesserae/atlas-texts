Start with the ground, because the ground is the first thing that is wrong.

It is flat, in the way a floor is flat rather than the way a plain is flat — there is an intention in it. It is pale, the green of old celery gone to grey, and it is warm through the soles of your feet, warmer than the air. It gives very slightly. Press a thumbnail into it and a bead of clear liquid rises and stands there, trembling, then slides away along a seam you hadn't noticed. The seams run in long parallels, thumb-width, with a faint ridge to each, and if you follow one with your eye it goes out across the level and does not stop until distance takes it.

There is no soil. There is no stone. Kick at a rough patch and you find more of the same beneath it, browner, denser, laminated like the pages of a book that has been wet and dried and wet again — and still alive, still sweating when you expose it. The plateau is not on top of anything. It is the plateau.

You are high. You know it from your breath, from the blue that is too dark overhead, from the way the sun lands on the back of your neck with the flat force of something that has come a long way and lost nothing on the journey. And yet the air at your knees is thick as a laundry, because the ground is exhaling. Every square metre of it is breathing out warm vapour, and the vapour cannot decide whether to rise or to lie down, so it does both: a haze at ankle-height that shivers, and columns above it that lean and dissolve. Everything within reach is beaded. The drops have a faint sweetness if you taste them — not sugar exactly, more the smell of a cut stem, something green and unfinished.

Now lift your eyes, because the ground has been the whole of your attention and the place is not small.

The shelf runs out for kilometres in every direction before it ends, and where it ends it ends sharply, a lip, and beyond the lip there is nothing but cloud, a white floor a long way down, so that the whole plateau seems to be laid on top of weather. Standing on the level you can count perhaps eleven, twelve things that are not the level. They are far apart. Between the nearest two there is a walk of half an hour across nothing, across the breathing grey-green, with your own shadow the only upright in it. Their scatter is not random — you feel that without being able to prove it — but it is not a grid either. It is the spacing of things that need room.

The nearest is a colonnade. Or the beginning of one: nine columns in a row, then a gap where a tenth should be, then two more, then the row simply stops. The columns are the same tissue as the floor, darker, ribbed, and they rise from it without a joint — there is no base, no line where column meets ground, the floor simply gathers itself and goes up. They are each about the height of two people. Their edges are sharp. That is the thing you keep coming back to, walking among them: everything is sharp. The fluting is crisp as if cut this morning. Nothing has been softened. No rain has rounded these, no wind has scoured them, nothing has grown over them or into them, and you understand slowly that this is because none of it has existed long enough to be worn. This is a young place. The whole shelf is young. It is being made right now.

You know this because it is loud.

The loudness comes first as a mistake — you take it for your own blood, for the thin air ringing in your ears. Then a column at the far end of the row cracks. Not breaks: cracks, with a report like a green branch bent past its limit, and the sound goes across the plain and comes back off something you can't see. A fissure opens along its length. Sap runs out of it, a lot of sap, pouring in a sheet down the fluting and pooling at the base where the floor drinks it. The column leans. Over the next twenty minutes, with a long groaning that you feel in your teeth, it folds, slumps, and is taken back down into the level, which closes over it with a sound like a wet sheet being wrung. Where it stood there is a low welt, already smoothing.

And at the other end of the row, the tenth column, the one that was missing, is coming up.

It rises the way a plant rises when you watch it in time-lapse, except you are not watching in time-lapse, you are standing there in the heat with your shirt stuck to your back. A boss swells from the floor. It squares itself. It reaches. The fluting appears as it climbs, as though it were being drawn up through a mould, and all the while it ticks and creaks and every so often gives a snap and a spray of fine droplets that hang in the air and catch the light. By the time it is as tall as its neighbours the ninth has begun to weep, and you realise you have been standing inside a sentence that is being written and erased at once, and that the colonnade will never be complete, that complete is not the point.

You start to walk, and the walk is where the scale of the thing arrives.

The level is not as level as it seemed. It has swells in it, long slow ones, and they are moving — a ridge two hands high passes under you in the course of a few minutes, lifting and setting you down, and you turn to watch it travel on toward the lip, where it will do whatever ridges do there. Far off to the left something is happening that involves a great deal of noise: a dark mass is coming up out of the floor, blunt, the size of a house, and it is tearing as it comes, fibrous ropes of it snapping with reports you can hear from a kilometre off, and steam is rolling up around it in a column that leans over in the high-altitude wind. It has the outline of a building. It has, as it heaves, the beginning of a doorway in one face, a dark rectangle, and a run of steps going up to it that come into being tread by tread from the top down, so that for a while they are stairs that begin in the air and end nowhere, and then the floor rises to meet them.

Nothing will go through the doorway. There is nothing here that goes through doorways. You have not seen a bird. You have not heard an insect, and at this heat, in this wet, the absence of insects is a pressure on the ear as strong as the noise. Nothing crawls on the columns. Nothing has ever nested in them. No seed has been carried here by anything; whatever this is, it makes itself, all of it, without help — pollen, if it has pollen, moving only in the steam. The loudness is the loudness of a single thing working alone. Cracks, hisses, the long tearing of tissue, the pour of sap into hollows; and beneath all of it, if you stop and let your feet feel it, a hum that is not sound so much as the whole shelf under load.

You come to a cistern. It is exactly what it looks like: a rectangular basin, edges knife-clean, full to the lip with liquid clear as glass and warm as a bath. Steam lies on it. The floor slopes toward it in a gentle drainage that is too precise to be accident, and the seams you noticed at the start all run this way, feeding it. You crouch. The bottom is the same pale tissue, and it is pulsing — very slowly, a swelling and settling, and with each settling the level in the basin drops a finger's width and with each swelling it refills. The cistern is drinking what it holds and giving it back. It is not storing water. It is thinking about water, over and over, the way you might turn a thing in your hand.

Further on, the level tears open ahead of you without warning. A crack runs across your path with a sound like a sheet of ice giving, and stops, and steam blasts up from it, hot enough to make you step back — and through the steam you see down into the shelf, a metre, two metres, the layered brown of the older tissue, and something in the depths moving, a slow pale turning like a limb finding a new position under a blanket. Then the edges of the crack begin to come together. Within a quarter of an hour it is a seam like the others, ridged, weeping a little, and you would not know.

The afternoon does what afternoons do here. The cloud below the lip, which has been lying quiet all morning, begins to rise. It comes up over the edge in a long roll, cool at its leading face, and the whole shelf leans toward it — you see the columns of the far colonnades tilt a degree, you feel the floor under you go taut — and then the cloud is on you and the heat turns to a wet warmth that is almost worse, and every surface streams. The shelf is drinking the sky. The noise changes pitch: fewer cracks, more pouring, a great soft hiss like a kettle that has been taken off too late. In the white you can see nothing but the next few metres of floor and the columns immediately around you, running with water, and it is possible, for a while, to believe you are standing in the ruin of something rather than in its making.

Then the cloud goes over and down the other side, and the sun comes back onto your neck, and you can see again that the eleven structures are not the eleven you counted. Two are gone. One is new — a wall, a single long straight wall a kilometre off, waist-high, running from nowhere to nowhere with the beautiful useless confidence of a first line on a page. A third has changed shape while you weren't looking, gained a second storey, lost its steps.

You could stay a long time working out the grammar. There is one — you are sure of it — the way the columns come in rows of nine, the way every cistern is oriented the same, the way the stairs always come down from the top. It is not building anything you could live in. It is not building anything for anyone. It is high and hot and alone above the cloud and it is rehearsing, over and over, in a hurry, with the sharp edges and the loud voice of something that has only just begun and does not yet know what it is for.

Underfoot the floor lifts you and sets you down, and a bead of the sweet water rises at your heel and runs away along its seam toward the nearest basin, where it will be thought about, and given back.
