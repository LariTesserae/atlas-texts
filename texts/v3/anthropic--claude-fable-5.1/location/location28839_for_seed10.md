The plateau is the oldest thing here. Whatever mountain it was has been rained on for so long that it no longer has a shape—only an altitude. It sits a little above the cloud deck in most weather, a flat slab of grey stone the size of a county, and the stone is worn past smoothness into something closer to softness, the way a word loses its edges when it has been said too many times. There are no ridges. There are no boulders. There is nothing on the plateau that would cast a shadow if there were enough light to cast one.

Water lies on it in a sheet an inch deep, and this sheet is not still, though there is no wind. It trembles in the way of a surface over machinery.

You notice the sound before you understand it. The plateau is loud. It should not be; it is empty as far as you can see in every direction except one, where the horizon comes up short like a wall of felt. But there is a pulse in the air, a low industrial thud, the kind of sound a piling driver makes when you are three streets away and it is coming through the ground rather than the air. It arrives from everywhere. It arrives, sometimes, slightly before the thing that makes it.

The things that make it are scattered across the flat at intervals of a few hundred metres, and at first, in the dimness, you take them for hills. They are not hills. There are no hills left here. They are vegetation.

Each one is a mound of moss and sedge and something else—something that is neither—maybe forty metres across, dark green going to black in the wet, and each one is working. Not growing. Working. The mass heaves. A section of it near the top swells and splits and a column of steam goes up, white against the grey, and it goes up with a sound like a valve releasing. Then the mound sags, and water that has been drawn up through it comes seeping back down its flanks, warmer than the water on the flat, and you can see the warmth as a faint disturbance where it meets the cold sheet. Then the mound begins to draw again. The whole cycle takes about a minute. There is nothing built in it. There is no metal, no seam, nothing that was assembled. It is peat that has been alive on this plateau for so long that it has organised itself into a pump, and it pumps because that is what it has become.

Walk toward one and it does not get closer at the rate it should. This is the first thing that is wrong, or the first thing you allow yourself to call wrong. The distance folds. You will be a hundred metres away for a long time and then you will be at the foot of it without having crossed the intervening ground. The sheet of water shows this: your reflection lags. It catches up when you stop.

Up close the mound is warm, and the warmth has drawn everything living on the plateau to it. Small birds—dozens, hundreds—cling to the vegetation around the vent, feathers soaked, taking the heat. They do not fly when you come. Frogs pack the warm seep at the base in a slow-moving crust, layered two and three deep, and beneath them something with too many legs moves through the moss in a way that suggests it is not so much walking as being passed along. Midges rise from the steam in columns and are folded back down into it. There is a smell of hot vegetable matter and iron.

And things come out of the mound that are not birds or frogs. Not often. But you will see, if you stay through a few cycles, the moss on the flank bulge and part and something step out of it that is the size of a hare and has a hare's shape, more or less, and is made of the same wet black-green stuff as the mound. It stands. It shakes. It takes a few steps into the cold water, and it is a hare, or it is enough of a hare that the birds go quiet the way birds do. Then it comes apart—not dies, comes apart, into strands that drift and settle and are drawn back into the mass by the next intake. The mound does this constantly. It makes small animals, and the animals live for a minute or an hour, and then they are unmade. Some of them get further than others. Some of the frogs in the seep, you begin to suspect, are the mound's frogs. Some of the birds. There is no way to tell which, and it may be that the distinction stopped meaning anything here a long time ago.

Step back and look across the flat and you will see that every mound is doing this. Steam going up from thirty, forty places under a low sky, the columns leaning in no consistent direction because there is no wind to lean them and so they lean toward each other, or away, according to something else. The thudding is their intake, out of phase, so that the whole plateau sounds like a factory floor with no walls. Between the mounds: nothing. The inch of water. The soft grey stone underneath it. Hundreds of metres of emptiness, and then another mound, working.

It is cold. Not the cold of ice—there is no ice; the ground is too warm underneath, the whole plateau is being gently heated from within by the slow combustion of a peat layer that has been oxidising for longer than there have been words for peat. It is the cold of altitude and wet and no sun. Your breath goes up and joins the general fog. The light is the light of an hour before dusk and it has been that hour for as long as anyone has been here to see it.

Look at the horizon that is too close. It is not a wall. Walk toward it and it stays too close and then, without your having reached it, you are looking back at the plateau from the other side of it, and there are the same mounds, working, but from here you can see that some of them are not mounds. Some of them are the same mound. The same steam column, the same bulge on the north flank, the same knot of frogs. The plateau is repeating itself at intervals, and the repetitions are not exact—one has the hare stepping out, and one has the hare coming apart, and one has no hare, only the swelling in the moss where it will be—and you understand that you are looking at the same place at slightly different times, laid out flat.

This is what the erosion has done. The stone is finished; nothing more can happen to it. And a place where nothing more can happen to the ground begins, eventually, to wear at other things. The distances have worn thin. The sequence of events has worn thin. Sound wears through and arrives early. The mounds—the only thing on the plateau that was still moving when everything else stopped—have kept moving, and their movement has become the only clock, and the clock is not quite in agreement with itself from one place to the next. What looks like turbulence, the steam and the heaving and the constant making and unmaking, is the plateau's stable state. It has been in exactly this storm for so long that the storm has a geology.

Toward the centre—if there is a centre; there is a direction the sound gets denser—the mounds stand closer, and some of them have begun to reach toward each other. Not with branches. With intake. A mound will draw in and the mound beside it will lose a little of its mass, strands lifting off across the gap and joining, and the second mound will draw in return. They are exchanging themselves. Out there the steam columns merge into a low pale ceiling and the light under it is the colour of the inside of a shell, and the thudding is continuous, and in the water between them, which is warm now and cloudy with silt, things are swimming that were never made by anything but the mounds—soft-bodied, deliberate, some with the sketched suggestion of eyes—and they go under the flanks and come out changed, or do not come out.

You will not want to go there. It is fine not to. The plateau is large and most of it is empty, and the emptiness is not a lack. It is the room the works need. The cold flat water, the soft stone under it, the birds huddled on the warm places, the columns rising, the thud that arrives before the fall. Somewhere behind you a hare is stepping out of the moss and shaking itself dry, and somewhere ahead of you it has already been taken back, and neither of these is later than the other, and the plateau, which is done, goes on.
