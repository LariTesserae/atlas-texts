You hear it before the ridge lets you see it. A sound with the bulk of weather — the kind of noise that is less heard than pressed into — but under the roar there is a grain, a texture, a hundred thousand small hard events happening at once: snaps, pops, the wet tearing of something splitting along its length. It comes up the slope through the soles of your feet.

Then the ridge drops away and the valley is there, and it is green all the way down and all the way across, and it is a city.

Not a city overgrown. A city *of* growth. From up here, in flat warm light, you can see that it has streets. They run in the way streets run — converging, forking, widening into what can only be called squares — but they are laid out in vine and buttress root and the paving is a single continuous leaf, veined, the size of a district. The blocks between the streets stand as tall as any built thing, and they are trunks, stacked and fused, fluted like columns, hollowed by their own decision into rooms. Something like a wall circles the whole of it, a hedge thirty feet thick, and it is the newest thing in sight: pale green, unhardened, still visibly lengthening at the top edge the way a fingernail lengthens if you watched it for a year, except that here you can see it move.

The valley floor is low and it holds heat. Everything down there is warm — not sun-warm but body-warm, the heat of fermentation and rapid cell division, and it rises out of the city in a shimmer that makes the far edge tremble. The air is thick. There is no rain but everything drips. Condensation forms on the underside of every surface and lets go in fat drops, and the smell coming up is crushed stem and sap and something like bread.

You go down.

---

The noise at street level is a wall. It fills the chest cavity. Words spoken here do not leave the mouth. The dominant tone is a low tearing groan, continuous, which you eventually understand is the sound of wood expanding faster than its own bark, everywhere, all at once — a city-wide splitting. Over that, a rhythm of cracks like gunfire: pods going off. Somewhere a deep concussive settling, over and over, which is whole structures giving way. A hiss so high it is almost a color: the mass unfurling of leaves. And under all of it, constant, a rushing, which is sap moving in trunks the width of towers, moving so fast and under such pressure that it sings in the wood like water in old pipes.

Nothing else. No birds. No insects. In a place this warm and this wet and this green the silence of animals is louder than anything. Flowers open in the walls beside you — huge, fleshed, structured obviously for something with a tongue or a proboscis or a beak, hung with nectar the consistency of syrup — and nothing comes. They wait for the length of a breath. They drop. New ones open above them.

The street underfoot gives slightly. It is leaf, a single leaf, and you can see the midrib running up the center of the road like a median, and the veins branching off into side streets, and where the side streets end the leaf is still growing, its serrated edge advancing over the mulch of yesterday's district.

---

The materials are these. Green wood, unhardened, still bruisable — if you press a thumb into a wall it leaves a mark that darkens and heals over while you watch. Bark that has not had time to roughen; smooth as skin, sometimes striped with the pale scars of growth so fast the outer layer simply split and rejoined. Aerial roots, dropped from above like rope, thickening into pillars when they touch ground, sometimes thickening into pillars before they touch ground, hanging in the air with their feet six inches above the pavement, waiting for the pavement to rise to them. Which it does. Fiber, everywhere: hair-fine, spun out of the joints between things, drifting in the warm updraft, settling, taking root in a crack and becoming a stem by the time you look again. Sap, weeping from every fresh seam, amber and then quickly hard.

Nothing here is old. There is no moss because nothing has stood still long enough to be colonized. There is no wear on the paving. Edges are sharp — leaf-margins, the bevel of fresh-split wood, the corner where two trunks have met and fused at a perfect right angle as though drawn. Where things fail they fail wholesale, collapsing into a fast black rot that steams, and the rot is gone in an hour, and something pale is standing in it.

The rooms are the strangest part. The buildings open — there are doorways, arched, the arches formed by two stems leaning together and knitting. Inside there are chambers, and the chambers have proportions. A room the height of a standing person, roughly. A step of a height a leg could climb. A ledge at hand level. Something long ago learned the shape of being lived in and is repeating it without the original, and the repetitions drift: a doorway too narrow to pass, a stair whose treads are all the same tread, a window looking into the solid wood of the wall it is set in. Nobody has ever entered these rooms. They are being grown for occupation by something that does not arrive, and the city does not stop making them.

---

The rules do not hold.

You notice first with the light. It is a normal afternoon, high haze, shadowless in the way overcast days are, and yet the walls cast shadows — sharp ones — and the shadows are not where the walls are. They are a few paces ahead, on the leaf-paving, the shapes of towers that have not risen yet. Later you look back down the street and the towers are there and the shadows fit them.

A trunk ahead of you is growing backward. You watch it draw itself in, its crown of leaves closing like hands, its branches shortening, the whole of it sinking down to a green shoot and then a split seed-case and then the case closing over the seed and then the seed rising, slowly, up through the air into the pod on the parent branch that it fell from, which closes. The parent branch remains. The sound this makes is exactly the sound of growth, played the same direction.

Rain falls in one square, hard, in a column exactly the shape of the canopy that is about to spread over it. The canopy spreads. The rain stops. Everything under it is dry.

There is a plaza where the paving-leaf curls at its edge, and the curl continues over and around until the plaza is a leaf standing on its edge, and if you walk along it you are walking along the street you came in on, which is the same leaf, and the plaza is at the end of it, ahead of you, and behind. You do this several times before stopping. The noise is no different in any of the places.

You cut a stem — it is easy; the wood is soft — and count the rings. There are many more than there can be. The rings continue in a smaller scale down into the pith, the way a fern coils into itself, years inside years, none of them lived.

And here is what you come to understand, walking: the city is not growing *in* space. It is growing space. The valley was smaller this morning. The streets you cannot see are being extruded, and what they are extruded into was not there.

---

Toward the center the density is total. Trunks stand near enough to brush both shoulders. Above, the canopy has closed — not shading, exactly, because light gets through it green and heavy — but closing, so that the sky is a lattice with a thousand small openings, and the openings shift. Vines cross between buildings at every height like rigging. Everything touches everything. Where two surfaces meet they fuse, so the whole core of the city is a single organism, though it was not this morning, though it is also several.

In the center there is a river. Not water — this is not a wet place, not really; the moisture is all breath and sweat — but sap, moving in a channel of bark down the middle of a wide boulevard, thick, translucent, hot, carrying in it small green motes that may be seeds and may be cells. It moves faster than a river should on ground this flat. It is the source of the rushing tone that underlies the noise, and standing beside it the sound is not in your ears at all. It is in your teeth. The channel is a vein. The city is a leaf. You have understood this since the ridge, and it does not help.

Where the sap-river ends it does not pool. It rises. It goes up a trunk hundreds of feet in diameter, the largest single thing in the valley, a column whose bark has never had time to form and stands raw and pale and running with condensation, and near its top something is happening continuously: the wood splits along its length with a report you feel as a blow, and out of the split the crown pushes up another story of itself, branch and leaf and arch and room, and the split heals below it, and splits again above. The tower has been getting taller for as long as you have been here. It is not getting closer to the sky. The sky, up there, is receding at exactly the rate.

---

You watch a district die.

It is off to the side of the great trunk, a neighborhood of low fused domes, maybe an hour old. The sound there changes first — the groan of expansion drops out and there is a new note, a long descending creak like a ship. The domes darken. It is not shadow; the material itself is changing, the green going bronze and then umber, and a wave of heat comes off it that you can feel from the boulevard. Then it goes. Not a collapse so much as a subsiding, the walls losing conviction, folding inward with a deep soft crumpling roar into a black steaming mass that spreads and levels itself and begins, before it has finished spreading, to sprout.

The sprouts are not domes. They are something spired. The district is coming back as a different district, and the streets that led into the old one are unpicking themselves and re-laying in new directions, the paving-leaf folding and refolding like a hand finding a new grip. Within the time it takes to walk the length of the boulevard the spires have roofs. Within another they are opening rooms. Within another the rooms are lit from inside by nothing.

This is not decay. Nothing here has time to decay. It is a metabolism. The city eats its own body and grows its body out of what it ate, and the shape it grows is the shape of a city because that is the shape it knows, and it does not know why, and it is very loud, and there is no one in it.

---

At the far edge, where you finally come out, the hedge-wall is growing over the valley's rim and onto the slope you came down. It is quieter here — the noise behind you now, a pressure at the back of the skull. The flowers still open toward nothing. The condensation still drips from every underside. The ground is warm through your soles.

You put a hand on the outer face of the hedge. It is soft, and it gives, and the mark you leave darkens and begins to close, and while it closes it grows — under your palm, against it, the wall pushing outward by a hair's breadth in the second you touch it. You take your hand away. There is a room forming there now, in the green, about the size of a hand. It has a doorway. It has a ledge at the height a thumb would rest.

The valley behind you is a little larger than it was. The noise reaches farther than it did. Up on the ridge, in the ordinary afternoon light, the shadow of a wall falls across the grass, sharp, well ahead of the wall.
