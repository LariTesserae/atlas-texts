The plateau begins where the air stops being enough. You climb the last of the scree and the ground simply levels, all at once, into a surface that has decided how it wants to be and has been that way for longer than there have been eyes to check. It is basalt, or was, cooked to a dull ochre skin that flakes if you push a thumbnail into it but has not flaked on its own in an age. There is nothing loose. Everything that could fall has fallen and been ground into the shallow hollows, and the hollows themselves are old enough to have gone smooth at the lips. The wind that shaped them stopped needing to a long time ago.

It is hot in the way high places are not supposed to be hot. The sun is ordinary — a white coin in a sky gone pale from thinness — but the rock underneath holds its warmth like a kiln that was never let cool, and the heat comes up through the soles and the air above the surface shakes with it in long standing ribbons. There is no shade. Nothing here has ever needed shade. The one concession to water in all of it is a seam of gypsum, chalky and pink, running for a few hundred metres along a crack and then stopping, the fossil of some lake that misjudged this place before the place was finished.

Nothing grows. Not lichen, not the black crust of desert varnish, not the pretence of a seedling in a crevice. The mineral world is complete here and closed to applicants.

And it is loud.

That is the first thing you get wrong. You hear it before you crest the edge, a low, vast, working sound, the sound of a turbine hall heard through a wall, a rolling-mill sound, a sound with pistons in it. You assume a facility. You assume that over the next rise there will be a stack, a compound, a fence, a road with tyre marks in the dust. You walk for an hour and there is nothing. The plateau extends, flat and empty, to a horizon that is very slightly convex, and the sound does not get closer or farther. It is under you. It is the kind of loud that you stop being able to hear as a sound and start hearing as a pressure — a valve opening somewhere for forty minutes at a stretch, the long shriek of something bearing enormous load on a bearing, a rhythmic clank that comes once every eleven or twelve minutes with the patience of a thing that has no reason to hurry. There is an electrical component to it, a hum in the teeth, and if you stand still long enough the hair on your arms lifts.

There is nothing built here. You have to hold both facts at once. There is not one made object on the entire shield — no bolt, no wire, no cut stone — and the place runs like a refinery.

---

What is here is the fauna, and the fauna is the trouble.

You see the first of them at what you take to be about six kilometres: a flank. Only a flank — a wall of hide the colour of the rock, ribbed, rising from the surface and going up perhaps forty metres and then simply ending in the shimmer, not tapering into a back or a shoulder, just terminating where the air stopped being able to carry the information. It is breathing. The slow lift and fall of it is the forty-minute valve.

Then, much nearer, off to the left, a leg. Not attached to anything. A column of the same hide with a knee in it, planted in a hollow that fits it exactly — that has, you realise, been worn to fit it, over a duration you do not want to think about. The leg is not connected to the flank. There is empty plateau between them, kilometres of it, dead flat, shimmering. And yet when the flank swells the leg takes weight, the knee flexes a degree, the hollow rings like a struck girder, and the clank comes rolling across to you a little afterwards.

Animals are supposed to be in one place. That is more or less what an animal is. These are not. Whatever holds a creature together in the ordinary world — the rule that says a body is continuous and occupies a volume and can be walked around — has come loose up here, worn thin like everything else, and what remains is the animal without the requirement of being all in the same location. A shoulder here. A haunch beyond the horizon. An eye, once, half-lidded, set into what you'd taken for an outcrop, tracking you with no head around it. The creatures are enormous and the plateau is almost empty; both are true. There is perhaps one presence to every ten cubic kilometres of air and rock, and yet no part of the plateau is unoccupied, because each presence is spread through all of it, thin as a smell.

The herd — you come to think of it as a herd, though it might be one animal, or a number that isn't an integer — grazes on heat. That is the industry. The rock has been storing sun for the whole of its existence and the animals draw it down through the soles of those planted legs, slowly, with a metabolism that has no interest in speed. What comes out the other end is sound. Sound and a faint electrical charge that ropes along the ground in visible lines when the light is right, and a smoke — not smoke, a hot exhalation, mineral-smelling, that vents from fissures you had assumed were geological. They are nostrils. The whole shield is one distributed, low-yield, exquisitely stable engine, and no one made it, and nothing tends it, and it has been running at exactly this output since before it had anyone to disturb.

---

You try to walk to a leg. This is an error you will make several times before you stop.

The leg is close — a kilometre, at most, a dark column with heat-shimmer wrapped round its base. You walk toward it and it does not get nearer. Not in the mirage way, where a thing recedes proportionally; it stays exactly where it is, at exactly that apparent size, while the ground behind you accumulates. After an hour of this you turn around and the plateau edge you climbed up is gone — there is more plateau there now, the same flat ochre, the same hollows — and the leg is still a kilometre off and has not moved, and you understand, in the flat unpanicked way this place induces, that distance here is not measured in metres. It is measured in animals. You are a certain number of animals from the leg, and walking does not change that number, because the animal you are inside of is the thing you would have to cross.

Time keeps a similar bargain. The clank comes every eleven minutes by your count, but your count has begun to wander, and you notice the sun has not moved. Or has moved very slightly. The stride of one of these creatures — the lifting of one column from its worn socket and the setting of it down in the next socket, a few hundred metres on, where a hollow of exactly the right depth already waits — takes what feels like a day. You watch a foot come up. You watch the shadow under it, the only shade on the plateau, a blot of blessed dark with the socket's floor visible inside it, shining, polished by ten thousand identical descents. You watch it come down again. Somewhere in the middle of this you eat something, and sleep, or something that passes for sleep with a turbine hall running through the bones of your skull, and when you look again the foot has settled and the plateau is ringing and the sound has its clank back.

Slow. Loud, hot, enormous, and slow. Whatever happens here happens on the schedule of rock cooling. The herd is not migrating. It is not going anywhere. It grazes the shield in a circuit so long that the hollows it wears are geological features, and the shield feeds it, and it vents the shield's stored sun as noise into an atmosphere too thin to carry it far, and the whole arrangement has been in balance for so long that balance is the wrong word — balance implies something that could tip.

---

There is a moment, late, when the light goes long and copper and the shimmer lies down, and you see more of it than you had.

Not more animal. Less air in the way. The flank you first saw is still there, and now beyond it, at a distance the eye refuses to credit, another, and another, ridged walls of hide standing up out of the plain at intervals of the same vast size like the pillars of a hall with no roof. The legs, when you can see several, do not line up with the flanks. Nothing lines up. Bits of creature, standing scattered over a region the size of a small country, each one breathing at its own forty-minute rate, each one warm, each one venting, each one connected to all the others by nothing you can see and by something you can absolutely hear, because the sound — you understand it now — is the sound of them being one thing across a distance. It is the noise of coherence being maintained without proximity. It is what a body sounds like when it has to hold itself together through a hundred kilometres of empty rock.

An eye opens in an outcrop near you. It is the size of a room. It regards you with the unfocused attention of something that has never had a reason to focus, and you feel, standing in the copper light on the hot worn shield with the plateau's engine going through your teeth, that you are not being seen so much as accounted for. Added to the mass. A small warm irregularity on the grazing surface, one more thing giving up its heat.

The eye closes. The clank comes. Somewhere to the north a column of hide lifts an inch out of a socket it has been wearing since before the gypsum seam was wet, and the whole plateau, empty, thronged, unbuilt, industrial, takes a breath that will finish sometime tomorrow.
