The noise reaches you before the place does. From the road, where the land is still whole, it comes up through the floor of the car and then, when you stop and open the door, through the soles of your boots: a roar with no direction, low and even, like standing inside a sea shell that has been scaled up to the size of a county. You will spend the rest of the day trying to locate its edges and failing. It has no edges. It is the medium the place is suspended in.

The road runs along the top of the old bog, which is still intact here — a brown-purple skin of heather and lichen and dead grass, rusted and stiff with cold, dipping slightly toward the middle as raised bogs do, a dome that has begun to sag. Then, without ceremony, the ground ends. There is a face. It drops five or six metres, nearly vertical, a wall of the ground's own interior, and below it the basin opens out.

It is enormous and almost empty. You can see three, perhaps four kilometres in every direction across a floor that is black and combed into long parallel ridges, and on the far side the land rises again to another face, another skin of heather, another road. Between the two rims there is nothing taller than a person except the machines, and the machines are far apart. The sky is low and grey and does not so much light the basin as fail to darken it. It is the middle of the afternoon. It feels like the last hour before night.

The face you climb down is not weathered. That is the first strange thing about it, once the sound has stopped being the only strange thing. The peat has been cut in a clean vertical and the cut is recent — a few seasons at most — so the layers are still legible, unslumped, sharp-edged, the way a road cutting looks the week the bulldozers leave. The top metre is fibrous and reddish, and if you pull at it you can feel individual stems, sphagnum and cotton grass and the small woody twists of heather, each one dead but still a thing. Below that it darkens and packs. Halfway down there is a horizon of birch bark, papery and white, from a forest that grew here for a few centuries when the climate dried and was then drowned and buried when it wettened again. Below that the peat is black and smooth and greasy, and the plants in it are no longer plants, only the memory of plants, an undifferentiated mass with the odd pale seed in it like a tooth. Rain has begun to work at the face — there are new rills, thin as pencil lines, and at the foot a small apron of crumbs — but nothing more. The face is young. It has not yet learned how to fall.

At the bottom you are standing on ground that was under the ground for four thousand years. This is the second thing. The floor of the basin is not soil. Dig anywhere with your hand and you get the same black plant paste, cold as a cellar, damp without being wet; there is no gravel, no stone, no earth in any ordinary sense. Everything you walk on, everything you can see to the far rim, everything down to the clay that lies another three or four metres beneath your feet, is made of plants. Vegetation here is not something that grows on the land. It is the land. The living plants that remain — and there are some, we'll come to them — are only the thin green edge of a body that is almost entirely dead and almost entirely intact, an ocean of moss that stopped being an ocean and became a floor.

It is cold in the way that damp places are cold, which is to say the cold gets in and does not leave. The surface of the milled peat has frozen into a crust that gives under your weight with a small dry sound you cannot hear over the roar but can feel in your knees. Under the crust it is soft. Under the soft it is frozen again. The ridges run away from you in perfect parallel, fifteen metres apart, each one a windrow of peat harrowed up over the summer and left to dry and waiting for the machines to come back for it; between the ridges the fields are combed flat, and along each edge runs a drain. The drains are the only water. They are cut sharp, half a metre wide, straight as ruled lines, and each holds a few centimetres of black water skinned with ice. If you crouch you can see under the ice: it is not clear, it is the colour of strong tea, and it is not moving, or not moving at any speed a person could perceive. The whole basin drains very slightly toward its centre and the water follows, a millimetre an hour, through a grid of ditches that meet at a pumping station you cannot yet see.

You can see the machines, though. There are five or six in the whole basin, each one alone. A harvester, parked for winter, with its long rotating milling drum tilted up like a raised paw; a tractor with a ridger; a row of rail bogies coupled and left on a narrow-gauge track that runs off toward the sound. They are painted a yellow that the light does not permit to be yellow. Condensation has beaded on every cold surface and frozen there, so the machines are furred with tiny white crystals along their upper edges and dripping on the sides that face the wind. Nobody is near them. Nobody is anywhere. The sparseness is the third thing: a place this size, this worked, this loud, and you can turn a full circle and count the human-scale objects on your fingers. A pump house the size of a garden shed, concrete, with a single lamp already lit above the door. A tar-paper hut, older, from when this was cut by hand, its roof half gone. A line of poles carrying a cable. The rails. That is all.

The sound is coming from the station.

It stands at the northern rim, where the rail line climbs a ramp back to the level of the old bog, and from down on the floor you see only its upper half: a squat brick hall, a chimney, two hyperbolic cooling towers, one of them steaming. A conveyor runs down from it into the basin on a lattice of steel, half a kilometre long, then turns and runs along the rim, a covered belt on trestles. The conveyor is running. Everything in the station is running. It burns the basin — that is what it is for — and it has burned for fifty years and will burn for as long as there is floor left to burn, and in the process of turning ten thousand years of moss into electricity it makes a noise that fills the ten thousand years' worth of space the moss used to occupy. Fans, mainly. The forced-draught fans that push air into the boilers and the induced-draught fans that pull it out again through the stack, each the diameter of a house, turning at a speed that makes a note below the bottom of hearing, and above that note a shout — a continuous high-pressure steam vent on the boiler house roof, white and horizontal in the wind, screaming. The conveyor adds a rattle. The cooling tower adds a rain sound, water falling through the fill inside it and hitting the pond at the bottom, a sound of storm without storm. The wind takes all of it and pushes it flat across the basin, where there is nothing — not a tree, not a wall, not a rise in the ground — to stop it. The basin is a dish. The station sits on its lip. Every point on the floor is equidistant from nowhere, and the sound is everywhere the same.

It changes how you look. You cannot hear your own footsteps, or the crust breaking, or the ice on the drains creaking when you step near it, or your own breath, and so the place goes visually very quiet, very still, as if you were watching it through glass. The steam from the tower is the only fast thing. Everything else is either stopped or so slow it might as well be.

Along the drains, and along the toe of the cut face, and in a few abandoned bays at the western end where the peat ran too thin to be worth milling, the living plants are trying again. Sphagnum first: small cushions of it, red and lime-green, luminous in the failed light, each one a colony of a plant that is mostly water and mostly dead, growing at its tips and dying at its base and by dying making more of the floor. Around the cushions there is cotton grass, its summer tufts gone grey and ragged, and in the more sheltered bays some birch seedlings, knee-high, bare, the same species whose bark you saw halfway up the face. They are doing what they did last time. Given four thousand years without interruption they would refill the basin. They will not be given it. The cold has stopped all of them for the season and they stand in the black fields like something scattered rather than something growing, as sparse as the machines.

There is almost nothing else alive. A hare, once, large and brown and stopped at the far end of a field, watching you for a full minute before it goes, and you never hear it move. Later a harrier, low and grey over the ridges, tilting, riding the same wind that carries the roar, hunting whatever the hare's kind leaves for it. Midges would be here in summer, in a cloud you could not stand inside, and dragonflies over the drains; not now. The cold has emptied the basin down to the machines and the moss and the two animals, and both of them are passing through.

What holds the place is time, and the way the sound and the slowness pull against each other. Everything visible is slow. The moss grows a millimetre a year. The water in the drains moves too gradually to see. The face at the rim will take a generation to slump into a slope. The harvesters take three or four centimetres off the floor each season, and the seasons are short and the winters long, and the whole apparatus of rails and conveyors and pumps exists to move a landscape a few centimetres downward at a speed just barely faster than the landscape can remake itself. It is not a violent process. It is patient and mechanical and very large, and it will go on until the clay shows and then it will stop. But the sound is not slow. The sound is the fire, and the fire is the only thing here that runs at the speed of a living creature, taking in each hour the compressed years by the tonne and letting them out through the stack as heat and through the fans as noise. Standing in the basin you are standing in the empty space where the years used to be, and the noise is the years leaving.

Toward four the light gives up the pretence. The station's floodlamps come on, and the pump house's single lamp is joined by others along the rail line, small and yellow, kilometres apart, each showing a circle of black ground and a few ridges and then nothing. The steam plume goes from white to orange underneath. Frost begins to whiten the ridges from the top down. The drains hold a last pale reflection of the sky and then hold only the lamps. You climb the face by the rail ramp, up through the black metre and the birch horizon and the red fibrous metre and out onto heather, onto the old surface, onto the top of the ground — and the roar does not lessen at all, and does not lessen at the car, and does not lessen at the road, and is still there, a little lower, folded into the sound of the engine, for a long time after.
