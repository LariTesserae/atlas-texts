You come to it through a crack that is still too narrow for shoulders, so you go in sideways and the rock takes the skin off one knuckle. The rock is sharp everywhere. Nothing here has been touched by water long enough to be rounded; every edge is the edge it had when it first broke, and it broke recently.

The heat reaches you before the light does. It is not the heat of fire but of a kitchen with all the pots on — wet heat, air that has already been breathed by stone. Then the crack turns and the brightness arrives, and for a moment you cannot see anything at all, only white.

It is a room. That is the first honest word for it, though nobody made it. Perhaps four long strides across, a little less in height, roughly the shape of a lung. The walls are pale grey and glassy where they have been cut and dull where they have not, and the cut surfaces are everywhere, overlapping, like the facets on a stone that a jeweler gave up on. The light comes from these. Not from a lamp, not from any one point — it comes evenly off the working faces of the rock, a flat and shadowless white, the light of an overcast noon. Your hand held out in front of you casts no shadow on the floor. You have to look at it for a while to believe that.

The floor is wet. All of it. A film of water lies over the stone and gathers, at the low end of the room, into a pool no wider than a bathtub and not much deeper than your wrist. The pool is perfectly clear and perfectly still except where the drips land in it. The drips come from everywhere above — from the ceiling, from the sharp lips of the facets — because the walls are sweating. The whole room is condensing. Where the stone is being worked it gives up its water, hydrogen and oxygen let go from the minerals that were holding them, and the water beads on the surfaces and slides and drops. The drops are warm. Everything is warm. The pool would be too hot to keep your hand in for long.

Nothing here has been placed. It is worth saying that plainly, because the room is so evidently made and so evidently not built. There are no tools, no marks of tools, no scaffold, no seam where a thing was joined to another thing. There is only the stone, and the fact that the stone is being removed a grain at a time by something that works at the scale of grains. Whatever it is does not have a shape you can point to. It is in the surface. If you put your face close to a bright facet — and you will not want to, for the heat — you can see that the surface is not quite still. It has a kind of grain to it that moves, the way the surface of a river has a grain. The wall is retreating. It does this at something like a millimeter an hour. If you sat here for a day the room would be very slightly larger and you would not have seen it happen.

So this is where the newness comes from. There has been no time for anything. The ceiling has one thin place where a drip has fallen in the same spot for long enough to start leaving something behind — a nub of pale deposit the size of a fingernail, the whole geological history of the room in a single small bump. Otherwise the stone is clean. No moss, no crust of centuries, no smoothing. Just the flat brilliant faces and, between them, the rougher unworked rock, which is the ordinary dark grey of the ground you came down through, and looks, against the brightness, almost black.

The room is nearly empty. Its features can be counted. The pool. The nub on the ceiling. A ridge of unworked rock that runs across the floor like a spine and divides the room into a higher half and a lower, wet half. A place on the far wall where the facets have cut deeper than elsewhere and made a kind of alcove, brighter than the rest, in which nothing sits. That is all. The emptiness is not the emptiness of a place that has been cleared; it is the emptiness of a place that has not yet had time to fill.

It is quiet in the way a bathroom is quiet after someone has run a bath and left. Drips. The particular flat note a drop makes falling into shallow water, and the softer note it makes falling onto wet stone, and the very rare sharp tick when one lands on a dry facet and instantly is not dry anymore. Underneath these, if you hold your breath, there is a sound so low you might be inventing it — a hum, or less than a hum, a pressure — which is either the rock being worked or your own blood in the heat. The crack you came through lets in a faint movement of cooler air and, with it, sometimes, a sound from outside: wind over the ground, once something like a bird. The room does not echo. The facets take sound and scatter it and give nothing back.

There is life, but you have to look. On the far side of the pool, where the film of water on the floor is thinnest and hottest, there is a faint orange sheen on the stone, no thicker than a breath on a mirror. It is not a plant. It is barely a thing. It is a mat of something that likes it here — the water, the heat, the minerals coming off the walls in solution — and it is growing at the pace of the walls, spreading outward as its floor is revealed. Where the room is oldest, near the entrance, the orange is a little darker. Where the room is newest, in the bright alcove, there is none yet.

And once, in the time you are standing there, something crosses the ridge of dark rock in the center of the floor. A small thing, a hard-shelled thing, red-brown and the length of a grain of rice, moving with the unhurried certainty of a creature that has never been hunted. It goes from the shadowless brightness of one side to the shadowless brightness of the other and disappears into a fault in the rock. That is the only animal. There is nothing here for it to eat except the orange sheen, and nothing here to eat it, and the crack it came out of goes down further than the room does, into whatever dark and cooler country the room is being cut out of.

The dynamic of the place is not violent. It is a set of slow, steady processes that have found each other. The walls retreat and release water. The water films the floor, gathers, drips, evaporates in the heat and beads again on the cooler unworked rock and drips again — a rainfall the size of a room, going around and around. The pool holds its level almost exactly; what it loses to steam it gains back from the ceiling. The orange mat follows the receding walls like grass following a glacier, if grass were a stain and a glacier a lit wall. The air moves very slightly, in through the crack low down and out through the crack high up, carrying heat and moisture into the ordinary ground above, where, if you had looked before you came down, you would have seen a small patch of soil that is always damp and warm and never frosts.

Stay long enough and the brightness stops being the main thing. Your eyes give up trying to find a source and settle, and the room becomes simply a room — hot, wet, sharp, white, hardly anything in it, going about its business. It does not look like an achievement. It does not look like anything was wanted from it. It looks like a place where a certain kind of weather has begun to happen underground, and the weather is making itself a place to be, and the place is only just getting started.

When you leave, the knuckle you cut on the way in is already stinging with the mineral water that has dripped on it. Outside, the air is cool and ordinary and full of shadows, and every one of them looks strange for a moment, like a mistake.
