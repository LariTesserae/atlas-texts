**The Works at Sirrun Tarn**

The rim comes first, because everything else is below it. You climb the last hundred meters over rock that has not yet decided what shape it will keep — fresh black scoria that rings when a boot dislodges it, blocks of obsidian with edges that would open a hand, tuff still soft enough to take a fingernail. Nothing grows here. There is no lichen, no crust of moss in the shadowed cracks, none of the grey-green scurf that ordinarily starts on a mountain within a few years of its exposure. The rock is too new for that. It is also too high; the air up here is thin and hard and tastes of iron, and the wind moving across the plateau has nothing to catch on.

Then the rim, and the crater beneath it, and the water.

The tarn is not large. You could throw a stone across it if you had lungs for the effort at this altitude — perhaps a hundred and twenty meters rim to rim, roughly circular, walled all the way around by cliffs that drop straight to the surface without beach or shelf. It has the proportions of a well or a cathedral nave stood on end: a small footprint and an unreasonable depth. The water is very clear and very cold, the blue of the inside of glacier ice, and where the sun strikes the surface you can see down and down past the first glassy ledges into a lit interior that goes on much farther than the width of the pool would suggest.

And you can hear it. Standing on the rim, in open air, at a distance, you can hear it — a sound like a distant mill or a rail yard heard from the wrong side of a hill: a deep irregular knocking underlaid by a grind, a rasp, and over both of them a continual popping and crackle as of wet timber in a fire. The sound comes up through the water and out into the air, and it is loud enough that when the wind drops you can feel it in your teeth.

---

Go down. The walls will let you, in one place, where a fissure has slumped into a chute of loose glass. The water at the surface is only a few degrees above freezing and it closes over you without ceremony.

Underneath, the sound becomes the world.

Water carries sound better than air — faster, farther, with less loss — and the tarn is a sealed vessel with stone walls, so nothing escapes and nothing decays. The knocking that was distant from the rim is now inside your chest. It comes from everywhere at once, from the walls, from below, from the water itself, and it takes some time for the ear to sort it into its parts.

The walls are covered. That is the first thing. Every surface below about two meters is coated so thickly with living things that the rock itself is only visible in patches, and those patches are not black basalt but something dull and coppery, faintly metallic, as if the stone had been plated.

The bulk of the coating is tube-dwellers. They are a hand's length long and they stand out from the wall in dense ranks, tens of thousands of them, packed so close that the tubes touch and fuse and share walls like the cells of a honeycomb. The tubes are white, calcareous, ridged. From the mouth of each extends a dark muscular body that does one thing: it draws in and pushes out. A long slow intake, a pause, a hard exhalation that snaps the body back into the tube with a *knock* against the calcareous rim. Each animal knocks perhaps once every four or five seconds. There are tens of thousands of them, unsynchronized, on every wall. The result is a percussive roar with no rhythm and no center, a rain of hammers, the sound of a factory floor where every machine runs on its own clock.

They are pumping the lake. This becomes clear after a while, watching the fine particulate drift. Each exhalation drives a small jet outward from the wall; together, the jets move the entire body of water in a slow rotation, a turn that takes — you cannot tell how long. Hours. Days. The tarn has no inlet and no outlet, no wind reaches its surface with any force, and the temperature is nearly uniform from top to bottom, so nothing else would move the water at all. The animals move it. They are its circulation, its engine, and the knocking is the engine's sound.

---

Below the pump-ranks, where the walls begin to shelve inward, the second sound resolves.

Here the tube-dwellers give way to grazers — low, flattened, plated things the size of a spread hand, thousands of them, moving across the raw glass of the young crater wall at a pace so slow you have to look away and look back to be sure they have moved at all. Each carries on its underside a rasping organ that it drags across the rock. The rock is obsidian, or a glass near enough to it. The grazers are not eating the glass. They are eating a film that forms on it — a slick, faintly ochre chemical skin laid down by the vent fluid that seeps through the crater floor — and the only way to take the film is to take the surface with it.

The grinding is continuous. It is lower in pitch than the knocking, a sustained mineral rasp, and it has a texture the knocking lacks, a fibrous quality, as of a great quantity of sand being poured over a great quantity of sand. And that is what it produces. Each grazer sheds a thin plume of pulverized glass as it works. The plumes join, fall, drift in the slow rotation, and settle. The floor of the tarn — dozens of meters down, visible in the clear cold light — is not black rock but pale grey powder, deep enough that the shelves nearest the bottom have soft rounded profiles under it, drifted like snow against a fence. This is the crater's first sediment. The lake is too young to have any other. The grazers are manufacturing geology, a few grains at a time, on a schedule of centuries, and in the meantime they are also, incidentally, polishing the walls of the crater to a finish no weather could give it.

---

The popping — the crackle that from the rim sounded like wet fire — is the strangest.

It comes from the open water. The tarn's middle depths are thick with swimmers: flat ribbony things a forearm long, translucent, dense as smoke, slipping over and under one another in the slow turn of the current. There is barely a cubic meter without three or four of them. They do not move much on their own; they ride the rotation. And every few seconds one of them discharges.

It is a small event when it happens singly — a click, a flicker of dull violet along the ribbon's edge, a brief taste of tin in the water if you are near enough. But there are thousands of them and the discharges come in waves, in cascades that pass through the shoal like applause through a crowd, and the sum is a crackle that never stops. It is loud enough to feel on the skin. Whether it is signal or byproduct, courtship or metabolism or simply the way the animal keeps itself alive in water this cold, there is no telling.

What it *does* is legible. The water of the tarn is rich in dissolved metal — the vent fluid brings up copper and iron and traces of others — and where a discharge grounds against the wall, metal comes out of solution and stays. This is the coppery plating on the rock. It is thin, uneven, thicker near the depths where the swimmers concentrate, and it grows. The walls of the crater are being electroplated, atom by atom, by the incidental voltage of a population of ribbon-shaped animals riding a current driven by tube-worms. In fifty years the plating might be visible from the rim. In a thousand the crater might be lined.

Nothing here was built. There is no artifact in the tarn, no structure, no made thing. But there is industry, unmistakably: intake and exhaust, milling, plating, the production of stock and the accumulation of output, all of it running on schedules so long that the workers do not appear to be working, and all of it louder than any factory has a right to be.

---

Other presences fill the volume between.

Near the surface, a layer of bells — gelatinous, pale, the size of a fist — hang at a fixed depth, so many that from below they blot the light into a shifting lace. Each holds a bubble of gas and adjusts it by minute degrees to stay where it is. They eat what drifts up from the mill. They contribute no sound.

Along the ledges, in the drifts of glass-dust, things burrow: segmented, pale, blind, visible only as ridges moving under the surface of the powder, slow as a tide going out.

In the cracks between the pump-tubes, smaller crawlers work at the tubes themselves, cleaning or robbing them, and here and there a tube stands empty and plugged with something dark, and the crawlers gather there in numbers.

At the very bottom, where the vent fluid actually enters — a scatter of seeps across the crater floor, each marked by a small chimney of orange sinter and a shimmer of warmer water — the density is total. Every surface is animal. The pump-ranks stand shoulder to shoulder; the grazers move over the grazers; ribbon-swimmers crowd so thick they brush the walls and ground themselves continually, and the plating there is thick and bright and lumpy, and the sound is beyond description, a solid thing, a pressure.

It is cool even here. The seeps are barely warm. Whatever furnace made the crater has banked itself; what leaks up now is the residue, a slow chemical exhalation, enough to feed a lake but not to heat it. The animals are not tropical. They are cold-water machines, running on a thin fuel, patient because they must be.

---

Where did they come from?

The crater is young — the walls say so, every unweathered edge of them, and so does the powder on the floor, which would be rock by now in any older lake. There was no lake here twenty years ago. There was no tarn, no seep, perhaps no mountain in this shape. Whatever rose up out of the plateau rose recently and violently and then filled with meltwater and rain, and in that first cold clean water there was nothing at all.

The nearest possibility is the least comfortable: that they came up with the fluid. That somewhere below this plateau, in the deep aquifers the eruption breached, there was already a population living on the chemistry of hot rock in total darkness, and that the eruption gave it a window — a sealed cold basin, lit from above, with the vent still trickling through the floor — and it came up through the seeps and set to work. The animals here do not behave as if the light matters to them. They do not orient to it. The bells at the surface may be the only creatures in the tarn that know the sun exists.

Or they fell in. Or they were always going to be here, the way rust is always going to be on iron.

---

You surface eventually, because the cold demands it. The air on the rim is thin and the wind has picked up, and behind you the tarn goes on knocking and grinding and crackling, no louder and no softer than before, as it will tonight and tomorrow and next year. The water turns at its unmeasurable pace. The walls thicken by a film. The floor rises by a grain.

Down the slope, the loose scoria tinkles and shifts and nothing lives in it. Nothing green will come here for a long time yet; the rock has to break first, and the only thing breaking rock on this mountain is doing it under a hundred meters of water, for its own reasons, at the volume of a foundry.
