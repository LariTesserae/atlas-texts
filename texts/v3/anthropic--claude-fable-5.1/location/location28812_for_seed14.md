The floor of the basin is the flattest thing for a thousand kilometers in any direction. It took longer than mountains to make. Everything that ever fell through this water—shells, clay, the ash of forests that burned when the coast was somewhere else, the fine dust of continents ground to nothing upstream—came down here and lay still, and the weight of what came after pressed it, and the slow flow smoothed it, until there was no longer any hill or hollow, only a plain of grey-brown ooze that varies by less than a hand's width across the whole width of the valley. It was a valley once. There are walls, far off in both directions, but they have slumped and been buried to the point that they are more like a gradual thickening of the dark than a feature. The place has finished becoming what it is. There is no more shape to reach.

The water above it is a little above freezing and has been for as long as the floor has been flat. It is so cold that it is heavy, and being heavy it sits here, in the low place, and does not leave. Ordinary years, it moves at the pace of a clock hand. Nothing is going on. A grain of clay may take a month to descend the final meter.

This is not an ordinary year, or an ordinary day within it. Somewhere far off and higher up, a mass of denser water has spilled over a sill and is falling down the long incline into the basin, and where it arrives it does not so much flow as shove. The whole body of water above the plain is in motion—not fast, by the standards of air, but relentless, a current that leans on everything at once and does not pause for breath. And what it leans on is the ooze. Centuries of settled silt lift off the floor together, not as a cloud but as a thickening of the water itself, so that from the bottom up to well above the height of a house there is no longer a clear distinction between the sea and the seabed. There is a suspension. It is the color of nothing because there is no light to give it a color, but if there were, it would be the brown of old tea.

This is what fills the valley now, from wall to wall: particles. Clay flakes thinner than the smallest thing an eye can resolve. The broken skeletons of single-celled things that died in sunlight decades ago and have been drifting down ever since. Fragments of copepod shell. Sand grains that once belonged to a beach, worn round. Tiny flecks of mica that would flash if anything lit them. Every cubic centimeter is occupied. There is no gap in it, no pocket of clear water, no room between one presence and the next. To move through it is to push through a substance, cold and smooth and gritless, that closes behind whatever passes and leaves no trace of the passage. Anything that stops moving is immediately coated, then covered, then gone.

It is nearly silent. Water carries sound well, but the fine suspended load soaks it up the way snow soaks sound out of a forest. The storm has no roar. There is a faint, continuous hiss that is not quite a sound—the sum of an uncountable number of grains sliding past one another—and beneath that, occasionally, a low shift, felt more than heard, when a bank of sediment somewhere loses its footing and slides. Then the hiss again. Then nothing that could be distinguished from nothing.

Darkness, almost total. The surface is far away and it is winter there; no light has touched this floor in a very long time. But not quite total. Every so often, deep in the murk, there is a small cold light—a point of blue-green that appears, holds for a second, and is gone. Something disturbed by the current has flashed to startle a predator that is not there. Sometimes there is a smear of it, a pale trail a hand long, as a bit of luminous slime is dragged and stretched by the flow. These are the only lights, and they are very small, and they make the darkness larger rather than smaller.

Living things are rare and they are mostly waiting. A sea cucumber the size of a forearm, pale and soft, has drawn itself into a knot and is holding to the bottom with everything it has while the water tries to take it. Brittle stars, a few dozen across an area a village could stand on, have flattened themselves and pulled their arms in. A single rattail fish, long-bodied and slow, hangs in the current with its nose upstream and its eye—large, useless here—open to the storm, drifting backward a little, correcting, drifting backward. Nothing hunts. Nothing here can afford to. The food comes down from above, when it comes, and the only strategy is to be present when it lands.

Plants do not grow here and never have. But they arrive. Flattened into the floor, half exhumed by the current and now shedding fibers into the flow, is a length of tree—a whole trunk, bark long gone, wood soft and black, that fell into a river during some flood on a continent and traveled for years before it was heavy enough to sink. Near it, a mat of eelgrass that died in a shallow bay and was carried out and down, still recognizably grass, still green in the way that a thing can be green in the dark. There are clumps of kelp stipe scattered across the plain like dropped rope. All of it is being slowly eaten. All of it will be gone, and more will come, at intervals too long for anything here to remember the last.

And there is one object. The current found it an hour ago and pulled the mud off it and now it lies exposed on the floor, being scoured. It is a stone the size of a fist, dense and dark, with a groove worked all the way around its middle—a groove made by a hand with another stone, patiently, so that a cord could be tied there and the stone could be hung from the bottom edge of a net. It is the only shaped thing in the basin. The cord is gone. The net is gone. The hand is gone. The boat it dropped from, and the surface it dropped through, and the coast the boat left, are so far above and so long ago that they might as well be another world. The stone has been buried and uncovered many times. The storm will bury it again before it ends. Nothing here knows that it is different from any other stone.

The current is not a passing event. It is the shape the place takes when it is disturbed, and it has been disturbed like this before—many times, at intervals of years or decades, whenever the water upstream grows heavy enough to fall. Each time, the floor is picked up entirely and carried, and set down again a little distance along, in a slightly different arrangement of the same grains. Nothing is added. Nothing is removed. The plain is remade without being changed. This is the final form of the place: not a stillness but a very slow, very old churning, in which everything that exists here has already been ground as small as it can be ground, and is now only being moved from one part of the dark to another.

In a few days the flow will weaken. The heavy water will find its level. The finest clay will hang for weeks, then months, before the last of it comes down. The rattail will settle to within a fin's breadth of the bottom. The brittle stars will unfold. The floor will be flat again, as flat as it has been for longer than there have been people to drop stones into it, and the light will still not come, and the cold will not lift, and the whole width of the valley will be full, floor to invisible ceiling, of water that is waiting to be told to move.
