Start with the roof, because the roof is where the light comes from.

Somewhere above there is a plain — you will never see it from here — and the plain is floored with a bed of gypsum forty or fifty feet thick, laid down in a sea that dried out before anything with a spine existed. Over an unimaginable span, water worked underneath it. The soft salts below dissolved and were carried off; the harder gypsum stayed. What remains is a ceiling: a single sheet of cloudy, fibrous, faintly amber crystal, thin in places to the width of a hand, stretched over a cavity that runs for miles in every direction with a rise-and-fall of maybe two hundred feet.

Daylight comes through it. Not dimly. Gypsum in thin section is nearly transparent, and where the sun stands over the plain the ceiling ignites — a vast, sourceless, milk-white glare with no shadows that point anywhere in particular. Where the sheet is thicker the light is honey-colored; where it has fractured and re-healed it throws bands and lozenges of brightness onto the floor that slide as the day moves. Nothing here is ever dark while the sun is up. People who live here squint permanently; the animals have no eyes to speak of.

The floor, then.

It is salt. Halite, mostly, gray-white and sugar-textured, with layers of darker anhydrite showing through like the grain in wood where something has cut down into it. The floor is not level and it does not stay where it is put. Salt under pressure creeps. It flows the way ice flows — plastic, patient, refusing to stay a solid in any honest sense — and in this cavity the pressure is uneven, the ceiling bears down harder here than there, and the salt answers by moving. Not geologically. Perceptibly. A mound you walked around this morning will be a low shoulder by the end of the week and a shallow pan by the end of the season, and somewhere else a fold is rising to take its place. The whole floor is heaving, slowly, in a constant silent boil.

And on this floor there is a city.

---

It is hard, at first, to see it as one. It has none of the compression you'd expect — no streets pressing on streets, no walls sharing walls. The structures are spread far apart, a few dozen paces between one and the next at the least, sometimes a hundred or more, and between them is open salt, bright and bare. Standing at any one point you can see perhaps forty or fifty of them before the ceiling, which sags toward the floor in the distance, cuts off the view. But the pattern continues past that. Walk in any direction for an hour and there are still buildings, at the same loose spacing, the same low profiles, the same pale stuff. There is no edge that anyone has found. There is only a point where the ceiling meets the floor and the city can go no further, and then, if you follow that seam, more city.

The buildings are worn to nothing.

That is the first impression and it is nearly correct. They are hemispheres, lozenges, low elongated humps like the backs of animals half-buried. Whatever corners they once had are gone. Whatever edges — gone. A wall is a rounded slope that becomes a roof without any line to mark the change. Doorways are ovals that have been smoothed and re-smoothed until they look less cut than eroded, the way a sea-cave mouth looks. Some of the structures have subsided until only a knee-high rim remains around a hollow; some are cracked open along their whole length by a fold moving beneath them and have been abandoned to slump. Everything looks ten thousand years old. Everything looks like the last stage of a process that has run all the way to the end, the shape a thing takes when it has stopped resisting.

Which is true, in one sense. And in another sense the building in front of you is eleven days old.

---

The material is the salt itself, and the salt is quarried with hand tools — antler, bone, wedges of the harder anhydrite, and flint, which comes from somewhere and is traded, scarce and prized, its edges resharpened until the pieces are too small to hold. A block of halite is cut from the floor in a series of grooves and levered up. It is not heavy for its size. It is carried, or dragged on hides, or rolled if it is rounded enough, to where a wall is going up, and it is set in place, and it is wetted.

Wetting is the whole of the craft. The air here is saturated — not raining, never quite raining, but the cool of the cavity condenses the moisture out of it constantly, so that the ceiling sweats and the walls sweat and every hollow in the floor holds a shallow lens of brine. A builder takes a hide bag of this brine and pours it along the joint between two blocks. The salt at the surface dissolves. The two blocks slump toward each other, minutely. The brine, made heavier by what it has taken up, sinks and spreads and dries at the edges, and what was two blocks is now one, fused, grown together. This is done again. The wall rises, is wetted, sags, rises. It is never straight because it cannot be, because every joint is a little collapse. A finished wall is a wall that has finished settling, which is also the wall that is already starting to dissolve at the base where the floor's own brine reaches it, which is also the wall that the floor is beginning to carry somewhere else.

Every building has the same slumped, ancient, fluid shape because that is the only shape salt and water together permit. The builders don't impose it. They have learned to start there. A new hemisphere goes up looking already worn — the blocks pre-rounded, the joints over-wetted, the whole thing nudged toward the form it would take in a century anyway — because a structure that starts settled settles less. Ten thousand years of erosion, achieved on purpose, in a week and a half. Then the floor moves and the building splits and they cut new blocks and start again forty paces to the left.

That is what the spacing is for. Everything needs room to travel.

---

The city, then, is in a state of constant furious construction. Understand that. It is not a ruin. There is more building going on here than in any place you have ever stood — at any given moment, in any direction, someone is cutting, someone is carrying, someone is pouring brine, someone is dragging a slumped structure's blocks out of a fold to reuse them, someone is walking a line of the floor and marking with a bone peg where it has begun to swell. Whole clusters of hemispheres migrate across the salt over the course of a season, abandoned on one side and rebuilt on the other, following the flow of the floor the way a herd follows grass. The city as a whole is probably moving. Nobody knows in which direction, because there is nothing fixed to measure it against.

And all of it is nearly silent.

Salt does not ring. Struck, it gives a dull, close *tock* that dies within a few feet. Dragged, it hisses very faintly, a sound like dry breath. Poured brine makes no sound at all except a small tick when a block settles. Feet on the floor are quiet; the surface is soft, crumbling, sugar over harder sugar. The ceiling is far enough overhead that nothing echoes off it, and the humid air deadens what little does carry. The people — it is easiest to call them people — do not speak much and never call across a distance; there is no point, the sound won't reach. They gesture, or they walk over. A crew of a dozen dismantling a failed hump and carrying it off makes less noise than one person breathing hard in a closed room.

So the turbulence of the place doesn't come to you through your ears. It comes through your eyes, in the glare. You see the movement everywhere before you understand what you're seeing: figures crossing and recrossing the bright salt at every distance, bent under blocks, small with distance, dozens at a time in the middle ground and more at the edge of sight — like watching a beach from a cliff, the whole surface stirring — and none of it making a sound you can separate from your own pulse.

---

The animals are the other half of the motion.

They are pale, all of them, and most are blind. The largest and most common are things the size of a small dog, long-bodied, many-legged, with plated backs the color of the salt, that move in loose herds of forty or a hundred across the open floor with a rippling, fluent gait — not scuttling, gliding, the legs too many and too fast to read individually. They feed on the films of something — bacterial, algal, nobody here has a word for it — that grow in the brine pans where the light is brightest, pinkish and orange scums at the pan edges, the only color in the place besides the amber of the roof. They also eat each other's dead. They also, if a wall is left too long unwetted so that the surface goes crusty and dry, lick it, which is why the builders keep everything damp: a herd can take a fist's depth off a neglected structure in a night. They are not hunted. They are not tame. They go where they go, and a herd crossing a work site parts around the crews the way water parts around a stone, and nobody looks up.

Smaller things live in the joints of the buildings — thumb-length, translucent, thousands of legs or hairs, that come out when the brine is poured and drink it at the seam. On the ceiling, hanging from it in the thicker, cooler stretches where the light is amber rather than white, are colonies of something soft and hanging, gray, fringed, that move very slowly and drip. Under the floor, in the brine that fills old dissolved voids, there are eyeless fish the length of a finger, and the builders sometimes break through into one of those voids while quarrying and the fish come up gasping in the sudden light and the herds converge.

None of it makes a sound. The herds' hundreds of legs on the salt produce a whisper you can hear only when they pass within arm's reach, and then only as a change in the quality of the quiet.

---

The rule of the place, if you spend long enough to feel it: nothing is permitted to be finished, and nothing is permitted to be new.

A building that looks new is a building that hasn't been shaped right, and it will fail sooner than the others — the sharp edges concentrate the dissolution, the level courses find the fold-lines and crack. A building that has been left to finish is a building that has been abandoned, and the herds are already at it. Between those two failures the whole city lives, in the narrow condition of *ancient-and-in-progress*, every structure a worn hemisphere with wet seams and a crew beside it, every crew already glancing at the floor for where the next one will need to go.

There is no old quarter. There is no founding site. Ask, and the gesture is a slow circle that takes in the entire cavity, the whole heaving floor, the whole bright roof, and means something like *this is the old part*, or *all of it is*, or *where else would it be*.

At the end of the day the light through the gypsum goes from white to gold to a long, deepening orange, and the shadows that have been absent all day arrive at once, streaming out from every hump and rim in the same direction, and the whole city, for the first time, has edges. The herds settle into the pans. The crews pour a last round of brine and go into the hemispheres, which are warmer than the open floor by a degree or two and hold the day's light for a while in the walls, a dull glow that fades. The condensation thickens. Somewhere a block settles with a tick, and then another, and the floor keeps moving, and nothing else is heard until the roof lights again.
