There is no high ground. That is the first thing, and it takes a while to understand it, because the eye keeps searching the fog for something to rest against and finding nothing, and after a while the eye gives up and rests on the fog itself. Whatever was here before — hills, ridges, escarpments — has been worn down over a span of time so long that the wearing is complete. The plain is not eroded. It is erosion's conclusion. The stone underfoot has no grain, no bedding planes, no direction. It has been handled so long by water that it has forgotten which way it was laid down.

Water stands on everything because there is nowhere lower for it to go. It does not pool so much as film: a skin a finger deep over the whole world, sheeting and shivering, never draining, never quite freezing. The air is one degree above ice and is itself half water. Fog does not roll in. It is simply the condition of the sky, a grey-zinc ceiling that rests a few hundred feet up and glows all over with no sun behind it, a light like the inside of a shell. Everything sweats. The few stones that break the surface — worn to the shape of knuckles — run with condensation, beads gathering on their undersides and dropping into the film below with a sound that would be small if there were anything else to hear.

But there is a great deal else to hear. Long before the plain resolves out of the fog, it is audible: a sound like a city heard from under a bridge at night. Low, constant, mechanical. A knocking with a rhythm to it, thousands of knockings that fall into and out of phase with one another so that the whole thing surges and slackens like breath. Under the knocking, a hiss — steam. Over it, sometimes, a long horn-note, a single animal call held for the better part of a minute, that carries across the flat and arrives from every direction at once.

Then the herds.

They cover the plain. Not the way animals cover a plain in a temperate world, in clumps and drifts with grass between them. There is no grass. There is nothing between them. They stand shoulder to shoulder from where the fog opens to where it closes again, and the ground is not visible under them anywhere, and the only reason to believe in the ground at all is the film of water that shows through the gaps at their feet and mirrors their bellies back up at them.

They are warm. That is why the plain steams. Each body runs hot in the cold, and the wet air touching it flashes to vapour, so that every animal stands in its own small plume and the plumes join and lift and become the fog, which condenses on the animals and runs off them and back into the film and is warmed again. The sky here is made of them. It has probably always been.

Up close, they are difficult to call animals and difficult to call anything else. Their hides are plates — not scales, but overlapping jointed panels with an oiled look, dark blue-grey and beaded with water, that slide across each other as the creature moves with a sound exactly like a heavy door on a good hinge. The legs, of which there are six or eight or more, are columnar and articulated in too many places. Along the back, a row of vents; the hiss comes from here, on the out-breath, and the breath is hot enough to feel from a few paces off. The heads are low and blunt and mostly mouth. They move, most of them, very little. They stand, and they knock, and they breathe.

The knocking is what they do. It is the industry.

Each creature works the ground beneath it. A forelimb rises and comes down, rises and comes down, with the regularity of a piston, driving something into the silt or drawing something out of it. Whatever it works is passed along the body, plate by plate, in a peristaltic ripple that runs from head to tail and is taken up by the animal behind, so that a line of them standing nose to tail is a conveyor, and a herd of ten thousand standing in interlocking lines is a mill. Material moves through them. Silt goes in at one end of the herd, grey and formless, and something comes out at the other — small hard shapes, dark, extruded from vents in the hindquarters and dropped into the water, where they sink. Rods. Flat rounds with a hole through them. Lengths of a dull cable that has the look of drawn metal and is not metal, exactly, but something a body grew and then let go of.

Nothing is built from any of this. Nothing has ever been built here. The products drop into the film and the film takes them and over an immensity of time they are ground back into silt and go in again at the front of the herd. There is no factory. There are no walls, no floors, no shaped stone, no mark anywhere that a hand made on purpose. There is only production — production with no producer and no product, arrived at by no one, by the simple pressure of a very old world working its way down to the one thing that it does.

It is not clear that the herds began as animals that became mechanical. It is equally likely they were machines that, left in the wet for long enough, learned to breathe. Where the plain has settled into its final form, that distinction has eroded along with everything else.

There is an ecology, and it is not gentle. Smaller things live in the herds the way birds live on the backs of cattle, except that these are the size of large dogs and run along the animals' spines on many legs with a scuttling that is too fast to follow, feeding on the condensation, the extruded scrap, the soft joint-tissue between plates. When one of the great beasts falters — and they do falter; the sound will stutter locally, a knocking dropping out of the rhythm — the small ones converge on it before it has stopped moving. Larger things come after, from underneath. The film is deeper than it looks in places, or the silt below it is loose enough to swim through, and long low shapes push up between the legs of the herd with their backs breaking the surface like something surfacing in a harbour, and take what has fallen, and go back down. The herd closes over the gap within an hour. The rhythm resumes. It is not a rhythm anything has chosen, and there is no one to mourn.

Electricity moves in the water. It is not clear where it comes from — the animals, the friction of the plates, the sky — but the film carries it, and in the dim light a faint blue crawling can be seen along the surface, following the lines between the herds like current in a trace. Sparks jump between plates when two animals shift against each other. Some of the small scuttling creatures do not run along the backs at all but along the water, at the exact boundary where the film meets the air, riding the charge; where two currents cross, there is a flash and a smell like a struck stone and one fewer of them.

The plain is old enough that it has stopped agreeing with itself.

Distance does not hold. A herd at the fog-line appears the same size as a herd nearby, and when walked toward does not grow, and when reached is the same herd, or the nearby herd is the far one, or they were always both. The horizon comes and goes. Sometimes the fog opens and the plain runs out under it for what must be a hundred miles, flat and steaming and packed with bodies, and sometimes it closes to fifty paces and the world is a room. There is no way to tell the difference in the sound.

The animals repeat. Not resemble — repeat. A particular beast with a cracked plate on its left flank, a chipped mandible, a hiss with a catch in it, stands three lines to the south, and stands ahead, and stands, when one turns, behind. It is not three animals. The plain has been ground so smooth that individuals no longer have edges, and a single thing can be present in as many places as there is room for it, and there is very little room. Perhaps the herds are not many animals at all. Perhaps they are one, folded.

Time thins in the same way. A knock is heard and then the foreleg rises that made it. A beast falls and the scavengers are already on it and then it falls. The horn-note that carries across the plain is sometimes the note of a creature that has not yet called, and when it does call, the sound has already gone by. The film mirrors everything and the mirrored plain is not quite the one above it — a leg more, a leg fewer, a herd facing the other way — and it is the reflection that ripples when the real one moves, a moment early or late, and both are wet and both are cold and there is no basis for calling one of them the ground.

Nothing green. Nothing that photosynthesises, nothing that could. But the stones that break the surface wear a mat of something — a slick, coppery, almost invisible growth that is warm to the touch, warmer than the stone, and that spreads where the herd-steam is thickest, and that is the only thing here which does not knock, or breathe, or flash, or move. It may be the oldest thing on the plain. It may be what the plain is settling toward.

Weather cycles, in the sense that the fog thickens and thins. Rain, in the sense that the fog occasionally gives up on holding itself and lets go all at once, a fall of drops the temperature of the air, which does nothing to the film and nothing to the steam and which the herds do not notice. Ice forms overnight — if there is a night; the light does not change, but there is a slackening in the knocking that could be a night — in a lace around the stones and the outer legs of the outer herds, and the day's heat, which is the herds' heat, takes it back. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is carried away. Everything that happens here has happened here, in the same place, for longer than the stone remembers, and will go on happening in the same place, because there is no other place, because there is no relief.

Stand long enough and the cold comes up through the film and the knocking gets into the ribs and the fog fills the mouth with the taste of warm metal, and the temptation is to think this is a ruin — the ground floor of some civilisation that made engines and then became them and then forgot. But there is no ruin. Nothing was lost. There is no wreckage of anything anywhere under the water, and the silt goes down clean and grey and gearless as far as anything has dug. This is not the end of a world that built. It is the shape a world takes when it has stopped needing to be told what it is. Everything is ground down. Everything is working. The plain has been finished for a very long time, and it is loud, and it does not stop.
