**The Upland Mat**

Start with the altitude, because everything else follows from it. You are high enough that the sky has gone a bruised, oxidized color even at what should be midday, and the sun, when you find it, is a small hard disc that gives light without much warmth. The air is thin and cold and utterly dry. Breath does not fog. Nothing here has been wet in a very long time — the water went down, and down, and down, ages ago, and now lives in a world below the cloud-floor that you can sometimes see far off at the plateau's edge, a grey shelf of vapor with the whole rest of the planet under it. Up here there is only what stayed.

What stayed is the plant.

It is easiest to call it a plain, because that is what it looks like from a distance: a flat expanse running to every horizon, dark greenish-black, faintly rumpled like a blanket someone smoothed with a hand and then walked away from. But the ground is not ground. There is no soil. There is rock somewhere below — an ancient shield of basalt, cold to its core — and lying over it, for hundreds of kilometers in every direction, is a single continuous living tissue, perhaps a meter thick where it is thin, tens of meters where it has folded over itself, and it is the only vegetation and it is all of the vegetation and there is nothing that grows here that is not it.

It has been here long enough to forget its own edges. Where it once grew as separate colonies, pushing up against each other, the seams have healed into faint raised ridges, low as old field-boundaries, running across the plain in slow curves that no longer mean anything. Wind has polished its upper surface. Cold has shrunk it. What was once presumably a riot — mounds, fronds, whatever it did when it was young — has settled, over deep time, into this: a flatness so patient that the eye slides off it.

---

Get down and look. The surface is not smooth after all. It is made of countless small overlapping scales, each about the size of a thumbnail, dull and slightly waxy, the color of wet slate that is not wet. Press one and it gives, then pushes back. Between the scales, in the seams, a thin dark film glistens — not water. It smells faintly of pine resin and of something metallic, coins in a cold hand. This is the plant's solvent. It moves things around inside itself using an oil, or something like an oil, that stays liquid well below the temperature where water would have locked solid up here. Every cell of it is built around this chemistry. There is not a drop of water in the whole living country.

The plant is cool to the touch, a few degrees warmer than the air, which at this altitude counts as body heat. And it is not still. Hold your palm flat against it and you feel the pulse — slow, regular, one beat every eight or nine seconds, a soft internal pressure that travels under your hand from somewhere to somewhere. Something is being pumped. That pulse is the same across the entire plain. Walk a day and put your hand down again and it is the same rhythm, the same phase. It is one thing.

---

Now the sound, which you noticed first and have been trying to ignore.

The plateau is loud. Not intermittently loud — continuously, the way the inside of a ship's engine room is loud, so that after a while you stop hearing it as noise and start hearing it as the shape of the space. The bulk of it is a low drone, below the range of comfortable listening, that you feel in the sternum. That is the plant's vascular system, thousands of cubic kilometers of oil being moved under pressure through channels the size of drainpipes, and the channels resonating like organ pipes because they are hollow and long and the whole plain is a single membrane stretched over a stone drum.

Over the drone there are cracks. Sharp, percussive, irregular — like distant rifle fire, like ice breaking on a lake — and each one is a place where the plant has contracted in the cold and split a scale, or where a pressure line has vented. After a crack, a hiss follows, and if you are close you can see it: a thin plume of pale vapor jetting up from a seam for a second or two, heavier than air, sagging back down and pooling in low places as a mist that is not water and that you are careful not to breathe. The whole plain does this. Stand at dusk when the air is cooling and the cracks come faster, a slow fusillade rolling across the country from wherever the shadow-line is, and you can *hear the cold arrive* before you feel it.

And under and through all that, the animals.

---

The plant is not alone, though it is the only plant. Things live on it and in it, and there are a great many of them, and none of them are quiet.

The commonest are the grazers — call them scrapers — flat, wide, segmented creatures the size of a dinner plate up to the size of a door, armored on top in the same slate color as the plant so that from any distance they vanish, revealed only by their movement. They move in herds across the surface, dozens or hundreds, and they eat the plant's scales by prying them up with the front edge of their carapace and rasping the tissue beneath. The rasping is audible from a hundred meters, a wet-sounding grinding that has no wetness in it. Behind a herd the plain is lighter, raw, pale grey-green where it has been scraped, and you can see the plant already beginning to seal itself — a darkening, a beading of oil along the wound — and in a week the scales will be back. The scrapers do not seem to damage the plant so much as tend it. The scraped patches, later, are where the plant grows fastest, as if it needed the abrasion. They have been doing this to each other for so long that the distinction between grazing and grooming has worn away.

The scrapers are eaten by things that come up out of the plant. The surface is riddled with pores — you will step over dozens without noticing, each a puckered dark hole the width of an arm — and from these, without warning, an animal like a long muscular tongue with a bone hook at the tip will surge out, wrap a scraper, and drag it down into the channels below. It happens in under a second. The sound is a slap and a shriek and then the drone closes over it. Then the herd, which has scattered outward in a ring, drifts back together and resumes rasping, because it has to eat, because the plant is the only food and the plant is also where the danger lives. This is the shape of most lives here.

There are things in the air, too — this high, the air is too thin for anything heavy to fly, so they are large and light, membrane-winged and nearly translucent, riding thermals the plant itself generates. They cry constantly, a hollow two-note call that carries enormously in the dry air, and they are what you will hear most at night. They nest in the standing things.

---

The standing things. Yes.

The plain is flat, but not entirely. Scattered across it, far apart — you will see three or four at once from any high point, and the nearest is often an hour's walk — are structures that rise out of the mat. Some are twenty meters tall. Some are two hundred. They have the shape of something between a tree and a cooling tower and a bone: a flared base where the plant has climbed up around them, a shaft of pale, ridged, chitinous material that is not the plant's tissue, and a crown that is open, fluted, sometimes broken.

The plant has grown over all of them, in the way ivy grows over ruins, except that this took ten thousand times as long and the ivy is a single organism the size of a continent. Their bases are sheathed in it, thickly. Their shafts are laced with dark vascular cords that climb the ridges and disappear into the fluting. And the crowns *do* something.

What they do, as far as anyone can tell, is make the sky.

Stand below one at the moment the cold arrives, when the cracking is loudest, and look up. The crown of the spire will be faintly luminous — a dim violet glow in the fluting, the color of a gas discharge — and the vapor that the plain has vented all day, the heavy, sagging not-water mist, will be *rising* toward it, drawn up in a slow spiral that thickens as it climbs, and at the crown it goes into the fluting and does not come out. The plain breathes out, and the spires breathe in. Across the whole plateau, in the dusk, you can see them: a scattering of violet points on the dark horizon, each with its slow-turning column of pale vapor feeding into it. Whatever the plant exhales, the spires recover and return to it — down the vascular cords, into the pulse. Nothing is lost. It has been a closed loop for so long that the loop is the landscape.

Nobody knows what the spires are made of. The material does not weather. You can chip it, barely, and the chip is warm and stays warm for days and then is not a chip anymore, is a bit of grit indistinguishable from the plant's own dust. It appears to be — this is the phrase used — *not entirely present*, in the way that a very fast fan blade is not entirely present. Things pass through the fluting that should not fit. Sound behaves oddly near them: the drone goes silent in a perfect ring about ten meters out, and then resumes.

Whoever built them built them to do exactly this, in exactly this cold, on a mountaintop that had lost its water and needed something to make life possible anyway. And then built or planted or released the plant. And then — nothing. There is no trace of them. The plant grew over the trace, and the plain flattened, and the spires kept working, and that is the whole record. This is not a ruin. A ruin is something that has stopped. This has not stopped. It is only very, very old, and has become so simple that it no longer needs anyone to understand it.

---

The town is not far from here. You can see it from the base of the nearest spire, once you know what to look for: a cluster of humps on the plain, lower than the spire, a slight thickening of the mat, a place where the ridges run in straight lines rather than curves.

It was built *into* the plant, not on it. Whoever lives here — people, more or less, thin and swaddled and slow-moving in the cold, hard to tell how many, perhaps a few thousand spread across a settlement the size of a small valley — did not cut the plant or clear it. They shaped it. They found, at some point in their long occupation, that the plant will grow around a form: put a shell of woven cord on the surface and hold it there and in a season the plant has climbed over it and sealed it and a year later you can take the form out and there is a room, dark green-black and warm-walled, that pulses faintly and is heated from within by the oil moving through it.

So the town is a warren of these rooms, connected by grown tunnels, mostly below the surface, roofed by the plain. From outside you see only the humps and the straight-line ridges and, here and there, a vent-chimney where the plant has been coaxed into a hollow tube and the exhaled vapor of the households rises in a thin column and drifts toward the spire. The town's infrastructure is the plant's infrastructure. Its water — its solvent — comes up through taps grown into the walls, warm and resinous, and they cook with it and wash in it and drink, carefully, a distilled fraction of it. Their light is dim and violet, small tapped-off filaments from whatever runs in the vascular cords, cool to the touch and steady. Their doors are muscles. Their streets are the seams.

They are loud, too. Everything here is. They speak in a pitched register that carries over the drone, half-sung, and they hammer on the plant's skin with staves to signal across distances — the mat is an excellent drum, and a message struck at one end of the town arrives at the other as a shudder underfoot before it arrives as a sound. When a scraper herd moves through — and they do, right over the roofs, hundreds of armored plates rasping in the dark above the bedrooms — the whole town rattles with it and nobody wakes.

They keep the pore-tongues out of the settlement by a method that is simple and unsettling: the town's children spend their afternoons walking the perimeter and stamping on the pores in a particular rhythm, and the tongues below learn the rhythm means *do not come here*, and the plant, which is in some sense the tongues' house, folds those pores closed over a few generations. The town's edge is a ring of healed-shut mouths. Beyond it the plain is alive with them.

---

What the people here understand about the spires is this: they are not to be touched, and they must be *tended*, which is a different thing. Tending consists of walking to the nearest spire at intervals — there is a calendar, based on the frequency of the cracking — and clearing the plant's scales from a certain band at the base, where the vascular cords enter, so that the cords stay exposed to the air. If this is not done, the spire's glow dims over a few years and the vapor stops rising and pools instead, and the plain around the spire goes pale and soft and the pulse under it stutters. Nobody in living memory has let this happen. Nobody knows why it works. They know that the ones who lived here before them did it, because the band around every spire is worn smooth to a depth of centimeters by a thousand generations of scraping hands.

They do not think of themselves as the builders' heirs. They think of themselves as one more animal that the plant tolerates because it is useful. The scrapers keep the surface young. The tongues keep the scrapers moving. The gliders carry seed-dust — the plant does not seed, exactly, but it sheds a fine oily powder that the gliders track through the spire-crowns and disperse across the plain, and where it lands the mat thickens. And the people keep the spires' feet clear. The plant has arranged all of this. Or the plant *is* all of this — a machine so old and so complete that its parts have become its ecosystem, and its ecosystem has become its parts, and the difference is not one the plant is interested in.

---

Go out at night onto the open mat, away from the town's humps, and stand still.

The cold comes down hard and the cracking rises to a roar and then, as the plain finishes contracting, slackens to sporadic reports. The drone steadies. The gliders call, two notes, from far off. The sky is very dark — the small hard sun is gone and the stars are close and unwavering in the thin air, more of them than seems reasonable — and on every horizon there are the violet points, four, six, nine of them, each with its faint pale column rising, turning. The plain between you and them is black and flat and pulsing very slowly under your boots, and somewhere in it a herd is grinding, and somewhere a pore opens and closes.

It is not a place that is dying. Every indicator you would use to judge that — the flatness, the age, the dry, the dim — belongs to something that finished changing an unimaginable time ago and has been doing the same thing ever since, at full volume, in the cold, without any need of witnesses. You are welcome to stand here. The plant will not notice. If you stay long enough, and are useful, it will grow you a room.
