The plateau is called, on the older maps, the Pan, and it is roughly the size of a small province: a table of pale stone lifted a mile and a half above the plains, with edges that drop away on three sides and a fourth side that rises further into a range nobody has much reason to cross. From below, on a clear day, you can see steam standing off it in a white column that leans with the wind. From below you can also hear it, faintly, as a pressure behind the ears rather than a sound, the way you can feel a large engine idling in a building you have not yet entered.

On the Pan itself the sound is everything. It is the first fact of the place and eventually the only one.

---

The stone weeps. That is the simplest way to say it. The plateau is a great warm sponge of limestone sitting over something hot, and water comes up through it everywhere — not in springs exactly, though there are springs, but as a general oozing, a sweat that beads on any exposed face of rock and runs, and pools, and runs again. It comes up warm, about the temperature of a body, and it comes up heavy with lime, so that wherever it slows down it leaves itself behind. The whole surface of the Pan is terraced in shallow scalloped basins of travertine, cream and buff and the grey of old teeth, each basin brimming and spilling into the next, a landscape built entirely from the slow deposition of its own drainage. Walking across it is like walking across the inside of a kettle that has been boiled every day for ten thousand years.

Very little grows. The water is too mineral and the ground is too much a crust; a seed has nowhere to get a root. What there is: mats of algae in the warm basins, orange and rust and a green so dark it reads black, lying in the water like wet felt. A grey lichen on the north faces of things. In the lees of the larger structures, where dust has accumulated and been rained on, there are patches of a stiff sedge that nobody has named and a low succulent with leaves like small purple thumbs. That is the vegetation of the Pan. From any high point you look out over miles of bone-coloured ground with nothing standing on it but the works.

---

The works are the city.

It was never a city in the sense of a place where people gathered to live near one another. It was built as a machine for getting water off the plateau — or possibly onto it; the records are unclear on which direction the original engineers considered the problem to run — and the people came afterwards, to tend the machine, and stayed because the machine did not stop. What stands now is the result of about a hundred and forty years of that tending. The Pan is covered, thinly but completely, with infrastructure: pump houses the size of cathedrals, brick and iron and now mostly stone, because the weeping water has skinned everything in travertine and the brick shows through only where someone has recently chipped it clear. Aqueducts on arched piers walk across the terraces in straight lines for miles, carrying water from where it rises to where it can be dropped over the edge. Standpipes and pressure towers and cooling stacks stand about at intervals of a quarter-mile or so, with wide empty fields of scalloped stone between them. From the air it would look like a plan for a city drawn by someone who then forgot to fill in the blocks.

The gaps are the point. Between any two installations there is a walk of ten or twenty minutes across ground that is entirely empty except for water and the noise. You are always in the city; you are almost never near anyone. The population is a few thousand, scattered in dormitory blocks attached to the pump houses like barnacles, and a person may go days seeing only the two or three others on their shift.

Everything is encased. This is the slow work of the place: the water beads on an iron rail and evaporates and leaves lime; it does this hourly, for decades; the rail becomes a smooth pale bar twice its original thickness with no trace of its original surface. Stairs have become ramps. Bolts have become bumps. The oldest pump houses have lost their windows to it — not broken, but filled, the glass now a milky inch of stone with the shadow of a sash somewhere inside. Chipping crews go round with hammers on a rotation that takes eleven years to complete a circuit of the Pan, and by the time they return the crust is back to about the thickness it was. The city has reached a state in which it grows and is cut back at the same rate, and this has been true for longer than anyone working there has been alive. Nothing is new. Nothing is ruined either. It is simply, entirely, in its own condition.

---

The sound.

It comes from two sources that have merged so completely that nobody separates them anymore. The first is the water going over the edge. The Pan drains on three sides by falls — not one great cataract but hundreds of them, wherever a terrace runs out of table, a mile-long fringe of white dropping into steam. Warm water falling a thousand feet through cool air makes a roar with a peculiar soft floor to it, a boom rather than a hiss, and the escarpment throws it back inward so that the whole plateau sits inside its own reverberation. The second source is the works. Every pump house holds engines — steam at the core, electrical now around the edges, the two systems bolted to each other in ways that only the oldest fitters understand — and every engine runs, and has run, and the pipes carry the vibration of it out across the stone so that the ground itself hums at a pitch you can feel in your molars. The aqueducts ring. The pressure towers moan when the wind is in the north.

Together it is deafening in the literal sense. New workers are issued wax and wool for the first year and told not to expect it to matter. By the third year most have lost the upper register entirely; by the tenth they are deaf in the way the Pan makes people deaf, which is not silence but a permanent internal roar, the sound of the place carried away inside the skull. There is a sign language. There is a system of lamps and coloured flags on every tower for talking between installations. The dormitory blocks have thick walls and nobody in them speaks above a mutter, since speaking is pointless and muttering is a habit of the mouth that persists.

What is strange — what people notice only after they have been there some time — is that the sound has structure. It is not one noise. Each district of the Pan sits in a slightly different chord: the eastern falls are lower than the western, the old high-pressure works around the Fourth House have a note in them like a struck rail, the water passing through the long aqueduct to the south edge sings a fifth above everything else. Workers can tell where they are on the plateau in fog, at night, blind, by the sound alone — and more than that, they can tell the season by it, and the hour, because the flow shifts with rain on the range and the engines are throttled to match, and the whole plateau breathes in a cycle that takes a year to complete and is audible, to those who know it, as a slow modulation of the roar. They say the Pan is playing something. They say this without particular wonder, the way one might say a river is high.

There is a further claim, made mostly by the very old: that the sound has been getting lower. Not quieter — lower in pitch, by a fraction, over their working lives. Nobody has instruments capable of settling it and nobody has tried. The explanation offered, when one is offered, is that the travertine is thickening on everything, on the pipes and the towers and the arched piers and the ground, and thick things ring lower than thin ones, and the city is very slowly being tuned down by its own skin.

---

Fauna is almost absent. The noise and the water between them make the Pan uninhabitable for anything that hunts by ear or needs to drink something other than warm chalk. There are, at the edges, where the falls go over, a few colonies of a dark swift that nests in the spray zone and appears to be entirely deaf; they fly in silence through the steam, feeding on whatever insects the warm updrafts lift from the plains below. In the algae basins there is a small red crustacean, blind, no bigger than a grain of rice, in numbers that make the water look rusted. And there are the eels, which nobody has explained. Once every few years a pump house draws one up from the deep works — pale, thick as a wrist, eyeless — and it lies flexing on the intake grate until a fitter throws it back. Where they live, in what reservoir under how much stone, is not known. The engineers' drawings of the sub-plateau workings run out about three hundred feet down and below that there is only the word *warm*, in the hand of someone dead a century.

---

The weather is a wet warmth that never quite becomes rain. The plateau makes its own cloud; the steam from the falls and the basins rises and cools and comes down again as a fine settling mist, so that the air is always at the edge of saturation and everything not encased in stone is beaded. Clothes do not dry. Paper rots in a week. The light comes through the standing haze diffused and shadowless, a bright grey most days, and on the rare clear evenings when a wind off the range shreds the cloud the low sun strikes the wet travertine and the whole Pan goes gold and pink for twenty minutes, every tower and pier and terrace edge lit along its wet contour, and the workers come out onto the roofs of the pump houses to watch it, and say nothing, because there would be no point, and because they have seen it before and will again.

---

The engines run. That is the last thing to understand about the place. They have run for a hundred and forty years and the question of why — what the water is being moved for, whether the plains below need it or fear it, whether the Pan would flood or empty if they stopped — has passed from urgent to academic to something more like a piece of weather, a condition of the world. The younger fitters, when pressed, will say the pumps keep the works from drowning. The older ones say the works exist to keep the pumps running, and smile, and turn back to the valve they are chipping free. Supplies come up the switchback road from the plains once a month on an electric lorry that grinds for six hours and arrives coated in pale mud; it brings coal and coffee and cable and new workers, and takes down the ones who have finished, and they go home to the plains and lie awake in the terrible silence of ordinary places and hear, inside their heads, the note the Pan was holding when they left it.
