The first thing to understand is that you are very high, and that you will never see how high.

Your body knows. The breath comes short at the top of each inhale, as though the air has a ceiling you keep bumping against. Your ears hold a pressure that does not resolve. Your pulse is quick and shallow and stays that way. In another place these would be the signs of a mountain, and there would be a view to go with them, land folding away below in blue distances, the sense of having climbed above something. Here there is nothing to climb above. The plateau is not the top of anything. It is simply where the world stopped, a tableland so broad that its edges, if it has edges, are somewhere past the reach of any walk you could take, and so worn that nothing on it rises more than a few dozen meters above anything else.

And it is dark.

Not the dark of night, which implies a day. This is a dark with no opposite. You stand in it for a long time before your eyes concede that it is not absolute. Above you there are stars, but few, and they are old stars, dull and orange, scattered thin across a sky that has more black in it than anything else. Whatever burned here once has gone out, or gone away, and what remains overhead is the leftover light of things very far off and very tired. Under that light the ground is a slightly different quality of black from the sky. The horizon is a line where one black meets another, and you find it only by looking for the place where the stars stop.

You crouch and put your hand flat on the ground.

Fine dust, then stone. That is all your hand reports. Not warm, not cold. You lift your hand and try again, expecting the ground to have some temperature, because ground always does; ground is cool at night and hot at noon and holds the memory of one into the other. This ground has no memory. It is exactly the temperature of your palm. You press your cheek to it and it is the temperature of your cheek. It is as though you were touching the inside of your own skin.

This is the rule of the place, and you will learn it over and over. Everything here is the same temperature, and that temperature is the temperature of a living body: warm, unremarkable, without edges. The air is that temperature. The dust is. The stone underneath the dust. Nothing here is warmer than anything else, and so touch, which in the dark should be your best sense, is half-blinded too. You can feel shape and texture. You cannot feel *kind*. A hand laid on a wall cannot tell you whether the wall is stone or metal or fired clay, because the tell of material in the dark is how quickly it drinks your heat, and nothing here drinks anything. The world has come to rest at one temperature and stays there, and you are a small disturbance in it that it will absorb without noticing.

---

You walk. There is no direction to choose, so you choose one.

The dust is shallow and does not shift under you. It has settled so long it has a crust, not hard, but coherent, and your footprints press into it with a faint dry sound that is the loudest thing for kilometers. You are aware of this. You are aware that these are, in all likelihood, the only footprints on this plateau, and that when you are gone they will remain for longer than the memory of you does.

After some time, perhaps twenty minutes, perhaps an hour, you notice that a column of stars is missing.

It is a stack. You know this only because you walk toward the absence until your hands find it: a curve of masonry, wider than your arms can span, rising past your reach. The surface is smooth in a way that masonry should not be. You feel for mortar lines and find them, but the bricks have no edges. Each one has been rounded like a stone in a riverbed, the joints between them worn into shallow grooves, so that the whole column feels less built than grown, or less grown than *eroded into*, as if the stack had been carved out of a single body of rock by something patient. You walk around its base. It is perhaps twelve meters across. At one point there is an opening, an iron door long since fused into its frame, and the frame too is rounded, and the rivets along it are domed smooth as pebbles.

There was wind here once. That is what the smoothness means. Wind carrying dust across this flat for a very long time, scouring every corner off every thing. And then the wind stopped. Wind is a difference in pressure, and pressure is a difference in heat, and when the heat evened out the wind had nothing left to move it. The rounding froze at the exact stage it had reached. Everything on the plateau is worn to precisely the same degree, no more and no less, and it will never be worn further. You are touching the last moment of a process, held.

You crane your neck and find the top of the stack against the stars. It should have a crisp rim. It has a soft one, like the mouth of a bottle that has been in the sea.

And at its base, where the door is, if you kneel and put your eye to the gap between the fused door and its frame, there is — something.

Red. The faintest imaginable red. Not light, exactly; the eye cannot resolve it into a shape or a source. More the *idea* of red, in the same way the sky above is the idea of not-quite-black. It is coming from very deep inside, down in what would be the furnace throat, and it is the color of metal that is hotter than its surroundings by the smallest amount that still counts as hotter. You hold your eye there for a long time. It does not flicker. It does not brighten or dim. Whatever is down there has been at that exact temperature, that exact color, for longer than the concept of a shift or a working day, and it is not cooling, because to cool it would have to give its heat to a world that has no more room to take it.

---

From the first stack, you find the second by walking.

There is a line in the dust. You feel it with your feet before you understand it: two parallel ridges, a little over a meter apart, running dead straight. Rails, sunk to their heads in sediment, the sleepers between them long gone or long buried. You follow them. They lead you in a straight line for what you count as eleven hundred paces to the next stack, which is the same as the first in every way you can measure. Then eleven hundred paces more to a third.

This is the city. This is how you begin to understand it.

It is not a city in the sense of a place where things crowd. It is a city in the sense of a plan. Every structure stands at an interval from every other, and the intervals are regular, and between them is nothing but the flat and the dust and the rail. In the dark you cannot see the plan, but you can walk it. You turn ninety degrees at a stack and find another rail line, and follow it, and find another stack at the same distance. You find a place where rails cross and there is a switch, its lever frozen in the dust, its throw-bar smooth as bone. You find a long low shape that your hands tell you is a wall, and then that it is a building, a hall a hundred meters on a side, its roof gone or never there, its floor paved in some poured stone that is smooth and slightly domed and takes your footprints not at all.

Inside the hall there is a wheel.

You find it by walking into it. It is a flywheel, you decide, or the rim of one, standing higher than your head on a horizontal shaft, its face broad and slightly convex under your hands. Metal, you assume, but it is the temperature of your hands, so you cannot be sure. You put both palms flat on it and lean.

It does not move.

You walk around it. It is perhaps six meters across. On the far side there are gear teeth, each one rounded at its tip like a molar. There is a second wheel meshed with the first, and a third, smaller, meshed with the second, and then a shaft going down through the floor into the dark below the dark. You put your ear to the shaft.

You hear nothing. And then you go on hearing nothing, deliberately, for a long time, until the nothing begins to have a texture, and the texture is a note. It is so low it is less a sound than a slight pressure behind the sternum. It does not vary. It could be the machine. It could be the plateau. It could be your own blood, finding a pitch in the thin air. You cannot know and you do not try to know.

Whether the wheel is turning is a question the place declines to answer. You mark a tooth with a scratch of your thumbnail. You will come back in some hours and find the scratch exactly where you left it, which will tell you either that the wheel is stopped or that it turns at a rate your hours cannot measure. You suspect the second. You suspect that everything here is running, that the machines were never shut off, that they simply slowed as the gradients they drank from grew shallower, until they reached the speed of the world itself, which is a speed so close to zero that only the world can tell the difference. Somewhere in this hall a gear may have completed a single rotation since the wind stopped. Somewhere below your feet a piston may be halfway through a stroke it began before the sky went dark.

---

The silence is a material, like the dust. It is what happens to a place when there is nothing to move the air.

You learn its grain. There is the low pressure-note, if it is real. There is the sound of your own body, which is loud here — your breath rasping at the top of each shallow inhale, your heart in your ears, the creak of your knee when you crouch. There is the dry hiss of your feet in the dust. All of these sounds die within a few meters. The thin air does not carry them; the flat has nothing for them to bounce off.

And then, very rarely, there is something else.

A click.

You are standing beside a rail line when it comes and you stop as if struck. It was small, sharp, metallic, and it was far off — you have no idea how far; in air this still and this thin, a sound can travel a great distance before it dies, and arrive with all its edges intact. A relay closing. A latch. A rod reaching the end of a very long travel and touching a stop. Some mechanism somewhere in the grid completing one step of a sequence that has been running since before anyone was here to hear it.

Then nothing. You wait. You count to a thousand. Nothing. You will hear it again, perhaps, in some hours, and it will be the same click, from what may be the same place or may be a different node in the plan, and you will never learn what it is. It is one of perhaps a dozen sounds this whole plateau makes, and they are spaced across the hours the way the stacks are spaced across the flat: regularly, sparsely, according to a scheme you can walk but cannot read.

---

The pipes are the closest thing here to a road.

They run between the halls on trestles at about the height of your shoulder, wide enough to lay a hand on while you walk, and unlike the rails they were never buried, so you can follow them without stooping. They sag between supports in shallow catenaries that have not deepened in ages, because the metal has finished creeping and settled into its final line.
