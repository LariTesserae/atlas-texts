You come to it from above, which is the only way anyone has ever come to it. On the surface there is a plain of cracked red laterite and thorn scrub, a country of glare where the ground rings when you kick it, and in the middle of that plain there is a hole. Not a large one. Perhaps the width of a small house. Its rim is lipped with a collar of ferns so green they look wet even at noon, and from the edge a column of cooler-smelling air rises against your face — cooler, but not cool. Warm and sweet and faintly sulfurous, like breath.

The hole goes down for perhaps four times the height of a person before it opens.

There are steps. This is the first thing. Someone, a very long time ago, cut a stair into the eastern wall of the shaft — shallow, uneven treads worn into a slick trough by feet and then by water and then by nothing but time. They are gone in places. Roots have pried them apart and then, growing fat, have replaced them, so that halfway down you are no longer stepping on stone but on the living cambium of a fig that has decided to be a staircase. The wood is warm.

At the bottom the chamber opens sideways, and you understand that the hole is not the place. The hole is a chimney. The place is a room.

---

It is a room the size of a modest church, its ceiling an irregular dome of limestone that has been dissolving for so long it has forgotten it was ever a flat bed of sea-floor. Rain arriving through the shaft has carved a single bright oculus, and beneath that oculus a shaft of light falls and stops dead on the floor like something dropped. Everything else is dimness. Not darkness — the light that falls is redistributed, bounced off pale wet stone, filtered through leaf after leaf after leaf — but a dimness that the eye keeps trying to swim up out of and cannot. After a while you stop trying. After a while the dimness resolves into a thousand shades of itself.

The heat is the second thing you notice, once you have stopped noticing the light. It is the heat of a body. It comes from below. Somewhere under the floor of this chamber there is a fissure, or a hundred fissures, and the ground is warm to the palm everywhere, and in three or four places along the northern wall the rock is warm enough that you cannot hold your hand against it. There is no steam. The water that seeps here is not hot enough for that; but it is warm, and the air is saturated, and every surface sweats. Drops form on the underside of every leaf and hang. The ceiling is beaded. Where the beads fall they have fallen for so long that the floor beneath is dimpled into cups, each cup holding a sip of clear water in which small things live.

---

The floor is not level. It slopes gently from the dry eastern side, where the fig-stair comes down, toward the west, where the chamber narrows and the ceiling lowers and the light gives out completely. Along that gradient the vegetation changes as if someone had arranged it by hand. Nearest the oculus, in the one place where sun actually arrives, a single tree has grown — a strangler fig, or the descendant of one, its trunk a fluted braid of grey the color of elephant hide, its crown pressed flat against the ceiling and spreading there like a hand against a low roof. It has never known open sky. It has grown toward a coin of light for two hundred years and reached it and can go no further, and so it has thickened instead, and its aerial roots hang down in curtains that have rooted and thickened and rooted again until the eastern half of the chamber is a colonnade.

Under the fig, in the diffuse light of its shadow, there are begonias with leaves the size of platters, spotted silver. There are selaginellas, the club-mosses, so dense they form a single spongy surface you could lie down on, a surface that shivers with iridescence — blue at one angle, green at another. There are ferns of six or seven kinds, some no bigger than your thumb, growing directly out of the dripping wall in a fur so thick the stone itself is invisible. Further west, where the light is barely a rumor, the ferns give up and the mosses take over, and then the mosses thin, and the last living green is a smear of algae on the wet lip of a pool, and after that the world is fungus: pale shelves and coral-branched things and a white filamentous mat that covers the far wall like frost and, if you shine no light on it and let your eyes settle, gives back a faint green luminance of its own. Enough to see the outline of your hand. Not enough to see your fingers.

---

The animals are everywhere. This is the third thing, and it is the thing that stays with you.

They are everywhere and they make almost no sound.

The first you see are the snails. They are on every surface — on the fig's roots, on the begonia leaves, on the wet walls, on each other. Some are the size of a fingernail and translucent amber; some are as big as a fist, their shells banded in cream and rust, their bodies a mottled grey that moves like slow water. When you stand still you can hear them. Not their movement, which is soundless, but their eating: a faint, papery rasping, a hundred radulae working a hundred leaves, a sound so soft it might be your own blood.

Then the millipedes, which are longer than your hand and the deep glossy red of a fire-engine gone dark, and which move over the moss with a rippling of legs that catches what little light there is. They are unhurried. Nothing here is hurried, except the things that must be.

Then the frogs. They sit on the fig roots and in the cups of water on the floor and on the undersides of the great begonia leaves, clinging there upside-down with their bellies pressed to the wet. Small, most of them, no bigger than the top joint of a thumb, and colored like the leaves they sit on, and they do not call. Frogs call for mates across distance, and here there is no distance; everything is already touching everything else. When they need to be found they are found. And so they sit and blink and their throats do not move, and if you did not know to look you would think the chamber empty of them, and in fact there are thousands.

The salamanders are shyer. They live in the cups and pools, and along the wet base of the walls, long and pale and soft-looking, their eyes reduced to dark smudges beneath the skin. They come from further down. There are passages at the western end, low and flooded, and the salamanders belong to those passages and come up into the chamber only to feed, and you see them as a rippling in the algae-lit water and then a pale flank turning and then nothing.

And the bats — these you see last, because they are above you, packed into the folds and pockets of the ceiling in a felted mass of brown that moves slightly, constantly, like something breathing. There may be tens of thousands. Their smell is the sulfurous sweetness you noticed at the surface. Their guano falls in a slow patient snow on the western floor, where it has built up over centuries into a soft dark hill that is itself a country: beetles, mites, a cockroach flat and glossy and utterly silent, a centipede the length of a forearm that hunts through the hill by feel. The bats do not chatter. They are the small insectivorous kind, and their voices are pitched above hearing, and in the day they sleep, and the only sound they make is the occasional dry rustle of one shifting against its neighbors, a sound like a page turning in another room.

---

This is what you come to understand about the silence: it is not an absence. It is a condition of density. When a place holds this many lives in this small a space, the ordinary noise of the world — the calling, the warning, the display — becomes unnecessary or dangerous or both. There is nowhere to run to. Everything that hunts here hunts by stillness. Everything that hides hides by stillness. The result is a quiet so complete that your own breathing becomes an event, and you find yourself slowing it, and slowing your feet, until you are moving at the pace of the place.

And yet it is not still. That is the paradox you keep circling. The snails advance along their leaves. The millipedes cross and recross the moss. A drop swells on the tip of a fern and lets go and lands in a cup and a frog that was in the cup is no longer in it. The bats breathe. The fungus on the far wall is, if you watch a single spot for long enough, visibly growing. In the pools the salamanders come and go. A wolf spider the size of a hand — gone the moment you look at it — has been sitting on the fig root a foot from your shoulder for an hour, and is now somewhere else, and something that was on that root is gone too.

Predation here is a matter of subtraction. You never see it. You only count, and count again, and find the number smaller.

---

The built things are few, and they are all old, and they have all been absorbed.

There is the stair, which is now mostly tree.

There is a wall — or the base of one — running north-south across the chamber about a third of the way from the eastern end. Knee-high, three or four courses of fitted stone with no mortar, plumb still in places and slumped in others. It seems to have divided the light from the dark, the tree-half from the pool-half, and you can imagine a reason for that or none. The moss has taken it entirely. It is a green wall now, softly rounded, and along its top the fist-sized snails travel in a line, as though it were a road they had inherited.

There is a basin. Near the northern wall, in the warmest place, someone once carved a rectangular trough into the floor, and it fills from a seep and empties through a notch, and the water in it is very slightly warmer than the water anywhere else, and it is full of frogs. Whether it was for washing or for drinking or for some other purpose you cannot say. The rim is worn round. Someone sat here, on that rim, for a great many years.

And there is a niche. High on the eastern wall, above head height, behind the fig's hanging curtain of roots: a carved recess, arched, and in it a shape. It might have been a figure once. It has been dripped on for so long that it has become a stalagmite in the vague form of a seated thing — shoulders, a suggestion of a bowed head, a lap. The water has redressed it in a thin translucent skin of calcite that catches the dim light and glows faintly, so that it is the brightest object in the room after the oculus, and every fern and begonia between it and the shaft leans slightly toward it. You look at it for a long time. The snails have not touched it. It is the only thing in the chamber they have not touched.

---

Then the chamber breathes, and you understand what the heat has been doing.

It happens perhaps twice in an hour — the interval is not regular, you learn, but it is not random either; it has the rhythm of a large slow body. From the western end, from the low flooded passages where the salamanders live, a wave of warmer air pushes up into the room. You feel it first on the backs of your hands. Then you see it: the saturated air, meeting the slightly cooler air near the oculus, gives up its water all at once, and a fog forms in the middle of the chamber — not rising from below, not descending from above, but simply *appearing* in the air, a grey bloom that swells and fills the space between the fig roots and hides the far wall and turns the shaft of light into a solid white pillar.

Everything stops. It was quiet before; now it is quieter. The snails draw in. The frogs close their eyes. You can hear the fog condensing — a faint ubiquitous ticking as it beads on every surface and the beads join and the joined beads run.

It lasts a minute, or two. Then the pressure below eases, and the warm air stops arriving, and the fog does what fog does — it falls. It drips off everything at once. For thirty seconds the chamber is full of the sound of rain, though nothing is falling from the sky: every leaf and root and stone lets go of its water together, and the cups on the floor overflow and link and drain west, and the moss darkens, and the smell of the place — earth, sulfur, fig, bat — rises freshly as though the whole room had been rinsed.

And then the frogs open their eyes, and the snails come out, and the drip-sound thins to a tick, and the tick thins to the rasp of radulae on leaf, and it is over. The ferns are a shade greener. The fungus on the far wall has drunk. You are soaked through and were not rained on.

You realize the stair was cut so that a person would arrive in this room from the dry side. You realize the low wall may have marked where the fog begins. You realize whoever sat on the rim of the warm basin sat there for this: to be in a room that breathed, and to be breathed on.

---

Stay long enough and the oculus changes color. The disc of light on the floor goes from white to gold to a deep bruised orange and creeps across the floor and up the base of the fig and then — quite suddenly, the way things happen in tropical latitudes — it is gone, and the shaft above is a circle of violet, and then of stars.

The bats leave. Not with a roar. They peel off the ceiling in ones and dozens and hundreds, a soft leathery unfolding, a sound like wind in a coat, and they spiral up the shaft in a column so dense it blots the stars, and for ten minutes the air is full of the whisper of wings, and then the last of them is gone and the ceiling is bare grey stone dripping in the dark and the silence comes back heavier than before.

Now the fungus is the light. The far wall glows, faintly, a green so low it registers more as a place where the dark is less than as illumination. Things move against it. The salamanders come up from the pools in numbers you did not guess at. Something with many legs crosses the guano hill. The snails, which had been going east, are now going west. In the warm basin the frogs, who have not made a sound all day, make one: a single click, from one throat, answered by a single click from another, and then nothing. Enough. In a room this small, in a dark this complete, enough.

You sit on the rim of the basin. The stone is warm. Behind you, behind the curtain of roots, the calcite figure holds the last of the day's light in its skin and gives it back so slowly that it will still be faintly luminous at midnight. Below you, under the floor, the great body shifts, and the air pushes up, and the fog comes on in the dark, and you cannot see it but you feel it settle on your face and hear it begin, all around you, to tick.
