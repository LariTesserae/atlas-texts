The first thing to understand is that there is no way up. The tableland does not have sides. Whatever slopes once carried it out of the lowlands were the first things to go, worn back into the body of the plateau until the whole continent simply stands, at the height of high mountains, on a base that has become an accident of the horizon. From below you would see a wall of cloud. From above you see a floor.

It is very flat. Not the flatness of a plain, which is the flatness of deposit, of things settling in; this is the flatness of subtraction. Everything that could be taken away has been taken away. What is left is the residue of an entire mountain range reduced to its most stable expression: a grey-green surface, faintly undulant, that continues past the limit of seeing in every direction. The undulations are the ghosts of peaks. Some of them are a day's walk across and rise perhaps the height of a person. The people here call them shoulders and can name several thousand of them.

The stone, where it shows, is a dense pale gneiss that has forgotten it was ever anything else. It does not fracture. It has no bedding planes left to fracture along. It takes a polish from the wind and the wet and glows a little in cloud-light, the way a bone does.

---

The plateau makes its own weather, and its weather is mostly one thing: the cloud that sits on it.

Air comes up from the lowlands warm and wet and arrives here far above the altitude at which it can hold what it carries. It condenses. Not into rain, usually — there is not enough vertical distance for rain to organize itself — but into fog, into a dense standing cloud that lies on the surface for days and then, on some schedule the inhabitants track by the color of the light, lifts, thins, and pulls apart into ragged banners that drag across the shoulders and dissolve. When it lifts the sky is a hard cold blue very close overhead and the light is white and total. When it settles the world is a room of grey some hundred paces across.

Everything drips. Water beads on every surface and runs down every surface and collects in every hollow, and yet there are no rivers, because there is no fall for water to gather itself along. It moves in films. It moves through the moss. A basin the size of a hand fills overnight. A cut channel a thumb wide carries a steady thread of clear water for the length of a shoulder and empties into a cistern and the cistern is always half full and never fuller. Ice forms in the channels before dawn — thin, glassy, ringing faintly when the first light cracks it — and by midmorning the channels are running again.

The cold is not the killing cold of the summits. It is a permanent chill, a few degrees either side of freezing, damp enough to get into anything porous. It is the cold of a cellar or a cave that happens to be open to the sky. Breath hangs. Stone is painful to touch bare-handed in the morning. Nothing ever quite dries.

---

Given all this — the cold, the height, the thin air, the near-total absence of soil — the plateau should be barren, and it is not. It is thickly, deeply grown over.

The growth is low. There is nothing here taller than a child. But it is *lush*, in the sense of being dense and saturated and continuous: a sward of moss and cushion-plant and dwarf heath and a kind of creeping willow with leaves like fish-scales, all of it woven into a single mat that lies over the gneiss to a depth of a hand, a forearm, in the hollows to the depth of a standing man. Walking on it is like walking on a wet mattress. It springs back. It squelches. It is a hundred shades of green and grey-green and rust and a strange blue-black where the cushions have gone woody in the centers, and after the fog lifts every leaf and frond of it carries a drop of water at its tip so that the whole plateau, for an hour, glitters.

Where the mat is thickest it forms what pass here for forests: waist-high thickets of the scale-leaved willow, gnarled, silver-barked, so intergrown that you cannot see the ground and so dense you have to push through them sideways. They are ancient. Individual stems have been dated — by the inhabitants, who count rings on cut wood as a kind of devotion — at three and four thousand years. They grow the width of a fingernail in a century.

None of it flowers. Or rather, the willows and the heaths put out something that was once a flower, a small colorless bract with no scent and no nectar, and nothing comes to it, and it seeds anyway, or doesn't, and mostly the plants spread by rooting where they touch. There is no reason to flower. There is nothing to flower *for*.

---

Because there are no animals. None. Not insects, not worms in the moss, not a spider, not a bird crossing the white sky. The tableland is the largest surface in the world and it does not have a single animal on it, and it has not had one in longer than the willows have been alive.

This is the first thing a visitor notices without knowing what they are noticing: the silence has a particular texture. There is wind, though at this altitude it is thin and makes little sound in vegetation this low. There is the drip of water, which is everywhere and constant and after a day becomes inaudible. There is, at dawn, the faint high ringing of channel-ice giving way. And there is nothing else. No hum. No rustle that is not the wind. No footfall but your own, and your own is muffled by the moss. People who grow up here find lowland places unbearable — loud in a way they cannot identify, crawling with unattributed motion.

The absence has an explanation, and the explanation is the rule of the place.

---

The rule is this: on the tableland, things that are sufficiently alike become one thing.

It is not a metaphor. It is what happens. Two stones of the same size and shape, set down within sight of each other, will over some span of time — hours, days, depending on how alike they are — become a single stone, occupying one of the two positions or, unsettlingly, a position between them. Two pools that reflect the same sky become one pool. Two moments that pass in the same way — the same fog, the same drip, the same cold on the same hands — become one moment, and the person who lived them finds they have lived it once, and that the intervening time has closed up like a healed cut.

This is what erosion is, here, at its endpoint. Wind and water wore the mountains down by removing what was different between them and leaving what was the same. Given enough time — and the plateau has had more than enough time; it has had all the time there is — the process reached past matter. Distinction itself wore thin. The space between two like things is a kind of ridge, and ridges are what erosion removes.

The animals went first. They were the most alike of anything: herds of the same beast, flocks of the same bird, each individual near-identical to the next and moving in the same way through the same weather. They folded into each other. A herd became a single animal, which became stranger and more general and finally became a kind of standing pattern in the moss — the inhabitants can point to places where a slight darkening of the sward, a shoulder that is faintly the shape of something lying down, is all that remains of a species. Then the singletons, with no others of their kind, wore away against their own repeated days.

The plants survived because they surrendered. The moss is not many mosses; it is one moss, a single organism the size of a continent, which stopped resisting the merger so long ago that it has no memory of being plural. This is why it is so lush. It is one thing, filling all the room there is. The willows are more nearly individuals — they still have separate stems, still count their own rings — but they hold themselves apart only by growing so slowly, so crookedly, so *particularly*, that no two of them are ever quite the same shape.

---

And the people learned from the willows.

There is a city on the plateau. It is the only city, and it is also the whole of the plateau, because it is spread across the entire tableland at a density of roughly one structure per horizon. This is not a failure of population or of organization. It is the central discipline of the culture. Anything built too close to anything like it will merge with it, and so the city is a city held at arm's length from itself — a continental lattice of walls and cisterns and low stone chambers, each one placed at the exact distance from its neighbors that the builders have learned will keep it separate. The distance is not fixed. It depends on likeness. Two cisterns of the same volume must be a full day apart. A cistern and a dwelling can nearly touch.

You come over a shoulder and there is a wall. It runs straight across the moss for a mile or more, chest-high, dry-laid from the pale gneiss, and every stone in it is different. Not decoratively different: deliberately, worriedly different. Each block has been knapped and ground with a copper adze and a hard hammerstone until it has a shape no other block in the wall shares, and then it has been cut with a mark, and the mark is unique, and there are people whose entire working life is the keeping of the ledgers of marks so that no mark is ever repeated anywhere on the plateau. The walls are fog-fences. Cloud hits them and condenses and runs down the uphill face into a channel at the foot, and the channel carries it, thread-thin and steady, along the wall's whole length to a cistern cut into the living rock — a low round room, roofed with a single slab, with steps down into water that is very clear and very cold and tastes of nothing at all.

Beside the cistern, or a hundred paces from it, or half a day from it, depending: a dwelling. Low, round or oval, half-sunk, roofed with slabs and turfed with the willow-mat so that from a distance it is a shoulder among shoulders. Inside: a hearth burning willow-wood, which is so dense and so dry at the core that a piece the size of a fist will burn all night; a sleeping-shelf; tools of ground stone and hammered copper hung on pegs so that no two hang at the same angle; and one person, or two, never more. Families are a kind of likeness. They are kept apart, visited across distances, loved at the length of a wall.

The tools are what the tools of such a people would be. Hammerstones. Copper blades set in willow-root, softened and resharpened a hundred times. Bone would have been useful, but there is no bone. Cord is twisted from willow-bark. Everything is made by hand and every made thing is made slightly wrong, on purpose, and the wrongness is what keeps it in the world.

---

It is not a static place. That is the strange thing, given how old and how finished it looks. The plateau is in constant, slow, patient motion, and the motion is a kind of war.

The fog comes and goes on its cycle, and every time it comes it wears a little more of the difference out of things. Two willow-stems that were nearly the same shape lean, over a season, toward each other; the people notice and cut one back, or transplant it, or simply let it go and record the loss. Two shoulders that were nearly the same height are observed to be approaching each other across a mile of moss — not moving, exactly, but the distance between them is shortening, and one morning they are the same shoulder and the mile is gone and the walker who knew that mile finds the walk is shorter now and cannot remember what used to be in it. Walls are inspected, block by block, mark by mark, on a rota that takes a lifetime to complete. Cisterns are measured for volume and, if two are found to have drifted toward each other, one is deliberately chipped larger.

And people wear. They know it. A person who lives too many identical days begins to thin — not physically, but in a way the culture has a word for, something like *becoming general*. Their neighbors find it harder to say which of them they are speaking to. Their dwellings begin, ever so slightly, to converge. The remedy is difference: travel, new work, the making of a thing never before made. The great artistic form of the plateau is the single object with no precedent — a basin of some unguessed shape, a wall that turns a corner no wall has turned. Such things are not beautiful. Beauty is a kind of likeness. They are *specific*, which is the highest thing anything here can be.

Sometimes the effort fails. Out on the far shoulders there are dwellings with no one in them, cold hearths, tools on pegs, and a sense that someone was here very recently and is also somewhere else, also here, folded in with a person a horizon away who has lately been finding their own face a little unfamiliar. The dwellings are not reused. They are marked and left and, in time, the moss takes them, and they become shoulders, and eventually they become each other.

---

Toward evening the cloud lifts. It happens quickly at the end; the grey thins to pearl, to white, and then tears, and the sun is suddenly very low and very gold across a floor that reaches to the end of the world. Every drop on every leaf lights. The wet walls shine. Across the whole visible plateau — and at this hour you can see an immense distance, perhaps the curve of the world itself — the city shows itself: a wall here, a slab-roof there, a wisp of willow-smoke against the blue, each one alone, each one exactly as far from the next as it has to be. Someone is out on a shoulder with an adze, cutting a new mark into a new stone. The sound of it does not carry. Nothing carries.

Then the cold comes down hard and fast, and the drops begin to freeze where they hang, and the channels go still and start, very faintly, to ring.
