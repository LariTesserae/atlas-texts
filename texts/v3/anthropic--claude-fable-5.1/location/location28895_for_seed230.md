Start at the bottom, where the path leaves the scree.

The scree is grey and sharp and dry on top, and if you turn a stone over its underside is dark with wet. That is the first thing to learn about this place: the moisture is never where you look for it. It is under things, behind things, in the shadow side. The path climbs through the loose rock in a line of flat stones that someone set, edge to edge, a long time ago, and the stones are so exactly bedded that nothing has moved. You can feel the intention in them through your feet.

Then the walls begin.

They begin as a single retaining wall, dry-laid, shoulder-high, holding a terrace of pale gravel behind it. Nothing grows on the terrace. Then another wall above that one, set back a body's width, and another above that, and after the fourth or fifth you realize the whole shelf of the mountain has been built. Not built *on* — built. The rock face has been taken apart and relaid as a settlement, course by course, and the joints between stones are tighter than anything metal tools would bother with. These were fitted by hand and eye, by people who had time and nothing else, and who worked the stone by rubbing it against other stone until it sat.

---

The town — it is a town, though you could cross it in the time it takes to eat an apple — occupies a saddle between two shoulders of the ridge. On either side the ground drops away into cloud. The cloud is the water. It comes up the western slope in the afternoon and touches the walls, and the walls, being cool, take the water out of the air and hold it as a skin. In the evening the skin runs. It runs down the faces of the stones into the channels that were cut for it, narrow grooves along the base of each wall no wider than two fingers, and the grooves carry it down through the town in a slow braided system to the cisterns below the lowest terrace. The cisterns are always full to the same line. You can see the line. It is a band of darker stone, a hand's width, that has never been higher and never lower.

That is the second thing to learn. Nothing here is *becoming*. The fog arrives at the same hour, thins at the same hour. The stones on the north faces of the houses are always dark and slick, patched with a black lichen that grows so slowly it is indistinguishable from paint; the stones on the south faces are always dry and blond and warm to the touch in the brief afternoon before the cloud comes. There is no stain that is spreading, no crack that is widening. Whatever this place was going to become, it has become it.

---

The streets are stairs. Every street is a stair, and every stair is also a roof — the upper surface of the house below — and also a drain. A stair-street a body and a half wide runs up the middle of the saddle in shallow flights, turning at each terrace, and off it branch narrower stairs that go under lintels into courtyards you cannot see into from the main way. The houses are stacked three and four deep against the slope, roofed with flat slabs laid overlapping like scales, and the slabs are held down by nothing but their own weight and the care with which they were placed. The doorways are low. You duck. Inside, the rooms are cool and grey and smell of wet stone and of something faintly sweet, which is the moss that grows in the corners where the condensation collects. The moss is the greenest thing in the town. It is the size of a coin, and there is one in each corner of each room, and it has been that size for longer than anyone has been counting.

The tools are still here. Not many. A stone bowl with a stone pestle worn to fit it. A wooden beam, black with age, with wedge-cuts in it. A pile of flat river-pebbles — river-pebbles, brought up from some valley two days below — sorted by size on a windowsill. Nothing rusts because there is nothing to rust. The few pieces of metal you find are soft copper, a hook, a pin, green and unbothered.

---

Sound: the drip. Always somewhere, never quite where you are. A single drop landing in a channel behind a wall, and then, a long time later, another. The wind does not blow through the town — the walls are too tight for that — but it moves over the top of it, and where it passes the mouth of a stair you hear a low breath, a note held on one pitch, and then it stops. In the whole town there is nothing loose to rattle.

Once, on the highest terrace, a chough. Black, red-legged, standing on the parapet in the way birds stand on parapets everywhere, looking at nothing. It did not fly off when you came up. It walked a few paces along the wall, unhurried, then dropped over the edge into the cloud, and you heard it call once from below, and that was all the animal life for the day. Later you will notice a lizard in a wall-joint, flattened into the dark line of the mortarless seam, and you will not be sure whether it moved or whether you moved.

---

Vegetation, such as it is: the lichen, black and rust-orange and a pale grey-green that is almost the color of the stone. The corner-mosses. In the cracks of the highest, driest terrace, a cushion plant the size of a fist, hard as a stone itself, flowering — or having flowered, or about to; it is hard to tell — in tiny white points. And three junipers, or one juniper that has grown as three, on the eastern shoulder above the last wall, twisted over to lie along the ground away from the weather, with bark hanging in strips. It is the only thing in sight that looks like it has ever been alive in the ordinary way, and it looks like it stopped a long time ago and is simply continuing.

---

Whether anyone lives here you cannot say, and after a while you stop wanting to know.

A door is propped open with a wedge of slate. A cistern lid is off, and the water under it is perfectly still, perfectly clear, showing the pale stone floor a long way down. On the main stair, at the third turning, someone has set a flat stone across a channel so the trickle pools before it goes on, and in the pool a single leaf — juniper, a dry brown scale — turns very slowly in place and does not travel. These could be the marks of a hand that left this morning. They could be the marks of a hand that left when the copper was still bright. The place does not distinguish. It has been finished for so long that maintenance and abandonment look the same on it. The walls hold themselves. The water runs where it was told to run. If a family of three, or thirty, or none, moves through these stairs each evening in cloth soft with damp, quiet because there is nothing to say that has not been said in this exact spot before, you would not see the difference from the outside.

---

Go up to the top terrace and sit with your back to the last wall, the one that faces the cloud.

The stone is cool through your shirt and slightly wet. The light is grey and even and comes from everywhere; nothing casts a hard shadow, but nothing is dim either. It is the light of a room with high windows. Below you, the roofs step down in their scales, and the channels run in their thin dark lines, and here and there the pale slabs are darker in a patch where the fog has already begun to settle for the afternoon.

You could stay a long time. You would learn where each drip falls and how long between them. You would learn which stair breathes in a west wind and which in an east. You would learn that the chough comes at the same time and stands in the same place, that the leaf in the pool never leaves the pool, that the line in the cistern is the line in the cistern.

And the town would not learn anything about you. It has no room left for it. It is a made thing, made once, all the way through, by hands that are gone — and it has arrived at the state that made things reach when they are left alone in cool damp air at altitude, which is not ruin, and not use, but the third thing that has no name: simply *being there*, stone on stone, water running down.
