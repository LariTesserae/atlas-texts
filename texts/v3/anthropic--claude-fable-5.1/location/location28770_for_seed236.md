**The Caldera at Shell Height**

Begin with the rock, because the rock is what came first and everything here still remembers it.

It is a shallow bowl in the top of a mountain, perhaps three hundred paces across, with a rim that rises unevenly on all sides so that the sky is a ragged oval and the wind, when there is wind, passes overhead without ever quite coming down. The floor and walls are new stone. Not new the way a road is new — new the way a scab is. Basalt that folded over itself while still moving and then stopped mid-fold, ropes and coils and pressure-ridges, some of the surfaces glassed to a black shine so fresh they have not yet dulled, so sharp that a hand laid on them comes away cut without feeling it. Nothing has had time to round anything. There is no soil in the usual sense. There is ash in the low places, fine as flour, and there are crumbles of pumice in the cracks, and that is what passes for ground.

The stone is warm. Not hot — you could sit on it, lie on it — but warm the way a body is warm, from within, and this warmth has a texture: it is slightly stronger toward the center of the bowl and slightly weaker at the rim, and it has been slowly, slowly fading for longer than anything living here can remember. You feel the gradient more than you measure it. Cross the floor and the soles of your feet know which way is inward.

There is no water. This is the first rule and the one that shapes all the others. No stream, no seep, no pool, no dew. The air is so dry that the inside of your nose stings within an hour and your lips split by evening. Rain does not fall here — the mountain is too high and the clouds pass below it, a grey floor you can look down onto from the rim, and what moisture they carry never rises this far. Whatever lives in the bowl has learned to hold, to seal, to never let go.

---

And yet the bowl is green. That is the second thing you notice, after the sharpness, and it takes a moment to accept, because it should not be.

It is green the way a rainforest is green: layered, crowded, competing for light. But approach any one plant and it is wrong in some particular way. The leaves are thick, almost hard, with a surface like candle wax that does not take a fingerprint. Break one and it does not weep; it snaps, and the interior is a pale solid, oily rather than wet, the consistency of cold butter. These are plants that have given up on sap. They run on resin. Whatever fluid they use to move sugars and hold their shape is something heavier and more patient than water, something that does not evaporate in this air, and they hoard it inside cuticles so thick the leaves ring faintly when they touch.

They grow straight out of the young rock. Their roots do not seek water downward; they spread laterally across the glass surfaces and grip, and where two roots cross they fuse. They seem to be feeding on the basalt itself, or on the mineral dust that settles into every crevice, or on the warmth — there is a theory, if anyone here were the sort to have theories, that the plants are drawing their metabolic heat from the stone the way the plants of lower places draw it from the sun, and using sunlight only for the small work of making pigment.

They come in tiers. Lowest, a mat of something like moss but crisp, that crunches softly underfoot and springs back. Above that, rosettes the size of a spread hand, leaves in dark spirals with pale edges, spaced almost too regularly across the ash. Above those, shrubs with trunks like braided cable and crowns of narrow blades that turn edge-on to the noon light. And in the center of the bowl, where the warmth is strongest, five or six things that could be called trees — trunks of a reddish material that is not bark, more like layered horn, and branches that do not so much spread as lean, all in the same direction, toward the wall of the bowl where the cliff faces the afternoon.

The whole assembly is dense enough that from the rim you cannot see the floor. From the floor you cannot see more than a few paces in any direction. Leaves touch leaves. Roots run over roots. The smell is thick and sweet and resinous, like a cedar chest left in the sun, with a mineral sharpness underneath — the smell of hot stone.

---

It is only when you have been walking among the plants for a while that you understand the walls of the bowl are not entirely rock.

The inner cliff faces — the sides of the caldera, rising twenty or thirty times your height — are covered in construction. Not everywhere; the construction follows the warmth, and it is thickest on the faces that hold the afternoon light and thinnest on the cold north curve. But where it exists it is total. Tier upon tier of cells and chambers and galleries, built out from the rock in a material that is translucent amber at its newest and deepening to opaque brown-black where it is old, so that the wall is a kind of stratigraphy readable at a glance: pale at the top and edges, dark at the base and center.

The material is wax. Or something that is to wax what the plants' resin is to sap — a heavier, harder, more permanent cousin. It has been laid down in layers a hair's thickness at a time, and the layers show, like the grain in a shell. Chambers open onto ledges; ledges connect to ramps; ramps spiral up to higher tiers. There are hollows the size of a fist and hollows the size of a room, and between them run galleries just wide enough for something the size of a cat to pass. Some of the openings have lips built out around them, curved like the mouths of pitchers, and these face upward and outward toward the center of the bowl and seem to be catching the warm air as it rises off the floor and funneling it inward. Others, higher, face down and away. You can hold a hand near one of the upper vents and feel a very slow breath moving out, barely warmer than your skin.

There are no tools here. Nothing has been cut, hammered, fitted, or tied. Everything has been secreted, shaped by a body pressing against it while it was still soft, and left to cure. The regularity is the regularity of animals, not geometry — the chambers are all roughly alike because the things that make them are roughly alike, and the ramps all curve at the same angle because that is the angle a certain kind of body finds easiest to climb. And yet it is a city. It has districts. The lower tiers, closest to the warmth, are the nurseries, and their walls are the thickest and their openings the smallest. The middle tiers are dense with storage cells, many sealed, and if you break one open (you should not) you find it packed with a substance that is half pollen and half resin, a dry sweet paste the color of ochre. The upper tiers are open galleries, and this is where the builders spend the daylight hours, and this is where you finally see them.

---

They are everywhere, once you know to look. The density is startling — this is not a place where you come across an animal now and then; it is a place where you are never not within arm's length of several. They are on the ramps, in the openings, on the leaves of the trees, on the ground among the rosettes. There are perhaps thousands in the bowl and it does not feel crowded, only *full*, the way a tide pool is full.

The builders are the size of a small dog and shaped like something between a beetle and a tortoise: a domed, segmented back in the same amber-to-brown range as their architecture, a low flat head with no visible eyes, six thick legs that end in pads rather than claws. They move slowly and with great deliberateness, and they are utterly silent. They do not call. They do not click or hum or scrape. Their feet make no sound on the wax. When two of them meet on a ramp they stop, and their heads touch, and they stand that way for a while — a minute, sometimes longer — and then one of them backs into a side-opening to let the other pass. Whatever passes between them passes through contact, through the faint vibration of the substrate, through chemistry. They are, you come to believe, entirely deaf, and the city they have made has no acoustics because it has never needed any.

They feed on the plants, or rather on the resin. You see one clamber up a trunk to where a branch has been scored — many branches bear these scars, clean shallow grooves in the horn — and press its mouthparts to the wound and stay there. Nothing appears to happen. Ten minutes later it is still there. An hour later it has moved a hand's breadth. The plants do not seem to be harmed; the scoring is old and healed over in most places, and the trees lean toward the built wall as if they had grown that way on purpose, and perhaps they have.

And the builders are not alone. Threaded through and around them are the rest of the bowl's population, all of it quiet, all of it slow, all of it adapted to the same dry, warm, patient conditions. There are things like stick-insects as long as a forearm that stand among the shrub-blades so still and so perfectly matched in color that you find them by their shadows. There are small round creatures with no obvious front or back that roll rather than walk, from one rosette to the next, and sit in the spiral centers doing something you cannot see. There is a kind of flattened, many-legged thing that lives in the galleries alongside the builders and cleans the walls, apparently welcome. There are moths — or things that fill the niche of moths — with wings like flakes of mica, which do not flutter but glide from one high ledge to another in the still air, and land without a sound, and fold up, and vanish against the amber. In the ash of the floor, if you look closely, the surface is stitched with hundreds of tiny burrow-mouths, each ringed with a collar of hardened resin, and things live in them that you never see, only the collars.

Nothing here hunts audibly. Nothing here calls to a mate. If there is predation — and there must be, with this many bodies — it happens the way everything happens: slowly, by touch, in silence. You get the sense, after a while, that the silence is not an absence but a medium, that the whole bowl is speaking constantly in a register you have no organ for, and that your own footsteps and breathing are a kind of blaring that the creatures here are politely ignoring.

---

Light comes into the bowl the way it comes into a courtyard. The rim cuts off the low sun in both directions, so there is no dawn or dusk to speak of, only a long morning shadow that retreats down the west wall and a long afternoon shadow that climbs the east. At noon the light is white and vertical and the black glass of the floor throws it back up, and the undersides of the leaves are lit as brightly as the tops, and the wax walls glow from within like something held up to a lamp. It is never dim while the sun is above the rim, and it is never harsh, because the leaf-canopy is dense enough to break it up. The dominant color is a warm green-gold with the black stone showing through underneath and the amber wall above, and in this light the builders on the walls are almost invisible, the same tone as their city.

At night the warmth of the stone is the only heat, and the bowl holds it; you can feel the air settle and pool. The moths come out in numbers then, and glide. That is the closest the place comes to activity.

---

The third rule, after the dryness and the silence, is the slowness.

The bowl is young. The rock is barely cooled. And yet the city on its walls is old — older, by the count of layers, than the plants at its feet, some of which have trunks that must have taken a very long time to reach their thickness. This is a contradiction that resolves only one way: the builders did not come here after the rock cooled. They were here, or nearby, and they moved onto it while it was still too warm to touch, and they have been moving ever since.

Look at the base of the built wall, where the wax is darkest. It is being abandoned. The lowest chambers are empty, their openings sealed from inside with a final layer, and the sealing is old. Above them, the nursery tier is active. Above that, the storage, and above that the galleries, and above *that*, at the very top of the construction where the wax is pale and translucent and still faintly soft, new cells are being laid down. The whole city is climbing the wall. It is following the warmth as the warmth retreats — or rather, as the stone cools from the center outward and the zone of ideal temperature moves toward the rim, the city moves with it, one layer at a time, a finger-width in a season, an arm's length in a lifetime. The plants follow too: the oldest, largest trees are at the center where it was warmest longest, and they are leaning toward the wall because the wall is where the future is, and beyond them, closer to the rim, are the younger shrubs and the newer rosettes and, at the very edge where the moss-mat is only beginning to crisp across the glass, the frontier.

Nothing here happens at a speed you could watch. But everything here is happening. If you could stand still long enough — a year, ten years — you would see the whole bowl turn itself slowly inside out, the green and the amber creeping up the walls, the black floor emptying, until one day the center is bare stone again and the city is a ring around the rim, and the builders, feeling with their padded feet for a warmth that is no longer there, begin to press their heads together and confer.

Where they go then, no one who has been here knows. Perhaps there is another mountain. Perhaps there is another vent, on the far side of this one, and the rock there is still soft, and a scout with no eyes and no voice is already standing on it, waiting for it to cool enough to touch.
