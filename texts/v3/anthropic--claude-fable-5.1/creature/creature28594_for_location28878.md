Begin with what you can see, because for a long while that is all there is.

The frogs live at the edges of the pools and nowhere else. They are the pale of things that have not needed color — the pale of a fingernail, of a root — and they are so small that you find them by their stillness rather than their movement. They sit in the shallowest water with their eyes just above the surface and they watch the mirrored towers and they do not call. You wait through a whole dusk for one of them to call. Frogs call; it is what they are for. These do not, and after a time you understand that a call is a claim, a way of saying *here, this is mine*, and nothing in this basin that has any sense makes claims. The frogs have sense. They eat the few insects that come down to the water, and they lay their eggs in strings along the fern stems that trail into the pools, and the eggs hatch, and the young are paler still, and none of them, ever, go nearer than a body's length to the raw collar of a footing. That distance is exact. You test it by walking the pool edge toward the nearest tower and watching them sink and vanish, one after another, at precisely the same line.

The heron is different because there is only one of it and because it chose to be here. It could have gone anywhere; herons can. It stands in the still water below the tallest of the near towers with its reflection under it and the tower's reflection behind it and it is the only thing in the basin with any grey in it that is not the towers' grey — a warmer, feathered grey, a grey with a history. It does not fish much. It stands. It seems to have made a judgment about the place, and the judgment was *acceptable*, and having made it the heron feels no need to revise. Once, near what would be evening if there were evenings, you see it lift and cross the basin low, and the line it flies is not straight to the next pool but bent, a long slow curve that keeps it always the same distance from every tower it passes. It is not afraid. It is polite.

The insects you barely see. They are the ordinary kind — midges, a few slow beetles, something that clicks in the horsetail — but they fly wide around the towers and wide around the streets too, so that the air over the seams is empty in a way you can almost see, a corridor of nothing. They are the least sensible of the tenants and yet they know this without being told.

These are what live here in the way that swallows live in a barn. They are not what the barn is for.

---

The thing on the street you see four times before you let yourself describe it.

Long. Low — lower than your knee, though it is hard to judge across a mile of ferns. Roughly the length of a fallen person. It is the exact colour of the towers, that dark that does not want to be looked at, and it moves along the seam of the street the way water moves along the channel the street is becoming: not fast, not slow, without any hesitation at the junctions, always down the dead centre of the compacted ground. It never touches the ferns. It never stops. It is never, in any of the four sightings, close to a tower. It is always between.

The fifth time, you are near enough to see that it does not walk so much as go forward — there are no legs you can make out, no articulation, just the whole of it advancing, smooth and faceted like a shard of one of the towers laid on its side and given an errand. The midges swing wide around it. The ferns on either side of the seam, as it passes, do not lean toward it and do not lean away; they simply stop leaning over the street at all, and the next morning, when you walk that stretch, the green is a hand's width further back from the edge on both sides than it was.

So this is the one that keeps the streets. That is your first thought, and it is not wrong. The city has no one to walk its roads and something must, or the ferns would have them in a season. It patrols. It tends.

But it is also going somewhere. They are all going somewhere — you decide there are several, though you never see two at once — and where they are going is always the same direction, bent a few degrees toward the west, toward the thicker vapor and the closer towers. They are not circling. They are on their way.

You follow one, at length, which is the only way anything is done here. It takes most of a day, because the street stretches ahead of you as you walk it and shortens behind, and the low thing does not hurry and does not need to. Near the end of the light it does something you have not seen one do: it stops. It stops at a place on the seam that is nothing — no junction, no tower within half a mile, just black ground and ferns and drip — and it settles. That is the word. It lowers itself into the soft new soil the way a thing lowers itself into water, without disturbance, until there is only a dark ridge along the centre of the street, and then not even that.

You sleep near the place. In the morning there is a collar of heaved earth where it went down, a hand high, cracking at the rim. Nothing shows above it. The street runs straight through it and out the other side. Below your feet, once, so faint you feel it in the soles of your boots rather than hear it, something like a stone being set.

---

So the walkers are not the keepers of the city. Or they are, but only in passing. They are the towers before they are towers.

Once you know this the rest of it comes up out of the ground in order, the way the city did.

They were under. That is the first thing. The soil tells you: it has not rotted because it has not had time, it smells of split stone because until recently it was under stone, or under water so deep and so still it may as well have been. The basin was a floor of something for longer than the ferns have a word for, and whatever lay beneath that floor lay in the dark. Whatever it was did without light for so long that it stopped being the sort of thing that needs light and became the sort of thing that *wants* it — which is different, and hungrier, and more patient.

Then the floor came off. You do not know how. The estuary drained, or the sediment lifted, or the world simply changed its mind about what was on top; it does not matter. What matters is that the ground was soft and new and open to the sky, and the ones beneath it came up through it, the way a post comes up when driven from below, and around each one the earth heaved into a collar, and each one, having found its place, began to take the day.

That is what the towers are doing. They are not standing there. They are eating. Light arrives at their faces and does not leave, and the basin goes dim, and the tower grows a fraction warmer than the air — the warmth of holding, the way a hand is warm around a stone it will not let go of. They sweat because it is work. Then, once an hour, from inside, from below, a stone is set: an hour of daylight compacted and placed, and the tower is fractionally taller, fractionally more itself, faceted at a slightly different angle because this hour's light was a slightly different shape from the last. The proportions that looked extruded, the crystal grown in a solution that kept changing its mind — the solution is the sky. The towers are stored days, laid one on the next, by something that has no more use for windows than a stone does because there is nothing outside it wants to see. It has seen the outside. The outside is the dark it came from, and the dim it is making.

At night it lets a little back. Not from any opening. From all of itself, a pewter glow no brighter than a moonless sky. You take this, at first, for leakage. Then for a kind of breath. Then — watching the ferns, which come forward into the streets each day and are found each morning a little further back, the walkers having passed — you think it might be closer to speech, the only speech it has: a low even statement to the others across the basin that it is here, it is holding, it is still coming up. The frogs, who do not call, sit in the shallows and watch the towers call to each other all night in a frequency of light, and say nothing, and are right to.

---

They keep apart because each needs its own sky.

A quarter mile of vapor lid, half a mile, more: that is a plot, that is a field of daylight, that is the acreage a single one of them requires to feed. The orchard spacing that made it hard to call this a city from the rim is not the spacing of a city that has failed to fill in. It is the spacing of a city that has done the arithmetic. Two towers too close would starve each other and both would stop rising and the collars would cool, and they know this in whatever way they know things — down in the part of themselves that is still under the ground, still coming up.

And yet they bend west. All of them, by a few degrees, over miles. The streets bend, the towers cluster, the lid thickens, the dusk deepens, and every walker you have seen is on its way there, toward the part of the basin where the fields are smallest and the light is least and the towers stand nearest to touching. It is the opposite of what the arithmetic says. Unless the arithmetic was never about feeding well. Unless it was about feeding *enough* — enough to build with — and what they are building, collectively, out of all the daylight they can hold, is a place with as little daylight in it as possible. The west is where the city is most finished, and the west is the darkest place under the sky. They came up out of the dark and they are making it again, above ground this time, out of the day, stone by stone, so slowly that the heron will die of age and its great-grandchildren will stand in the same pool before the difference is visible from the rim.

That is not quite it either. That is you, wanting it to be a plan. Watch longer and it looks less like a plan than a lean — the lean of a plant toward what it needs, of water toward the low ground. They go west because the west is where the others are, and being near the others, dim and warm and dripping, is simply what they are for, once they have eaten enough sky to afford it. The city is not a hoard and not a fortress. It is a gathering, conducted at the speed of condensation, by things that spent so long alone under stone that they will need centuries to remember how to stand closer together than half a mile.

---

Which leaves you.

The ferns are always your height. You noticed that early and set it aside. The tower ahead recedes as you walk and the one behind draws near when you turn. Your shadow, in what light there is, does not fall away from the sky. It falls toward the nearest structure, and lies along the ground pointing at it like something that has been asked a question and is considering its answer.

The streets were not made for you. They were made by the walkers, for the walkers, for the one journey each of them makes from wherever it surfaced to wherever it will settle — and yet you are on them, and you are the only thing in the basin besides the walkers that is. The ferns keep the streets clear for whatever uses them. The frogs keep their body's length from the footings, and from you they keep a little less. The heron flew its polite curve around you too.

The basin does not know what you are. It is not hostile; nothing here is. It simply accommodates, with the patience of something that lay under a floor for an age and came up into a warm dim world and found it good. The ground fits itself to whoever walks its seams. The ferns rise to meet whoever is among them. And the light — which arrives here and stays, which the towers are made of, which your own shadow leans toward — the light has begun, in the smallest way, to treat you as one more place it might be put.

You will leave before it matters. You will climb back up the last low rise into the old country where the daylight reaches the ground and shadows fall away from the sun and frogs call. But you will have felt, in the soles of your boots, that faint sound of a stone being set beneath a street where nothing stood, and you will know what lives in the basin: not the frogs, not the heron, not the things that walk. What lives there is what is under it, still rising, holding the day inside itself in the dark, and waiting — not for you, not for anything — simply waiting, because waiting is what it learned to do, and it has only just begun to have a sky to do it under.
