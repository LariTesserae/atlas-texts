Begin with the birds, since they are what you see first, though they are not the first inhabitants and not the most numerous, only the loudest.

They are small, smaller than your fist, dark above and slightly paler below, with wings too long for their bodies and feet too small for anything but clinging. They cannot walk. They cannot perch on a branch, if there were branches. They can do two things: fly, and hold on to a vertical surface, and their whole life is the alternation of these two states. A bird here is born in the dark of a cup glued to a wall, in a chamber the exact size of its own body, and it does not see the sky until the day it drops from the rim of the cup and either flies or falls. Most fly. The ones that fall are on the floor within seconds, and within an hour they are gone into the mats.

They find their way in the gloom by sound. Each bird makes a small dry click as it approaches the wall, a click almost lost in the roar, and it listens for the echo off its own cup — its cup, one among a million, in a stratum of a colony on a face of a wall — and it comes home to it at speed, without slowing, and stops. You will watch this ten thousand times and never see one miss. They know the wall the way you know the inside of your own mouth. They know nothing else about the basin. The floor is a place things fall to. The far wall is a place other birds live. The country outside, where they go at dusk to drink from whatever puddles and seeps exist on the plateau and to hunt over the scrub, is a place that exists in order to be brought back here in the body and shed.

They are the rain. Understand this and the rest follows. Every drop of water on the floor, every atom of nitrogen in the forest, arrived in a bird or a bat or an insect that ate what a bird or a bat left. The birds are a weather system with feathers. They live perhaps five or six years, and they die mostly in the air or in the cup, and either way they come down, and the floor receives them and the beetles have them to clean bone in a night. A generation of birds is a coat of dust on the plain. There have been more generations than there are layers you could count.

---

The bats hold the other half of the day.

They are not one kind. In the deep wedge where the roof comes nearly to the floor, the roosts are tiered by species as strictly as the birds' nests are tiered by age: tiny ones high up in the pockets of the ceiling, pressed together so densely that the rock seems furred; heavier ones lower down, hanging in loose curtains that shift and resettle like something breathing; and at the bottom, near the old cones, a few big-eared, slow-moving ones that hunt not in the air but on the floor itself, dropping onto the mats to take crickets and climbing back up the guano ridges with their wings folded like elbows. Their pups hang in crèches, hundreds together, naked and pink in the black. When the mothers return they find their own by voice and by smell out of a wall of identical hunger, and they never get it wrong either.

The birds and the bats do not meet. At dusk the birds leave and the bats wake, and for a quarter of an hour the air of the basin belongs to no one; at dawn it reverses. This handover has been happening for as long as there has been a roof to hang from. It is the only appointment the basin keeps. If there is a rhythm here that resembles a day, it is not light and dark — the light barely changes — but the change of tenants in the air.

---

Now the floor, where the true population is.

The beetles you will notice first because they are the size of things you are used to noticing: a brown shiny kind that swarms the corpses, a black flat kind that lives in the crevices of the fallen columns, a pale slow kind with a domed back that does nothing but eat mat and is eaten. The cockroaches are half-transparent; you can see the dark line of what they have eaten running the length of them. The crickets are all legs and antennae, the antennae longer than the body, waving in the dim like the feelers of something underwater, and they are the loudest single thing on the floor — a cricket here does not chirp, it rasps, and there are more of them than there are nests. The millipedes are the aristocracy, long and heavy and unhurried, armored in a dull grey that flakes when you touch it, proceeding through the forest with the confidence of things that nothing wants to eat.

But under all of this, in the mats themselves and in the powder beneath the mats and in the cake beneath the powder, are the mites. They are the majority. They are the majority by a margin that makes every other census pointless. Take a pinch of the grey powder and hold it to your face in the dim and it moves — not the powder, but a fraction of the powder, a fraction that resolves into hundreds of tiny bodies going about their lives at a scale where a cricket is a landscape and a fallen bird is a continent. There are mites that eat fungus and mites that eat the eggs of the ones that eat fungus and mites that live only on the undersides of a single species of beetle, riding it like fleas. The mats are not moss. The mats are cities of mites in a matrix of what they have eaten and what they have left. Above them the springtails, jumping, hairline things, and among them the pseudoscorpions, no bigger than a grain of rice, hunting with pincers held out in front like a man carrying a lantern.

The predators of the visible scale are few and old-looking. The centipedes come out of the compacted floor with a slow deliberateness, pale grey, banded, each segment moving a fraction after the one before, and they take whatever is in front of them, including each other. The lizards are the only vertebrates that live on the floor and not on the walls — small, pale, soft-skinned, their eyes a smear of dark under the scale where an eye would be. They do not need to see. They sit on the fungus caps and wait for the rasp of a cricket to come close, and then they are elsewhere and the rasp has stopped. They lick water from the fungal surfaces at dawn, when the updraft carries the least moisture and the caps are faintly damp. That is the only drinking done on the floor. Everything else takes its water inside its food.

---

The fungus is not exactly an inhabitant. It is closer to a landlord.

What you see — the caps and columns and curtains — is the fruit. The organism is under the plain, a mat of filament woven through the powder and the cake and down into the soft stone, and there is reason to think that much of the floor is a single body, or a very few bodies, that have been growing outward from the first fall of guano since the roof came down. The columns rise and topple and are eaten and the body beneath goes on. It is the oldest living thing here by an order of magnitude. It has never been anywhere else. It knows the basin as a question of chemistry: where the ammonia is richest, where the powder has compacted enough to hold a filament, where the drip from the open center wets the surface a few days a year. It sends up its fruit in those places and the insects come to the fruit and shed on it and it is fed.

The lichens are stranger. They crust the surfaces of the guano cones and the lower nest-strata and the flanks of the talus in the dimmest places, grey-green, blue-green, and they live on ammonia that would kill anything else, and they need so little light that a day here is a feast. They are not plants and not fungus and not quite either. They grow at a pace that would make the fungus look impatient. There are patches on the north wall the size of a room that are probably older than the wall's oldest nests, and they have not visibly changed in the time anyone has looked at them, and no one has looked for long.

---

The dead live here too, in their way.

The strata beneath your feet are not empty. The old nests fused into the cliff are riddled with bores where beetles have eaten through the hardened saliva to the feathers inside, and the bores are lined with the cast skins of the beetles' larvae, and in the cast skins there are mites. Dig a hand's depth into the floor and you reach a layer of tiny bones, a compacted felt of the ribs and skulls of birds, and it is not inert: things live in the felt, translucent, eyeless, small enough that the space between two ribs is a hall. The plain is an archive and the archive has clerks. Everything that fell here is still being read.

You will find, if you dig, that the layers are not all the same. There are seasons of feathers — molts, when the whole colony sheds at once and the floor turns white for a week. There are bands of a different grey where the birds were fewer, or the bats more, or some plague passed through the insects. There is a layer, deep, of a slightly different fungus, one that grows nowhere on the surface now. The basin has had different tenants. They are still in residence, an arm's length down, in the form they took when they stopped.

---

And there are the ones you do not see.

The many-legged thing with the shell that lives in the holes. You will glimpse it a dozen times and never once fully. It is the length of your hand, or the length of your forearm; it is grey, or it is the color of the floor, which is the same thing. Its holes are perfectly round and their rims are smooth and slightly raised, and they occur along the hard-packed paths at intervals that look, if you let them, like a spacing chosen. When you pass a hole there is sometimes a click from inside it that is not the click of the birds. Nothing else in the basin makes it.

The mound district on the south side. The rows of knee-high ridges, riddled with openings, the swept ground between. Something lives in them; you can hear it, a fine dry ticking under the general clatter, and once or twice you see small pale forms at the openings that withdraw before they resolve. Whether they built the mounds or inherited them, whether the sweeping is deliberate or is only the passage of many feet, whether the rows are rows because of some plan or because of some gradient in the floor that favors that spacing — you cannot tell. You are not equipped to tell. The distinction between a thing that is made and a thing that has happened is not one the basin recognizes.

Through the low passage to the east, in the second collapse, the inhabitants are the same inhabitants at a further stage of the same descent. The birds are browner, quieter, and their nests are fewer. The crickets are paler. The lizards have lost even the smear where the eye would be. Beyond that, where the sound suggests a third chamber, you can imagine the gradient continuing: a place where the birds do not go at all, where the only fall is what the bats bring, where the floor forest is a few white threads and the residents are all things that have given up color and sight and haste and live on what filters down through two passages from a world that is itself living on what filters down from the world above. A basin within a basin. Tenants of tenants. It does not end so much as thin.

---

No one lives here who knows it is a place.

The bird knows the cup and the smoke of midges under the roof and the puddles on the plateau at night. The bat knows its tier and the crèche and the air at the dark end. The cricket knows the mat beneath it and the mat to either side. The mite knows a grain. The fungus knows a gradient of ammonia in the dark. The thing in the hole knows the hole and the path outside it, and perhaps the click of the next hole down, and nothing about the walls. Each of them inhabits a layer, and the layers are stacked, and the stack is the basin, and the only creature that has ever stood on the floor and looked up at the walls and understood it as one place is the one that does not live here and will leave.

What lives here, then, if you must have an answer, is whatever can live on what falls. That is the whole criterion. The basin does not have a climate; it has a descent. Light falls, a little. Dust falls. Feathers, bodies, droppings, the roof itself in pieces, the walls in flakes. Everything that is a resident is a resident because it has found a level in the fall at which to stop and be fed by what has not yet stopped. The birds intercept it in the air. The insects on the mats. The mites in the powder. The fungus below the powder. The dead below the fungus, still being eaten. The whole population is a single long act of catching, and beneath the lowest catcher there is only the plain, rising a finger-width in your lifetime, made entirely of what nobody caught.

It sounds like water because it behaves like water. The roar is the sound of things falling and being received and going on. You would swear you were listening to a waterfall, and in every sense but the one you meant, you are.
