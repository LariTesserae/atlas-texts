Ask what lives here and you get the wrong answer first, which is nothing. That is the answer the eye gives from the rim. It's not a stupid answer. It's just measuring at the wrong scale, in both directions.

Start below, because that is where it starts.

Under the floor of the basin, under the cones and the pools and the thin grey leaves of dried outflow, under the clay the willow stands in and the clay the trestles were driven into and the clay that was somewhere else when they were driven, there is more clay, and it is wet, and it is dark, and it is full. Not full of anything you would call a creature. Full the way a lung is full, or a stomach: a population that is also a chemistry. They have no light and want none. They have no oxygen and would die of it. They eat what was buried before there were fields — leaf-fall and shell and the soft parts of things that sank into a sea that has since gone to grass — and they eat it very slowly, and what they breathe out is gas. Whether the gas that drives the cones is theirs, or comes from deeper and hotter places and only passes up through them like weather through a crowd, is a question for the company that laid the pipe, and the company that laid the pipe no longer exists in a form that could answer. Either way they are down there. They were down there before the basin was a basin. They will be down there when the cotton-grass is under two metres of what the cones are making now, because that is what they do: they take what falls into the dark and they convert it, molecule by molecule, at a few degrees above freezing, into an exhalation, and the exhalation is what you are watching. Every bubble that closes like a book is a breath from something that has been alive, without interruption, without a single individual, since before the country had a name. The cold is theirs. They don't know about the surface. They don't know about anything. That is a kind of living too, maybe the oldest kind — the kind that has no outside.

Come up a little.

The orange-grey crust in the dry corners, the one you didn't kneel to look at: that's the second tenant. It lives on the breath of the first. Where the gas comes out into air, where sulphide meets oxygen and iron comes out of solution, something colonises the seam. A mat. A film. Bacteria, mostly, in layers no thicker than paint, coloured by what they eat and what they leave. They are what the flies are for, and the flies are what the wagtail is for, and so the one bird walking the pipe with its judged, deliberate feet is standing at the top of a food chain three storeys tall whose foundation is a few grams of rust-coloured slime on a cone that hasn't moved in a season. The mat has no strategy except to return. It lives on the old places, and the old places are the ones about to change, so its whole existence is the interval between two eruptions. A cone goes quiet; within weeks the crust arrives; it spreads outward along the drying skirt; it thickens, orange to grey, grey to something almost black; and then the lid it has been colonising gives way underneath and a fan of cold slurry comes down over it and it is gone, buried, one more grey leaf in the section. And on the next cone over, or the same one when it quiets again, it is already back. It does not remember the flooding. It doesn't need to. It is not the kind of thing that survives events. It is the kind of thing that occurs whenever the conditions occur, which is a different way of being permanent.

The wagtail is not a resident. The wagtail is a reader. It comes down when the flies are low and it knows which pipe will hold and it knows, from the way a pool's skin is dimpling, when to be on the other side of the basin. It has learned the rules faster than you have because its life is shorter and its errors are final. When it lifts and resettles further along, a moment before the trestle groans, that is the only intelligence here that could be called attention in the usual sense, and it is passing through.

Now the pipes.

You would not ordinarily say a pipe lives somewhere. But look at the swallowed run — twenty metres under, a cone built up over it like a saddle, the cone's mouth still working the seam. The pipe was put there to take something away from the basin. It is now a thing the basin does. Slurry rises around it and falls back along it and dries on it and the next flow comes over the dried layer, and inside the steel the gas still moves, a little, because the cone happens to be venting straight into a crack the mud opened in the pipe wall years ago, so that the basin is now feeding its own extraction without anybody having arranged it. The pipe has a temperature, and it is the temperature of the mud. It has a motion, and it is the motion of the trestles settling. Cut it open and the inside would be lined with the same orange-grey as the dry corners: the mat has moved in there too, where the gas is richest. So the pipe is inhabited, and the pipe is also, in some sense you don't want to press too hard, an inhabitant. It keeps a rhythm. That is more than the sheep do.

And the valve. The valve clicks over on a schedule that someone set. The transformer up the slope hums because a meter somewhere is still turning and a bill is still going to an address and someone — an office, a department, a cost centre inside a company that has been bought three times and renamed twice — is still paying it, probably without knowing what it is. There is a line on a spreadsheet a long way from here, in a building with carpet, that says something like *Site 14 — telemetry & pressure mgmt*, and next to it a number, and the number is the basin. That is the basin's only remaining name outside the basin. The valve does not know this. The valve knows: pressure up, open; pressure down, close. It has been faithful to that for longer than most marriages. The gauge on the stanchion twitches every few seconds because it is watching the same thing the valve is watching, and reporting it to no one, and it is, as you noticed, the only face here, and it has the expression of a face that is counting.

So: the deep things, breathing. The crust, returning. The bird, reading. The machinery, keeping time. You could stop there and it would be a fair census. But there is the shed. There is the stool inside the shed, and the cup.

Someone comes.

Not often. Once a quarter, say — the interval at which the spreadsheet requires a signature. They drive the grass-crowned road in a vehicle with a logo that is not the logo on the pump's maker's plate, nor the one after that. They park on the rim where the sheep have been. They come down over the lip of grey clay, not up, and they stand where you are standing, and they look.

They did not choose this. It was on a list of sites, and the sites were divided among the people who do this kind of work, and this one was at the bottom of someone's list and then the bottom of theirs. Their predecessor is a name on a clipboard and a set of numbers written in a hand that got smaller over the years. The stool and the cup were here when they arrived. So was the corrugated shed, on its spit of firmer ground, and the door was already hanging. They have never sat on the stool. They think about it every time.

Their job is the gauge. Read it, write it, photograph the marker post for the file though the post now leans so far the number has to be read lying down and they no longer lie down. Note any change to infrastructure. They stopped noting the trestles years ago; there was a box on the form for *structural deterioration — action required Y/N* and they wrote Y for the first three visits and nothing happened and then they wrote N and nothing happened and then they understood that N was the truth, because the basin has never once stopped and the trestles have never once been anything but going, and there is no state to return them to. The company does not want the gas any more. The gas is a rounding error. What the company wants is not to be the party that turned off the transformer, because turning it off would be a decision, and a decision has a name attached, and the valve has done its job so long that nobody now employed can say for certain what would happen in the buried run if it stopped. So the bill is paid. So the valve clicks. So someone comes.

They know the rules. They learned them the way you learned them: by standing there. They know the pale mud is thin and the dark mud is thick and neither will hold them, and they know where the tussocks are, and they know which tussocks have gone under since last time, because that is the only calendar the basin keeps and they have started keeping it too, privately, in the back of the book, in a hand that is getting smaller. They know that the vent by the pump house door has not decided. They know that the pool with the domed back in it — they have a name for that pool, which they have not told anyone — bulges and settles at a rhythm just slower than breathing, and they know this because they have stood beside it for longer than the form requires and matched their breath to it and not stopped themselves.

It is only mud. They will tell you that. They will tell you with the exact tone you used on yourself, standing there: *it is almost certainly only mud*. And then they will tell you what only mud contains, because they have had time to think about it. The flywheel, not yet, but soon. The pipes before these pipes — there were pipes before these, you can see the stubs on the far side, a different gauge of steel, an older company, their trestles wholly gone. A sheep, at least one, because they saw it happen, in the second year, a ewe that came down for a reason no ewe could give and stood on a crust that was not the ground. Afternoons. Thousands of afternoons, each one a grey leaf, each one the whole output of some cone that isn't there any more, going down further than any fall exposes. The domed back in the pool is not a thing. It is the basin turning over everything that has come down into it, the way a slow stomach turns, and the shape you keep seeing is just the shape that process makes when you watch it from above.

They go back up. They sign. Next quarter they come again.

And this is where the census gets hard to close, because you have to decide whether they live here. By the ordinary measure, no — they have a house and a kettle and a bed and none of them are in the basin. But by the basin's measure, which is the only measure that has worked so far: what lives here is what keeps returning to the same conditions. The crust returns. The gas returns. The valve returns to open and returns to closed. And the one who comes returns, and has started to come down a little earlier than the schedule and leave a little later, and has started, without quite deciding to, to sit — not on the stool, not yet — on the step of the pump house where the floor tilts, out of the wind, looking at the flywheel, listening for the click.

The pipe had no choice. That is what you said. Nothing with weight comes down except the pipes, which had no choice. But the pipe did not become part of the basin by being forced. It became part of the basin by staying while the basin kept going, until the mud stopped being a thing that happened to it and became a thing it was in. That is the mechanism. It has no malice. It does not need anyone to kneel. It only needs them to be still while it is not.

Here is how the census ends, if it ends.

One quarter no one comes. Not because anything has happened to them; the list was reassigned, or the department was folded into another, or the line on the spreadsheet finally lost the argument with the line below it. A month later the transformer's hum stops. The valve does not click. Pressure in the buried run goes up and comes down and up and nobody's needle counts it. Whatever the valve was preventing either happens, quietly, in the mud, in a place no one is standing, or turns out never to have needed preventing. The gauge's face goes still. The wagtail notices nothing. The crust, on the cone by the shed, spreads a little further than it used to, because the shed's spit of firmer ground is drier now, no wet flush from the stub a hundred metres off. Then the cone under the shed decides, and the stool and the cup go down together, one grey leaf.

And underneath, in the dark, in the cold, in the clay under the clay, the oldest tenant goes on eating what fell into the sea and breathing it out as bubbles, at a few degrees above freezing, without an outside, without a name, exactly as it did before the pipes, exactly as it will after the last of them has been turned into country.

That is what lives here. It was never a question of who. The basin is not a place something lives in. It is the living, seen from the rim, at the wrong scale, on a grey afternoon, by someone who has not yet gone back up.
