It would be honest to say that nothing lives there, and it would be nearly true, and the nearly is where the lake keeps its residents.

Start with what must be there, because it is the only thing the light will show you. The mosses.

They are not visitors. They are the successors. The valley people cleared the terraces and walled them and cut them twice a summer for a thousand years, and when the people carried the last hay up and did not come back, the terraces did not go to nothing; they went to moss. It is a plain thing to say and it took the lake a long time to do. The first cushions began as flecks on the wet side of the field walls in the years the water was still rising, when the pastures were shallows and there was still enough light at the bottom for almost anything. Then the light left, a little each year, at the same rate the water came, and the moss did what the people never could — it stayed, and simply slowed down to match.

A cushion the size of a body has been growing for centuries. There are no rings to count, moss keeps no calendar, but if you cut one open — nobody has — you would find the green skin lies on a body of its own dead growth, brown and then black and then something with no colour at all, compressed year on year, and every layer is a summer's ration of the memory of a direction. What the lake gives them in a year is a hand's width of light. What they make of it is a hand's width of moss. The lake rose at that pace. They live at it. It is the speed of the place.

On the flat terraces they lie in ranks, and the ranks follow the walls. They have taken the shape of the mowing. Where a man scythed a curve around a boulder there is a curve in the moss around the boulder. Where a terrace edge was built up with a course of larger stones the moss has thickened over it into a lip, so that the field steps and the moss steps and the drowned man's steps are the same steps. Nobody planned this and nothing eats it back, so it persists in a way that the intentions of the living never do. The moss holds the outline of a thousand years of work that no one is doing anymore, and it will hold it as long as the ice holds the outlet.

In the forest they went up. This is a different kind of life from the meadows. The cushions on the ground are settlers; the curtains in the trees are climbers, and they went up the dead larches in the first decades, when the bark was still on, and by the time the bark slid off they were rooted in the wood grain itself and did not fall. Now they hang. They are the only thing in the forest that moves — the turbidity current in summer, the faint stir of the spring turnover, and the curtains move with it, all of them together, a whole hillside of grey trees swaying a foliage that is not theirs. If the lake had a wind this would be its weather. The living trees at the top of the slope leaned into a wind and kept the lean after the wind was gone; the dead trees below them have been given, by a tenant, a new lean that changes with the season. The forest has been re-leafed by something that has no interest in it.

Then there is what falls in.

Nothing came up the river. That is settled. But the lake has a ceiling, and the ceiling is the sky, and the sky is not empty. And at the head of the valley there is the glacier, and the glacier is not dead either.

On its surface, in summer, the ice melts into a pockmarked country of little holes, each with a fleck of black dust at the bottom, and in those holes live the small animals of the ice — things without names outside a laboratory, slow red worms as thin as thread, rotifers, the water-bears with their eight blunt legs. They live on the algae that live on the dust. They have never known a place that was not frozen or about to be. And every summer the melt carries some of them down the crusted face of the ice, over the rockfall, into the lake.

They are the only animals that have ever entered the valley. They are the size of a grain of pollen, they cannot see, and they arrive into water that is colder than anything they came from and darker than anything, and they sink, and they land — on a cushion, on a shingle, on a rope of moss in the trees — and they do what such creatures do when the world stops being possible. They stop. The water-bears draw their legs in and shut. The rotifers go still. It is not death. They have a word for it in the laboratories, a word for the state that is neither living nor its opposite, and the lake is full of them, a scattered dust of animals that have suspended themselves.

Some of them could wake. Bring them up to light and air and warmth and a certain proportion would stir, unfold, and go looking for the algae that are not there. Some are past that, and have been for a hundred years. There is no way to tell one from the other by looking. They lie together on the moss like seed. They are the lake's third snowfall — after the snow itself, after the slush that sinks through the upper water and dusts the branches white for an hour — a snowfall of visitors who got here and could not go on and did not go back. And nothing has come to eat them either. Nothing will. They accumulate.

With them, some years, comes colour. The snow on the ridges in late spring goes red where it lies old — it is an alga, it lives in the snow, it is the only red thing in the whole high country — and when that snow slides into the lake and sinks, the alga dies of the dark long before it reaches the bottom, but it reaches the bottom red. So there are springs when the black-green cushions on the upper terraces carry a bloom of rust-pink for a week, until the silt covers it. The valley people would have seen the red snow every year of their lives and thought nothing of it. The moss has never seen it. It receives it.

And the mill wheel turns, and you will want to count it as one of the living, and you must not. It turns because the summer inflow leans on its paddles and because the axle was made of a wood that does not rot. That is all. It has no preference. If the current stopped it would stop and feel nothing. The temptation in a place like this — where the trees grow after they die and the hay waits and the ice sings — is to grant life to whatever moves, and the discipline of the lake is that almost nothing that moves is alive and almost nothing that is alive moves. Hold on to that. It is the second thing to hold on to, after the height.

---

Because the thing that actually lives there — the thing the whole valley has, without meaning to, been arranged around — does not move at all, and you would swim through it without knowing.

Go back down to the shimmer. The plane of slight blur at the height of the eaves, where the new water lies on the old. It was described as a boundary, a matter of density, oil on water. That is what it is. But look at it with a lamp, closely, for longer than a visitor would, and the blur is not only a bending of light. It has a grain. It has a faint colour — a brownish green so slight it reads as a stain on the water rather than a thing in it. And it has a thickness. Two hands, three. Everywhere in the basin, at exactly that depth, spread from the foot of the drowned falls to the foot of the ice, cut through only by the chapel gable and the tallest of the standing trunks.

It is alive. It is the most alive thing in the valley by a distance that cannot be measured, and it is made of individuals so small that there are more of them in a cupped hand of that water than there were people in the whole history of the village.

They are a kind of bacterium that lives on sulphur and light, and they need both, and they need them together, and there is exactly one place in the lake where both are found: the seam. Below the seam the old water has no oxygen, and the drowned soils and the hay in the byres and the rust-red needles have been leaching sulphide into it for as long as they have lain there — very slowly, at the speed of the place, but continuously. Above the seam there is light. Not light as you would call it. The memory of a direction. A brightness so faint that no plant could make anything of it and no eye could confirm it. But these have been built, over a length of time that makes the lake young, to live on almost nothing, and the almost nothing at thirty metres in a brown-green dusk is, for them, a full day. The sulphide rises. The light falls. Where they meet there is a living.

So the shimmer is a population. A sheet of bodies laid along the one plane in the valley where the past and the present touch, taking a little from each. And this is the thing that takes longest to understand, longer than the cold, longer than the absence of fish: they are the only things in the lake with a preference. The moss grows where it landed. The water-bears lie where they fell. But the sheet holds its depth. In summer, when the inflow thickens the upper water and pushes the boundary down a hand's width, the sheet goes down a hand's width. In the still of late winter, when the seam rises, they rise with it. They cannot swim, not really — but they can sink a little less or a little more, and across centuries that has been enough. Every one of them is always at the seam. Every one of them has always been at the seam. It is the only address in the valley.

They do not eat, in the way the lake has forbidden. Nothing grazes them and they graze nothing. But they take the sulphide, and the sulphide comes from the year that drowned, and so the keeping — the perfect, sealed, weighted keeping of the lower water — is not quite perfect. It is being lived on. At its very rim, at a rate no instrument would register, the preserved autumn is being drawn up into a green so faint it has no name and made into more of the same. The village has its lofts in the new water and its byres in the old, and between them, at the eaves, exactly at the eaves, it wears a ceiling of the only thing awake.

The chapel gable stands up through it. The bell hangs in its arch with moss on its shoulders, and the plane passes through the arch a little below the crown, so that the bell hangs with its shoulders in this year's water and its mouth in the old year's, and the mouth of the bell — silent, ropeless, that no current in the lake is strong enough to swing — is full of them. It has been full of them for centuries. It is the one vessel in the valley that has never been empty.

That is what lives there. Moss that has taken the shape of the work. A dust of small animals holding their breath. And a sheet of the smallest possible lives lying along the seam between what is kept and what is new, at the height of a house, in the cold and the dark, taking the memory of a direction and the slow leak of a drowned autumn and making, out of the two together, the only continuing thing.
