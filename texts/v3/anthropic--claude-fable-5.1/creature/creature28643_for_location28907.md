What lives there is mostly the size of your thumb, and there is no end of it.

Get close to one — they let you, in the sense that they do not move away, which is not the same thing — and the resemblance to the plant stops being a resemblance. The back is not shell. It is bark. It has the same fine longitudinal grain as the stems, the same colour progression from the head backward, ivory to amber to brown, so that a single animal carries its own age along its length the way a stem does along its height. The legs are stiff jointed blades, six or eight or twelve of them, and they move the way the fronds unfold, with a small dry crack at each joint. The mouth is a split, a seam, exactly like the seam on a pod. When it closes on the tissue it is eating there is no lip, no tongue, no wetness. There is just the seam pressing and a fleck of bark coming away.

They are born as buds. You will see it if you watch a wall long enough: a small swelling in the bark, ivory-pale, the size of a fingernail, which for an hour might be a shoot and then is not — it splits, and instead of blades it is legs, and the thing stands up out of the wall it was part of and begins, at once and without pause, to eat the wall. There is no interval. There is no first meal. The mouth is working before the last leg has pulled free.

For a day they are soft and pale and they eat and they are eaten, by each other, by the shearers, by the people. For a few days after that they are amber and hard and they eat and they climb and, if they have wings, they fly. The winged ones are the same animal as the crawling ones. You watch it happen: a hard amber crawler pauses on a stem, and its back splits along a seam it did not have a moment before, and two stiff dry blades lever up out of the split and lock with a snap and it is a flier and it is gone, clattering down the valley in the gold haze. Nothing chose this. The stem it was on was old. The tissue was tough. The plant, reaching through the animal, opened it.

Then they go brown. Then they slow. You find them by the thousand on the older stems, dark, still, legs folded under, seam shut, and the bark has begun to grow over them — not consuming them, not exactly; taking them back. The grain of the stem runs across the grain of the back and where the two meet there is a ridge like the ridges around the old cut marks, healed. In a week there is a bump on the stem. In a month there is a stem. Sometimes, on the oldest black wood, you can see the bumps in rows, in drifts, in the shape of a crowd that stopped where it stood, and the whole dead trunk is a mass grave that is also the thing it is a grave in.

They do not die, is the point. There is no moment you could call that. They stop being separate, which in this valley is the only death there is.

---

The shearers are the same animal, later.

It takes a while to accept this. They are so much bigger — the length of a boat, the height of your chest, plated in shields the size of dinner plates — and they move with such deliberateness, that long low slide along a stem at walking pace, that they seem like a different order of thing. But look at the head. It is a seam. Look at the legs, twenty or thirty of them, stiff blades that crack at the joint. Look at the colour running back from the mouth: pale, then gold, then a long amber middle, then a tail end that is brown and dull and that the small things are already crawling on and, you realise, already eating, and in places already beginning to grow over.

What makes a shearer is not being born one. It is not stopping. Most of the small things go brown at the ordinary size and fold their legs and are taken back. Some do not. Some, for reasons that are the plant's reasons, keep eating past the point where the others slow, and the bark of their backs keeps thickening, and they moult — you find the moults, whole hollow animals of translucent amber, light as paper, that crumble when you touch them — and they come out of each moult a size larger and paler at the head and they eat. They eat the way the fold opens: continuously, at a fixed rate, without appetite. The trench they leave is exactly as wide as their mouth and exactly as deep as the bark and it runs behind them along the stem in a long straight pale line, and where the trench is the tissue is raw and bleeds resin and the small things pour into it in a river and the plant, wounded, grows.

The shearers are how the plant reaches its own surface. They are its hands, or its plough. Where they have been, the growth comes up fastest and rawest and most ivory, and you can read the recent history of a stem by the pattern of its ridges, each ridge a healed trench, each trench a shearer that went along here some days ago at walking pace and did not look up.

They keep moulting. They keep growing. And then at some size — larger than any you see moving — they go down. The trenches curve off the stems and into the folds, into the dark and the hot air and the white pollen, and do not come back up. You think, standing at the lip of a fold with the warm resin-wind in your face, that this is what the heave under the far ridge was. Something that had gone down a shearer and kept eating and kept growing, in the dark, against the hot rock, until it was the size of the fold it was in, and then the size of the ridge above the fold, and then it was not possible to say whether it was in the plant or was the plant, whether the plant was growing over it or it had become a way the plant moves.

You do not go into a fold to find out. Nobody who comes here does.

---

Except that someone did, and you find him.

Not him. What was him. Deep in, where the marks are oldest, on a black stem that has stopped growing so long ago its surface has gone glassy, there is a shape at the base that is not a shape the plant makes. It is too symmetrical in the wrong way. Two long bumps side by side, a ridge between, a rounder swelling at one end. It is entirely bark now. The grain runs over it unbroken. But you have seen the small things half taken and you know what taking looks like, and this was taken, sitting, with its back to the stem and its legs out in front of it and its head down.

Beside it, sunk to the rim in bark, is a pod-husk bowl, and there is a line of scores on the stem above it, cut with a stone edge, forty or fifty of them, close together, small. The last several are shallower than the others. The last one is barely a scratch.

He counted days. He was here for weeks. He did not go up. Whether he could not — the slot had closed, a ridge had risen, the way was gone and he did not find another — or whether he sat down in the noise and the heat one day and decided he would rather be taken than climb back out into the silence, there is no way to know and the valley does not preserve reasons. It preserves surfaces. It preserved his.

He is the only one you find. But the marks go on past him, older and older, and you begin to think about how long people have been coming down the slot, and how many slots there have been, and how many of the bumps on the oldest wood are the shape of something that sat down.

---

The people who come and go back up are a small number of families from the dead country, and you can tell them by their hands.

Their hands are amber. The resin gets into the creases and the skin under the nails and it does not come out, and it hardens there, so that a resin-cutter's palm has a faint hard glaze on it that clicks against stone. They cut it from the shearers' trenches, walking behind the animal at a respectful distance with a pod-husk held under the raw edge where the bleeding is heaviest. A day's walk behind one shearer fills three husks. Set on the pan in the sun the husks cool and the resin sets clear with a skin on top, and it is carried to wherever they carry it, and burnt, and traded, and — you learn this from a small thing, a pattern of beads on a cord left tied to a stem — worn.

They know things. They know the stems by colour and will not stand on ivory, will not sleep on amber, will not trust brown past the third day. They know the sound the crust makes before it splits, a long rising tick under the roar that you would never pick out and that they can hear the way a sailor hears a change in the wind, and when they hear it they move away from that part of the ceiling and wait for the blade of daylight and the fall of glassy shards. They know the shearers do not turn and can be walked beside. They know the folds by the change in the pollen, gold to white, and they do not go near the white. They tie a cord around a stem on the way in, at the height of the tallest person's reach, and on the way out they look for it and read how far the floor has risen while they were down, and if it has risen more than an arm's length they hurry, because it means the plant is having a fast day and the slot may be closing.

They do not have a word for the plant that means *plant*. The word they have — you piece this together from very little, from how a cutter's daughter says it to her father when they think they are alone on the pan above — is closer to *the one who is coming up*. It is a name for something that has not arrived yet. They do not fear it. They talk about it the way you would talk about a season, or a relative who is a long time away and known to be on the road.

They eat the small things, roasted on a bead of resin until the seam pops. They say the pale ones are best. They say never eat a brown one. They say, and here the daughter lowers her voice further so that you almost lose it under the memory of the roar, that the brown ones are already going back, and if you eat one you take the going with you.

---

And there is the plant.

It lives there in the way that a fire lives in a hearth, which is to say it is not a thing that is in a place but a process that a place is happening to. It has no centre that you could find. Cut into it anywhere and it is the same all the way in: warm dry tissue, plumbed with resin, growing. It has no top; the top is the crust, and the crust is the plant's oldest skin, fused and glassy and long since stopped, and the plant is pushing it up from underneath and splitting it and growing through the splits toward the light and hardening there and becoming more crust. The dead yellow pan is not ground the plant is under. It is the plant's shed surfaces, a hundred thousand years of them, lying on top of each other in the sun.

And it has no bottom, or none you will reach. The folds go down into the white pollen and the hot wind and there is something growing there too, in the dark, close to the rock, something that does not need the milky light, and the shearers go down to it and do not come back and the ridges heave.

What it is, is an egg that has been hatching for as long as there has been anything to notice, and whose hatching is the only weather in a place with no other. It does not know you are there. It does not know the people are there, or what they carry away, any more than a tree knows about the bird. It is not aware in any sense you could use. But it is not indifferent either, because indifference is a stance and it has none. It has only the one motion, which is up, and out, and toward, and all the animals that live on it are ways of doing that motion faster, and the people who come down into it are, for the length of a visit, ways of doing it too — carrying its blood out into the light where it burns clear, wearing its beads, breathing its dust, going brown.

You put your hand on the pan, and it is warm, and there are cracks around your fingers. Something is coming up. It has been coming up for a very long time and it is not in a hurry and it is not going to stop, and the country for miles around you, silent and yellow and dead, is not where it isn't. It is where it was. The living part is underneath, seething, unfinished, and every day the roof is a little thinner.
