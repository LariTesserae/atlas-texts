The bats do not live here. That is the first thing to set aside. They sleep here, and they breed here, and they have made the ceiling into a country of their own, but their lives happen elsewhere — over the river, over the streetlamps, in the insect-thick air above the sewage works. The vault is their bedroom. Every night they leave it, and a thing that leaves every night is a lodger, however long it has kept the room.

Nor is it the crowd on the floor, quite, though the crowd has a better claim. The cockroaches and the crickets and the sunless beetles do live here entirely; they have never been anywhere else and cannot conceive of anywhere else. But there is a way in which nothing is being asked of them. The guano falls, the guano is eaten, the eaters die into the guano. They are the room's digestion. Nothing in them is answering the room, because nothing in them has ever had to. The vault has been the whole of the world to them for so long that it is not a place. It is simply what is.

The fern, for a while, seems like the one. It is the only thing down here that visibly wants something — light, and a little more of it — and does not get it, and has neither grown nor died in the space of anyone's memory. There is something almost civic in that. But wanting is not living, and holding is not living either. The fern is a photograph of a plant.

The thing that lives here lives in the sump.

Go to the far end, past the manifold, to where the failed seal pours. The lamps do not reach this far; you would need your own. The water comes down warm from a joint that gave out years ago, strikes the hot main on the way, hisses, and falls the rest of the distance as a broken rope of drops into a pool the size of a bathtub set into the floor where the slurry never quite cured. The pool is perhaps knee-deep. Its outflow goes over a lip of flowstone and away under the gravel to wherever the room's water goes. And in it — you'll see them when you have stood still long enough, because they will not be where the light is — there is a population of fish.

Small fish. None of them longer than a finger. Nobody put them here. Some year when the culvert backed up in a spring flood, a handful of fry or a spawning of eggs came in with the surge from the daylit stream a kilometer and thirty meters overhead, and the water they arrived in drained away, and they were left in this pool, in the dark, at a temperature no fish in that stream had ever felt, and they did not die.

They were, to begin with, some ordinary thing — a minnow, a bleak, the sort of fish a child catches in a jar and does not bother to name. That is roughly what they still are. Their bodies have the shape of a fish that once swam in current, and their fins are still made for that. But they are pale now in a way that no fish upstairs is pale. Not white; not yet. The color of a fingernail. The color of the flowstone's terraces where the water has just moved on. In the light of a torch you can see the dark thread of the gut through the flank and, in the larger ones, the faint shadow of the spine, as though they were being drawn in pencil and someone had stopped before the shading. Pigment costs something to make, and down here it buys nothing. Generation by generation the ones that spent least on it have had a little more to spend on the rest of themselves, and so the pool has been slowly bleaching its own inhabitants, the way the concrete has been slowly bleaching itself onto the floor.

Their eyes are still there. That is worth saying plainly, because the story wants them gone and the story is impatient. They have eyes, and the eyes are the eyes of a stream fish, round and black and a little too large for a face this size. But the fish do not use them for anything. There is nothing to see. What they navigate by is the pour — the falling water is the loudest object in their universe, a permanent weather standing at one end of the pool, and every fish in the sump knows where it is at every moment with its whole body, through the lateral line, the way you know where the floor is. The pumps are their other weather. When the motors cut in, the pool trembles for a second across its whole surface, and the fish tighten toward the middle of the water and hang there, tails just moving, until the trembling settles into the steady roar and they loosen again. They have done this every few hours for their entire lives and the lives of everyone before them. It is not fear. It is closer to the way a body arranges itself before a familiar sound. Some of the older ones do not react at all.

They eat what falls. The pool is downhill from the ceiling in every sense: guano comes down, and drowned crickets come down, and with the guano comes the whole small economy that eats it — the amphipods, the worms, the larvae of whatever flies breed in the wet — and the fish take all of it. They feed at the surface mostly, rising to the dark meniscus and taking whatever has just arrived, and because the pool is warm and there is no winter they are never not hungry and never not growing, slowly, and never not spawning. There is no season to wait for. Somewhere along the warm side of the pool, where the pipe's radiant heat comes through the flowstone, there are eggs stuck to the stone at every stage, and fry no longer than a grain of rice hanging in the shelter of the terraces, at every hour of every night of every year. The colony overhead pours out at dusk and comes back at dawn in a great two-directional flood. The fish do not have dusk. They have the pump schedule, and the pour, and the ceaseless small arrival of food from above, and out of these they have made a life that has no edges.

And they are inside the geology. This is the part that takes longest to see. The lip of the sump is flowstone, and the flowstone is growing. Every drop that leaves the pool leaves a little lime behind on the sill, and the sill is rising, by the width of a drop, forever. The pool is very slightly deeper than it was when the fish arrived. The terraces on its warm edge, where the eggs are laid, were not there when they arrived. The pool is being built around them by the same process that is building the room, and they are adjusting to it at the same pace — the depth, the temperature gradient, the shape of the shelter — so that at no point does anything change. It is one of the only places you could stand and truthfully say that a thing is being made and a thing is being made to fit it, together, at the speed of stone, and that the second thing is alive and has eyes.

There is one you will notice if you stay. Larger than the rest, the palest of them, so far gone toward translucency that when it turns broadside in the torch beam you see the shadow of your own light passing through its body onto the flowstone behind it. It holds station near the pour, nose into the churn, in the one place in the pool that has anything like a current, and it does not move from there for the others. Whether it is old, or only large, there is no way to tell. It has never seen the stream it descends from. It has never seen anything, in the sense that seeing means anything up there. It has lived its whole life in a room made of heat and roar and the tremble of pumps, under a ceiling of animals it has never known were animals, at the bottom of a building nobody thinks of as a building, and it has become — patiently, without intention, without any of the wanting that keeps the fern where it is — exactly the thing that this place produces when it produces a thing.

The colony will come back down the shaft before light. The pumps will cycle. The seal will go on failing at the same rate it has always failed at. And at the far end, under the pour, in water the temperature of a fever, the fish will rise to the surface for whatever has just fallen, and settle, and rise again, and be a little paler than their parents, and a little closer to the stone.
