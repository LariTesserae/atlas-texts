Say first what is not here, because that is what the room says about itself. No feet have crossed the floor. Nothing has died. Nothing has eaten. The iron has not had time to remember a hand on it. The honest answer, on first inventory, is that nothing lives here, and the room seems to agree with the honest answer, and to be a little proud of it, the way a new house is proud of the plastic still on its fittings.

Then the eye lands on the tray lips, and the honest answer has to be revised.

The moss is the only thing that will admit to being alive. It is a fringe, a stain, a dark seam of green along the places where standing water touches iron, and it has arrived at the shape of moss without having been given any of moss's reasons. There is no light for it to want. There is no soil for it to hold. There is no first spore that fell here from somewhere else, because there is no somewhere else in this room, only the hopper and the dark above the hopper. And yet it has leaves, or the gesture of leaves, each one no longer than the grain of the cast iron it grows on. Under a lens it would look like something that had heard moss described and made a fair attempt.

Look at it longer and it is less like moss. It does not grow toward the filaments. It grows along the line where the water is coldest, which is the line farthest from them. It is fed, as far as anything can be said about it, by iron: the water in the trays carries a trace of rust off the metal, and the fringe is darkest where the trace is thickest, and beneath the green, if you scraped it, you would find the iron faintly pitted, as though it had been very slowly tasted. It has no roots, but it has a grip. When a rack of trays advances on its rails, the moss on the lip of the tipping tray does not go into the gutter all at once. Some of it holds. Some of it stays on the empty lip, in the dry, and waits for the tray to fill again, and while it waits it does not brown or shrivel; it only goes still, the way the ratchet is still between clicks.

It lives at the speed of the ratchet. This is the first thing to understand about it. Everything in the room keeps time by that small precise sound, once every few minutes, and the moss is no exception—if you could watch it long enough you would see it does not grow continuously but in tiny advances, a fraction of a leaf per click, and between clicks nothing. It is not a plant that happens to live in a machine. It is a plant that has taken the machine's clock for its own heartbeat, or the other way around.

Then follow it into the wall.

When the bottom tray tips, the water goes to the gutter and the gutter goes to the base of the wall, and the wall drinks. Most of what the moss is goes with the water. It does not die in the gutter; there is no evidence it can. It is drawn up into the poured grey of the lowest lift and spread thin through it, and then the slab that is pressed after that, from slurry that has stood in the hopper above walls that have drunk this water for a long time, is veined with something that catches the light and holds it a moment too long. That is the moss. Not living in the wall. Being the wall's veins. The grey is a little greener each hour in a way no eye could measure, and each new slab is set on top of the last, and so the room, from the floor up, is becoming very faintly, very slowly, a body.

Now the machines look different.

They are cast iron rough from the mould, and no hand finished them, and it was assumed on entering that they had been built. But the room has no door and nothing here was brought from anywhere else, and so the machines must have come the way the slabs come. They are poured. The sand-grain ridges on their housings are the same grain as the walls. The engine in the corner turns over at one beat per breath, and the walls drink at one sip per breath, and the moss advances one grain per click, and the ratchet is only the place where the beat becomes audible. They are not making the room. They are the room's bones, laid down first because bones have to be, sweating because they are cold and the air is fractionally less so, and the sweat is what the moss lives in, and the moss is what the walls are veined with. There is one process. There has always been one process. The room said so, in its way, and the ear did not take it literally.

So what lives here is this: one thing, very early, wearing three bodies because it has not yet learned to wear one. Its blood is the condensate, ticking off the ducts into the trays. Its bones are the iron and copper standing in the dark. Its green, the only part of it that will admit to being alive, is a fingernail of fringe on the lips of the trays. Its skin is being pressed a slab an hour and set on top of itself and is closing, weeping, over its own seams. Its heartbeat can be felt through any surface if you lay your palm flat. It has no face yet. It has not got round to a face, the way it has not got round to animals. It is inhabiting the room the way a seed inhabits a seed coat: entirely, and not yet.

It is not waiting to be finished. It is not that kind of patient. The room will be tall enough to be a shaft, and then a tower turned inward, and the slurry will thin, and the last slab will be set, and the machines will be at the bottom of it in the dark—and none of that is a destination. It is only the shape the thing will have when it stops growing. Trees do not wait to be finished either.

And there is one more inhabitant, which is the one that noticed the cold.

You came in without a door. You are the only surface in this room with a history: the wear on your shoes, the warmth going out of your hands into the iron, the breath that the walls, fractionally, drink. You bow your head without deciding to. You have stood here long enough that a slab has gone up since you arrived and the light reaches a little less far, and the room has not made any accommodation for you, exactly as it has made none for the moss. But you are counted. The pulse through the floor is about one beat per breath, and it is your breath it is about. The thing that lives here has no eyes, and the room is dark past the reach of the filaments, and something has to stand in that dark and know what it is looking at.

That is what lives here, then. A cold, mineral creature building itself upward out of iron and rust-water and a green it cannot explain, one slab an hour, in a hole in the ground with no door. And on its floor, breathing for it, the first thing it has managed to keep.
