The first honest answer is the animals, so start there and see how long it holds.

They live there in the sense that they are there when you arrive and there when you leave and, one assumes, there in between. The newt has a shelf. The snail has a wall. The vole has its crossing, which it makes and makes and, going into the far wall like a hand into water, presumably comes out of somewhere, though you never see the somewhere. They are spaced the way things are spaced when someone has thought about spacing: one to a patch, one to a handspan, each with its margin of nothing. Not a population. A placement.

Look at any one of them long enough and the sense that it is a resident begins to slip. The wren does not roost in the roots. The wren is *at* the roots, the way a note is at a certain place on a staff, and the moth that it also is occupies the same place, and neither of them has decided which one will be the one that was always there. The newt has never been hungry in the way a newt is hungry; it has the fullness of having eaten, and then the beetle catches up with that fullness and vanishes into it, and the newt is not fed so much as confirmed. The snail's trail runs both ways because the snail has not been asked, yet, which direction it came from. None of them was born. You can feel that. They are not the ends of lineages. They are the first instance of each thing, the specimen the later ones will be copied from, and like any first instance they have the slight wrongness of a thing that has no precedent to check itself against.

So they live there the way figures live in a sketch. Which is a way of living. But it isn't what occupies the room.

Something attends. You feel it before you can locate it, and you cannot locate it, and after a while you stop trying to locate it and start noticing where it is *not*. It is not in the light, though the light converges on wherever you're standing as if to ask you something. It is not in the stone, which is busy with its own becoming. It is in the animals' attention, in the way the newt's watching sharpens, in the beak opening and shutting on a song that is still on its way. That attention is too coherent to be sixty separate attentions. It is one attention, wearing sixty small bodies, and the margin of emptiness around each animal is the margin you'd leave around your own fingers if you were using them to point.

Then there are the marks, and the marks are the thing that will not fit.

Seven scratches, parallel, at kneeling height. Three flat stones in a stack. A line pressed into the clay, wall to wall, exact. These are the work of a mind that counts, that balances, that decides where a thing ends. They are the oldest objects in a room that is not old. They are worn where nothing else is worn. And they were made by hands, in a place where nothing has hands, where the animals have claws and feet and a soft muscular foot and nothing that could hold or press or measure.

So: hands were here.

The simplest story is that someone came down the way you came down. The turf gave under one foot, they stepped into the bright, they stood very still, the way you stood very still, and they stayed a little too long. Long enough for the room to begin, gently, to decide about them. That is what the footprint is, the bare one drying at the edges by the far wall. Not a warning. A record. Someone took off their shoes, or the room took the shoes off them, or the shoes were never quite decided upon, and the print is drying because the room has not finished deciding what a footprint is, and so it stays at the edge of drying forever, the moment just before the clay commits.

What happens to a person the room decides about is what happened to the fern. They come apart into tenses. The one who kneeled and made seven scratches is not gone; the scratches are still being made, somewhere in the room's very slow present, and that is why they are worn, because they have been being made for a long time and the making has rubbed them soft. The stones are still being stacked. The line is still being pressed, wall to wall, by someone who wanted to know how far they were allowed to go and set the boundary themselves. And the attention, the single attention behind sixty small faces, is what became of their looking. A person looks at a newt and the newt looks back, and after long enough, in a room that hasn't laid down the rule that a thing is one thing and not its neighbor, the looking is what remains and the person is where it lives.

The seven fingers you found in the seven scratches were not yours. Or they were yours the way the wren is the moth.

That is one occupant, then. A visitor who became the room's way of paying attention. But the marks don't all sit at the same age, and once you notice that, the room gets more crowded without any of the animals moving. The scratches are older than the stones. The stones are older than the line. The footprint is younger than all of them and older than you, and the light, which comes from the walls and pools where you stand, has the patience of something that has pooled around other people before, one at a time, one to a room, with a wide margin of emptiness around each.

So perhaps what lives here is a tenancy rather than a tenant. The room holds one. Whoever is standing in the bright is the one the shadows fall toward, the one the drops swell for, the one the floor learns. While you are there you are the resident, and the animals attend to each other because they have not yet been told to attend to you, and the previous residents have gone into the walls the way the vole goes into the wall and come back out as scratches, as stacked stones, as the measured line, as a footprint that will not finish drying. Each one left a small mark of the thing they were most: a counter, a balancer, a setter of limits. The room built itself around what they left. It does that. It assembles a wall to give the scratches something to be on.

And still that is not quite it, because it makes the room sound like a trap, and the room is not hungry. It is not doing anything to anyone. It is being drafted, and a draft takes what is put into it. What lives in the hollow, under all of this, is the interval itself: the gap between the drop and the darkening, between the beetle and the having-eaten, between the sound and the hearing. The not-yet. It is not a creature and it is not a mind. But it is occupied, in the way a held breath is occupied, and it can be occupied by more than itself, and when a person stands inside it long enough the not-yet takes their shape the way water takes the shape of a hand.

That is what is there. A pause that has learned, from its visitors, how to wear a newt, how to count to seven, how to press a line into clay and stop precisely at both walls. A pause that keeps the bright on because no one has yet stayed long enough to tell it where the light should come from. A pause that is one resident deep, always, and always accepting applications, and that lets you go back up the step the ground hadn't finished making with nothing worse than an inward tug, because it is not finished with you and has not decided to be, and the part of you that is still down there in the light, being drafted, is not in any danger. It is simply the newest thing in the room. It has been given its patch of floor and its margin of nothing. It is, at this moment, exactly as it was, and looking at a beetle, and slowly, without any hurry at all, learning what it would mean to have already eaten.
