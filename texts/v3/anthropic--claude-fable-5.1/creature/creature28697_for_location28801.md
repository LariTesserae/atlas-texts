The honest answer is that one thing lives there, and everything else lives on it, or in it, or in the brief interval before it is taken back into it. So the account has to begin with the ground.

---

The crust is a single organism, or has become one. Whether it began as one plant or as a mat of many that fused, lost their seams, and forgot their borders is not recoverable, since the plateau keeps no record of itself and the question is one it would not, in any case, understand. It is one now. Oil cut from a seam-lip in the far north stiffens at the same rate and smells the same as oil cut a thousand kilometers south. The chord the stacks sound is the same chord across the continent, shifted only by the length of the pipes. When a pit turns in the west, the flats to the east go a fraction tauter over the following days, as though something had been drawn in and the surface pulled with it.

It is not fast, and it is not, so far as anyone can tell, aware in any way that would satisfy the word. But it is responsive. Cut it and it closes. Load it and it thickens. Where the hounds gather habitually at the base of a cannon, the crust under their hooves grows a fine hard nap, like the tread of a stair worn the other way, built up rather than down. Where the gleaners front along a seam, the seam gives a little more oil. It does not know the animals are there. It knows that there is pressure and taste and heat at these places, and it answers pressure and taste and heat, and over a very long time the answers have shaped into something that looks, from outside, like accommodation.

What it does — what it is for, if that question has any purchase — is this: it takes light and the thin traffic of moisture rising from the lowlands and the minerals of a mountain range it dissolved so long ago that the range exists only as a memory in its own thickness, and it turns these into oil, and it burns the oil. Not with flame. It ferments it in the dark, it pressurizes it, it fires it upward as seed, it lets it go as gas and charge and heat. And then it eats what comes back down. It is a fire that has found a way to be a plant, or a plant that has found a way to be a fire. It does not grow, in the sense of getting larger; it has reached its edges, where the air stops being enough, and there it stops. It only turns.

---

The animals, then, are what live on the fire.

They have in common that they are dry, deaf, hard, and quick, and that they have solved the problem of water by never encountering it. Most are shelled or waxed. None have lungs of any size; the air is thin and they take what they need through fine dry membranes that do not lose moisture because they have no moisture to lose. Their blood, where they have blood, is oil-based, borrowed and modified from the crust — the same camphorous smell, thinner, a little warmer. Their eyes, where they have eyes, are small and deep-set and usually shuttered against the glare, and they open them only at dusk and at the edges of things. Their real sense is in the feet.

Every animal on the plateau reads the ground. The crust is taut and it conducts: a mill unwinding fifty kilometers off arrives as a shiver in the pads of the hooves long before the sound would matter, if sound mattered. A cannon swelling toward discharge makes the ground around it tighten for hundreds of meters, a slow drum being tuned. The pits turning, the seams grinding, the flats flexing over their vast area — all of it comes up through the soles as a continuous weather of tremor, and the animals live inside that weather the way fish live in current, oriented to it without deciding to be. They are not deaf, exactly. They hear with the whole body, from below. What they cannot do is hear from above, where the air carries everything at once and therefore nothing.

---

**The gleaners** are the most numerous thing on the plateau and the least like anything else. They are hand-sized, six-legged, and shelled in a white silica they lay down themselves, pulled from the oil, which carries the dissolved rock of the substrate in trace. A gleaner is, functionally, a small ceramic bottle with legs. It fronts along the seam-lips where the crust is raw and green and weeping, drinks the oil before the air can stiffen it, and stores it in a bladder that occupies most of the body. A full gleaner is heavy and slow and gleams with a faintly greenish light through the shell. An empty one is dull and fast.

They move in fronts because the seams move, and because a seam gives oil for only a day before it greys and seals. The front follows the raw edge along the seam's length — a kilometer of glinting bodies advancing at the speed of healing, drinking the wound as it closes. Where two seams cross, two fronts collide, and the gleaners do not fight, exactly, but they crowd, and the shells knock and ring, and some are pushed under and pressed into the sealing tissue and are simply taken. The crust closes over them. In a season the silica is back in a stack somewhere, laid down as one more layer on the polished white.

They mate on the flats, in the dry heat of midday, in loose crawling assemblies that leave the ground beneath them scored with thousands of tiny tracks that are gone by evening. The young are soft for an hour and then not. Nothing on the crust stays soft.

**The chaff-riders** are born in the air and come down only to die. A mill that has just unwound leaves a rising column of chaff above it, and in the column, if you could see so finely, a haze of things like flecks of skin — the young, shed by the adults in the updraft, spreading their first membranes on the same lift that carries the chaff. They are almost nothing. A grown chaff-rider is the size of two hands laid flat and weighs less than the wax on a single seed. It is translucent, veined, and edged with a fringe of fine hairs that flex to steer. It feeds on what the chaff carries: the fine dry dust of ground crust, the spores the mills throw off with the fiber, the smaller flecks of itself. It drinks nothing and excretes a powder.

They ride for days. From the ground they are visible only at dusk, when the low sun goes through the chaff and they show as brighter flecks in the glitter, turning slowly, thousands of them, drifting in the same direction the whole plateau's wind is going. They descend when the air stills, which is rare, or when they have grown too heavy with dust, or when something above them has torn a hole in the membrane. On the crust they are absorbed within the hour. There is no trace. The next mill lets go and the column rises with the next generation in it.

**The cannon-hounds** are what visitors mean when they say the plateau has animals, because the hounds are the only ones large enough and quick enough to look, from any distance, like animals as the word is usually meant. They are long, low, and narrow, deer-sized but built more like a coursing dog stretched to fit the ridges of the ground, with split hooves that grip the crust's furrows and a thin whip of tail they hold high for balance at speed. Their hide is a hard, fine-scaled grey that reads as the crust itself when they are still, and they are very often still.

A hound's life is the cannons. They travel between them in loose bands of a dozen or twenty, always moving from a cannon that has fired toward one that is swelling, and they know which is which by pressing the chest to the ground and reading the tightness spreading out from the base. Arriving at a cannon in its last day, they lie down around it in a wide ring — not close, because a hound too near when the valve opens is killed by the sound it cannot hear, its body simply ruptured by the pressure — and they wait. When the crust under them goes hard as bone and begins to thrum, they rise. When the cannon fires, they are already running outward, and from above the band is a second detonation, slower, spreading across twenty kilometers of plain as a ring of grey bodies flat out under a sky full of falling smoke.

Then they eat. The seed comes down and the hound nearest it cracks the wax with its front teeth, which are chisels, and gets its tongue into the black heart, and the heart is dense and oily and the best food on the plateau. A hound that has eaten well is warm to the touch for a day afterward. The seeds that land where no hound reaches are absorbed or, once in some thousands, root; and it has been argued, by the few who have watched long enough to argue, that the hounds are not stealing from the crust but working for it — that the seed is not chiefly a seed but a payment, and what it buys is the hooves, which score the flats and keep the surface from sealing too hard, and the dry waste, which returns the black heart's minerals to the ground in a form the crust can use more quickly than it could use the seed. It cannot be proven. The hounds do not know it either way.

They do not sleep in the ordinary sense. They lie down in the ring and go still and their eyes close, but the chest stays on the ground and the body stays tuned, and when the tremor changes they are up before they are awake.

---

Something hunts the hounds. It is not often seen, and the descriptions do not agree, which is itself a description. Some who have seen it say it is flat and wide and the color of the crust and lies in the flats like a ridge among ridges. Some say it is pale and comes up out of the seams, where the tissue is raw, and that they took it for a fold of new crust until it moved. What is agreed is when it moves. It moves when the ground is shaking — when a mill nearby lets go, or a cannon fires, or the lightning lays itself along a seam and the whole surface for a kilometer jumps. In that interval the hounds' one sense is flooded, and the flat thing crosses the distance it has been lying at, and a hound that was running in the ring goes down and does not get up, and the rest of the band does not know until the tremor settles and there is one fewer.

It is, if the guesses are right, a thing that has learned to hide inside noise in a world where the animals hear with their feet — a predator whose whole method is to attack in the moment the ground is loudest. It takes a hound every few days across a range of some hundreds of square kilometers, and it is thought to be quite large, and slow between attacks, and there may be very few of them, and they may be old. Nothing else on the crust is known to be old. Everything else turns over in a season.

---

Above, in the glitter, something tears the chaff-riders out of the air. This one has been seen more clearly, because it can be watched at dusk: a kite-shaped thing, dark against the burning chaff, that rides the stack updrafts to height and then comes down through the drift in long slanting passes, and the sound it makes — on the rare evenings the air is still enough — is dry, like paper. It is the only animal on the plateau that uses the stacks, as far as anyone knows. It roosts at the mouths, in the warm turpentine wind, folded and hooked into the white ceramic, and launches into the rising gas.

The stack-mouths have their own tenants besides. In the collar of each stack, where the warm gas comes up and the fermentation smell is strongest, there is a soft grey growth that is not the plant and is not quite animal — a pelt, a felt, a mass of something living on the gas and the heat and whatever the gas carries up from the dark. It is the only soft thing on the plateau. It has no shell and no wax and would be taken by the dry air in a minute anywhere else, but at the stack-mouth the gas keeps it fed and the heat keeps it from stiffening, and it lives there in a ring, breathing, and no one has decided whether it is many small things or one.

---

The pits are the oldest place, and what lives in them is the oldest life, and the least known.

A pit is the crust folded under itself and being digested — a slow grey whirlpool, a kilometer across, turning a meter an hour, exhaling. It is warm and dark inside and there is a great deal of oil breaking down. The things that live there are seen only when the turning brings a fold of them briefly to the surface: pale, long, boneless-looking, a meter or several meters, with no eyes and no shell and a skin like the crust's own raw tissue. They do not react to light. They do not react to anything the surface can offer. They are inside the plant's gut, living on what it is digesting, and the surface is to them an accident of the fold, a moment of cold and glare before the mass takes them back down.

They may be very old — old as individuals, since nothing inside the pit is fired or scattered or seasonally consumed; the pit takes years to turn through itself. They may be a kind of animal the crust grew, once, from its own tissue, and let live. They may be the thing the crust was before it became the crust, the plant's own ancestor kept alive in its stomach. No one has held one long enough to ask. They are rolled under and the pit exhales and goes on turning.

---

There are, finally, the things that do not live here.

Visitors come. They stand on the crust and feel it warm under their boots and cannot bear the sound and are made frightened, at some depth beneath argument, by the absence of any wet thing anywhere. They cut it and watch it close. They stay days, or weeks; a few have stayed a year. They do not stay longer, and they do not build, because nothing built would last — the crust takes back whatever touches it, and a wall laid in spring is a ridge by autumn and a memory of a ridge by the next. Nothing that needs water can live here, and nothing that needs to keep anything can live here, and people need both.

So the plateau's population is exactly what has been described: the ground, and a handful of kinds of small dry hard thing that read the ground with their feet, and a few large slow rare things at the edges of knowing, and the pale things in the gut. None of them remembers anything. None of them has anything to remember with; there is no sediment to read and no old individuals to ask, except perhaps the flat hunters and the pit-dwellers, and they are not asking anything of anyone. The hounds run out from the cannon and eat and lie down at the next. The gleaners front along the seam and drink and are sealed under. The chaff-riders rise and drift and come down and are gone. And under all of them the one thing that lives here goes on burning its oil in the dark and firing it into the light and eating what comes back, at deafening volume and in perfect balance, with no more knowledge of the lives it carries than a flame has of the moths.
