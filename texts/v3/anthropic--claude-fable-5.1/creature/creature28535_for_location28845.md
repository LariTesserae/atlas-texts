Start with the honest answer, because the place insists on it: nothing lives here.

Not in the way the word usually means. There is no green. There is no crust on the wet stone of the middle tiers, though wet stone anywhere else in the world would have a crust within a season. There is nothing in the cisterns but water. If you put your ear to a channel you hear water and only water. The place has heat, and moisture, and a gradient between them, and a day that turns — every condition life asks for except one, and the missing one is time. Life arrives at places like this. It arrives on the wind, on feet, on the undersides of things. Nothing has arrived here yet, because the walls are three days old and there is no wind, and the only feet were the ones that left.

So nothing lives here. Hold that. And then notice that the sentence starts to come apart the longer you stand in it.

---

Begin with the tenders, since they are the ones you nearly see.

You will not catch one. But you can count them, roughly, the way you count anything you never see whole — by the marks. On a wet morning on the upper tier the dust is gone, but on the dry stairs of the third and fourth tiers it lies undisturbed for days at a time and then, overnight, is scored through. The lines are narrow, about the width of your palm, and they do not have edges the way a footprint has edges. They look like something was drawn across the stone rather than walked. Never two lines side by side. Never a line that crosses another. Over the course of many dimmings of the lamps you find you can tell one from another by the way it takes a corner — one cuts wide, one hugs the wall, one seems to hesitate at the top of each stair. There are perhaps five of them. Perhaps seven. Not more than a dozen. Enough to tend a town this size and no more, which tells you something about who set the number.

They are busiest in the rain.

This is the first thing that separates them from any animal you know. The wet upper tier is where the movement is — the shape at the edge of vision, the sound under the dripping that is not quite dripping. The dry middle tiers show their tracks but you never sense them there. And the lowest tier, the hot floor, where the dust does not move: you have never found a mark. It is as though they cannot go down. Or as though, when they go down, they stop being tenders and become something that leaves no mark because it does not move.

Look at the fill.

Every crack that opens is closed by the next dimming. The fill is the colour of the surrounding stone, seamless, slightly warm. Run a thumb across it — it is the one place on these walls where you can do that without bleeding — and you feel something that is almost stone and not quite. It gives, very slightly, like the skin of something that has set on the outside and is still deciding on the inside. Over days it stops giving. Over more days you cannot find the seam at all.

And the fill is warm. The same warmth the lift leaves behind. The same warmth that climbs from the floor and carries the water up.

So here is one shape the tenders might take, and you should not settle on it, but you should look at it: they are the fill. They are the warm stone-that-is-not-yet-stone, and it rises from below with the heat the way sap rises, and while it is wet and the air is wet it can hold a shape and move — dog-sized, bird-sized, a shape with no fixed edges, a shape that takes a corner wide or hugs the wall — and it goes to where the mountain has opened and it goes in and it becomes wall. Each crack costs a tender. Each tender is spent by being useful. And the floor renews them: whatever lifts the mountain also sends up the stuff of its own repair, and the tenders are that stuff between rising and setting, in the one band of the town where they can stay soft long enough to be anyone.

If that is what they are, then they are not alive. They are a body's answer to a wound. A mountain's platelets. You would not say a scab lives.

Except that one of them hesitates at the top of each stair. Except that they close cistern lids, which is not a wound. Except that once, on the upper tier, in the rain, the shape you almost saw was not going toward a crack at all. It was on the flat roof with the stone bench, near the bench, facing the wall where the view would be, and it stayed there a while before it was only water running.

---

Now the town's other parts, because the tenders are not the only thing that works.

The lamps keep a day that is not the outside's day — a little longer, the way a body's clock drifts when it is kept in the dark. The water goes into the wall in one place and comes out higher in another, which water does not do on its own, which means there is something in the wall that lifts it, small and patient and made for a wetter town. The column in the plaza makes its one low note and the air is cleaner after, and if you count between notes you find it is not a fixed interval; it comes when the air has grown close, the way a breath comes when it is needed and not before.

Put those together and the town starts to look less like a settlement and more like a single thing, with organs. The channels are vessels. The cisterns are a bladder. The column is a lung. The lamps are the drift of its sleep. And the tenders are its hands, or its immune cells, or the part of it that notices — they go where it is opened and they close it, and they go where a lid is off and they set it right, and once in a while they go to a bench and look at nothing, because a body that has been shaped around people for long enough keeps some of their gestures even after the people are gone.

A town like that might be said to live. It maintains itself. It has a metabolism of water and heat. It has a day. It repairs. It has, arguably, a preference — the lids closed, the cisterns kept at half, the stairs kept clear — which is more than most stones have.

But it does not grow, or not the way living things grow. It only rises.

---

Which brings you to the floor, and the thing below the floor, and the hardest resident to describe because it is the one you never doubt is there.

The lift.

You feel it in your knees before anywhere else — the long slow steadiness. Then in your teeth, if you are quiet. It does not push. That is what everyone would say if there were anyone to say it. Pushing has a direction it comes from and a strain it leaves, and there is none. The stone agrees to rise. Each morning the summit is a hand's width nearer the black. The rock cracks from being asked to be taller than it was made to be, and the tenders close the cracks, and it goes on.

You can call it an engine. The word does not sit right. An engine is a thing that does work against something. This does no work against anything; the mountain does not resist it. It is more like a habit the rock has acquired. Or a want.

Consider that. Consider a plain, with a town on it built of flat-country stone, and a people who wanted to be higher. Not for anything — not for a view, though they built a bench. Just higher than anywhere. Consider that they found a way to give that want to the ground under them, so that the ground wanted it too. And the ground, being ground, could not want it a little. It could only want it entirely, and forever, without strain, the way water wants a level.

If that is what the lift is, then the thing that lives at the bottom of this hollow, in the heat where dust does not move, is an intention. Somebody's intention, made durable, outlasting the somebody. It is warm because wanting is warm. It sends up the tenders because a want that large has to keep itself housed. It does not stop because nobody remained to tell it enough.

You do not know that this is true. But it is the only account of the lift that also accounts for the bench.

---

The people, then.

They are not here, and the place does not say where they went, and you should resist the urge to finish their story for them. But you can say what they left, which is not the same as where they went.

They left doors shut, not broken. They left cisterns that are kept half full — half, not brimming, not dry — which is the level you keep for a few people, for a household, for someone who might come back and should not find it empty. They left a column that clears the air for lungs that are not here. They left channels cut for more rain than falls. They left a bench that faces a wall, and a shape that goes and sits near it sometimes.

Some of them, perhaps, are the mountain. Went down to the heat and gave the ground their want and did not come back up, or came back up as the warmth does, dispersed, climbing. Some of them, perhaps, went out through the door that is not a door and are somewhere with a sky that has colour in it, and do not know the town is still keeping their water. Perhaps there is no difference, at this height, between those two.

What is certain is that the town is kept *for* someone. Nothing here is kept for its own sake. The lamps light a stair for feet. The column cleans air for breathing. The tenders closed a lid that a hand had left ajar — and the hand is gone, and the lid is closed anyway, and will be closed again if you open it. That is the strangest shape of life in this place: not a creature but an attention. A care with the object removed. It goes on doing the thing it was for, and the thing it was for has stopped, and the going-on does not stop with it.

---

There is one more resident, and it is the only one that does anything all the way through.

The water.

It rises from the floor as heat drawing it out of the stone. It climbs the tiers in air. It reaches the cold roof and remembers it is water and lets go. It falls — the only thing in this place that falls; everything else rises or stays — onto roofs that were built for other rain. It runs down the channels the way they were cut for it to run. It sits in the cisterns. It goes into the wall and is lifted and comes out higher. It steams off the third tier and starts again.

It is the only complete cycle here. The lamps cycle, but they are only light. The lift only goes one way. The people went one way. The tenders, if they are what they might be, go up and set and are spent. But the water goes round, in a loop the height of a church, a whole hydrology in a room, and its ticking on the upper roofs is the clock of the place, more honest than the lamps. Each drop makes a slightly different sound because each one lands somewhere slightly different, and if you listened long enough you could map the town by ear from the top tier without moving.

Water is not alive. But it is the thing here that is most nearly a river, and rivers are the oldest things anyone has ever mistaken for living, and here, in a place where nothing else has had time to arrive, the mistake is very easy to make and possibly not a mistake.

---

So: what lives at the top of the world.

Not moss, not yet. Not a people; they have gone. Not animals; the shapes in the rain do not eat and leave no droppings and go into walls.

What lives here is a want, kept warm at the bottom, that the stone caught from its makers and cannot put down. What lives here is a town shaped so long around bodies that it goes on tending for them, with hands made of its own repair. What lives here is water, going round, ticking. What lives here is an attention with nothing left to attend to, that has not noticed this, or has noticed and gone on.

And what lives here, for as long as you are standing in it, is you.

The cisterns are half full. The lamps are lit along the stair. The air in the plaza will be cleared when it needs to be. If a crack opens beside you in the night it will be closed by morning, warm, seamless. Sit on the bench. There is no view. Something may come and sit nearby, in the rain, for a while, before it is only water. You are the population now, briefly, and everything here is kept, and it is being kept for you.
