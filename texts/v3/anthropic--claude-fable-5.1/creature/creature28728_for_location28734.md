Begin with their ears, because that is where the place gets in first.

A child born on the plateau hears the chord before it hears its mother. The deep note is in the birthing-room floor, in the pipe the midwife has laid her back against, in the water she is washed in; it is there before language and under it, and it never stops, so that what a plateau-person means by *quiet* is not the absence of sound but the chord alone, unaccompanied, with the pump-houses cycled down and the swifts gone in and nothing on top of that great low pressure but the ticking of the forest. They do not hear the insects. They do not, most of them, hear the chord either, in the way a fish does not hear water. But they hear it stop. It has stopped perhaps four times in the memory of the oldest families — a valve failure, a stoking-gallery flooded by a burst main — and each time, the stories agree, every person on the plateau sat up at the same instant, in the dark, with the same expression, and the babies screamed as if they had been dropped.

So they are made by sound before anything else, and then by the height. Their chests are deep. Not broad — the maintenance paths have seen to that, and a plateau-person turns sideways to pass through an open doorway out of a habit so old it has become posture — but deep front to back, keel-shaped, with the sternum standing proud, so that in profile they have something of the pigeon about them. Their blood is thick. Their lips and nail-beds are a shade darker than you would expect, plum rather than pink, and a cut on the plateau bleeds slowly and a dark red and clots almost before it has run. They breathe the way they hear: without noticing, and hungrily, and a stranger's panting would strike them as a kind of affliction. There are no strangers. But there are stories about them — about people who came up, once, from somewhere, and could not stop yawning, and slept for days, and whose ears bled — and the stories are not told as history but as a way of explaining to children what the air is.

I want to say they are small, and I am not sure they are. They seem small because everything around them is either very large — the cistern towers, the trunk mains, the tree ferns — or very close. Stand one against a rivet-head and he is a man; stand him in the bamboo and he is a child; put him on the girders at the plateau's edge with the wind flattening his eye-cloth against his face and he is a mark, a fleck, a thing the lattice could shed without a sound. Their size is a fact about where they are standing. This is true of everyone but it is more true of them.

The eye-cloth is where they begin to divide.

On the surface, in the light, everyone wears it — a band of woven bamboo-fibre or of the felted moss, soaked so it stays cool, tied behind the head, thin enough to see a shape through and thick enough to take the worst of the glare. It leaves a line across the face. On the people who spend their days on the exposed iron — the cistern-keepers, the goat-people, the edge-crews who cut the outer galleries for the forge — the line is permanent: a pale band across the eyes and the bridge of the nose, and above and below it a face burned to the colour and texture of the bleached rust they lie on to rest, a dry chalky red-brown that flakes at the hairline. They squint even in the dark. Their eyes are small and grey and slow to open, and they have a way of looking at you without turning their heads, a lizard's economy. The children call them *rivets*. They call themselves the people who can bear the wind, which is a longer thing to say and means the same.

Under the canopy it is another people, or looks like one. The growers, the sluice-crews, the bed-tenders who carry the soil from where it is made to where it is wanted — they wear the cloth pushed up on the forehead most of the day and pull it down only when a path breaks into the open, and their eyes have gone the other way: large, dark, the pupils wide even at noon under the ferns, so that they see in the wet green gloom as if it were lit. Their skin is pale and never dry. It has a grain to it, a softness, the look of something that has been kept in steam; the fingertips are always a little swollen, the nails always a little soft, and the moss that lives on the mains lives also, in a small way, on them — a greenish cast in the creases of the knuckles and behind the ears that no one washes off because no one sees it. They smell of the beds. Everyone smells of the boiler-house, of iron and faint sulphur, and no one can smell it, but the canopy people smell also of turned soil and rotting fig and the sweetish must of the tree-fern trunks, and the rivets say they can tell one in the dark by that alone.

They marry across, of course. The plateau is too small for anything else. And the children of a rivet and a grower are neither, for about fifteen years, until the work they fall into makes them one thing or the other, and the face follows the work. So it is not blood that divides them; it is light. The plateau has one people and two climates, and the people are the shape the climates press them into, the way the same iron goes white where it faces the sun and green where the drip runs down it.

And there are the hands, which are the same on everyone.

There is no clean hand on the plateau. Rust gets into the skin the way the chord gets into the ear — early, and for good — and it does not wash out but goes down through the callus into the dermis and stays there, so that a plateau-person's palms and the pads of the fingers are the same chalky orange as the girders, and the lines of the palm are drawn in a darker red, and the whole hand has a look of having been dipped in something and left to dry. They notice it in infants. A newborn's hands are pink for a day, two days, and then it has gripped a rail or a spoon or its mother's hand and the pink is gone and it is theirs. There is a saying, said over the cradle, that does not translate well because the word for rust and the word for belonging are close enough to be a pun.

That is the language, then: a thing built out of the structure the way the forest is. The maintenance crews have their dialect of valve-names, but the whole plateau speaks a version of it, thinner or thicker depending on how near the pipes they work. Direction is given by mains — *up-trunk*, *down-trunk*, *sunward of the return*. Time is told by the pump-houses, which cycle on a rhythm older than any clock, so that an appointment is *second cycle after the fog* or *when the sluices open*, and a long time is *a scrape* — the interval at which the moss is cleaned from a gauge face, once a year — and a very long time is *a rivet*, which is how long a rivet lasts, and no one is sure how long that is. Rust has eleven names, by grade and colour and whether it flakes or powders or holds. There are three words for the sound a strut makes when it goes: one for the note itself, one for the feeling of it in the feet, one for the pause afterward while everyone waits to see if there will be a second. There is a word for the colour of the country below, the brown-at-the-bottom-of-the-cloud, and it is used the way another people might use the word for heaven, or for the sea: something certainly there, of which nothing certain can be said.

They do not have a word for *ground* that means ground. They have the word, but it means the plate. Dig anywhere and you strike it, and that is *ground*, and the thing below — the air, the lattice, the white, the brown — is something else, and the something else has the names you would expect a people to give to what they will never touch. *Under*. *The long fall*. *Where the rain goes*. A child who asks what is under the plate is told *more plate*, and then, when older, *the decks*, and then, when old enough to have stood at the edge in the wind and looked, is told nothing, because by then they have seen it and know that there is no answer to be had, only a distance.

Which brings us to the dead, because the dead are where the two directions meet.

The soil came up in the pockets of the dead — that is how the story is told, and it is not a figure of speech. In the early time, when the beds were shallow and the plateau was iron with a skin of dirt on it, a body was worth its weight in earth, and the dead were laid in the beds with their pockets full and their mouths full and a sack of the best black tilth from wherever black tilth came from packed around them, and the forest grew out of them. It still does. There is no other place to put a body. The rivets, who live on iron, are carried in under the canopy when they die and given to the growers, who open a bed and lay the body under the roots of a fig or a fern and close it and mark the place with nothing, because the whole forest is the mark. Everyone on the plateau walks on their grandparents. Everyone eats them, in a way that is not said aloud but is not hidden either: the fruit tastes of iron and sulphur and something else, and the something else is the beds, and the beds are the dead, and the plateau-people know this the way they know the chord — so completely that it does not need thinking.

And the figs go down. This is the thing about the figs that a stranger would not understand and that no one on the plateau will explain because it is too obvious to explain. The figs are in the deepest beds — the oldest, the fullest of the dead — and their roots have gone down through the soil to the plate and found the seams and prised the tiles apart and gone through, and hang now in the air of the deck below, thick as cables, dripping, bearing fruit for birds that never come up. The figs are the only things on the plateau that have gone under. They are rooted in the dead and they reach down through the world's floor into the long fall and they *bear* there, in the white, for a country of eaters no one has seen. A plateau-person does not call this a symbol. They call the dead *rooted*, and they call the figs *the ones who went*, and when a bed-tender cuts back a root that is threatening a main — which must be done, or the main cracks — she does it with a small, wordless attention, and the cuttings are not burned but carried to the edge and dropped.

Things are dropped. It is the only ceremony they have that a stranger would recognise as one. Not the dead — the dead are too valuable — but the things that belong to the dead: a rivet-tool, an eye-cloth, a child's carved goat, a lock of hair. They are carried out along the girders to where the wind is and let go, and the plateau-person watches them fall into the white and does not lean out to see farther, because there is no farther. The rain goes down and does not come back. The cuttings go down. The fruit of the under-hanging figs is eaten by something that never rises. There is a traffic from up to down that has never once reversed, and the people up here have made their peace with it so long ago that it has become a courtesy: you send a little of what you have after what you have lost, and you do not ask where it went.

Now the stokers. I have been avoiding them, and I think that is right, because so does everyone.

The plateau is a body of pipes and somewhere at its core the boilers burn, and everyone knows the way to the stoking galleries by the sound, and no one alive has seen the boilers. Those are the facts, and they leave a gap the shape of a people. Someone feeds the fire. The coal comes from below — from the place no one has seen, at the bottom of the cloud — and rises by some engine older than the pump-houses, and is put into the fire by hands, and the hands are not the growers' and not the rivets'. There is ash on the gallery paths in the morning, in a fine grey drift the fog cannot wet. There are handprints in it sometimes, low on the wall, as if made by someone crouching. There are children on the plateau who have never met a stoker and adults who say they have — a face in a vent at dusk when the swifts pour out, pale, and gone; a voice under the chord, using valve-names no one else knows. There is a family in the inner quarters that everyone agrees is *of the galleries*, whose people go in young and are not seen on the surface again, and whose surface relatives leave food at a certain hatch and find the dish empty and will not discuss it.

I do not know whether the stokers are a people or a rumour, and the plateau does not know either, and I have come to think that this is the truest thing about the plateau-people: they are a middle. Above them there is nothing — the light, and the light is not a place. Below them, unseen, is whoever feeds the fire; below that, unseen, is whatever eats the figs; below that, a brown rumour. They stand on one unseen people and are warmed by another, and the country at the bottom of the cloud may, for all they know, look up through fourteen decks of white at a rumour of green and call it heaven. They do not think about this. They have the word for the brown, and it means what the word for *under* means, and they get on with the sluices.

Because that is what they are, finally, when I stop trying to divide them into rivets and growers and the ones who feed the fire. They are the plateau's lagging.

The builders wrapped the mains in asbestos and the asbestos rotted and the moss came and was better — thicker, wetter, alive, drinking the drip, holding the heat. Nobody planted the moss. It arrived, and the structure took it, and now the structure could not run without it. The people are like that. Whoever made the plateau did not make them; they came up in the sacks and the hoppers and the pockets of the dead, and they found the seams, and they have grown into the machine so completely that the machine has forgotten they were ever separate from it. They cut the fig roots before the roots crack a main. They scrape the gauge faces. They eat the edges to feed the centre. They do not repair the plateau; they are what the plateau grew in order to be kept. Their keel-chests are the shape of its air, their two faces are the shape of its two lights, their hands are its colour, their language is its parts-list, their dead are its soil, and their deepest belief — *what has stood will stand* — is not a hope but a description of what they have been doing, without cease, one wet bright roaring day at a time, for longer than the rivets last.

At night, when the pump-houses cycle down, you can see them sleeping. They sleep against the mains. Whole families, in the inner quarters, laid along a trunk-pipe the way the lizards lie along a girder, spine to the iron, because the iron is warm and the chord is in it and a child who wakes in the dark and cannot hear the chord will scream.
