The first answer is the plants, and it is not wrong, only thin.

They live here in the strict sense. They take in and build up. They are oriented; they respond; they hum. But stand among them long enough and the word *live* starts to slide off them. A thing that lives could be otherwise than it is. These cannot. They are the exact shape of the sound that made them — the roar written out in a vegetable script over a span of time nobody has a number for — and they are no more the resident of this room than a frost-fern on a window is the resident of the breath that made it. They are what the noise looks like after it has been going on long enough. They are a record. You do not say that a record lives in the archive.

The second answer is the engine, and this is better, because the engine is the only thing in the room that does anything. It turns. It draws and it gives. It has, in a manner of speaking, a pulse. But a pulse is not a tenant. The engine is running, and running is not the same as dwelling; a thing that dwells could stop and still be there. If the flywheel seized tomorrow the plants would stand exactly as they stand now, uneaten, unfallen, and the room would be quiet, and something would still be in it. You feel that. The engine is the loudest thing here and it is not the thing you are aware of being watched by.

The dust comes closest. Copper-red, fine as flour, laid down in rings. It is born off the flywheel and it becomes the garden and in between it lies on the floor holding the shape of the standing wave, so that the whole room is a diagram of itself. If the room has blood, this is it, in transit. But blood does not live in a body. It belongs to something.

So look at the threshold.

---

It is the one bare place. Everything beyond it is packed to the rock with growth, and everything before it is the ledge and the pewter sky, and the line between is a lip of stone worn smooth. Worn — not by hands, the description is right about that — but not evenly either. Grit breathed against a surface for ten thousand years would polish it; this is worn *in a shape*. There is a shallow dish in it, a little off centre, the size of something standing. Something has stood here. Something stands here, at the exact seam where the roar meets the open air, and has stood so long that the mountain has taken its print the way the rock outside took yours.

You will not see it. You know it the way you came to know the sound, when your ears gave up reporting noise and began reporting pressure: after a while, as a condition of the room rather than a thing in it. It has no body you could break a frond off. It has no heat; the engine is warm as a body and it is not the engine. What it has is the agreement.

Remember how the plants grew up to the housing on every side and stopped there, *exactly, as if they had agreed to.* Something agreed. The growth and the machine do not negotiate; there is a third party, and it is the one that holds the line. It is what is *between* the engine and the garden — the fact of their arrangement. It is the level of the note. It is the elevenness of the lamps. It is the reason the print does not fade.

---

Here is what it is, once you stop trying to see it and let it come up through the floor.

It is a keeper.

Not a guardian — it defends nothing, there is nothing here to defend against, and if you came up the ledge with a hammer it would not stop you. Not a gardener — it planted nothing and it tends nothing. What it does is *hold*. That is the whole of its nature. It holds the pitch of the roar where the pitch is. It holds the lamps at their glow. It holds the warmth in the column and the warmth in your palm after you have taken your palm away. It cannot let go of anything, and this is not a matter of will. It is a matter of constitution, the way it is the constitution of water to run downhill. There is no downhill in it. Whatever comes to rest in this room stays at rest.

And this — not the engine, not the dryness — is why nothing dies here. Death is a kind of letting go, a thing being released from its form back into circulation, and the keeper has no release in it. The fronds do not fall because falling would be a loss and the room does not lose. There is no mould because mould is the world reclaiming a thing, and the room reclaims nothing; it only keeps. It is also why nothing is born. Birth needs room, needs something cleared away to make space, and nothing is ever cleared away. The garden reached its size and stopped not because the engine's output ran out but because the keeper had, at that moment, exactly as much as it could hold, and from then on it simply held it.

It is the reason the rock is dry. You were told the water had been gone so long the stone forgot how to hold it. That is backwards. Something here holds so completely that the water, once, was taken in and never given back — is in the mountain still, somewhere below the pipes, kept. The dryness is not an absence. It is the outward face of a perfect retention.

---

It was here before the engine.

You can tell because the engine was *brought*. It has a maker's mark and a plate and bolts driven into bedrock; someone carried it up here in pieces, or built it here from pieces carried, and set it running, and left. And the question of why anyone would haul a machine to a dry room at the top of the world answers itself once you know what lives in the room. They found a place that holds. They found a place where nothing runs down and nothing wears out and nothing is ever let go of, and they put in it the one thing that must never be allowed to stop, and the room has kept it running ever since, the way it keeps everything. The engine is not the keeper's heart. The engine is the keeper's *charge* — the thing it was given.

What the engine is for, what the pipes carry down into the stone and what they bring back, is not written on the plate you can read. It might be on the one you cannot. That plate leans against the back wall with its face to the rock, and the growth has moulded itself around its edge so exactly that you know it has not been moved since before the plants were tall enough to notice it. Someone turned it inward. Either the maker, so that whatever was written would be read by the room and not by visitors, or the keeper itself, in the only act of choosing it has ever made, so that the instruction would face the thing it was addressed to. Both are the same, in the end. It is a letter to the mountain, and the mountain has it, and the mountain does not answer.

The keeper does not read. It does not need to. It holds the plate as it holds everything, and the holding is the reading.

---

The iron ring, the three links of chain, the single rung: it keeps those too, and it keeps them without knowing what they were for. It could not keep the people. That is the limit of it — it holds marks, not bodies; the impression and not the thing that made the impression. The bolt heads were worn to soft domes by a grip that is not here anymore, but the shape of the grip is here, and will be. The rung is one rung because someone meant to go up toward the light and got exactly that far, and whether they thought better of it or were simply *done*, the meaning-to is what remains, bolted to the rock, kept.

You have added to it. You did not intend to. The print on the ledge is yours and it is now in residence; if you came back in fifty years it would be there, exactly, and so would the clean powdery break where you snapped the frond — still fresh, still layered like slate, still not weathered by a single day, because weathering is a loss and the room does not lose. The warmth you laid on the column has been taken from you and will be held at the temperature of your hand for as long as the lamps burn, which is for as long as there is a keeper to hold them, which is as near to always as a mountain gets.

It does not want anything from you. Wanting is a shape of lack and it has no lack in it. If the room seems to watch, it is only this: that it is *attentive* in the way a held breath is attentive, all the way through, with nothing left over. It stands at the threshold, in the shallow dish it has worn there, at the seam between the roar and the sky, and it faces outward — toward the weather, and the streams, and everything far below that moves and eats and is let go of — and it keeps what it has been given, and it has never once put anything down.
