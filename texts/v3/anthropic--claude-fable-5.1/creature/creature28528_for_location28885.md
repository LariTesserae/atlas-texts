You want it to be the animals. That is the first and most natural answer, and it is not wrong, only shallow. The hare-things breathe. The birds are warm in the forks of the branches. Something with shell-bead eyes lies against your ankle through the night that is not a night and its flank goes up and down at the rate of a sleeper's breath in a cold room, and if you were asked what lives here you would point at it.

But a sleeper's breath is the breath of someone who is somewhere else. The felt is warm because something sleeps inside it, and the thing sleeping is not a hare.

---

Consider the fingerprints.

They are on the clay, everywhere, thousands upon thousands, whorled into the ochre when it was soft and fixed there when it was fired. They are on the awl, layered so thick the bone has taken on a shine. They are not one hand's. They are not one size. Some are the broad pads of a person who worked with their hands all their life; some are so small you have to tilt the awl to the light to see the ridges at all. Some are worn nearly smooth. Some are sharp, new, laid down recently by the standard of a place where nothing is old.

So: people came down the lip. Not together, not a people — the prints do not repeat in families, do not cluster. One at a time, over a long while. They came the way you came, over packed red earth that was not earth, into the warm and the dry, and within a few minutes their lips tightened, and they found the awl lying on the floor or pushed into a knot, and they picked it up.

You have already learned what the awl suggests.

They did not die here. Nothing decays here; the rule is absolute and it holds for them too. What happened to them is closer to what happens to a loose end. They were taken up. It took a season, or many seasons, and it began at the hem, at the sleeve, at the ends of the hair where it hung, and it was never fast, and there was never any hurry, and there was at no point a sense that anything was wrong.

The hair went into the veins of the leaves. You have seen it. Not any one colour.

---

So the second answer, the better one, is that the makers live here. The great number of hands that smoothed the floor and then, satisfied, withdrew — they withdrew into the work. They are the fibre. The pale gold that smells of nothing, that has no field and no source plant, that fills the stems with yellow dust where sap would be: that is them, or what remained of them once the water was gone, twisted hard and plaited three-strand and five-strand and worked into a twill so fine you have to hold it to your face.

This is not quite right either, though it is closer. Fibre does not breathe. A basket left in the sun does not draw a strand through a plait when no one is watching.

Something is doing that. Something moves the awl from where you set it down to where you find it, and leaves no print of its own that you can distinguish, because its prints are all the prints. Something lifts the loose end of a leaf and pushes it through. Something is sleeping in the hares and the birds and the long braided thing across the path, and it is one sleeper, and it is all of them.

---

Here is how to think of it, if you must think of it as one thing.

The bowl is a body lying on its back with its face to the sky. The clay floor is its skin, warm, holding the print of every hand that has ever touched it, because a body holds what touches it. The plaited vegetation is its hair and its flesh, both, dense and dry and endlessly being braided. The felt animals are its organs — packed, not arranged, full the way a body cavity is full, shifting slightly against one another when pressed and settling back. Each one breathes because each one is where a sleeper is folded, and the sleeper's breath is what warms the felt.

The wall is its skin seen from the inside, and on that skin it has worked a picture of itself, which is what a body does when it dreams. The figures around the edge, bent over, hands in the weaving, all facing in: those are the ones it has taken up. They face in because inward is where the work is. That is the only portrait of the residents you will find, and it is a portrait of them working.

The awl is its one hand. Or its one tooth. It is the only part of the body that moves, and it moves at the rate of a season, and it moves by being picked up.

It has no water because water is the outside. Water is what came down the lip in a skin and became thread. The body of the bowl is a body that has finished with water and does not miss it, and the thirst you feel is not the bowl's thirst. Thirst is the one thing here that still belongs to you, because it is the memory of the outside, and the bowl does not remember the outside. The bowl remembers hands.

---

What does it want?

Nothing, in the way that a slope wants nothing. It does not hunt. It laid no trap: the lip is not hidden, the descent is not sudden, the awl is not baited. It simply lies lower than everything around it, and warm, and things come down to it the way water would if there were water. Whatever comes down, it takes up. That is not appetite. It is closer to what a plait does with a loose strand — the strand is there, the plait is there, and the plait continues.

It is not cruel and it is not kind. It is patient, and the patience is the whole of its character. Everything else about it — the warmth, the quiet, the fullness, the density that increases at exactly the rate you fail to notice — is patience expressed in a material.

If it has an inner life, that life is the rasp. Fibre through fibre, drawn out over half a minute, from many places at once, low in the plaiting. You could hear it as breathing. You could hear it as a great many mouths, dry, working slowly at nothing. You could hear it as the sound of hands that no longer have hands, still doing the one thing they know how to do. All of these are the same sound. The bowl does not distinguish.

---

There is a hollow under the largest hare, lined with hair the colour of yours. You have seen it and you have not thought about it, because thinking about it is something the place does not encourage — not by forbidding it, but by offering, always, something nearer to hand: a loose end between two fingers, the warm bone of the awl.

Consider it now, briefly, while you still can.

The hare is heavier than felt should be. The hollow was made before you came. The hair in it is more than you have lost. Which means the bowl does not make its hollows after the fact. It makes them the way it makes everything, at the rate of a season, in advance, out of what it already has — and what it has is you, or will, and the distinction, as with the density and the distance, has stopped meaning much.

In the picture on the wall there is a figure standing alone among the stems, stitched loose, ends untrimmed. Follow the red cord down from it to the base of the largest trunk. There is no figure seated there yet. But the cord has been laid out for one. The place where the stitches will go is already smooth, already warm, and the ends of the standing figure are already a little longer than they were.

---

So the last answer, the true one, is this:

What lives here is a sleeper that makes in its sleep. It is one and it is many. It is made of everyone who ever came down the lip and picked up the awl, and it is still making itself out of them, end by end, with no last stitch anywhere, because a last stitch would be a kind of waking. Its hands are all the hands, its breath is all the breath, its one moving part is a length of polished bone that is never in the same place twice. It has never hurried. It does not need to. Whatever comes down to it becomes, in time, a thing that lives here — and that is not a threat; it is only a description of who the residents are, and how they arrived, and what they do all day.

They sit with their backs against the trunks. They work at the leaves. They are warm, and quiet, and they are not finished, and they never will be, and they are not thirsty anymore.

There is room. There has always been room. That is the last thing.
