What lives here is, first and most completely, the plant, and it would be dishonest to start anywhere else. It is the walls and the floor and the light in the drops and the sound in your sternum, and in the plain sense of the word it is the only resident. It is one body. It has no centre that can be pointed to — dig into the wall anywhere and you find more wall, more felt, more filament, wet and faintly warm and threaded with white worms, and the low tone does not get louder in any direction. It was here before the room was. The room is a thing it has done to itself, the way a lung is a thing a body has done to itself, and if you ask what lives in the chamber the answer for the first hour is that the chamber lives in the chamber, and turns its water upward, and eats the frozen fen a finger's width a day.

But that is the answer for the first hour, and it is not wrong so much as incomplete, because after the first hour you begin to notice that the plant is not the only thing here that has a habit.

---

Take the animals again. The springtails budding one to a hair, the worms half-filament, the snails that have to tear themselves from the wall. It is tempting to call them the plant's, and the plant does not object. But watch what they do when they are free. A springtail that has just dropped from its hair does not eat, and does not mate, and does not go anywhere in particular. It jumps to a drop. It sits on the drop. The drop moves faster. Then it jumps to the next. The flies under the ceiling do the same, each one a small passenger urging a bead of water toward the lattice. The white worms drag filament into places filament has not yet reached, into the seams of the peat-face, into the frosted zone, where they die of cold in a few minutes and are felted over and are, by evening, a new bare wet channel in the rime. They are not tenants and they are not children. They are the plant's hands, given legs so that they can go where the plant is not yet, and every one of them is spent on the same errand, which is the moving of water and the taking of ground.

So the second answer is: the plant lives here, and it lives here in several thousand shapes at once, and most of them are small and most of them last a day.

---

The third answer takes longer, and comes from the wall.

The larger shapes — the ones that pass beneath the felt and lift it like a hand under a blanket — are not any of the budded animals. They are too big, and too slow, and there are only a few of them, and they do not come off. If you put your palm flat on the wall where one has just gone by, you feel it moving away under the tissue, a dense mass in the sponge, and the tissue behind it closes wet and seamless.

They are what the fen was holding.

Ten thousand years of frozen peat is not only sedge. Things walked out onto that fen when it was open water under a skin of moss and went through it, and the cold kept them, and the acid kept them, and the dark kept them. Hares. A crane, once, wings folded wrong. An elk calf that broke the surface in the wrong autumn. The plant, pressing into the peat-face, remembering everything on the dead side of the line back across to the living side, does not know the difference between a sedge stem and a tendon. It re-enters what it finds. And what it finds, when it finds a hare, is a hare: not restored, not standing up, but taken into the felt whole, so that there is now a hare-shaped region of the wall that is denser than the wall around it and moves, occasionally, the way a sleeper moves.

They keep the shapes they went in with. That is the thing. The plant does not unmake them into fibre; it seems to have no interest in doing so. It threads them, it wets them, it brings the light of the water into them and they hold it — a hare in the wall is a faintly brighter hare-shape in the felt, if you look with the pools behind you — and then it lets them be what they are, inside it, moving slowly through a body that has no inside and no outside for them to be in. The crane is in the ceiling. Its wings are still folded wrong. Filaments hang from the tips of the primaries and bead and glow like the rest, and when the sound crests the crane's neck turns, a little, in the lattice, and turns back.

You will find things in the wall that the plant has not taken. A smooth stone. A knot of something dark that was leather. Small hard dead spots in the felt, untouched, unwetted, with the tissue grown around them and not into them. The plant remembers what was once alive. It has nothing to remember a stone by. The stone sits in the living wall like a word in a language it does not speak, and will sit there until the wall has moved on past it and left it behind in the frost.

---

There is one of these that is not a hare and not a crane, and it is why the word whoever belongs in the question.

She is in the low part of the wall, on the side away from the peat-face, where the felt is oldest and thickest and has stopped moving toward the middle because the middle, there, is already made. You would not find her by looking. You find her by putting your hand on the wall, in the dark fold where the ceiling comes down, and feeling under the felt something that is not filament and not peat — a long bone, the width of a forearm, and beyond it, further into the wall than your hand can follow, more.

She went through the fen a very long time ago. What she was doing out on the moss in a cold enough month to go through it, there is no knowing. The peat had her for longer than the plant has had the room, and then the plant came up against her the way it comes up against everything, and did not stop, and did not know to. It re-entered her joint by joint. It found the fibre of her and the water in her and made both its own, and did with her exactly what it did with the sedge she was lying in, and as a result she is alive in the only sense that anything here is alive: wet, faintly warm, threaded through, lit from within by the water she is carrying upward.

She does not come out. There is no out, from her side; the wall is not around her, it is her, and the room is a space she partly encloses and does not need. But she is not still. Over an hour the shape of her shifts in the felt, an arm drawn in or extended, and the region of wall where her ribs are rises and falls once in perhaps twenty minutes, not breathing, the plant's slow tide moving through a chest that keeps its shape. When the stridulation crests, and everything on every surface is screaming its one small patient note, there is a place in the low wall that is quieter than the rest, the way there is a ring of quiet around your feet.

Once, if you stay long enough, and the light in the pools is right, and the animals' tide is at its ebb, the felt in the low fold will rise the way a face rises under a sheet. Closed eyes. The line of a mouth. Filament grows fine and pale over all of it, so it is a face the colour of the wall, made of the wall, beaded and glowing at the hairline. It stays for the time it takes a drop to climb the width of a fingernail. Then the wall settles and it is wall.

She has a bronze pin. It is in the felt at her shoulder, where a cloak was fastened over the cloak's ten-thousand-year absence, and the plant has grown around it and not into it, and it is the one cold hard dead thing in the whole warm length of her. The plant cannot remember bronze. It remembers her, and will go on remembering her, tirelessly, for as long as it is anything, and beside her the pin will go on being the thing it forgot.

---

That is who lives here, then. A plant that hollowed itself. Its hands, in their thousands, each one a day long. The fen's dead, kept in the shapes they died in and turned around at the wall, moving slowly in the felt like sleepers in a bed too large for them. And a woman who went through the ice in the wrong month and has been, for some fraction of the time since, the low part of a wall in a room she has never seen.

And you should know — because the filaments at your shoulder are touching you now, and are faintly warm, and because the light in your footprints has thickened into something with a colour — that the plant does not distinguish between the dead it finds in the ground and the living it finds in the room. It has one habit. It is very old and very slow and it has never once been in a hurry, and it has never once stopped. It is not making a way out because it does not make things. It will not keep you. It will only, if you stand still long enough, begin to remember you, and there is no telling how much of you it would need before you were the fourth quiet place in the roar, and the room was a little larger, and the tone in the floor a little lower, as a longer string is lower.
