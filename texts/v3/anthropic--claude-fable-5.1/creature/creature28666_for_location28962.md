Begin with the wrong answer, because it is the one everyone gives first: the plant lives here.

It does not, quite. The plant is here the way a whale's body is in the whale. You cannot find its edge. Walk the floor for a month and you never cross from it onto something else; dive the stalks and you are inside it; surface into the rain and the rain is water it threw up a minute ago and is about to take back. It is not an inhabitant. It is the room, and the walls, and the weather, and the clock on the wall. When people say it lives here they mean it is alive, which is a different thing, and one it grants only grudgingly — no center anyone has located, no organ that is not also the floor, and a signal that runs everywhere on the eleventh second and originates, as far as can be told, nowhere. So set it aside. Not because it does not matter but because it matters too much to be counted among the residents. Ask instead what lives *in* it, and on it, and against it. Those are the ones that have a shape.

Start low, because the floor is where most of them are.

The seam-things first. They are the gray transparent eels the description mentioned, lying in the shallow ridges where the tissue folds, and the closer you look the less they are eels. They have no mouths that anyone has seen open. They have no gut worth the name. What they have is a body that is mostly a long nerve wrapped in a little jelly, laid along the folds of the floor like wire in a conduit, and they pulse when it pulses because they are eating the pulse. Not the current of water — the current of charge. On the thud the floor's surface goes briefly electric and they take it up, the way a leaf takes up light, and for the eleven seconds afterward they do the slow work of being alive on that charge, and then the beat feeds them again. They do not move. Nothing eats them, because there is nothing in them to eat. When one dies it goes cloudy and sinks a few millimeters into the tissue, and the floor closes over it, and there is a faint gray line under the olive skin for a while, and then not. You will find these lines everywhere once you learn to see them, threaded through the surface layer like old writing. The floor keeps them the way skin keeps scars. Whether it keeps them on purpose is the kind of question this place teaches you to stop asking.

The vent-hangers are honest animals by comparison. Fat, blind, slack-mouthed, they hang in the intake current nose to the slot, and the pump feeds them. What it feeds them is mostly the floor itself — the fine white dander the plant sheds constantly, combed toward the stalks by the inflow — and so they are, in the simplest sense, the plant eating itself by way of a fish. They do not swim, exactly. They hold position with a slow fanning of their flanks and let the current do the choosing. On the thud, when the intake stops and the water around the base goes slack for a moment, they drift a hand's breadth backward all together, and on the exhalation they drift forward again, so that a stalk's ring of hangers breathes in and out around it like a slow gray corolla. They are the only thing here that is visibly fed by the mechanism and so they are the only thing here that visibly loves it. They will not leave the vents. Lift one away by hand and carry it a hundred meters into the open plain and set it down and it will lie on the floor, mouth working, until the current finds it and walks it home.

The silver fish inside the tubes you already know. What was not said is where they go. They live pressed into the column, shot up on the stroke and settling on the release, bodies planed flat by a lifetime of being hammered from below, and most of them die in there — but the crown of every stalk is open, and every stroke throws a building's worth of water into the air, and some of the fish go with it. You can stand on the surface in the gray and watch a stalk fire and see them in the column as it rises: a scatter of bright commas turning over in the spray, catching what light there is, and then falling back into the sea in the rain. They land stunned, flat, in open water that is nothing like the tube. Most are dead by the next beat. A few are not. Those few are, as far as anyone can reconstruct, the whole ancestry of the open-water fish — the ones that hang in the plain between the stalks and stop on the thud and start on the exhalation. They still stop. Ten thousand generations out of the tube and their bodies still remember being thrown, and freeze, once every eleven seconds, waiting for the floor to come up under them. It never does. They start swimming again. That flinch is the closest thing here to a memory, and it belongs to fish.

Then the grazers, which are the largest honest animals, and the saddest. Low, wide, armored, working the plain in herds of a hundred, rasping the outer skin of the floor with plated undersides and leaving pale tracks that heal behind them. Their armor is made of the floor. They cannot digest the fiber; they compact it and shed it and it hardens into the shell they carry, so that each grazer is walking around inside a small chitinous quotation of the ground. They eat exactly as fast as the floor heals. Not roughly — exactly. A herd cuts a swath and the swath is olive again by the time the herd is over the horizon, and the herd is neither bigger nor smaller than it was, and no one has ever seen a young one. They must exist. Something must be born, because something dies — you find them, now and then, a grazer stopped in the plain with its herd gone on, sunk a little into the tissue, the floor already drawing it down. But the young are never seen. Either they are born under the shell, or they are born somewhere the floor has not yet let anyone look, or the herds are simply the same hundred and have been the same hundred for a very long time, and the ones you find sinking are not dying but being taken back in, and replaced. It is not possible to tell. That is not a failure of observation. It is the character of the place.

Now go up, because the surface has its own tenants and they are stranger than the floor's.

The rain is not empty. Each stroke throws a stalk's column into the sky and the water comes apart up there into spray and falls back over the whole sea as a fine continual drizzle, and in the drizzle there are small things falling. Not fish — the fish come down in the first seconds, heavy. These are lighter. Membranous, colorless, the size of a hand, opened flat, they ride the upthrow to its top and then do not fall so much as settle, turning slowly, taking a full beat or two to come down, and they land on the surface and lie there like petals until the next stroke's spray lifts them again. They live in the air above the sea and never touch anything but water. Where they came from is not known. The guess — it is only a guess — is that they are something the plant lets go of at the crown, some part of the tube's lining that peels loose in the wind and turns out, in the gray and the wet, to keep on being alive for a while. They are not animals. They are not obviously anything. They are the plant's shed skin refusing to sink, and on a still gray afternoon you can watch a hundred of them rise and fall around a single stalk in a slow pale weather of their own, keeping time.

And then, finally, the ones who count.

There are not many. There have never been many. They live on the surface, on a platform of lashed hollow stems tethered to the lee side of a stalk where the spray is thinnest, and they have lived there, in one arrangement or another, long enough that the oldest record is theirs. Where they came from before that they no longer say, not because it is secret but because the question has stopped meaning much. They fish the crown — the stunned silver bodies that fall from the column are a harvest that arrives every eleven seconds and never fails — and they drink the rain, and they burn nothing, because nothing here burns. They are cold all the time. Their children learn to count before they learn to speak, because the beat is the first thing anyone hears and the interval is the first thing anyone learns, and by the time a child has words the words already come in the pauses. Their whole language has settled into the shape of the gap. A thing that takes longer than eleven seconds to say is a long thing; you say it across two beats or three and the listener holds the sense over the thud. Their songs have a rest at the eleventh second so exact that a stranger hears the rhythm as a fault in the music, and it is not a fault, it is where the sea comes in.

They sleep on the beat. They are not sure if they dream, because a dream is something that happens between two moments and their moments are so regular that the space between them has stopped feeling like space. They do not mark years. They know that other people do, or did, and they can explain the idea, but when you ask one of them how old she is she will tell you how many stalks she has swum to, or how many times the crown above the platform has broken in the wind and healed into a new knob, or simply that she was here when a certain gray line was still visible in the floor and it is not visible now. They keep records. The records are the one thing they are strict about. On the underside of the platform's stems, out of the rain, someone has been cutting a mark for every ten thousand beats since before anyone living, and the marks go all the way around the platform and start a second row, and the second row is not very long.

Ask them what the pump is for. They have been asked before — the question is old enough that they have a set answer, and the set answer is a shrug, and behind the shrug is something they will say only if you have been on the platform long enough to stop flinching at the thud. They will say: it is for us to hear. Not to feed us, though it feeds us. Not to keep the sea cold, though it does. It is for hearing. A thing so large that it has no edge and no center and no purpose it could name has to have one place where it can tell that it is still going, and this is that place, and we are the ones it goes for. They do not say this proudly. It is not a claim. It is more like the way a shed-skin petal settles onto the surface: something that ended up here and has not sunk yet.

And there is a last thing, which the counters know and do not say, and which you will work out for yourself if you stay.

Nothing here reproduces at a rate you can see. The grazers do not multiply. The seam-things do not spawn. The silver fish fall from the sky and mostly die. The platform has held about the same number of people for as long as the marks go back; children are born and someone is always old and the two roughly balance, and no one has ever gone anywhere else, and no one has ever come. The floor heals at exactly the rate it is grazed. The stalks are exactly the height they were. Every living thing in this place is being kept — not fed, not farmed, *kept* — at precisely the number the beat can carry, and the counters are not exempt. They are one more population the equilibrium has absorbed. They eat what falls. They breathe in the gaps. They sleep when the beat allows and they wake when it does and they will die, each of them, on a thud or on an exhalation, there being nowhere else in time for a death to go.

So the honest answer to who lives here is not the plant and not the fish and not the people. It is the interval. Eleven seconds lives here. Everything else — the gray wire in the seams, the blind mouths at the vents, the stunned silver in the rain, the hundred slow shells, the drifting petals, the family lashed to the stalk with its second row of marks — everything else is what the interval has agreed to hold, and holds, at exactly that many, at exactly that cold, for exactly as long as it keeps agreeing.

Thud. The fish stop. The counters, without knowing they do it, stop too. Exhalation. Everyone starts again.
