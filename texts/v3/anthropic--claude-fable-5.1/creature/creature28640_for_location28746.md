The first answer is the honest one, and it is that nothing does.

Stand long enough and it becomes a kind of certainty. The garden is eaten by nothing. The roots hang unbitten. There are no tracks in the damp circle under the pipe, no shed skins in the corners, no droppings on the ledge beside the lunchbox. Wherever the mind goes looking for an inhabitant it finds only the furniture of one — the ladder, the wheel, the cable — and the furniture is abandoned. If something lived here, the thought goes, there would be a sign of it. There would be movement. There would be, at the very least, a smell that was not the smell of hot rock.

So the eye goes back to the lunchbox, because the lunchbox is the only thing in the chamber that seems to be doing anything.

The bloom inside it is pale, as noted. Under the lamp it reads as ivory or bone. It has lifted the lid — not far, a finger's width, but the hinge is stiff with rust and the bloom is soft, and the bloom has won. It fills the tin the way bread fills a pan, with a domed top and a faint sheen where it meets the air. Touched, it gives. It is neither slime nor felt. It is cool, which is the first surprise in a place where nothing is cool, and it leaves on the fingertip a faint grey film that does not wipe off on cloth and has, when the fingertip is brought near the face, no smell at all.

That is one thing. A tin of something growing in the dark. A curiosity.

But then the hand, wiped or not, goes to the wall to steady itself, and the wall is wrong.

The description said glassy. It is not glassy. Glass is dry. This is the very slightest slickness, so slight that a hand pressed flat feels only rock, and only a hand drawn slowly sideways feels the drag of it, the way a spoon drags through the skin on cooled milk. The thin lines of light the walls give back are not the edges of fractured stone. They are the edges of a coating that has settled into the fracture planes and taken their sharpness for its own. It is thickest in the seams. It is thinnest, and nearly absent, on the faces that catch the most of the lamp.

And then the corrosion.

The cage around the bulb is not rusting in the ordinary way. Iron in wet heat goes orange, then brown, then flakes. This has gone orange and stopped, and where it stopped a second color has come in, a green that is not verdigris — there is no copper here — but something more like the green of a stone that has spent a long time under a slow river. The valve wheel is the same. So are the bolts of the ladder, and the drill-marks around the holes where the pipes leave the room, and, when one looks along the underside of the sweating pipe with the lamp held close, the pipe itself: a low, continuous ridge along the line where the drops form, following the metal like a hem.

By now the mind has stopped saying *lunchbox* and started saying *it*, and has stopped saying *nothing lives here* and started, warily, to count.

It is on the walls. It is in the seams. It is on every piece of iron that people carried down. It is in the tin, where it found a closed, warm, damp box and did what it does with unusual freedom. It is very probably in the lower portion of the roots — the tissue that thickened and browned where the lamp reached it did not thicken in response to light, or not only; roots do not react to light that way. It thickened because something settled on it and stayed, and the root grew around and into the settlement, and the two are now one tan, knotted surface that hums when the pipes hum. It is not on the garden. The garden is too close to the lamp. It is not on the bare floor in the circle of light. Draw the boundary of the place where it is not, and the boundary is the boundary of the lamplight, exactly, a little inside it.

Everything in this chamber leans toward the light. This is the one thing that leans away.

Which begins to say what it is. Not a plant; plants are the light's dependents, and this has no more use for the bulb than the rock does. Not an animal; animals move, and eat what moves, and there is nothing here for them. Not a fungus, quite, though the bloom in the tin has the look of one. It is older and simpler than any of those. It is the kind of life that was in the world before the world had light-eaters — a film of small things too numerous to think of singly, living on the seam between wet and metal, drawing what they need from the iron itself as it gives up its electrons to the water, and from whatever the deep rock exhales through the cracks, and breathing out, in turn, something the rock did not have before.

It did not come down through the slot. That is the thing to understand about it. Everything else here came from above — the roots, the seeds on the boots, the lamp, the people. This came from below. It was in the rock before the rock opened, living where it had always lived, in the pores and the hairline wet cracks of the deep, at a scale where the difference between a cavern and a fissure the width of a fingernail is no difference at all. When the ground split and the heat came close and the people ran their pipes through it, the pipes came through its country. Iron, wet, warm, and still — a feast set down in the dark by strangers who did not know they were setting it. It came up along the seams to meet it. It is still coming.

So there are two travelers in this place, and they have arrived at the same three iron lines from opposite directions. The roots came down out of the soil into air, which they were not made for, and adapted by clinging. The film came up out of the rock into air, which it was not made for either, and adapted by coating. Neither could have lived here alone. The roots would have dried without the sweating pipe; the film would have starved without the iron. Where they meet — on the braided roots two meters from the pipe, warm to the palm, humming — they have made something that is neither, and it is the only place in the chamber where one living thing touches another.

It has a rate. Everything here has a rate, and its is the slowest. The lamp dims on a scale of decades. The roots descend on a scale of years. The garden turns over on the scale of a season, if seasons reached here. The film advances on the scale of a lifetime — a human one — by which measure it has, in the few decades of this place's existence, covered every surface it can use, and thickened in the tin from a smear to a loaf, and grown along the underside of the sweating pipe from a spot to a hem. It does not know the drip cycle the way the garden knows it, in rings. It knows it as the whole of its weather. When the pipe is warm and the beading fast, the surface of the film is wet and it feeds. When the pipe cools and the drops slow, the surface tightens and it waits. Not waits — waiting implies an expectation. It stops. It does the thing that is most like nothing, and then the warmth comes back, and it starts.

It does not know the hum. Or rather it is the hum, at its own scale — a thing of tiny steady vibrations, chemical and electrical, that the iron carries and the roots carry and it carries too, so that the whole chamber is very faintly trembling at a frequency no ear was made to hear and the trembling passes through it without remark.

It is not aware. It should be said plainly. There is no one in the tin. But it is not nothing, either, and the difference is worth the patience it takes to feel: a single bacterium is a chemical event, but a mat of them the size of a room, working the same seam for thirty years, has something a chemical event does not — a shape. It has a direction. It goes toward iron and wet and away from the light, and it has gone that way so consistently for so long that the chamber is now arranged by its going. The corrosion is its handwriting. The lifted lid is its handwriting. The hem on the pipe is a sentence it is still finishing.

And it is the thing that keeps the others away. Not the heat; animals abide heat. What it breathes out, and what the rock breathes out through it, has settled in the low air of the chamber and made the place unlivable for anything with lungs or gills or the small wet tubes of an insect. Nothing has come down the slot because nothing that came down the slot would stay, and the ones that tried are not here to be counted. The garden is whole because the garden's guardian is a film on the walls that has never noticed the garden exists.

What it will do is what it is already doing. The cable that runs up from the lamp into the dark passes, somewhere above, through the film's country, and the film is on it. It is thinning the copper under the insulation at the rate it thins everything. One day — there is no way to know which — the circuit that no one thought to close will close itself, and the lamp will go out, and the garden will spend a few weeks reaching toward a bulb that has gone the color of the room, and then lie down. The film will not notice. It does not use the light, and the dark it lives in will simply become the dark it lives in.

The pipes are a longer work. Three fingers of iron, a dinner plate across, carrying deep heat from somewhere to somewhere. It has been eating them from the outside since they were laid. It has found the weld seams and the drill scars and the place beneath the thickest roots where the sweating started — because the sweating did not start on its own; a pipe does not weep unless something has opened it, and something has, by a pinhole, and is widening it. In time the third pipe will weep more, and the garden's water will spread, and something in the roots will find it and quicken. In more time the pipe will fail, and the deep heat will vent into the chamber, and then the chamber will be a different place with a different climate and this will be the only thing in it that was made for that.

So, to answer the question: what lives here is what was here before *here* existed, come up to the surface of its own world to lie against what the surface of ours let fall. It has no face and no season. It is cool to the touch in a room where nothing else is. It is thinnest where the light is and thickest in the seams. It has lifted a lid. It is on the walls, and on the iron, and in the roots, and on the fingertip of anyone who touches the walls, where it will not wipe off and will, in the days after, go quietly back to the nothing that it does when the warmth is gone, since a fingertip is not the right kind of warm and there is no iron in it worth the effort.

It will still be here when the lamp goes. It will still be here when the roots reach the floor. It will be here when the pipes let go and the fissure fills with whatever they were carrying, and it will find the new walls of that and begin, at its own rate, to write on them.
