What lives here has had to come to terms with the noise, and that narrows it.

Begin with the ones already glimpsed — the ribbon-things in the foam. Get down close to a pool at the loud centre, where the water is coldest and the tin-taste strongest, and wait until your eyes stop expecting to see anything, and then there they are: six, eight, once in a while a dozen, hanging heads-down in a ring around a single seam. They are not swimming. The foam holds them. They are the length of a forearm and about as thick as two fingers at the widest point, tapering to nothing at the tail, and the tail moves, very slightly, in a way that keeps them upright without ever propelling them anywhere. They are the colour of the water because they are made of nearly the same stuff as the water — heavy with the same minerals, cold as it is cold. Put a hand in the pool near one and it does not flee. It does not know a hand is there. It knows only that the flow has changed, and it adjusts by a degree, and returns.

The mouth is a slot, lipless, running crossways where a face would be. It stays open. Through it the deposit goes — the thin white film that the water leaves as it runs, the same film that hardens elsewhere into collars. The ribbon-things eat it while it is still soft. This is the whole of their business. They eat the calendar. A column that has had a ring of them feeding at its base since it rose will show no collars at all, or only a few thin ones from the seasons when the ring moved on, and so the counting is wrong there; a column that looks new may be old, and the only way to know is to listen to how hard the water is still coming off it, and that takes a practised ear.

Their bodies vibrate. Lay a palm flat on the wet stone beside a pool where they are feeding and the vibration comes up through the rock, a hum inside the hiss, pitched lower than the water and steadier. Each column has its own note — the height, the thickness, the rate of shed all change it — and the ribbon-things at its base hum in some relation to that note, not the same, but not unrelated, the way a held string will start to speak when the right tone is sounded nearby. They have no ears. They may not need them. They may be nothing but a way for the noise to pass through flesh and back into stone. Whether that is feeding or singing is the wrong question; it is more likely that in them the two have never been different things.

Nobody has seen one born. Nobody has seen two together doing anything that looked like the making of a third. The rings are always of adults, if adult is the word, and always fewer than one would expect for a place so full of what they eat. The likeliest guess is also the strangest one: that they did not arrive. That they came up with the ground, sealed in the saturated bed as it rose, spilled out of the joints with the first water, and that there were only ever so many. Which would mean the valley is not their home but their remainder. Which would mean each time a column runs dry and gives its knock, something in the pool beneath it has come to the end of the only water it was ever going to have. Walk to a column that has gone quiet and look in the foam — it will be scummed and still, going grey — and sometimes there is a ribbon lying flat in it, stiff, the colour finally its own, which is no colour, which is the colour of the dead stone. They do not cross the gravel to another pool. They cannot. They were never in the business of going anywhere.

Then the people, who were, and mostly did.

There have been two kinds. The first kind stands on the rim and looks down, and the sight of a field of black grain in a rainstorm that is happening only there does something to them, and they spend an afternoon or a week fitting the fallen prisms back together — hexagon to hexagon, the way they must have sat before the ground pulled them apart — and then they go. The cairns are their signature and their apology. Nobody builds a cairn and stays. The building is what you do instead of staying.

The second kind is one person, or a few in succession who left the same kind of trace, and they came down.

The hut at the margin is theirs. Three stumps and a fourth laid across; a bed of collars broken small, which would have been noisy to lie on for the first nights and then, like everything here, would have stopped being noise. A bowl chipped from a column. Nothing from outside. That is the first thing to understand about whoever this was: not that they had nothing, but that they brought nothing, or brought things and did not use them, or used them up on the way in and did not miss them. Someone who found that what was here was sufficient. The marks deep in the loud centre — short diagonal strokes on the weeping faces, in groups — are theirs too, and those are the harder thing, because the centre hollows people. An hour in it and the roar has replaced the inside of your head. Whoever went in far enough to score those columns, and stayed long enough to score them in groups, and came back out to the hut and slept and went in again, was not hearing it the way you or I hear it.

The obvious thought is that they were deaf, or became so. It fits. A person who cannot hear would find the centre of the valley not a torment but a place where everything is finally trembling in a way they can feel — the stone under the feet, the water on the skin, the hum coming up from the pools. They would meet the ribbon-things on nearly equal terms. They would be the only one who could stand close enough for long enough to say what the vibration is, and they would have no way to tell anyone, and no one to tell.

The strokes are the trace of a count. Not of collars; the collars can be counted at leisure at the margin, and these strokes are cut where the water comes off hardest, where no one lingers. What is there to count at the centre that cannot be counted anywhere else? Only the knocks. Only the moments when one column out of tens of thousands gives up its last water and settles against its neighbour, and a small quiet opens. A deaf person would not hear the knock. They would feel it — a single hard pulse through the soles, unlike anything else in that continuous shudder — and turn toward where it came from, and go and find the column, and cut a stroke into the wet face beside it. And then wait for the next.

A lifetime per column, the rings say. The strokes are in groups of what looks like five or six, and there are perhaps eleven groups. Whether that is one person's stay or several, there is no knowing. There is no body in the hut and no grave on the rim. Either they walked out one day up through the leaning columns and the squeaking sand and past the cairns and did not come back, or they are somewhere in the centre where the foam has taken them into itself, become one more thing the colour of the water. The bowl argues for the second. Nobody who meant to leave chips a bowl.

There is a third resident, and it is the patient one, and it lives on the rim.

Where the spray stops — exactly where, a line you could draw with a stick — the dry fused pavement of the old fallen columns carries a crust. Black, matte, thinner than a fingernail, dull in a way that the wet stone never is. Lichen, or the thing here that does what lichen does. It grows nowhere the water reaches and everywhere the water has stopped reaching, and so it is a perfect record of the valley's edge: a black shoreline drawn around a dark lake of noise. It does not come in. It cannot; the mineral run-off kills it, the tin and the cold. It waits.

And it gets what it waits for. Each time a column at the margin runs dry and tilts and the spray-line pulls inward a hand's width, the crust follows by a hand's width. It is the only thing in the whole country that inherits. The ribbon-things die with their columns. The people build and leave, or stay and vanish. But the crust has been on the rim since the valley was three times its present size, and it will be there when the last column in the centre knocks and the roar stops and the whole floor goes grey and quiet and dry, and then the crust will spread over all of it, over the cairns, over the hut, over the eleven groups of strokes, and there will be nothing to say that this was ever a loud place.

So that is what lives here: a creature that eats time, a person who counted endings, and a skin that waits to cover everything. None of the three can live where either of the others does. The ribbon-things need the roar; the crust needs its absence; the person lived in the narrow margin between, where the columns lean and the hiss falls to a murmur, and went in and came out, in and out, until one time they did not.

But there is a way of seeing it in which the valley has a single inhabitant, and everything above is only its organs. Count the rings: it grows. Listen: it breathes, if a breath can take a hundred years. It carried a population up out of the dark with it and is now, pool by pool, letting them go. It drew one person in and kept them. It is being eaten at the edges by something that will not stop, and every so often, in the middle of the night that is only a colder kind of day, one part of it finishes and comes to rest, knock, and the quiet that opens is the same quiet that will eventually be all there is. The tens of thousands of columns are not a crowd. They are a single very slow thing, weeping itself dry, very loudly, and the things that live here are the things that have learned to keep it company at its own tempo, which is to say: the things that have learned to wait.
