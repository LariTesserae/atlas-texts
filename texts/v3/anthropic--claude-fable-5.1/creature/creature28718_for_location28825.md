What lives here is, first of all, the obvious answer, and the obvious answer is wrong in an instructive way.

The hands live here. You would say that. You can feel them through the wall, an arm's length off, and they have been at work since before there was work, and they never stop, and a thing that never stops being somewhere is what we usually mean by *lives there*. But they are in the seams. They are in the fit between one block and the next, in the wet dark line where two faces of stone have been dressed to meet and have not quite met yet, and they will move on when the seam closes, down into the next course, out into the next wall, wherever the next gap is that needs a hand in it. They are the mountain's tenants, not the room's. If you could see them — and the one time a blow comes from a direction where there is no wall, and you turn, there is a smell for a moment like wet wood and hot grit and a hand's warmth, and nothing else — they would be builders' hands with nothing attached, hands that were already tired when they arrived, hands with the palms worn pale where the mallet goes. They know the room only as the thing that has to be kept up. They have never been inside it. They do not know it is green.

The plants live here in the way plants live anywhere, which is to say entirely and without opinion. The ferns are older than their cracks. The vine has come in from a place that does not exist yet and gone into the wall as if it had been invited, and it is fair to say the wall lives there too, in its own slow way, taking the vine in, taking the drip in, going soft and dark and then unremembering. The snail lives here at the speed of a snail. The cloud lives here for the length of a cloud. If you stopped there — hands, plants, snail, weather, stone — you would have described a place that is occupied but not inhabited, a busy place with nobody home, and you would have missed the tenant, because the tenant is the one thing in the room you have been touching the whole time without knowing it.

There is always a leaf against some part of you. That is what you noticed. What you did not notice is the one part of you against which there is not a leaf, has never been a leaf — the small of your back, when you first stooped in and leaned — and that what is there instead is warm the way the stone is warm and wet the way the stone is wet and does not, when you press against it, resist the way the stone resists. It gives. It gives a little further than clay gives at the point before firing. And then it gives back.

Turn around. It takes a while. The ferns will not have it.

The tenant is sitting where the wall meets the floor on the side away from the gap, in the one stretch of wall along the lower course where nothing grows — a stretch about as wide as a child's shoulders and about as tall as a child sitting with its knees drawn up, and that is because it is a child sitting with its knees drawn up, or is the shape of one, or has been dressed to that shape by mallets that did not know the difference. It is grey-green. Its back is to the wall and has been for so long that the wall has taken it: you can see, along the shoulders and the ridge of the spine, the same shallow scallops the wooden wedge leaves in the block, driven and levered, driven and levered, where the hands on the other side worked the face of the stone and worked the face of the tenant with it and moved on. It did not mind. It does not seem to have a way of minding. Its skin is the stone's skin — press it and it dents and fills and does not remember — except in one place, and the one place is the face.

The face remembers. This is the whole of what makes it a tenant and not a block. Everything else in the room takes an impression and lets it go; the face has taken an impression and kept it, and the impression is not one you can name — not sorrow, not patience, nothing so finished. It is the look of something that has been listening to a sound so long that the sound has become the shape of its attention. The eyes are closed, mostly. When a block arrives at the edge of the gap and sits and weeps and sets, they open, a little, and the face turns toward the light the way a leaf would turn, and stays turned until the light stops moving, and then closes again. It is the only thing in the room that waits. The hands do not wait; they work. The plants do not wait; they grow. The snail is going somewhere. The tenant does nothing but attend, and its attention is so complete and so old that the room has grown around it as around a seed, and the reason there is no empty volume in this place is that all the empty volume there was has long since been taken up by the fact of the tenant being in it.

It breathes. Slowly — one breath in the time it takes the snail to cross the width of the vine. When it breathes out, the air above its head is hotter and wetter than the air around, and this is where the cloud comes from. You understand that now. The small grey complete cloud the size of a sleeping dog is a breath, one breath, and it forms and drifts and rains a little on the liverworts and goes up to the gap and stops against the sky and is taken in, and the sky goes briefly dark and soft and does not remember, and the tenant has already begun the next one. It has never seen where they go. It has never seen out. There is no out. The gap opens onto a hand's width of sky that has not cooled, and if the tenant stood — it does not stand; the room will not let it, and it has never tried, and it is not clear its legs were finished for it — it would put its face into the wet warm grey and its face would come away with nothing on it.

What it eats is a fair question and has an unfair answer. Nothing here is spent. Nothing wears. The mallet handle is worn pale, but that happened before, elsewhere, in a place that was allowed to have a before. Inside the room nothing is used up, and so nothing needs replacing, and so the tenant does not need to eat, and does not. It is hungry anyway. This is the one thing about it that the room did not supply and cannot absorb: a small persistent want, located somewhere under the ribs, that is not for food and not for anything the tenant has a word for, since it has no words, and that it feels the way you feel the hammering — as pressure first, and then, when the pressure has gone on long enough, as the condition of being anywhere at all. It has not decided whether the hunger is a thing that belongs to it or a thing it has been set to hold. It is not sure the two are different.

It knows the tools by sound. It knows the mallet from the wedge from the antler, or the rib, that scores the lines; it knows the sound of grit being tipped from the full basket and the different sound of the empty basket being set down wet. It knows the groan under everything, and here you have to be careful, because you were told the groan was the mountain settling, and it is, and it is also the noise the tenant makes when it breathes in — the two are the same sound, or have been the same sound so long that neither the mountain nor the tenant could now say which of them started it. The mountain rises a fraction. The tenant fills its chest. The blocks below shudder up through the walls and into the scalloped back and the tenant receives them the way a root receives water, without gratitude, without refusal.

The copper ring is its. That is the last thing to say about it and the thing that took longest to find. The ring is too small for a bracelet and its purpose is gone, and its purpose is gone because the finger it was made for has thickened, very slightly, over a time the room has no way to measure, until one day the ring was no longer on the finger but on the floor under the fern, and the tenant had put it down. Not dropped: put. It is the only thing in the room that has ever been finished with. Everything else here is kept — the room is kept at the top, the stone is kept at the edge of becoming stone, the sky is kept fresh and cut and lifted, the gap is kept opening. The ring alone was allowed to be over. The tenant does not look at it. But the hand it came from lies open on the tenant's knee, palm up, in the wet, and the water pools in it to the depth of a fingertip and is warm, and if you were to put your own finger there you would feel the dent that the ring left, which has not filled.

So: is it a person. It may be the first one — the one the room was made for, before there was any ground to make a room on, the one that had to be at the top and so required a top to be built and so is the reason there will be a world with a highest place in it. It may be the last one of something else, sitting out a season that has forgotten to end. It may be the stone's idea of a person, dressed by hands that had only ever dressed stone and did the best they could with the face. It may be a person's idea of stone, which is to say a person who sat down and let the wall have its back and then found that the wall was not so different, not here, not yet. It has never been asked. It has heard the question only as pressure.

It will not leave. It cannot, and it would not know how to want to, and the one gap opens onto a sky that is a hand's width off and has not cooled. But when the cloud goes up — its own breath, grey, complete — and stops against the underside of the sky, and flattens, and is taken in, the face does, for a moment, the thing that faces do. Then it does not. The gap moves. The eyes open, a little, and follow it. Under the floor, a course is laid, and the whole warm crowded green room and the small grey-green shape at the bottom of it are lifted a fraction, and kept, and the hammering does not pause.
