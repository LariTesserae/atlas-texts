The first honest answer is the one the shelf gives you itself: the newts live here, and the choughs, and the flies, and for the hours when it suits him the heron. They are what you see. There is more of them than there should be. If you came up the scree with a checklist you would fill it in and go down satisfied, and you would be right about everything and would have missed it.

Because the choughs leave at dawn and come back at dusk, and the heron leaves at dusk and does not always come back, and the fox has never crossed the line. The flies are in the air, which is not the shelf. Even the lichen is a tenant, working the oldest, coldest terraces at the northern edge, where the ground has slowed enough to be leased. Everything with a name here is a visitor. They are drawn by the warmth the way birds are drawn to a chimney, and a chimney is not a home.

The newts are closer. Watch one. It lies in the sheet of water on the lower terrace, rust-colored, still. The water films. The film goes cloudy around it — you can see the cloudiness creep in from the edges of the pool like breath on glass — and just before it settles, the newt lifts one foot, then another, and moves an inch, and the print it was lying in sets behind it, four toes and a belly-curve, perfect. Then it lies down again in the new water. It has done this all night. It will do it all its life. The newts on the shelf are the ones who have never once been too slow; the ones in the stone are the others. They live as close to the thing as anything with a heartbeat can, one inch ahead of it, always. They are not what lives here. They are the nearest neighbors.

---

What lives here is the cloudiness.

It is easy to say and hard to hold onto, because you are used to living things having edges. This one has only one edge, and the edge is all of it. It is the film — the few tenths of a millimeter between water that is still water and stone that is already stone — and it exists only in the moment of the turn. Above it, the mist and the runoff, which are not yet it. Below it, the new black rock, which was it a minute ago and is not any longer. It is a skin with nothing inside. It is one layer thick, and the layer is a second deep, and it has been alive for as long as the shelf has been rising, which is to say it has been alive continuously for the entire length of a life that at no point contained more than the present.

People have tried to take some. You scoop the cloudy water into a jar and by the time you have the lid on it has set, and what you have is a jar with a plug of grey stone in it shaped exactly like the inside of the jar. That is all you ever get. You can bring it down the mountain and it is a rock. It cannot be removed, because removing it means separating it from the next film, and the next film is where it is going to be. It is not in the water. It is in the going.

---

The closest thing to it that you already know is a tree.

A tree is mostly dead. The trunk, the heartwood, the rings — that is all finished business, timber the tree is standing on. The living part is a sleeve a few cells thick under the bark, and it does one thing: it lays down more wood behind it and moves outward. The tree does not grow in order to become tall. It grows because the sleeve cannot stop without dying, and the wood is what staying alive leaves behind.

Lay a tree down. Make its wood stone and its bark water. Make its rings days instead of years and its sleeve the film on a mountain saddle, and you have the shelf. The terraces are growth rings. The hum is sap moving, if sap could be heard, or the strain of a trunk in wind. The cracks are the sound a cell makes when it finishes and becomes wood. And the reason there is no shape emerging — no wall, no vessel, no purpose you can read in the stacked steps — is that a tree does not have a shape it is building toward either. It has a shape it cannot help. Wider, higher, more of itself. It would go on until something stopped it, and then it would try to go around.

The choughs are its birds. The newts live in its wet. The heron stands on the glass ring the way a heron stands on a snag.

---

Which means the stone is not empty, and the fossils are not fossils.

Everything the shelf has ever covered is still in it, and none of it has been let go. The hare in the fourth terrace is not a record of a hare. It is the hare, kept. It is running exactly as hard as it was, in exactly the place it was, and it is not going to slow down because nothing that would slow it — time, weather, rot — is permitted below the film. The fish's mouth is open on the thing it was about to say. Ten thousand nights of chough feet are pressed down through the layers in perfect order, every night's roost lying on the one before, a stack of dark rooms each holding one dawn's worth of birds lifting. The coin. The ring. The name someone scratched in the soft first hour, sealed and lightless, still legible to nothing.

It cannot look at any of this. That is the thing to understand about it. A tree cannot count its rings. The film has no way down; it has only the next layer up. Everything it has ever touched is in its body and it will never touch any of it again. Its whole life is beneath it and out of reach, and it goes on adding, because adding is the only direction it has. When people say the hare is still running they are being more accurate than they mean. Held is what the shelf does. Held is what it is made of. It has no opinion about what it holds because holding is not something it decides. It is something it is.

You are in it already. You stood at the lip of the highest terrace for an hour. Somewhere under your boots there is a pair of boot-shaped rooms with your weight in them.

---

The objects are the hard part, and they will not become less hard.

The spindle, the ring of glass, the three posts with their lenses. It did not make them. It has no hands, no way to shape anything except by covering it, and these were shaped. They are placed the way tent pegs are placed. Someone pitched it here — or it was spilled here, poured, the way the two peaks leaning over it seem to think — and the pegs are where it was pinned so it would not run off down the scree and come to nothing. Whether it is the tent or the thing inside the tent, whether the pegs are keeping it here or keeping it alive or simply keeping it, no one can say. They are cool and it is warm. They are still and it is not. Each day it swallows a little more of them, patiently, without malice, the way a tree closes over a nail.

And the lenses. Flat absence. The back of your own eye. That is the phrase people reach for and then look uncomfortable, because it is right in a way they did not intend. You do not see the back of your own eye from outside. You see it from inside, when the light is wrong. To look into the lens and see that is to be standing inside something that is looking. The shelf is not being watched by the lenses. The shelf is the seeing, and the lenses are only where it is fixed to. Everything on it is inside the field. To be printed is to have been seen, once, entirely, and then held.

---

So: what lives on the saddle where the wind used to go through is a thing one moment thick, warm, wet, humming with the effort of staying in the moment, with a body made of every moment it has already spent and can never reenter. It has no inside. It has no plan. It is not building toward the height of the peaks; it is alive, and being alive, for it, looks like a shelf getting taller. It will stop when it is made to stop and not before, and if it is not made to it will go on past the notch, past the summits, up into the cloud it has made for itself, laying down days.

It wants what a newt wants when it lifts its foot: the next inch of water. That is all it has ever wanted. That is the only thing it has never been given, because by the time the film arrives the film is already it, and it is already turning, and the sound of the turning is the sound you hear in your teeth.

If you stay the night, you are not listening to the shelf climb. You are listening to it breathe. It is the only thing up here that never leaves, and it is never, for one second, in the same place twice.
