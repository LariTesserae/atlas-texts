Start with what you can count, because there is a great deal of it and it is honest.

The frogs are small and the color of wet plaster, so that a niche with a frog in it looks like a niche with a swelling in its back wall until the swelling blinks. They sit with the hind legs tucked and the throat pressed to the sill, and the throat is the only pale thing about them, a bag of skin that fills and empties, fills and empties, and each emptying is one knock. You can see, if you watch a single one for a while, that the knock is not made at the mouth. It is made against the niche. The frog throws its sound at the curve of the back wall and the curve throws it out, and a frog on the floor with nothing behind it has nothing to throw at, and that is the whole of the law, or the mechanical half of it. Below the frogs, where the pool's line runs along the lowest course, the newts occupy niches that are half full of water, and they hang in it with only the head out, and they say nothing at all. They are the wall's silent register. The frogs above them sound; the newts beneath them soak.

The snails are the age of the wall in a way the frogs are not. A snail of that size has been in its niche for a long time — they grow a ring of shell a year, roughly, and some of these have twenty rings, and the shell is the exact grey-brown of the plaster because the shell is made of the plaster: they scrape the lime out of the joints and eat it and lay it down again around themselves. They have shelled themselves in wall. The beetles come and go under them, black, unhurried, and appear to eat what falls from the swallows' eave, and they are too small to have niches of their own and use the snails' when the snails are out. The toads are another matter. The toads have the corners — where the oval's curve tightens and the niches come closer together and the sound piles up — and they are old, heavy, the size of a loaf of bread like the loaf-sized stone on the floor, and they do not move. You watch one for the whole of the afternoon and it does not move. Its sides go in and out. The pulse you feel in your seat is that. There are perhaps a dozen of them and they are the bass, and they have been the bass for a long time; some of them have the dry, split, glazed look of things that are older than they should be.

The upper courses turn over twice a day. By daylight the bats are folded at the very back of the niches, where the smoothing ends and the rough mud begins, and the swifts sit in front of them, facing out, so that from below each niche shows a small sooty face and behind the face a darker huddle. At the change of light the swifts go out and up and do not come back — they roost on the wing, somewhere above the cloud — and the bats come forward and drop, and the niches are empty for the length of the bats' two circuits, and then the swallows land in them. The swallows do not use their own cups at night. The cups are for the young, and the young have been gone for a season, and the cups are stuffed with the moulted down of every year's brood back to when the eave began. So the swallows sleep in the swifts' niches and the swifts in the sky and the bats out over the flat country, and by first light every one of them is back where it started, and nothing has been decided, and nothing has had to be.

Now notice what you have not counted.

None of these animals is old. The toads perhaps, a few decades. The snails, twenty years. A frog, three or four. A swift, ten if lucky, a bat a little more, a swallow far less. Set that against the wall — a wall renewed so many times it has stopped being straight, whose plaster is a record of its own repair going back further than the plaster can show — and the arithmetic does what arithmetic does. Every one of the thousand niches has held its frog or toad or swift for a few years and then held the next. Hundreds of tenants, in each. And every tenant has made the same note, because the note is not the tenant's. The note is the niche's. The curve of the back wall, the width of the mouth, the depth to which it was smoothed and the depth to which it wasn't: those fix the sound, and a frog is the breath that happens to pass through it this season, and the breath will change and the sound will not. What lives in the niche is the note. The animal is how the note is kept warm.

Look, then, at the one nearest your feet that has drawn back too far to see.

You lean. It does not resolve. The smoothed part of the niche goes in as far as the light does and then the light stops and the niche does not, and there is the lip — the dark, wetted lip that every niche has, where the moisture that comes through the wall from behind gathers and swells and drips. It is behind the toad. It is behind the snail. Each niche has it, a wet dark at the back that is deeper than anything sitting in the front. Whatever has drawn back into this one has drawn back into that, and you understand after a while that every animal in the wall has that behind it, and that the thing you could not see is not one thing in one niche but the depth all the niches share. The wall is hollow behind its tenants. The hollowness is wet. It weeps.

And the weeping goes down.

Follow it. It comes off the lip of the niche as a bead. The bead falls onto the floor and joins the shine that is already on the flag, and the shine is coming up as well as down — up through the lime of the joints, which sweat it — and the whole of it runs, very slightly, toward the middle, and enters the pool, and the pool does not rise. Rain enters the pool and the pool does not rise. A thousand drips a minute from a thousand niches and the level stays at the band of black slime on the surrounding stone, a finger wide, exact. Something under the flags is taking it. You were told it was a drain, and you told yourself it was a drain, laid once by hand of fitted stone and silted since to the right width. But think about that silting. Silt does not know a width. Silt chokes a channel or it doesn't; it does not stop at the aperture that passes exactly what arrives and hold there through wet years and less wet years for longer than the plaster remembers. That is not what silt does. That is what a throat does.

So try it the other way. Try it as the country is trying to tell you, all that flat-made country, the rubble and the tamped clay for as far as the cloud lets you see. Nobody fills a land to the horizon to raise a wall the size of an orchard. They fill it to hold something down, or to hold something back, or to make sure that everything for a long way around drains toward one place and that the one place is the only one where the ground still gives. They found a wet spot that would not take fill. They packed the world around it until the world was a plate and the wet spot was its center, and then they walled the center, and then they floored the walled place with flags they pecked to fit, and the water came up through the joints anyway, because it was always going to, and they let it. They built a mouth over a spring and it has been drinking ever since.

That is what lives there. Not the frogs, though the frogs are its voice. Not the toads, though the toads are its pulse, or its pulse is theirs; on that point the evidence runs both ways. What lives there is under the flags, and comes up through them as a shine, and is taken back down through a channel no one dug, and shows at the surface as a pool the size of a cart bed with nothing in it — nothing, not spawn, not larvae, though the frogs cross to it every night and squat at its edge and do what they came to do and go back, and in the morning there is nothing in the water. Whatever goes into the pool goes down. It is the one place in the oval that receives and does not show receiving.

And it listens. That is what the shape is for. A dished floor and an oval wall throw every sound to the center, and at the center is the one surface in the world that has never made a sound, still enough to be mistaken for a hole in the floor, and every knock and scream and hiss from a thousand niches arrives at it and goes in. Once a day, at the change of light, the arriving becomes too much or becomes enough, and the frogs seize up into their single pulse — one sound, off the wall, back off the far wall — and the rings run over the pool from the outside inward. Inward. Rings on water go out from where the stone falls. These go in, toward the center, and the center takes them, and your teeth hurt, and then the frogs remember they are separate and the pulse breaks into clatter and the mist comes up off the water like a breath let out.

The heron knows. It stands out on the tamped clay in its one shallow mirror where nothing lives and it waits, because a heron waits where frogs are, and there are more frogs here than anywhere in the country it has flown over. It has waited a long time. No frog has ever come out over the wall. No frog ever will. The heron looks in and you look in and neither of you goes down, and the difference between you is that the heron has understood why, and has stayed anyway, and you have not yet, and will.

The place does not need you. But it has a use for you, which is not the same thing. You are the loudest thing that has come to the top of the wall in a long while — you breathe, your clothes drip, your heart is audible to a toad — and the niches nearest you have gone quiet the way an instrument goes quiet when a hand is near it, and the sound has thickened everywhere else to make up the loss, and down in the center the water is holding its level, and holding, and drinking, and hearing you.
