Begin at the floor, because everything in the room ends up there.

The floor is alive the way a compost heap is alive — not with anything you would point to, but with a slow constant working. Put your palm flat on it and after a while you feel it: a faint dry ticking, like a watch under a blanket. That is the beetles. They are the color of the soil they make, dull black with a bloom of dust, flat enough to slide between packed layers, and there are more of them than there are of anything else in the room including the swallows. They do not hurry. A bat dropping falls from the ceiling and lands on the floor and within an hour it has been found, and within a day it has been taken apart and carried down, and within a season the place where it landed is indistinguishable from the rest. The floor is always sinking, a hair's breadth at a time, under the weight of what falls on it, and always rising, a hair's breadth at a time, because of what the beetles do with it. It is the same height it has been for as long as anyone has had a reason to notice.

There is a thing that eats the beetles. You see it only at night, and only for a moment, and only if you are already looking at the right place: a pale shape the size of two fists, with a nose like a soft wet finger and no eyes worth speaking of, that comes up out of the soil, takes something, and goes back down. Its fur is the fine grey of old ash. Its forefeet are broad and hard-nailed and turned outward. It does not know the room has walls or a ceiling or light; it knows the floor, and the floor is enough. The children catch them sometimes by lying still on their bellies in the dark with a hand cupped over a fresh beetle-hole, and they are the softest thing in the room, and the children hold them for a while and then let them go, because they do not taste of anything and because there is a feeling, never said, that they are the room's own.

The succulents grow up out of this floor like things that have decided to be furniture. They are knee-high, few of them higher, with stems as thick as a wrist and leaves like fat grey tongues. The skin of the leaves is tough and bloomed with a wax you can write in with a fingernail, and the writing stays for days. When a leaf is broken it bleeds a gel that is clear and cold and faintly sweet, and everything in the room knows the smell of it. The first to come are the wasps. Then the copper lizards. Then, if it is the right hour, a child with a shell. The plant does not seem to mind. It has been bleeding for whatever wants it since before there was a wall to hang a nest on, and it puts the loss back the way it puts everything back — slowly, out of the air, out of the one damp hour, out of the floor.

The swallows are the room's weather.

Up close, one at a time, they are small and neat and unremarkable: dark blue-black on the back with a dusting of rust at the throat, a pale rump, wings too long for the body, a bill so short it is hardly a bill. Their eyes are hard black beads with nothing in them you could call expression. Their feet are almost useless; they cannot walk, only cling. In the hand — and the people do take them in the hand, without any fuss, to look at a bad wing or to lift one out of a slumped nest — they weigh nothing and their hearts go so fast the beating is a hum.

There are perhaps ten thousand of them. No one has counted. The number rises and falls with a rhythm nobody in the room lives long enough to see the whole of; the people say only that there are years of many and years of few, and that in the years of few the snakes are thin and the children are quiet.

The swallows go up through the seam at first light in a rope of bodies and spread out over the plateau, where there is, to look at, nothing. But there is not nothing. In the cracks between the slabs live small hard-shelled things that come out in the first warmth; in the grass that grows knee-high in the shelter of the rubble, small hard seeds ripen and drop; in the air over the plateau, when the sun has heated the stone, insects rise on the thermals in numbers that would astonish anyone standing on the ground, because from the ground they are invisible. The swallows work this invisible harvest all day and bring it back in their throats, and the hard black eyes and the bills full of the plateau's hidden life are the reason there is a room at all.

Their nests are the room's architecture. A new one is a beautiful thing: a smooth ochre bulb the size of a cupped pair of hands, narrowing to a round mouth just wide enough for a swallow's shoulders, pressed against the wall and against its neighbors so tightly that the whole tier looks like one thing, like a swelling of the stone itself. The pellets are laid in courses you can see if you look closely, each one a beakful, each one pressed in with a small sideways shake of the head, dozens a day, until it is done. An old one is a different thing. The mouth crumbles, the bulb cracks, and then something else is living in it — a solitary wasp with a barred abdomen who fills the hollow with grubs and seals it with a plug; a copper lizard, who lies in the mouth in the afternoon with only its head out; a spider whose web across the opening catches the dust that falls from the tier above and grows grey and heavy and then is abandoned; and finally nothing, and the nest is just a bump on the wall with a dark eye in it, and a swallow comes and builds on top of it.

They defend the nests without method. Anything that comes near the tier gets hit — a soft, glancing, inaccurate blow, a body thrown and a flutter away, and then another, and another, from every direction, with the tearing-cloth noise going up all around. The man taking eggs on the high ledge does not flinch because he has been hit like this every egg season of his life, and his father was, and the swallows have been throwing themselves at people for so many generations that the throwing has become a kind of formality, like a dog that barks at the mail. The people take one egg in six, from six different nests, never two from one, and they will tell you the reason if you ask — a nest with one egg gone still fledges the rest, a nest with two gone is often abandoned — and the reason sounds like something worked out once, long ago, by someone who had to eat, and the rule has outlived every memory of the working-out. Now it is just what you do. The child who takes two eggs from one nest is not punished. She is looked at, once, by whoever is nearest, and she puts one back.

The people live in the swallows' shadow, in every sense.

There are perhaps forty of them at any time. There have been more; there have been fewer. They are small — the doorways tell you that, and so do the ladders, whose rungs are set at intervals that would make a tall person clumsy — and narrow, with long hands and long feet and toes that spread and grip, so that watching one of them go along a ledge is like watching a lizard, or a swallow's cousin that learned to walk. Their skin is the color of the wall. Not by birth: by dust. Dust in the pores, dust in the creases of the palms, dust worked into the hair until it stands stiff and grey-ochre and the parting shows only a slightly darker line. A person sitting still against the wall of the room is hard to see, and they sit still a great deal. Their eyes are large and dark and the pupils are very wide, and when they go up through the seam onto the plateau in daylight they go squinting and weeping, with a fold of fiber cloth held across the face, and they do not stay up long.

They speak, but not the way people speak in open places. The room is never quiet, and the pitch of the swallows fills the range where a voice would naturally sit, so the people have dropped beneath it. They talk in a low chest register, almost a hum, with a lot of the meaning carried in the hands. Across the room, from ledge to ledge, they do not shout; they wait until the other person looks, and then the hands say it. A stranger would think them silent. They are not. In the doorway of a room at night, four of them in a knot under one hide, the sound of them talking is like the sound of the wasps in the last of the afternoon, and it goes on a long time.

They are all, in some degree, kin. A child knows who its mother is and roughly who its father is and precisely which room it may sleep in, and those are the important things. Everyone knows everyone's hands. This is not a figure of speech. Every wall in the human tier has been patched and re-patched with palm-pressed pellets, and the pellets carry the palm: the breadth of it, the depth of the pressing, whether the fingers were spread or closed, a habit of smoothing the seam with the side of the thumb. The people can look at a wall and tell you who repaired it and roughly when — that one is the old woman on the third ledge, that one is her daughter's son, that one, low down and half-buried in swallow-cement, is a hand nobody living has seen. Sometimes a child lays her palm into one of these old marks and finds that it fits.

They eat what the room gives, in the order the room gives it. First and always the gel and the pulp of the succulents, which they cut in the morning and pack into gourds — not gourds, there are no gourds, but the hard rinds of the succulents' own fruit, hollowed and dried — where it goes sour in a few days and stays sour for a season. This is what they drink. A person here has never in their life drunk a mouthful of clear water and would not know what to make of it. Then eggs, in the egg season, which is twice a year for about a moon each. Then grubs: wasp grubs from lanterns that have fallen or gone quiet, beetle grubs from the floor, fat and pale and tasting of the floor. Then lizards, the blunt kind, which are caught by hand in the heat of the day when they are too warm to bother running, and killed with a twist, and split and dried on the racks in a day. Then, most nights, bats — not caught, but fallen, dropped by the thing at the seam and collected by children with baskets who go out at the height of the column and stand under it with their arms up and their eyes shut. Then seed, which they gather on the plateau in the cold hour before dawn, in the shelter of the rubble, working fast with their eyes streaming, and grind on the stone with the bowl in it, and eat as a paste with gel. Then, rarely, something larger: a snake, if one dies; a hawk that came down through the seam by mistake and beat itself against the walls until it fell; once, in a story everyone knows, a thing with four legs and hooves that fell into the room from the plateau and broke, and was eaten for a moon, and whose bones are the frame of the oldest drying rack.

Their tools are black glass, bone, fiber, and dung. The glass they find in the plateau's rubble, dark and shining and sharp when it breaks, and they knap it into edges the length of a finger and set the edges in bone handles wrapped with sinew. The fiber comes from the beard-plant, which hangs from every crack above them in ropes they can reach by climbing, and which, teased and twisted, makes a cord that will hold a body, and, woven loosely, makes the cloths they wring the damp hour out of. The dung is fuel. Their fires are the size of a cupped hand and they light perhaps a dozen of them in an evening and none of them lasts, and the smoke goes up and joins the smell of everything else and blackens the stone above the doorways one breath at a time.

They do not, as far as anyone can tell, worship anything. They watch. They have the flat interested patience of the lizards and the snakes and everything else that lives in a room where nothing needs to be decided quickly. When the blade of light touches the eastern wall they note where it falls, because that is the day's clock and everything is arranged around it — a child born when the blade is on the floor is named for the floor, one born when it is on the far wall is named for the far wall, one born in the dark is named for the bats — and beyond that they do not seem to require an explanation of anything. The room is old. They are in it. The floor is their grandmothers. The sun rises in the east.

Above the people, the wasps, who are the least concerned of anyone.

The lanterns are grey, layered, papery, and warm to the touch. Some are as small as a fist; a few of the oldest, under the deepest overhangs, are as big as a person's torso and hang in tiers like a stack of upturned bowls. The paper is made of chewed stem and chewed beard-plant and chewed anything, worked with spit, and it has the fine dull texture of ash. Inside, the combs are hexagonal and crowded and the grubs in them are white and blind and always eating, and the wasps go in and out of the round door at the bottom all day in a stream that thickens and thins with the sun.

They are not large — the length of a fingertip — and they are the color of the wall, striped in a way that reads as a kind of ochre at a distance and as two colors only when one is on your hand. They do sting. The people are stung several times a day and have been all their lives, and their bodies have stopped responding; a sting is a small hot point that goes away. Children are stung more and cry, and are not much comforted, and stop crying, and by the time they are grown they have joined the rest in the slow careful way of moving among the lanterns that the wasps seem to accept: no fast movements, no shadows thrown across a door, no breath directed at the paper. A person going along a ledge under a lantern walks like someone carrying something full.

When a lantern falls — and they do fall, when the overhang it hangs from finally lets go, or when a snake going along above it dislodges the stem — it is an event. The wasps pour out and hang in a cloud over the wreck for most of a day and then, having nowhere to go, disperse into the other lanterns or die. By evening the people are on it, splitting the paper, picking the grubs out of the comb by the hundred, dropping them into rinds. The paper itself they keep. It burns, but they mostly use it for wrapping: a fold of grey wasp-paper around a lump of gel keeps it a day longer; a stack of it under a sleeping child keeps the cold of the ledge off. There is a whole language in the room of things kept in other things' leavings.
