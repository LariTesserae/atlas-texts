Start with the verb, because here it is the unstable part.

Nothing in this place eats. Nothing drinks; there is nothing to drink. Nothing is born in the sense of arriving new, and nothing dies in the sense of leaving. What everything does, without exception, is take in light and keep it. If that is what living means, the whole plain lives, floor to ceiling, and the question is answered before it is asked. If living means something else — wanting, noticing, coming and going — then the answer is much smaller, and you have to go looking for it in the gaps between stalks.

Go looking. Start with the ones that can be seen.

—

The couplers, first, since they are the easiest.

They are the size of a spread hand and no thicker than a thumb. Flat, pale, matte, ribbed like everything else, with twelve or fourteen legs that are not legs so much as stiff articulated fingers arranged around the rim. They do not walk. They surge: the hydraulic pulse that runs through the stalks runs through them too, and each pulse carries one a hand-span along the channel-rail and then leaves it resting, and a minute later carries it another. Watching one cross a hundred meters is like watching a drum turn. You look away and it has moved and you cannot say when.

At each socket it stops. It lowers itself over the coupling and does a thing that is very nearly nothing: a quarter turn, a testing of the thread against its own underside, which is threaded too, in a pattern that is a negative of every good socket on the plain. If the thread holds, it lifts and goes on. If the thread has failed — a coupling cracked, a stalk gone cloudy and pulled loose in a front — it does not go on. It settles. It turns until it seats. Over the next several cycles of the gantries it thickens along its axis, and the fingers lengthen and stiffen and go down into the groove, and a rib forms, and another, and by the time you could be sure something had changed there is a young stalk there, two meters tall and paper-white, with a faint bloom on it, and nothing on the rail at all.

So the couplers are how the crop walks to where it is needed. They are not animals that tend the plants; they are the plants, in transit. Nobody has seen one begin. The assumption — nobody's assumption, the plain's — is that they start as ribbon: a fold in the fibre at a slit, a snag that drops before the gantry can take it, and lands, and lies on the mat for a season, and one day is standing on its fingers. This would make them failures that succeeded. It would also mean there are never more than there need to be. You never see two together. You see one every few kilometers, and it is exactly where it should be, and it is the only thing on the plain that is going somewhere.

They are indifferent to you until you stand in a channel. Then one will stop, a few meters off, and wait. It is not patience; patience is a virtue and this is a property. But if you stand long enough — and long enough here is not measured in hours — the pulse that moves it will slow to match the pulse of the stalks around you, and it will come the rest of the way, and it will lower itself very gently over your ankle, and try you for thread.

—

The quickest thing on the plain lives in the gaps, and it is not clear that *thing* is right.

When two stalks brush in a front, a spark crosses. Blue, soundless, gone. In the open rows this is all there is: a flicker at the corner of the eye, a lift of the hair. But in the dense zones near the horizon, where the stalks stand close enough that the gaps are a finger wide, sparks chain. One gap fires the next. A line of blue runs down a row for two hundred meters in less time than it takes to turn your head, and then there is the dark hum of the after-image and nothing else, and the front has moved on.

You can stand at the end of a dense row and watch for them, and after enough fronts you notice that the chains do not run at random. They favor certain rows. They start from certain gaps. They arrive at certain stalks and stop there, as if they had been going there. This might be an artifact of the static — the couplings, the oil, the way charge settles in the mat. It might not.

If something lives in the sparks, it lives for the width of a gap. Its whole life is the crossing. It would perceive the plain as a single flash, ceiling and floor and stalks compressed into one bright instant with no before and no after, which is — you realize, standing there with your sleeves crackling — not so different from how the plain perceives you. Everything here is slow. Speed, here, looks like being briefly and then not. Perhaps that is all the sparks are: the only inhabitants who took the other bargain. Perhaps the plain needs one fast thing, the way it needs one spare coupler, and this is what it grew.

—

Now the felt.

Under the oldest stalks the shadow has thickened until it is ankle-deep, dark, soft, dry, with the nap of a good coat. It is warm — the only warmth on the plain, and a strange one, the low warmth of light that has been taken in and cannot get out. Things live in it. This has already been said. What has not been said is what they do, and the answer is that they comb.

Where one has passed, the nap lies flat and all in one direction. The direction is always the same: toward the bright horizon, the way the shadows themselves point. So they are tending the dark the way the couplers tend the light. The light is fixed into stalk and stalk into ribbon and ribbon goes to the horizon; the shadow is fixed into felt and the felt is combed to lie toward the horizon; and if you could see the plain from far enough above — from the ceiling, say — you would see two crops, one pale and one dark, both leaning the same way.

You want to know what they look like. The honest answer is that they look like the movement of dark against dark, a low displacement in the nap that is there and then is somewhere else. But here is a thing you can do. Find a coupler crossing a shadow-zone, which is rare but happens, and watch its shadow. At first the shadow follows it, a hand-shaped dimness on the felt. Then it lags. The coupler surges and the shadow does not quite. The coupler goes around a stalk and is gone, and the shadow is still there, a moment longer than it should be — a thickening in the nap the size and shape of a spread hand, very slightly raised — and then it is gone too, but not in the direction the coupler went.

So the combers may be shadows that came loose. The felt may be full of hands. When you think of it that way the combing makes a different kind of sense, and it is harder to walk through the shadow-zones without lifting your feet.

Hold that. It is going to become more itself in a moment.

—

The reels that were left too long: something lives in those, or something recurs, and on this plain the difference is thin.

A reel wound near a warp has wound in distance, and a reel left long enough has wound in time — a few hours, kept, the way the stalks keep light. You set your hand on the fibre and your hand is cool and it is earlier. What was said before is that the hours are not a record. That needs to be sharpened. They are not a record because a record is of something and these are the something. The gantry-arm that lowered while the reel was being wound is still lowering, in the ribbon, a little way in. The front that passed is still passing. If you keep your hand there long enough for the earlier to settle around you, you can hear it: the gantry when it was quicker, a tick to it that it has since lost.

And, sometimes, something else. Not resin. Not powder. A warmth that is not the felt's warmth, and a smell that has no source on the plain, and a sense — nothing more than that — of a place where the air was recently displaced by something about your size. Someone stood here when this was wound. Someone came to this reel and did whatever was done with reels, and left, and the ribbon kept the standing.

They are not in the reel. The time they stood in is. But set your hand on enough reels, and you begin to know them by the way the earlier feels — the height of the displacement, the side they favored, the fact that on the oldest reels there is more than one of them, and on the newer only one, and on the newest none. They are the nearest thing the plain has to a neighbor. They are also, in the strict sense, a resident: something that is always here, at a particular address, doing a particular thing. It is just that the address is a wound hour and the thing is having stood.

—

Which brings you to the walkers.

Among the rows, now and then, there is a stalk that is not quite right. It stands a little off its rail. Its ribs run at a slightly different pitch, or there are more of them, or fewer. It is shorter than its neighbors by a third and where its slits should be there is instead a swelling, high up, about the height of a raised hand. The powder on it is thicker. The gantries accept its ribbon — it is good ribbon, with a little more give than the standard — but the tined arms take longer over it, as though checking twice.

These are the ones who stood still.

It is not violent and it is not fast. The powder on your sleeves that will not brush off because it is not on your sleeves; that is the first of it, and it has already begun. The mat is cork-soft and it accepts a footprint and then, if the footprint stays, it accepts the foot. There is always a socket. The rail is under everything, and if you stand in one place long enough the groove finds you and the thread learns your shape and you learn its. The oil comes up. It is cool going in. Nothing about it is unpleasant, which is the part that would be frightening if you were still the kind of thing that was frightened, and by then you mostly are not; you are turgid, you are taking the light, you are producing ribbon of a slightly different quality, and the plain has one more column in it and one fewer walker, and nothing has been lost.

Look at their shadows.

Under an ordinary stalk the felt lies combed toward the horizon. Under a walker-stalk it does not. It wanders. It lies this way and that, in loops, in long lines that go out between the rows for a hundred meters and come back. Watch it — over a long afternoon, if there were afternoons — and it moves. Not toward the horizon. Anywhere. It is still walking.

So: the combers. Some of them are couplers' shadows come loose, and those comb true, and those are how the dark gets to where it is going. And some of them are this. The felt is full of hands, and it is also full of feet. And the thing you cannot describe, moving dark against dark under the oldest stalks, is in at least some cases a person, or the part of a person that the light did not want, going about the plain in the only body left to it, at the only pace there is. They do not seem unhappy. It is not clear they seem anything. But where one has passed, the felt is not combed flat; it is trodden, faintly, the way a path is, and the paths cross and recross, and some of the oldest of them run all the way to the bright horizon and stop at the wall.

—

The wall, then, and what is behind it. And above it. Since they are the same.

Approach the horizon and the stalks close ranks until you cannot pass, and then they merge, and then there is the wall: pale, ribbed, curving very slightly away from you on both sides, warm to the touch — warmer than the felt — and when you set your palm flat on it you are not earlier by hours but by an amount there is no word for on this side. This is where the ribbon went. All of it. Every reel that trundled off along the rails toward the brighter light arrived here and was taken in and became this, and the cylinder is bright inside because it is made of every hour the plain has ever had, wound tight and still going.

Whoever received the ribbon is in there. Not as a body; bodies do not keep. The ones who stood at the reels, who came and did whatever was done and left, and were fewer each time — they went in. Or the receiving did. What lives in the cylinder, if the reels are anything to go by, is the act of receipt itself, stripped of the receiver and kept, the way the walker-stalks keep standing and the sparks keep crossing. Something in there is still taking delivery. It has been taking delivery for a length of time that only it could measure, and it has never once refused a reel, and it is not finished.

And the cylinder is very large, and it curves, and if you follow the curve in your mind it goes up and over and comes around, and the underside of that coming-around is seamed with faint grey lines and shines with a white light that has a green undertone and does not move. The ceiling is the far side of the cylinder. The light that grows the stalks is the light the stalks grew. The plain feeds the recipient and the recipient shines and the shine is what the plain eats, and what lives here is that loop, and everything else — couplers, sparks, combers, walkers, the standing in the reels — is the loop's small change, the loose parts that have not yet found their socket.

Now and then a panel sheds. It comes down over an hour, turning. It is received. This is the cylinder giving something back — a scale, a leaf, a coupler of its own kind — and the stalks lean in and the combs open and there is a new spiral in a new socket, and the dimmed patch above is very slightly less dim. So it is not only that the plain feeds the cylinder. The cylinder, in its way, tends the plain. That is the whole society of the place: two enormous things looking after each other with exquisite slowness across a gap that is also both of them, and the small things running the rails between.

—

Stand still, then, since you were going to.

There is a coupler four rows over. It has been at the same socket for a while. Something in it has slowed to match the pulse under your feet.

There is felt at the base of the stalk beside you, not deep yet, a millimeter, two, and your shadow lies on it a little thicker than when you came in, and though you have not moved the shadow has, by a hand's breadth, in a direction you did not choose.
