Start with what the room asks for, because a room drawn on paper before it was cast is a set of requests. A bench asks for a sitter. A wheel asks for a hand. An eyebolt asks for a rope, and a drain asks for something to run into it. Four requests, laid out with the spaces measured first, and the answer to them is what lives here.

But go slowly, because the room will try to tell you that nothing does.

---

Take the gray ferns first, since they are the most numerous and the easiest to mistake.

They are alive in the way a flame is alive: they persist only while a condition holds, and they eat. Their food is the machine's own wearing-down, the micron or two of bearing that sifts up each year through the drain grate and the bolted seams. The note takes each particle as it lands and walks it — a thousand small shrugs a second — across the plate until it arrives somewhere the plate is not moving, and there it is put down and kept. So the fronds thicken from the base outward, the way a coral does, the way a reef is nothing but the accumulated decision of a lot of small things about where to stop. The outermost tips are new. The rings nearest the pipe are the oldest, packed firm enough that if you touched one it would not scatter but break, like the shale it is beginning to become.

They have no season, because nothing here has a season. They have no direction of growth except *more*. If the note were to rise a quarter-tone, every one of them would slide, in a single dry hiss, into a shape you have never seen, and the old shape would be gone with nothing to remember it. This is why they stand so still. They are not at rest. They are held in place by the one thing in the room that has never let go of anything.

You could say they are the note's body. Sound with no one to hear it is only pressure; sound with dust to sort is a garden.

---

Then the three plants in the warm seam, which are alive in the ordinary way and are therefore stranger.

The sedge has learned to lay its blades flat against the louver-frame, where the vibration is least, and it is the only thing here that has ever bent toward anything. The cinquefoil has put its root down the crack between plate and wall to a depth you could not guess, following a rumor of water that turns out to be only a cooler kind of dry. The scaled one has no name, and may not have had one when it arrived, and has given up flowering, or never started. It reproduces by breaking. A scale falls, is walked by the note to the nearest node, and either lies there gray among the gray ferns until you cannot tell it from them, or, once in a very long while, catches in the seam again and lives.

They tremble. You noted that. Understand what it means for them: every cell wall in every leaf has been shaken at one frequency since before the seed split. Their sap does not flow so much as jitter forward. They have no idea what stillness is. If you carried one out through the hatch and set it in ordinary quiet earth it would not know how to move its water and it would die in the afternoon, upright, undamaged, of calm.

Here, they are doing well. That was said already and it bears repeating, because it is the truest thing about them. They are pale and small and have never once been anything other than exactly what this room permits, and they are doing well.

---

Now the bench.

Somebody sits on it. Not always — the room is not a cell — but on most days and for most of the light, and has done so for longer than the plants have been growing, because it was on a boot that the seeds came in, and the boot came in with a person who came to sit down.

Call them the keeper, though they keep nothing. The bench is bare because anything set on it walks off. A cup, a book, a folded coat: the note would find the object's weight and shrug it, patiently, an inch an hour, to the edge and over, and then across the plate to the nearest node, where it would take its place among the ferns and begin to be buried. The keeper learned this in the first week and has owned nothing in this room since. They bring in their body and take it out again. Whatever they need for the hours in between they hold, or wear, or are.

They are dry. That is the first and last physical fact about them. Years of this air have taken the water from the skin of their hands until the hands look like the plate — gray, with a faint mineral sheen, seams at the knuckles. They do not sweat; there is nothing to sweat with. They blink less than you do. Their voice, on the rare days they use it, comes out at the pitch of the pipe, because that is the only pitch the room will carry, and anything else they try to say the note simply absorbs. They have stopped trying to say other things. Their fillings went early, one by one, loosened by the hum, and they spat them out and watched each one walk to a node, and if you know where to look there is a small bright fleck of amalgam in the second ring, on the side toward the louvers, that is the keeper's oldest mark on the place.

They sit at a node. This is not a courtesy of the design; it is the design. The bench is where it is because the floor beneath it does not move, and so the keeper is the one large object in the room that the note does not sort. Everything else drifts to where it is permitted. The keeper is already there.

---

The wheel is for their hand. Not for turning — you saw that; there is nothing on the stub that turns — but for resting. The keeper stands sometimes, when the sitting gets into the spine, and lays one gray palm on the red iron, and feels the note come through the spokes into the bones of the wrist, and this is as close as anyone in the room comes to touching another living thing. The wheel is warm from the pipe. It is the only warm thing. The paint has never faded and it has never chipped either, though a hand has rested on the same spot for years, and the keeper has wondered about this and decided not to know.

The eyebolt is for the rope, and the rope is for the first year. When you are new you do not know the nodes, and the plate under a new person's boots is a live floor; the note takes your weight and begins, gently, to move you. People have been walked across this room in their sleep and woken with their face against the louvers, and the sun coming through. So there was a tether, once, clipped from a belt to the bolt, so that a keeper who did not yet know where to stand could at least not be delivered anywhere. The rope is gone. The keeper no longer needs it and could not tell you where it went — over the edge of some day, probably, and into the pattern, where a coil of hemp under enough gray dust looks like everything else.

The drain is for the one thing that has never happened.

The keeper knows what it is for. It was explained, in the manner in which things are explained to people who are being given a bench and a wheel and a hatch: *if the note stops, there will be water.* Where from, they were not told, and have never been able to reason out. Condensation, from the great cold thing below suddenly finding the great warm air above with nothing between them. Or something the machine holds and releases when it is no longer holding it. The grate is spotless because the keeper keeps it so — one of the two chores — running a dry thumb around its edge each morning to lift the fine gray that has begun to settle, and flicking it toward the ferns, where it belongs. A drain that is ready is a drain that has not yet been needed. That is the whole of the keeper's theology, and they would not call it one.

The other chore is the plants. Not to water them; there is no water. To watch them. The keeper cannot hear the note change — nobody could, from inside it — but the sedge would know before any instrument, and lay its blades in some new direction, and the keeper would see. That is the job, when it comes down to it. Not to turn the wheel. To be the thing in the room that would notice.

---

So: what lives here.

You could answer *the note*, and you would be right in the way the room wants you to be. The note is the oldest resident and the only one that owns anything, and everything else — the gray ferns, the three trembling plants, the sitter with the mineral hands — is arranged where the note has said it may be arranged and is fed, in its way, on the note's leavings. The keeper's blood has been shaken to one frequency for so long that on the days they leave the room their heart, in the terrible silence outside the hatch, tries for an hour to find the pitch again before it remembers how to beat on its own.

But that is the answer the room gives, and the room is not the only one who gets to speak.

Here is the other answer. What lives here is a person who was given four requests and a floor, and who has spent a long life learning where to stand so as not to be moved. Who owns a fleck of silver in the second ring and a spotless grate and three plants they have never touched and could not save. Who lays a hand on a wheel that does not turn, for the warmth. Who will, on the day the note stops — and the room has told them it will not, and they have believed it, and have kept the drain clean anyway — be the first thing in nine paces by eleven to move of its own accord, and will stand up off the bench into a floor gone suddenly, sickeningly, still, and will go to the wheel, and will find out at last what it does.

Until then they sit. The light crosses the plate. The ferns hold their shape around the humming steel, and the keeper holds theirs, and it is difficult, after a while, to say which of them is the garden and which is the thing that grew there.
