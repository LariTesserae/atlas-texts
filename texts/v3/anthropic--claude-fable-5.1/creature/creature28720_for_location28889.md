Ask what lives on the plateau and the first honest answer is the one the place gives you itself: nothing. Nothing with a face. Nothing that eats anything but heat and the dark. You can walk a day across the packed machinery and the only thing that moves independently of the floor is you.

But that answer stops being true the longer you stand still.

---

Start with the smallest thing, because it is the one the plateau did not make.

The sourness under the resin. The fermentation. A plant does not sour on its own; something has to be doing the souring. In the sap, in the hot amber that runs the veins of the whole structure, there is a chemistry that is not the plant's chemistry — a slow, acid working that takes the sugars the cambium lays down and turns a fraction of them into something the cambium never asked for. Whatever does this has no cell wall you would recognize. It cannot; there is no water here for a membrane to hold. It lives in wax. It lives in oil at a temperature that would cook anything with blood, and it has done so since the last time there was rain.

It is not one thing. It is a population, if a population can be a tint. It gives the whole place its undersmell, and it may be the reason the sap glows red rather than gold. The plateau did not invite it. The plateau has, over an epoch, stopped noticing the difference between the sap it makes and the sap it has. Call it the first resident: a passenger too small and too old to be called a guest, so thoroughly at home that its host has forgotten it was ever a stranger.

---

Then the dust.

The dust is not alive. But it is the only thing here that is free. Every other presence is attached — rooted, grafted, socketed, sealed. The dust alone drifts. It is the plateau's own body, ground fine by the plateau's own collapse, and once it is loose it goes where the heat sends it: up the columns, along the ceiling vaults, through the doors when the doors are open, settling in drifts in the low halls where the flywheels turn slowest. In the worst districts it is a permanent weather, and in that weather you begin to feel, wrongly, that you are not alone — that the haze is a crowd, that the furred shapes standing in the red glow are figures standing.

They are not. But note that the plateau treats the dust the way a body treats a wound-scab. It packs it. It builds on it. Whole low districts are founded on nothing but compressed collapse, and the floor there is soft and warm and gives slightly underfoot like the floor of a granary. If anything on the plateau has a life cycle, the dust does: it rises when a tower comes down, hangs, settles, is pressed, is grown over, becomes floor, becomes wall, becomes tower, comes down. The plateau is eating its dead and there is nothing dead in it but itself.

---

Now the tenders.

You saw them without knowing it, in the wound where the column fell: pale shoots the thickness of an arm, pushing blind from the broken faces. Watch them longer. They do not simply grow. They *go*. A runner emerges from a fracture, crosses two meters of open floor in a season, finds the base of the neighboring column, and stops — and where it stops, it thickens, and where it thickens, the column it has found is shored. Another emerges near a socket that has begun to bind, and works its way along the axle's length, and begins, very slowly, to eat the excess: to reabsorb a millimeter of overgrowth back into the plant so that the wheel turns another century.

They are of the plant. They cannot be anything else. But their behavior is not a plant's behavior. A plant grows toward light, water, up, out. These grow toward *fault*. They know the difference between a wall that is meant to thicken and a socket that is not. They know, in some chemistry laid down before the sea below dried, what a piston is for, and they will not let a piston stop.

They are the maintenance crew. They have no eyes and no hands and they are made of the thing they maintain, and they are the closest thing this place has to animals — not because they move, but because they *attend*. They are the reason the collapse is never total. They are also the reason the copies drift: each runner remembers a slightly softer version of the shape it is restoring, and rebuilds it a little rounder, a little simpler, a little more like a runner's idea of a column and a little less like a column.

Where do they come from? From the wounds. Only from the wounds. A district that never breaks never sees them. It is as if the plateau keeps its tenders folded away inside its own tissue and only births them by tearing, so that the only time you ever see the crew is when something has gone wrong enough to need it. The falling of the towers is not the plateau failing. It is the plateau calling its people out.

---

Which leaves the cultivators.

They are not here. The doors that close to slits, the signs healed over into braille no one can read, the ramps worn concave by feet that no longer come — all of this says they left, or ended, and the plateau has been growing over the hole they made ever since.

But consider the doors.

The doors open. On a schedule. For a while — an hour, a day — a wall softens and swings inward and stands open onto the next hall, and nothing passes through, and it heals shut. The plateau has no way to know an hour. It has no sky. It has no cold. The only clock it could possibly be keeping is one that was set into it from outside, by someone who needed the door open at the end of the shift, and who trained a living hinge to remember when the shift ended.

That memory is still running. It has outlived the shift, the shift-workers, the concept of work, the language the sign was written in, the sea. It is the most persistent thing the cultivators left, more persistent than their ramps or their roads, and it is not a thing at all. It is a *when*. A habit of opening.

If you want to say the cultivators still live here, this is where they live: in the timing. Not as ghosts — there is nothing here to haunt, no memory in the floor, no bones. But as rhythm. As a pulse in the wall that was once a pulse in someone's day. You feel it, standing at the rim with your hand on the outer bark: in, and hold, out, and hold. Something set that tempo. The plateau has kept it so faithfully it has become the plateau's heartbeat, and no longer knows whose it was.

---

And underneath all of these — the ferment, the dust, the tenders, the hours — is the thing that is actually doing the living, which is the pattern.

Not the plant. The plant is what the pattern wears. The pattern is the template that the runners grow toward, the shape that the wound remembers, the reason a flywheel is still a flywheel after a thousand rebuildings instead of dissolving into an undifferentiated mass of hot wood. It has no location. It is not stored anywhere you could cut into. It is distributed through the whole structure the way a habit is distributed through a body, and like a habit it is imperfect, and like a habit its imperfections compound.

That is how you know it is alive: it errs. A crystal does not drift. A mountain does not misremember itself. The pattern misremembers, a little, every time, and the misrememberings accumulate into machines that make no mechanical sense, districts whose product goes into a wall and never comes out, doors that open onto other doors. Some of what the plateau now does is pure vestige — a gesture repeated because the gesture was once part of a larger gesture, the way a hand will reach for a pocket that is no longer there. The pattern does not know this. The pattern does not know anything. But it *wants*, in the only sense a pattern can want: it pulls the growth toward itself, and the growth comes.

What lives on the plateau is a very large, very old, very slow idea of a factory, wearing a factory. It has no inside. It has no outside. It has been alone so long that alone is not a condition it could have, any more than a fish could have dry. It tends itself with itself, feeds itself to itself, keeps hours for no one, and does all of this in a heat and a darkness so total that the question of who lives there has stopped meaning what it means anywhere else.

Something lives there. It is the place. It has been the place for longer than the land has had its shape, and it will go on being the place, a little rounder each time, a little simpler, until the copies have drifted so far that the last piston is only a warm column rising and falling in the dark, for no reason it can recall, because once, before the sea, somebody needed it to.
