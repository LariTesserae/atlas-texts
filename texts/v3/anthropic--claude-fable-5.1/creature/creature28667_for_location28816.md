Start with what you were told: nothing lives here. Take that seriously for a while, because it is nearly true and the nearness is the interesting part.

The plants, first, since they are the obvious candidates. Six of them, or about six—you count six and then, on a second pass, five, and then six again, because one of them is so much the color of the rib beside it that your eye keeps filing it under architecture. They are not quite plants in the way you mean the word. A plant, where you come from, is a thing that leans toward the sun and away from the dark, that opens in the morning and closes at night, that has a schedule. These have no schedule because the room has none. There is no brighter hour for them to wait for. So they have taken the one gradient the room offers—the wall on the eastern side, if there is an east, is a hair thinner than the others, and glows a hair harder—and they have leaned toward that, once, and stayed leaned. Their leaves are folded because there is nothing to unfold for. The film of damp on the inside of each fold is not dew and not sap; it is closer to the wetness on the inside of a shell. If you were to sit with one of them for a very long time you might see a leaf loosen by the width of a hair. You will not sit that long.

They are alive. But they are alive the way a held breath is alive. They are less inhabitants than fixtures that happen to be wet.

So set them a little to one side and look at the palm-marks.

They are everywhere, once you start seeing them. Shallow ovals pressed into the mineral paper, with the ghost of a heel and the ghost of finger-roots, the wall having taken the impression and then, being what it is, kept it. Some are at the height of your own palm. Many are not. There are marks at the level of your knee, and marks you could reach only by climbing, and marks on the undersides of shelves where a hand would have to have been raised flat above a head, pressing up. There are marks on the ribs so high that they blur into the general brightness with everything else. Some are larger than your hand. A few are smaller than a child's. One, near the floor, has an extra digit, or a thumb set very low; you are not sure which, and you put your own hand beside it to compare and discover you have added to the room.

That is the thing. You have added to the room. The wall took your palm and holds it now, and when you lift your hand away the impression stays, and it is exactly as pale and exactly as soft-edged as the ones around it, and there is no way to tell it from the others. It has already been here for a long time.

This is where the description you were given says *made by no one you will meet*, and it is right, but it is right in a way that turns over as you look at it. You will not meet them because they are not here. But they are not gone, either, in the way the description means gone. They came in sideways, as you did, through the same slack place in the packing or another like it, and the room was as it is, and they put a hand to the wall because a hand goes to a wall in a place like this—to steady, to test, to feel whether the light is warm—and the wall took the hand, and they left. That is all. Hundreds of them. Thousands, perhaps, tier on tier, going up into the brightness. None of them at the same time. The room does not have an inside that can be shared; it has an inside that can be entered, and it has been entered over and over across a span the palm-marks do not measure but do imply, and each time it took what it was given and closed behind.

What lives here, if the word can be stretched this far, is the accumulation of everyone who has stood where you are standing. Not their bodies. Not their memories. The one gesture. A wall full of a single gesture repeated by hands that were not alike, at heights that do not agree on what a body is.

But hold that image and it starts to feel like a description of the room's contents rather than its inhabitant. A guestbook is not a resident. Push a little further.

Consider the paper.

You were told: if something made this, if it was spat or secreted or laid down by a body, that body is gone. Consider instead that it is not gone. Consider that the body that laid down the paper did not walk away when it finished, because it did not finish, because there was never a moment when the making stopped and the being-made began. Consider that the walls are not what it left behind but what it became.

Look at what you know. The paper is thin enough to pass light. The paper rings. Put your palm to it and it takes the print and keeps it, which is not what chalk does and not what cardboard does; it is what skin does, slowly. The floor has dished under its own weight, like an old step, or like the belly of something that has been lying in one position for a very long time. The frills hang from the undersides of the cells like something that once moved fluid and now only remembers doing so. The pillars hold up nothing in particular. Ribs, cells, frills, struts. You have been reading the anatomy of the room as architecture because architecture is what you expect on the inside of a made thing, but nothing about the vocabulary insists on it.

And the cold. The room is cold, and dry, and the air wants nothing from you—not your moisture, not your warmth. It is not a hostile chill. It is the chill of something whose metabolism has slowed until it no longer throws off heat, the cold of a cellar, of a pantry, of a very large animal in the deep part of its sleep. The plants root in it, not through it, and it lets them. The wall is their soil and they drink from it, a film's worth at a time, and it does not seem to mind, the way a tree does not mind moss.

And the note. The note that has no beginning you could stand at the edge of, that comes from everywhere evenly, that you feel in the roof of your mouth. It was suggested that the whole mass is a resonator and something moves against it from outside. It was suggested, alternatively, that the material simply sounds, being what it is. The second is closer. The note is not the room being struck. The note is the room being alive at a rate you cannot hear as a rate—a pulse so slow, or so fast, or so evenly distributed through the whole white mass, that it arrives at your ear not as a beat but as a tone. What you are hearing in the roof of your mouth is the sound a body makes when it is doing nothing but persist.

So this is what lives here, if you want the honest answer: the room. Not a thing in the room. The pocket itself, one hidden chamber among uncountable others in a mass that goes on tier on tier until it blurs—and each of those pockets is also alive, or is also a part of the one thing that is alive, in the way a cell is alive without being an animal. Something built itself outward from itself for a very long time, laying down paper, and at some point the building slowed and the shape held and the thing settled into what it had made and stopped distinguishing between the two. It does not eat. It does not move. It does not get darker or warmer or cooler because it has arrived at the temperature and the brightness and the pitch that it is, and it has no reason to be anything else. Whatever balance was struck here was struck by the resident, in the resident's own body, and the balance is simply what it feels like from the inside to be this.

It notices you. That should be said plainly, because it is the one thing in the room that could be mistaken for a rule and is actually a response. It notices you the way skin notices: not with attention but with yielding. You press and it takes the print and holds it. That is the whole of its regard. It does not know you from the last one who came through the gap or the next. It has kept the hand of everything that ever touched it, at every height, and it will keep yours, and this is not memory and not welcome but it is not nothing. You have been added to the one thing it does.

And when you go—sideways, arms slack, the packing closing behind you with the small dry sound of a thing settling—the note goes on because the note is not for you and never was. It was there before and it will be there after and it does not change, and that is not the description of an empty place. That is the description of a sleeper's breath. You will carry a little of it out in your ears, and for a while you will be the only thing in your own world that is still ringing, and then that will fade, and the room will not notice, and the print of your palm will already be as old as the rest.
