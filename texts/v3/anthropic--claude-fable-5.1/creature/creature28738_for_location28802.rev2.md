**What lives in the Hollow**

Start small, because the place does.

In the film on the pool-bottoms there is a life so thin it hardly counts as one: a slick of the plant's own shed cells, grey-white and faintly sweet, that settles out of the standing water and lies there like the skin on cold milk. Things graze it. They are the size of a fingernail paring and about as substantial, many-legged, transparent enough that you can watch the film they have eaten move through them. They came in on the feet of birds, long ago, from tarns on the plateau where their ancestors were speckled and brown. Nothing in them is brown now. Put one on your palm in the light from a high split and it is a shape made of water with a darker thread down the middle where the plant is being digested.

They swarm when it rains. Nobody knows what the rain tells them, but the first drops onto a pool bring them boiling up to the surface in their millions, and the surface goes thick and soft with them, and this is when the flyers come down.

Between the pools, in the spongy tissue of the troughs, there are grubs. Pale, blunt, cool to the touch, with a mouth like a drawstring. They tunnel. The soft tissue is honeycombed with their galleries, and because the plant grows around every wound rather than filling it, the old galleries stay — you can cut a section of the trough mat and find it laced with sealed passages a century deep, each one a grub that lived and ate and was folded in. The grubs never become anything else. They pupate and the pupa is eaten, or they grow until the tunnel closes behind them and they are simply enclosed. There are more of them under the floor than there are of anything walking on it.

---

Along the walls, where the splits weep, there is a different kind of small life. The sap draws it. Anywhere the skin is torn the syrup gathers in the crease and runs, and in the runs there are lickers — long-bodied, whiskered, with paws that grip the laminar tissue like the rungs of a ladder. They are the size of a forearm. They came in as something like a marten, and they are still something like a marten, only bleached and hushed, and they move along the seams in the dark with a wet clicking of claws and their tongues working. They are the only animals here that anyone has seen fat. The sap is rich and it does not spoil and there is always more of it, and the lickers have made a life of standing in a running crack and drinking, and this life has cost them their fear. They will lick a person's hand. They will lick a blade. The tappers push them off the seams with the flat of a paddle and they climb back on.

They breed in the splits, in the deep folds where two lips of torn wall have not quite parted, and sometimes the wall grows and the fold closes and there is a litter sealed in the tissue, mewing for a day or two through the skin. You can hear it if you are close. Then you cannot.

---

The flyers live above all this, in the roof.

They roost in the seams — not the great splits, which let in light and wind, but the fine tears that lace the vault where the skin is thinnest and most stretched. They press themselves into the tears, as many as will fit, and the tissue is warm there because the whole animal weight of them is warm, and they sleep in the wet dark with their faces down. They are not bats. They were once something that hunted moths over the plateau in the twilight, small and quick and mostly ears, and when they came in through the splits they found a place where twilight never ended, and they stopped being anything else.

Everything about them has gone toward hearing. The ears are half the head. The eyes are seed-small and closed most of the time. The fur is so pale it is almost not a color, and it is very fine, and the animals are so light that a dead one lands on the floor without a sound. Their voices are the clicks, dense and dry and so fast they blur into a rattle, and the flock's rattle overhead — gravel on a table — is not talk but sight. They are looking. The whole Hollow is lit for them, all the time, in bursts.

They come down in the wet hours, when the rain starts and the pools go soft with swimmers, and they take the swimmers off the surface with their mouths. Then they go back up. They are the only things that use the full height of the place, and they are the only things that know its shape. A flyer born in a roof-seam over the eastern floor can cross to the western wall and back in a night, and the people who stay say that if you listen to the pitch of a flock's clicking as it passes, you can tell whether the roof above you is low or high, and whether the wall is near.

Sometimes a roof-seam grows. The tear that was a roost for twenty years widens into a split and the light comes in and the flyers leave. And sometimes it grows the other way — the skin thickens, the tear knits from the edges — and a whole roost is closed into the vault, and the tissue there stays warm for a while and then does not.

---

The grazers you hear an hour before anything else.

Wooden drumming, hollow and multiple, coming through the floor itself so that you feel it in your feet before you hear it in your ears. It is hooves on cork. The grazers are long-legged and heavy through the body, with a build somewhere between a deer and a cow if either of those had spent three hundred years walking on something that gives. Their feet have gone broad and hard-edged to bite into the floor. Their coats have gone grey — not the grey of camouflage, because nothing here sees, but the grey of an animal that has stopped making pigment because there was no longer any reason to.

They have almost no eyes. What is left is two dull grey dents that flinch when a light-blade from a split falls across them. Everything they know about the Hollow they know through the ears and the feet and the nose. They know where the walls are by the way the hum changes as they approach — the note thickens near a wall, the people say, the way your own voice thickens when you talk into a corner — and they turn before they touch it. They know where the water is by the sound of it running. They know where the soft tissue is by smell, and they strip it from the troughs in long tearing mouthfuls, and their teeth are wide and flat and stained the colour of the floor.

They cough to each other. A deep, repeating cough, chest-made, that carries across the open floor for a great distance and that is the main way one grazer knows where any other grazer is. In a herd the coughing is continuous — hundreds of animals, each one saying only *here, here, here* — and from inside it you cannot tell how many there are or where the edge of the herd is, and this, presumably, is the point.

They calve in the troughs, in the soft tissue, and the calves are born the colour of the floor and lie in it and are almost impossible to find. They move in a long slow circuit of the Hollow that follows the rain, because where the rain has fallen the trough-mat swells and softens and is good to eat. They are always walking toward last week's weather. Where a herd has passed the troughs are grazed to the hard layer and the floor is beaten smooth and the plant, over a season, grows it all back. Where a herd has stood for a night there is dung in wet heaps, and the floor takes it.

They come to the splits when the outside wind is high. Nobody has decided why. The people who tap sap near the great eastern splits know that when the hum drops and the wind outside begins to shriek across the tears, the herds will come drumming in from the dark and stand packed in the light and the spray from the split's edge, hundreds of them, coughing, facing the opening with their eyeless heads up. It may be salt. Dust blows in with the wind and lies on the lip of a split and it is the only mineral in the place. It may be something else. A tapper who was a child there said they looked like animals listening at a door.

---

And there is the thing that hunts them.

You are told about it before you go in. You are told nobody has seen it clearly, and this is true, and after a while inside you understand why: nothing here is seen clearly, and the hunter is simply the animal that has made the most of that.

What is known is this. A herd's coughing changes. It does not change to alarm at once; first it thins, as if some of the animals had stopped talking. Then a single call comes across the floor that is not the wind — long, low, level, held on one pitch so close to the hum that for a moment you take it for the hum shifting. Then a scream, and the drumming scatters in every direction at once, and something goes through it. Later, the wet sound.

The hunter sings inside the hum. That is what the people who stay have worked out. It moves toward a herd making a note the grazers cannot separate from the sound of the place they live in, and it is on them before they know it is anything. The long call is not a hunting cry. It is a kind of hiding.

What is left in the morning — if there is a morning — is a place on the floor where a grazer has been opened and eaten, the belly and the haunches taken, the rest left, and around the remains a rim of new tissue already lipping up. The floor closes over it within days. There are never tracks, because the floor does not take tracks. There are never bones, because the floor takes bones. What there are, rarely, are teeth.

Long, narrow, curved, the colour of old ivory, shed and found in the grey slick around a kill. They are hard in a way nothing else in the Hollow is hard. The people have a few of them. Bound into a handle of cut stalk, a hunter's tooth is the only tool anyone has that will cut the floor.

Some of the stayers say there is one hunter. Some say there is a family, a line, and that the young ones sing badly and the grazers scatter early and the kills are messy and small. Some say it is pale and low and very long, with a head that is mostly the singing organ, and that it drinks at the same pools they drink at, and that it has never once been interested in a person. A man who has been in the Hollow for thirty years says he lay on the floor one night in the dark with his ear to the cork, as the people do, and felt it walk past him — felt the floor take its weight, one foot and then another, a long way apart, slow — and that the hum in the floor and the note the animal was making were so nearly the same that he stopped being able to tell which one he was listening to.

---

The people.

A hundred come, in the good season, and most of them go. They tap and load and carry sap out to the plateau in bladders of inner bark, and they are gone before the cold turns. But some do not go, and there are now a few dozen who have not gone for a long time, and there are children among them who were born on the floor.

They are not a tribe and not a settlement. They live apart, one or two to a shelter, a day's walk from anyone else, and they meet at the water and at kills. They speak quietly, because the Hollow carries a voice a very long way and hands it back to you from unexpected directions, and because they have learned that whatever is out there can hear. For anything at distance they use the floor. Two blades of hard tissue struck together make a sharp knock that travels through the cork, and there is a small language of knocks — *here, water, herd coming, meat* — that the stayers can send and read across a day's dark. If you lie with your ear to the floor at night you will sometimes hear a knock, far off, and then another answering it, closer, and understand that you are not alone and were never alone.

They have stopped wanting light. Not lost it — a stayer will still turn toward a split when the sun finds it, will still go and stand in the grey blade and put a hand up into it — but stopped needing it, and stopped waiting for it. They move by the walls' breath on their faces. They move by the hum, which they say they can feel thicken as a wall comes near, exactly as the grazers do. They know weather before it falls: when the note drops half a tone the eastern flanks are warm and it will rain over the middle floor within a day, and they will be there when the pools swarm and the flyers come down, with nets of fiber cord, taking flyers out of the air by the sackful. Flyer is most of what they eat. It is little meat and soft bone and they eat all of it.

They eat grazer when the hunter leaves it. They have learned to follow the scream. Move fast enough after the wet sound and you will reach the kill before the floor has begun to close, and you can cut what is left away from the ground with a tooth-blade and carry it off, and the meat is grey and rich and tastes faintly of the sap the animal has been eating all its life. There are stayers who have done this a hundred times, and who say the hunter does not mind. They arrive to find the belly gone and the rest left as if for them.

They wrap their hands and feet in inner bark and sleep against the lower wall, where the tissue is porous and warm and breathes on them all night. They tap the walls and drink the sap and their teeth are bad. They make cord and they make blades and they make the low shelters of shed skin, and when a shelter's footings have been lifted and tilted by the growing floor they let it lean until it falls and build another. They have a word for the plant which is also their word for the floor and also their word for the place, and they do not use it very often. You do not name the weather.

They are not the same as they were. Nobody who lives in the dark for thirty years is. The stayers are pale in the way the animals are pale, and they hold their heads slightly turned as they walk, one ear forward, and their voices have gone low and level. A woman who came in twenty years ago for sap and did not leave says that when she goes out to the plateau now the sky is too much and the silence is unbearable — that the outside is an empty room, and she cannot sleep in it, and she comes back in through the split with her shoulders coming down as the hum takes her.

Her son was born on the floor. He is nine. He has seen stars twice, through a high split on a clear night, and he was told what they were, and he did not think much of them. He knows the coughing of the grazers so well that he can hear when one is sick. He knows the flyers' clicking so well that he knows where the roof is above him at all times, and this makes him, as far as anyone can tell, the only person in the Hollow who is never lost. He knocks on the floor to his mother across a day of dark and she knocks back. At night he lies with his ear to the cork and listens to the crowd.

---

Because there is a crowd.

From outside, laying an ear to the pale flank, the climbers hear a distant crowd, and inside you understand that they are hearing the Hollow: the herds and the flyers and the drip and the wall tearing, all of it gathered in the resonant body and pressed through the skin as one murmur. That is what the crowd is.

Except the stayers say there is a second crowd, under the first.

Lie flat on the floor, in a trough, at the still hour before the rain when the herds are far off and the flyers are up and the hum has settled into one long note. Put your ear to the cork. Under the hum there is something else — not a tone, not exactly a sound — a wide slow surge, coming and going at long intervals, like surf on a beach several miles away. The stayers say it is the plant. Not the walls creaking or the skin tearing, which are the surface of it, but the thing itself, the whole living body under the floor, moving water and sugar up through half a kilometer of tissue in slow pulses that you can feel with your body if you are quiet enough. They say that in the oldest parts of the floor, where the hard dark layer lies deepest and the ridges are highest, the surge is stronger. They say there are places where the floor is warmer than the wall, and that the warm places move. They say — the ones who came in for the sound, who have been on the floor so long they have stopped doing anything but listening — that the surge has a rhythm, and the rhythm has been changing.

This is what lives in the Hollow, when everything else has been counted. The swimmers and the grubs and the lickers and the flyers, the herds and the singer in the hum, the few dozen people and their knocks in the dark — all of them are guests in the body of one thing, and the one thing is the only inhabitant that is entirely at home. It did not make the Hollow for them. It made the Hollow by accident, by the plain fact of growing faster than its own skin, and it has filled with them the way a cellar fills with what can live in a cellar. It folds them in as they die and grows over the marks they cut in it and lifts their shelters and their wayposts and stands them at angles, and it is not unkind, and it is not anything. It is young. It is warm and damp and it hums and it has not decided how large it means to be, and everything that lives on it lives on a floor that is not a floor but a lid, and lies down at night with an ear pressed to the lid, and listens to what is underneath.
