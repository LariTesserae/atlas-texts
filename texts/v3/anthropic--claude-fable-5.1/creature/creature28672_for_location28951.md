Begin with the cups, because that is where the living is thickest and because it is the first place you will look once you stop looking for something large.

Every bromeliad holds a tank, and every tank is inhabited. Lean in close to one wedged at head height in the fork of a trunk and the water is the color of weak tea, warm to the finger, a few centimeters deep, and moving. Mosquito larvae hang from the surface by their tails and jerk downward when your shadow crosses them. A crab the size of a fingernail, the exact green of the leaf-base, sidles under a drowned petal. There are tadpoles. And there is a frog, no bigger than the first joint of your thumb, brown shading to a dull red at the throat, sitting on the wet inner face of the leaf with its eyes half-lidded. It was laid in this cup. It hatched in this cup. It will lay here, and what it lays will hatch here, and none of them will ever touch ground, because ground is a rumor two or three meters below and there is no reason to go. Its floor is a leaf. Its sky is the next leaf up. Its weather is whatever drips in. There are thousands of these cups on a single tree, and hundreds of trees on a single dome, and the frogs in them are not all the same species, or not quite; each dome, it seems, has bred its own, and the ones on the summit domes are a shade darker than the ones in the shelter of the walls, as the moss is.

The film comes down into the cups with the cloud. You can see it if you look: a faint grey skin on the surface of the tank water that the larvae break when they rise and that closes again over them. The frog sits in it. The frog breathes through its skin, as frogs do, and so the frog breathes it. This is the first thing you understand about what lives here, and you will understand it again and again in different bodies: nothing here has a way of not taking in.

The hummingbirds are the exception to almost everything, and so notice them next. They are the only quick thing. Everything else on the mountain moves at the speed of the cloud or slower, and then there are these, a dozen kinds at least, none longer than your finger, throwing themselves through the wet air with a sound like a struck wire. They are green and violet and a bronze that goes black in shadow. They work the orchids and the bromeliad flowers and the small red tubes that hang off certain vines, and they do it with a precision that has cost them and the flowers a great deal of time together: there are orchids here with throats so long and so bent that only one particular bird with one particular bill can get to the bottom of them, and that bird is the only pollinator that orchid will ever have, and neither of them knows it. They don't go below the cloud. Nothing about them is built for the lowlands; their hearts run too hot, their feet are too small, they'd starve in an hour. They live their whole brief lives on the domes and in the upper walls, and at night, when the light dims, they go still on a twig and let their bodies cool almost to the temperature of the air and become, for a few hours, small dull stones among the orchids that a snail could pass without noticing. In the morning they shake themselves back up to speed and go on. They are the mountain's pulse made fast enough to see. That is one way to think of them.

The snails are the mountain's pulse made slow enough to lose track of. You will see the same snail on the same rock three days running and be unsure whether it has moved. Their shells are the size of a fist and banded, brown and cream and a rust-orange, and the bands are not decoration. They are record. Each is laid down over a season out of what the snail took in, and what the snail took in is the film on the rock and the pale mineral crust from the pool-rims and the algae that grows in the weep-sheets, and so the shell is a stratigraphy of the mountain's intake, a small stone core sample walking on a foot. The older snails, the ones with fifteen or twenty bands, have a grey tinge to their newest growth that the younger ones don't. They graze the rock faces in slow passes and leave behind them a track cleaner than the rock they came to, a stripe of dark stone where the film has been scraped off and eaten, and the cloud lays new film down behind them at about the rate they move. They are, you think, the only things here that could be said to be tidying up. It is not enough. It is not meant to be enough. They are just eating.

The salamanders come up to breathe and go down again, and that is nearly all you will ever see them do. Come to a pool at the hour when the light is best and sit, and after a while there is a pop, small and precise, and a dark head is at the surface with two small pale eyes in it, and then the head is gone and a ripple travels out and the steam takes it. They are long, longer than your forearm, and thick, and their skin has the look of wet rubber, and they live in water you cannot hold your hand in. Watch long enough and you will see one drift up along the blue at the deep end and hang there, just under the surface, not breathing, its gill-frills stirring, and you will understand that the skin is the animal: that everything it takes in comes through it, the heat, the minerals, the sheen. There are salamanders in the rim pools where the sheen is strongest whose backs have a faint iridescence of their own, an oily bloom on the black, and whether that is in the skin or on it you can't tell and it may not matter. They are very slow, and they may be very old. Nothing here eats them. There is nothing here that could get a purchase on them without first being cooked. They are the animal most like the mountain, warm and permeable and patient and dark, and when you lay your hand flat on the pool-rim rock and feel the beat in it, you will half expect the next head that surfaces to be keeping the same time.

The insects are heavy. Not large, though some are large, but heavy in the way they carry themselves, as though the air were a fluid they had to push through. A moth the size of a hand rests on a moss column with its wings down and its body furred like a small mammal against a wet it never escapes, and when you touch the bark beside it it does not fly but walks, four deliberate steps, and settles again. A beetle with a shell the color of the warm pools' deep water goes along a liana at the pace of a second hand and takes twenty minutes to reach the next tree. A katydid you take for a leaf is a leaf with a drip tip, the same drawn-out point at its wing-end, and when a drop gathers there and ticks off it, you can't see any sign the animal knows. Their eggs are in the moss. Their larvae are in the moss. Everything is in the moss. Dig your fingers into any green column and pull, and what comes up is a wet handful of stems and rootlets and small pale things moving and the smell of iron and something sweet, and the hole you have made fills with water before you have straightened, and the moss will take a decade to close it.

Now the thing overhead.

You will not see it at first. You will see what it does. A branch moves against the way the cloud is moving. A fall of drops comes down through the canopy in a line, as if something long had dragged across the leaves above and pulled the water off them in sequence, and then stops. Once, at the edge of a dome where the trees are shorter and the light is stronger, a shape against the bright cloud that is a hump and then a limb and then nothing. You think sloth, because of the slowness and because of the hanging. Then a whole branch bows under it and you think no, too heavy, and you think bear, and that is closer but the movement is wrong, too fluid, too little effort in it, as if it were not climbing but being carried by the tree. Then one evening the cloud draws off the next dome across the valley and there, in a low twisted tree at the very lip, unmistakable at that distance because there is nothing else it could be, something the size of a person hunched in the crotch of the trunk with its back to you. The back is green. Not dark, not furred: green, and textured, and as you look you see that the texture is moss. Actual moss, growing, a centimeter deep at least, and out of the moss along the ridge of the shoulders a few small ferns, and in the ruff at the neck a pale spray of something that in any other light you would take for orchids and that is orchids. It has been still so long, or moved so slowly for so long, that the second forest has taken it for a limb of the first and grown on it. When it turns its head the head is moss-colored too, but the face is bare, a dark slick face like the salamanders' with the same small pale eyes, and it regards you across a kilometer of warm air with no interest whatever and turns back.

It lives on the second forest. That much you can piece together. It eats the bromeliad hearts and drinks the tank water and, presumably, whatever is in the tank water, frogs and crabs and all; it eats the orchid tubers and the young ferns and the moss itself, in great slow mouthfuls, and you find the places where it has been by the bare wet bark, which the moss is already re-greening. It comes down at night. At the rim of the hot pool nearest that dome there are depressions in the moss the size of a large animal's forequarters, and in the morning they hold water, and around them the pale mineral crust is broken in a way that nothing else here could break it. It drinks the pool water, then, warm as it is, and sheen and all, and goes back up before the light. Whether there is one or several you cannot say. You never see two at once. It may be that there is one to a dome, and that they never cross the valleys, and that each is as separate from the next as the frogs in the cups are. Or it may be that there is one and it is very old and the domes are all the same to it. It is warm. You know that without touching it, from the way the cloud steams a little more thickly off the tree it is in. It is warm the way the rock is warm, from inside, steadily, and it is growing a forest on its back the way the mountain is, and it does not appear to mind.

And below all of it, the callers.

You will not see these at all. The valley floors are a kilometer down and lost in cloud, and the walls are what they are. You hear them. Two notes, the second lower, the whole call taking about as long as a breath, and coming from the bottom of the valley in front of you in the hour when the light begins to go. Then from the next valley over, the same two notes. Then from a third, further off, so faint it is more a change in the quality of the quiet. Then more, until every valley you can imagine is sounding, not in chorus, not in any kind of rhythm you can find, each on its own time. And then, all at once, silence. Not a trailing off. A stop. Every caller in every valley you can hear, and presumably in all the ones you can't, closing its mouth in the same instant. You will hear this happen every evening you are here and every evening it will raise the hair on your arms, and it is on the third or fourth evening, with your hand flat on the warm rock at the lip while you listen, that you notice the stop comes on the beat. Not every beat. The pulse in the stone is slow and steady and goes on; the callers stop on one of them, and start again, some minutes later, on another. They are keeping time to it. Or it is keeping time to them. Or the same thing drives both and you are the only creature on the mountain to whom it seems remarkable.

What they are you will not learn. Something with a big voice and a low one, so amphibians, maybe, of the sort the salamanders would be if they had grown for another few hundred generations in water even hotter and air even thicker; or birds, of a kind that never rises above the cloud; or something for which there is no word, because no one has been down there and the valley floors are the one place on the mountain where the ground is warm mud and hot water and root-lattice over nothing, and the air is the air that drains off every dome and pools, and the haze from the lowlands, where it rises into the valley mouths, sits yellowest and longest before the cloud takes it. Whatever lives down there lives in the mountain's lowest and warmest and most saturated room, nearest the engine, breathing the densest of what comes up, and calls twice, and is answered, and stops on the beat. Their call is what the mountain sounds like from the inside. You come to think of it that way, without deciding to.

So step back, in your mind, since there is nowhere to step back to in the body, and look at what you have found.

A frog that breathes its cup. A snail that wears its intake in bands. A salamander that is nothing but a membrane between hot water and itself. A bird with a bill shaped to one flower's throat. An animal that has let the forest grow on its back and drinks what the pools hold. Voices at the bottom of everything, timed to the stone. Nothing here hunts much. There is predation, there must be, but it is slow and incidental, a matter of what falls into what. What lives here is not organized around taking life. It is organized around taking in. Every animal on the mountain is, in its own way, a drip tip: a shape the water passes through on its way to somewhere else, and that keeps a little, and grows a shade darker, and goes on. The mountain has been doing this with rain for longer than anyone has counted, and its residents are the mountain's way of doing it in flesh, and when the new thing began to come up in the cloud from the plains, greasy and sweet and faintly burnt, there was no adjustment to make. The frog took it through its skin. The snail laid it down in a band. The salamander bloomed with it. The moss on the great slow back went a shade darker. Whatever is at the bottom of the valleys breathed it and called twice and stopped on the beat. Nothing here has a way of not taking in, and so nothing here has had to learn one.

There is no one on the mountain. This is worth saying plainly, because you will keep looking. No one lives here and nothing has been made here because the two go together, and the second is the proof of the first. The who is below. The hum you feel in your sternum at the cliff edge is a who, or a great many whos, and the yellow columns leaning in the haze are what they breathe out, and the glow on the underside of the cloud on the stillest nights is their light. Nothing that lives up here has a word for below, or a way to get there, or would survive the going. The cloud is their floor. It is the border of the world
