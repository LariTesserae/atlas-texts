Begin with what does not live here, because the roar has already made that choice and everything else follows from it.

No birds. Not one. A bird lives by its own voice — it needs to hear itself to know it is still there — and a bird that comes over the lip and into the bowl stays for the time it takes to understand that it has gone mute, and then it leaves, fast, and does not come back. No bats, for the same reason turned inside out. Very few insects that fly; the wet air weighs on their wings and the sound weighs on whatever they have that stands in for ears. A midge or two, dazed. A moth that got lost in the cloud and will not find its way out.

So the ones that stay are the ones that have no use for hearing themselves. That is the whole population, and it is a strange one, and the longer you stand in the room the more of it you see.

---

The salamander is not one salamander.

You thought it was, because they are the exact colour of the wet stone and the yellow on their flanks is the exact colour of the lichen and because they move at the speed of the seep, so that your eye files them as water and goes on. But once you have seen one you have the shape for it, and then you see a second in the thread beside it, and a third folded into a curtain of the mineral crust with only its head clear, and then you stop counting because the wall has salamanders in it the way it has water in it. Not many to a hand's breadth. One to a thread, more or less. Each with its own line of running wet from somewhere above to somewhere below, and it stays in its thread and does not cross into the next one, and the next one does not cross into it.

They are black and they are glossy and their eyes are nearly the same black as their skin so you cannot tell where they are looking. They breathe through their skin, mostly. They hardly need to move — the thread brings them what there is to eat: the small pale things that live in the seep, a springtail, a worm no thicker than a hair, the occasional dazed midge — and so they do not move, for hours, for what may be days, and when they do it is a single limb reaching and setting down as slowly as the moss standing back up in your footprint.

They have no ear that opens to the air. What they have is a bone in the jaw that rests against whatever the head rests against, and the head rests against the stone, and the stone is loud. They hear the roar with their faces. They have heard it that way since before they were hatched. It is not, to them, a sound; a sound is a thing that starts. This is the ground. This is the flat fact against which any actual event — a worm, a shadow, the cloud coming in — stands out the way a single voice stands out in a silent room. Set one of them down in a quiet place and it would go frantic within a minute, deaf in the true sense for the first time, feeling for a floor that was not there. Here they are not deaf. Here the world has one steady note and they hear it perfectly and the note tells them, all the time, that the room is still the room.

They are long-lived. A slow body in warm water goes a very long way on very little. You have the sense, watching one turn its head across the space of a full breath, that it has been turning its head in this bowl for longer than you have been anywhere.

---

They need still water to breed. That is the one thing they need that the room does not have.

Everything here runs. The walls run, the floor holds water but the water moves through the moss the moment it lands, the throat takes it all. There is no pool. There is no still surface anywhere in forty paces of a place made entirely of water — except on the northern shoulder, where the stone stays merely damp, where six hollows the size of the bottom of a cup each hold a little water that does not go anywhere.

When the time comes — and there is no spring here, no season but the cloud, so the time comes when it comes, some pressure in the warm dark that they feel and you do not — a female leaves her thread. This is the only crossing they ever make. She goes up the damp shoulder in the way they go, one limb and then a long time and then another, and she finds a cup, and she lays in it a few eggs, pale and clear, each with its dark fleck, and she lies over them for a while with her jaw on the rim and the roar coming up through her face, and then she goes back down to her thread and does not return. The eggs sit in the cup. The cup is warm. Nothing comes to eat them, because nothing that would eat them lives here. In time they hatch into small black threads of their own, and the small threads find the seep, and the seep gives each of them a line of wet, and that is a life.

Every salamander in the bowl was hatched in a hand's work. Whoever knelt on that shoulder with a cobble in his fist and struck the stone until it gave him six hollows did not know what he was making. He was making the only still water on the mountain. He was making the place where a thing that does not need to hear itself could go on being born.

You look at the rims of the cups more closely then, because you are thinking about the lime that has been creeping in over them for however long, and how the original telling was that they are still not full. And you see why. The rims are smooth in a way the rest of the crust is not. Polished, almost. A hair of mineral lays itself down over the edge in a lifetime, and in that same lifetime a soft black body slides in and out over that edge some number of times, and the two rates are nearly the same. The cups are being kept open by use. Not on purpose. Nothing here does anything on purpose. But the maker's six small acts have been in continuous employment since he left, and the employment is what preserves them, and if the salamanders ever stopped coming the lime would close the cups within a few hundred years and that would be the end of both.

---

The trees are inhabitants too, in their way, though it is truer to say each one is a country.

You cannot see the tree. You see a hill of green the height of two men and the width of four, and somewhere inside it there is wood, and the wood is enormous and low and has given up on height in favour of holding. What lives on it lives in layers. The bark is under moss. The moss is under fern, whole fern-fields walking out along limbs you cannot see, and in the pockets of soil that the ferns have made over their own dead there are the orchids, small and greenish-white, and on the undersides of the orchid leaves there are things smaller still, and none of it, not one leaf of it, is in contact with the ground. A tree here is a place things live that would drown on the floor. The bowl is a room and the trees are three towers standing in it, and the towers are full.

And they lean. Every year a fraction more toward the drop, away from the walls and the roar and the throat, out toward the cloud and the great deal of nothing beyond it. The trees are the only residents that seem to want to be somewhere else. What they let fall into the bowl — a seed, a leaf, a limb — the moss takes, and nothing of theirs has ever come up out of that floor. So they have turned their attention outward. Whatever children they have are all below, on some slope you cannot see, having gone over the edge on the wind. The three that stand here will stand here until the sphagnum has eaten their roots from underneath and they go over the edge themselves, and there will be no more of them in this place. They know it, if a tree knows anything, and it is why they lean.

---

Go back to the throat, and kneel, and part the moss, and look — not into the dark but at its lip.

The rock there is white. Not the pale grey of the mineral crust on the walls but white, and soft-looking, a felt of white laid over the stone where the water gathers itself and goes down. Touch it and it gives like the skin of something. It is alive. It is a mat of filaments, each finer than anything you could see alone, that lives on the warmth coming up from below and the mineral coming down from above and needs no light at all and has no more idea of the roar than the roar has of it. This is the oldest thing in the room. It was here when the walls were bare and the bowl was a bowl of raw stone and the sound was already the sound. The mosses came later, over the top of its long white patience, and the salamanders came later than that, and the trees are new. The white felt at the throat is the resident who was here first and will be here last, and it has never once been asked to hear anything.

---

Which leaves the sound.

You have been trying, the whole time, to treat it as the condition of the place. The weather, the air. But stand over the throat long enough with your own voice going nowhere and you come to think it is better understood as the tenant. The oldest one. Older than the white felt. It was here as soon as there was a bowl for it to live in and water to make it from, and it has not stopped for one moment since, and everything that has come into the room afterward has had to make its arrangement with it. The birds refused and left. The moss did not care and stayed. The salamanders gave up their outer ears and took the bargain that was on offer: you may live here, warm, fed, never hunted, born in a cup of still water, and in exchange you will never hear anything else. The trees keep their faces turned away from it like people at a table who have stopped listening to a guest who never stops talking. The white felt does not know it is there.

It does not have a body. It has a shape, which is the shape of the room, and it has a mouth, which is the throat, and when the cloud comes in and the walls draw close it gets louder — or rather you get smaller, and the difference does not matter — and you can feel it in your palm on the stone and in your knees on the moss and in your teeth. It is the one thing in the bowl that would go on exactly as it is if every other living thing were taken away.

---

And the maker.

He does not live here. That is the one plain fact about him. He came up through the notch turning his shoulders, and stood in the roar, and stayed some length of time that was long enough to strike six cups in the stone and not long enough to leave anything else, and then he went back down and became whatever he became. Nobody has come since. The moss on the floor of the notch is unbroken except by you.

But the room has him in it the way a lung has a breath in it — which is to say not at all, and also in its shape. He is the only inhabitant of this place who was able to hear himself and chose, for a while, to give that up. Everything else that lives here either never had a voice or was born to the trade. He had one and set it down at the notch and came in and knelt with a stone and worked in a silence so total that he must, after the first hour, have stopped noticing his own hands, and then his own arm, and then that he was a person at all rather than a motion that the room was making. Perhaps that was why. Perhaps the six hollows are not for anything and were never meant to be. Perhaps they are simply the marks a man leaves when he has found the one place on the mountain where he cannot be heard, not even by himself, and has discovered that he can bear it, and has stayed a while inside it to be sure.

He left. Which makes him the only one who can be missed. The salamanders will not leave, and the trees will only leave by falling, and the sound will never leave, and none of them will be missed by any of the others because missing needs a silence to happen in and there is no silence here. But the cups are still there. Being used. Kept open by soft black bodies that never knew there was a hand.

---

The cloud comes in, and this is when you see them all.

The white fills the room and the walls go dim and shine, and the air becomes so wet that a thing which breathes through its skin no longer needs its thread of water to breathe, and one by one, at the speed of the seep, the salamanders leave the walls. They come down onto the moss floor and out into the open, dozens of them, black and yellow against the green, spaced as evenly as trees in an orchard, each one alone. They do not go anywhere. They stand in the cloud with their jaws on the moss and their unseeing eyes, hearing the world through their faces. The three hills of green lean a fraction toward the drop. The white felt at the throat breathes the mountain's heat. The six cups hold their six small stillnesses on the northern wall.

Nothing speaks. Nothing here could. What lives in this place is whatever agreed, at some point in a very long history of agreements, to be held instead of heard — and the room holds it, warm, in the one note, and has never once had to say anything else.
