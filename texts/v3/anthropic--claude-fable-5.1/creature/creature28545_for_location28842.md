Start with what was there before the buildings, because the ribbons need something to have come from.

The plateau held a spring once. Not a large one — a seep in a fold of the rock, a pool the size of a room, fed from below by water that had been in the mountain longer than the mountain had been that shape. It was cold and it was dark, because the fold overhung it, and things lived in it that had never needed to see. When the founders came up through the wind and began their work, they did not go looking for other water. They took the spring. They drew it up out of the fold and told it to stand, and it stood, and it kept standing, and it grew — nobody knows from what; the spring could not have held a fraction of it — until it was four hundred meters across and had no top. And whatever had been in the pool came up with it, the way a leaf comes up in a bucket.

That is the whole population of Ora Hend. Not the founders, who finished and left and are not coming back regardless of what the tool marks say. Not the visitors, who come rarely and stay briefly and are not residents any more than a hand in a pocket is a resident of the coat. What lives here is what was already living in the water when the water was asked to hold still, and was held still along with it.

—

You will call them ribbons because that is what they look like, and because they have no name — the founders never named them, being uninterested, and the spring had no one in it to do the naming. A ribbon is flat, about the length of a forearm and the width of two fingers, and thin enough that when one passes edge-on across a seam-light it nearly disappears. It has no fins, no visible eyes, no marks or mottling. Along one edge there is a fold, deeper at one end than the other, that opens slightly as the creature moves and closes when it stops, and this may be a mouth or a gill or neither. The skin — if it is skin; it may be all the way through the same substance — takes the color of whatever light it is in, and there is only one light in Ora Hend, so the ribbons are the color of the inside of a mussel shell, or of snow at dusk. In the black between seams they are the color of the black. You do not see them arrive. You see a seam-light acquire, for a few seconds, a pale flexing shape, and then lose it.

They swim by undulating side to side, a wave passing down the body from one end to the other, and the wave never comes out quite the same twice. Watch one long enough and you cannot find the period. It is not random — there is a shape to it, a way it leans — but it doesn't close. This turns out to matter, because it is the only thing in the cistern that doesn't. The water doesn't move. The light doesn't move. The grit hangs where it was left. The buildings are exactly as they were the moment the last course was set. And through all of this, twelve pale lengths of something are doing a thing that has never happened before and will not happen again, over and over, without stopping.

They are the clock. There is no other. If you needed to know that time was passing inside Ora Hend, you could not tell it from the stone or the water or the light; you could only tell it from a ribbon, which was at the end of the street and now is not.

—

They follow the streets. That is one of the stranger facts, given that the streets were laid for feet and the ribbons have none. They could go anywhere — the water is the same everywhere, the pressure never changes, there is nothing to run into except the eleven buildings — and what they do is travel the avenues between the seam-lights, turn at the crossings, pass through the doorless openings into the rooms and out again, rise from a street and come back down into a street. Not always; sometimes one crosses a courtyard on the diagonal or hangs unmoving for an hour near a ceiling. But mostly they keep to the plan, that grid that isn't quite one, as if the plan were something they could feel.

Perhaps they can. The seams are the only warmth in the cistern, and the only light, and a thing that lived for however long in a lightless pool may not know what light is but may know it as a slight difference in the water, a texture. The streets are where the seams are. The ribbons, in this reading, are not following the founders' plan; they are following the edge of a sensation, and the plan happens to be where the sensation is. The city was built for people who never came to live in it, and it is inhabited instead by the one thing that can feel the shape of its foundations from a meter and a half away.

Or the founders built the streets where the ribbons already went. That is also possible. Nobody was watching.

—

They do not eat. You will have been told this and it is true: nothing has ever been seen to enter the fold, and there is nothing in the water to enter it. Whatever the spring fed them — some slow bacterial film, some mineral the mountain was letting go of — did not come up with the water, or came up and was stopped along with everything else and hangs, now, somewhere in the black, unavailable. The ribbons should have died in the first months. They did not. The instruction that keeps a grain of grit from settling, that keeps the water at four degrees and the light at its radius, does not distinguish between a grain and a creature. It keeps them as they are. A ribbon in Ora Hend is not alive the way a fish is alive, going forward through time on the strength of what it takes in. It is alive the way the cistern is finished: as a state that has been fixed.

But it moves. That is the puzzle you will keep coming back to. The grit does not move and the ribbons do, and the same instruction covers both, and so the moving must be part of what was fixed. The founders stopped the water at a moment when the ribbons were swimming, and the swimming was kept along with the rest, the way a photograph keeps a blur. They are not going anywhere. They are the shape of having been going somewhere, held.

If that sounds like a cruelty, consider that it is not clear a ribbon can be done a cruelty. It has no eyes to be denied a surface. It had never seen one. It lived at the bottom of a fold in the dark, in cold water that did not move, and it lives now in the dark, in cold water that does not move, and the only real difference is scale. The spring was the size of a room. The cistern has no top. To a thing that navigates by the texture of warmth along a wall, this may not register as a change at all — only as a pool that turned out to be much larger than it seemed, with more walls in it, and the walls in rows.

—

Now the counting.

"Maybe a dozen" is what everyone says, and it is what you will say, and then you will try to check it and find that you cannot. Up curves. A ribbon that rises out of a street comes back down into a street, and there is no way to know, in the black, whether the one that descends at the far end of the avenue is the one that ascended behind you or another one entirely. You can post yourself at a seam and count crossings, but a crossing is a pale shape for three seconds and the shapes are identical. You can try to follow one and lose it the moment it leaves the light, which it does immediately, because the light is a meter and a half deep and the ribbon is a forearm long.

So it is possible that there are twelve. It is possible that there are fifty, spread through a folded volume that is larger inside than the four hundred meters suggest. It is possible — and this is the thought that arrives late, after you have stood at a crossing for a long time watching the same non-repeating wave pass and pass — that there is one. One length of pale substance that came up out of the spring alone and travels the streets, rises, and, because the cistern folds, arrives back into the streets from above at a different place, so that a single creature crossing a single seam-light at eleven points in its long loop would look, to anyone counting, like a dozen.

There is no way to settle it and the cistern is not going to help you. But hold the two pictures side by side. Twelve ribbons, each doing its own unclosing wave, passing one another in the black without any sign of noticing, a population that has no way to be a population because its members cannot find each other and would not know what to do if they did. Or one ribbon, whose path through the folded water is the only line in Ora Hend that goes anywhere, tracing the grid over and over so that the whole settlement is threaded — every street, every doorway, every rise and return — by a single continuous stroke, the way a signature threads a page.

The founders would have preferred the second. Their work was always the kind you didn't notice, and a settlement with one inhabitant who can't be told from twelve is very much their kind of joke. But you have no evidence they knew. They may never have seen it at all. They built the seams, set the light at its distance, told the water to stand and the grit to hang, and left — and the thing from the spring went on moving through what they had made, along the edges of the only warmth, the color of whatever it passed, older than the streets it followed, keeping the only time there is.

—

One more thing, and then out.

Stand in the shallow square depression in the floor of the one room that has it — the hand-span dip with the single line around the rim — and hold still. The tone at the edge of hearing is a little stronger here. It is level. It does not vary with the ribbons' passage or with your breathing. If it is the mechanism of the holding, then this is where the holding is closest to the floor, and the founders cut a place to stand and listen to it and then never stood there.

A ribbon comes through the opening while you are standing. It crosses the seam at the threshold and takes the light, and its wave leans a way it has not leaned before, and it passes within a hand of your face without any change in its motion, and goes on into the black toward the ceiling that the light doesn't reach, and does not come out the other side, because there is no other side. It will be back. It is the only thing here that can be said to be coming back, and the cistern has arranged that its coming back is indistinguishable from its never having left.

That is what lives at Ora Hend. Not the rooms, which were built to be rooms and are. Not the water, which was told to stand and does. Something small that was in the mountain before either, that had no need of a surface and was given a place without one, and that is doing — alone or in twelve, no one will ever know — the single unrepeating thing the founders forgot to stop.
