Begin with the frog, since it is the one thing in the room that will look back.

It is smaller than you first think. The calcite rim it sits on is pale and the frog is the colour of the water, a weak-tea brown with darker bars along the hind legs, and it sits so low and so still that it reads at first as a lump of the deposit itself, something the water built and left. Then the throat moves. It is a common frog, *Rana temporaria*, the frog of every ditch and garden pond in the country, and there is nothing remarkable about it except where it is and how long its kind have been there.

Watch it for a while and you understand what the position is for. The frog is not resting. It is placed. The rim where it sits is the one spot in the room where the falling column throws its spray outward across the pool rather than straight down into it, and anything that comes down the shaft — and things do come down the shaft, blown leaves, a beetle, the insects that hatch in the gutters far above and fall — is carried by the mist toward that rim and set down on the water within a tongue's reach. The frog has found the place in the room where the room feeds it. Or its ancestors found it, and the frogs since have simply been born knowing, the way a cuckoo knows a route it has never flown. There is no pond here to breed in; the basin is too shallow at one end and too violent at the other. But there is the still water in the angle behind the walkway, a hand's width of warm dark water that the column never troubles, and in it, if you look, there are threads of jelly clinging to the moss. Not many. Enough. A few tadpoles a year in water that never freezes is a better bargain than a thousand in a pond that does. The frog does not know that. It only sits where its mother sat.

That is the first thing that lives here, then: not a frog but a seat, occupied.

The second thing is easier to miss because it is everywhere. The moss on the walls looks like a covering, a coat the concrete has put on, but it is truer to say it is one animal, or one body at least — a single sheet of a single species, the same plant from the waterline to the height of a tall man, joined shoot to shoot the whole way round the room with no break except the door. It does not have to be told where the water is. Every stem of it grew toward the mist and stopped where the mist stopped, and that upper line, so even it looks ruled, is the moss's own drawing of the air. It is drinking as you watch it. It has been drinking since before the pipe was warm. It does not need light much and it does not need soil at all, and the two things it does need — water and not being disturbed — the room provides without limit and without variation. The moss has nothing left to want. It is as close as a plant comes to being finished.

The ferns are its guests. The liverworts above it are its poorer relations, taking the air the moss leaves. The pale grass along the walkway is an accident that keeps happening. None of them are really the inhabitants. They are the furniture the inhabitant has grown.

Now look down into the water at the shallow end, near the drain, where it is only a film over the concrete and the light reaches the bottom.

The floor is not grey. It is ochre, a soft rusty orange shading to olive where the water deepens, and it is not paint and it is not mineral. Draw a boot heel across it and it smears, and the smear is slick and slightly warm, and within a day it will have closed over as if you had never been there. This is the oldest living thing in the room and the one that was here first. Before the moss found the walls, before there was any moss to find them, the warm mineral water carried down from the process the microbes that lived in the pipes above it, and they settled on the new concrete and made a skin, and they have kept it ever since. Iron bacteria, most of them — the same orange that stains a spring where groundwater meets air — and threaded through them a green that is not moss but algae taking what light comes down the grating, and under both a thin dark layer of something older still that needs neither light nor air and likes the warmth. The whole basin floor is this. The calcite rim under the frog is built on it and through it; the pale stone is half deposit and half the compacted dead of this film, generations of it, each layer sealed by the minerals it helped to precipitate. The travertine terrace that might exist in ten thousand years will be a reef. It already is one, a centimetre high.

This is the citizen. Everything else in the room is a lodger. The film does not sit or grow toward or wait for anything. It simply continues, at exactly the rate the water permits, and if the pipe ran dry tomorrow it would be the last thing to die and the first thing back if the water ever returned.

So: a seat, a sheet, a skin. Three lives at three speeds, and none of them yours. You could leave now with an honest account.

But before you go, look at the wall of the walkway behind you, about a metre and a half from the door, at the height of a sitting man's shoulders.

There is a patch there where the moss does not grow. It is roughly oval, about the width of two backs, and it is not bare in the way the spalled concrete is bare — not broken, not eaten. It is worn. The concrete there is smooth and darkened and faintly greasy to the touch, the surface of a stone step, a church bench, a stair rail. The moss around it has tried to close in and has been rubbed back so many times that its edge is a hard little ridge, like the callus a tree grows where a chain has been. Below it, the walkway's own surface has the same dark polish in two places, boot-shaped, and the rail above it has been held in one spot so often that the rust is thin there and the iron shows through grey.

Someone sits here.

Not lives — there is nowhere to lie down, nothing to eat but the frog's share, and the door has a corridor beyond it and the corridor has a works beyond that, and shifts, and a clock somewhere. But sits. Comes down the two flights of iron stairs and along the dry corridor and opens the soft-riveted door and closes it behind them, and sits with their back to that oval of wall and their boots in those two dark prints and one hand on the rail, and stays.

You can almost build them from the marks. The patch is low enough that they are not tall. The prints are close together — someone who sits with their knees up, or is small, or both. The wear is deep, deeper than one working life, and that tells you the most important thing: this is not a person. It is a position, like the frog's. Whoever sits here now inherited the place from whoever sat here before, and may never have met them, and did not have to. The room found the spot. The mist throws outward from the column at the far end, and the place on the walkway where it reaches you last and gentlest, where you are warm but not wet, where the noise comes at you from in front and not from all sides, is exactly there. Anyone who came in looking for a place to sit would end up in it. The moss knows where the water stops. The frog knows where the food lands. The sitter knows where the room is kindest, and it is a small, specific piece of wall.

What do they come for? Not the green. You do not need to come to a plant outfall to see moss. Not the warmth either, quite, though the warmth is part of it. They come, you think, for the same thing the moss has, and the film, and the frog on its rim: the absence of a gradient. Upstairs is a works, and a works is nothing but gradients — pressures, temperatures, deadlines, the humming wall, the hundred things that are not yet what they should be and must be made so before the end of the shift. Down here nothing is being made so. The noise, which upstairs would be a fault to be found and fixed, is here the one thing that cannot be wrong, because it is not a symptom of anything. It is just the water letting itself go. Sit in it long enough and it stops being loud. It becomes the size of the room, and then the size of your head, and then it is not a sound at all but a condition, like the dark in a closed cupboard, and inside it you cannot hear yourself think, and after a while you cannot hear yourself not thinking either, and that is the thing you came down for.

Perhaps it is a fitter on nights. Perhaps it is the person whose job it is, once a month, to look at the drain grate and confirm it is not blocked, and who has stretched that ten-minute job into forty for thirty years and will train their replacement to do the same without ever saying so. Perhaps it was, once, a boy, and then a man, and then a woman, and then whoever is on the rota now. The wall does not distinguish. It only records that someone comes, and has always come, and leans back, and the moss grows toward them and is pushed away.

They leave no other trace. No tin, no cigarette end, no name scratched in the deposit. That, too, tells you something — that whoever sits here sits like the frog sits, taking only the room and adding nothing to it, and would be embarrassed to be found. You will not find them. You have come at the wrong hour, or they have heard the door, which is impossible in this noise, or they simply are not down yet. But the patch is warm. Put your palm flat against it. Perhaps that is only the wall, and the wall is warm everywhere.

So this is who lives in the room, if living means having a place in it that the room made for you and that would be empty without you: the film on the floor, which was here first and asks least; the moss, which drew a line in the air and lies down against it; a frog on a seat of stone that is partly its own dead; and a mark on a wall the shape of a person who has found the only quiet in the works at the bottom of the loudest room in it.

Four tenants. None of them is doing anything. That is the whole of the lease. The room takes in what has stopped running down a slope — the water first, and then, in the long slow order of things able to notice, everything else that wanted to stop — and it holds them, and it goes on holding them, and the noise goes on, and the door is still open behind you, and you find that you have not moved for some time.
