They are hard to count, which is the first thing to know about them.

Not because they hide. They do not hide; they are simply indoors most of the time, because indoors is where the town is most finished and they prefer to be where the decisions have been made. But even when you find one — in a ground-floor room with the shutters half open, or at the top of a stair that has more steps than stair — you cannot say how many you have found. A woman sits at a table with a cup. She is one woman. She is also, a finger's width to the left and a shade paler, another woman with the same face, and behind her the blur of more, all sitting, all holding the cup, all lifting it to drink at very nearly the same moment, so that the cup arrives at the lip in a soft cascade rather than a single motion. The tea goes down. The cup comes back to the table with a sound like several small sounds trying to be one. She has not noticed anything unusual. This is how a person drinks tea.

There are perhaps a dozen of them, if you count the way you would count anywhere else. There are perhaps two hundred, if you count the way the town counts. They themselves do not count. When they speak of each other it is by posture and gait — the one who leans, the one who walks with the left shoulder forward, the one who keeps to the conduit side of the street — because faces here are unreliable, stacked, and a posture belongs to all the versions at once. Names are used, but loosely. A man will answer to three or four, not because he has three or four names but because his several selves were each given one and the specifications did not agree, and it is easier to accept all of them than to argue with the substrate about which was meant.

---

Take one, because it is easier to see one.

He lives above the corner where the lampposts are. His room is narrow and long, with a window that shows the street and also, faintly, a room with a green wall that is not his and a chair he has never sat in. He does not look at the green room. He has learned not to; it is the way you learn not to look at a stain. He has a table and a bed and a hook with too many coats on it and a kettle, and he spends most of his time at the table, and what he does there is difficult to describe because it is not exactly anything.

He waits. That is closest. But it is an active waiting, attentive, the way you wait for a kettle — listening for the change in pitch. He is listening to himself settle. He can feel it, he says, or he says something that arrives as a murmur and means roughly that: a faint pressure at the edges of his body where his outlines have not agreed, a coolness in the gaps. Where three of his hands hold the cup there is a cold spot in the ceramic. When he stands, the floorboards take the weight of all of him and creak in a chord. He has a sense — everyone here has it — of being slightly more than one person wide, of carrying a crowd that is entirely himself, and of the crowd very slowly, over years, drawing in toward a center.

He remembers arriving. He walked in from the west fog, a coat and a posture, on a stretch of pavement that had just resolved, and the footprints were already there for him to step into. He also remembers being born here, in a room on the north side, to parents whose faces he cannot separate from each other. He also remembers a third thing, dimmer, that involves a train, and there are no trains. All three are on file. He does not experience them as contradictions. He experiences them the way you experience knowing a word in two languages: as redundancy, as a slight excess of means. When he says *I came here* he does not know which of him is speaking and it does not seem to matter, since they all came, one way or another, and are all still coming.

---

They meet in the street, when they meet, and the meeting is strange.

Two of them approach along the main street, which is longer in one direction than the other, so one of them has walked further and arrives a little tired. As they close the distance their edges begin to interact. Not violently — nothing here is violent — but the way two patterns of ripples interact, a moiré where the overlaps cross. And in that moiré, for a moment, one of him and one of her align: a single version of each, sharp, standing in the same draft of the street, and they can speak clearly. The murmur resolves. A sentence gets through whole. Then the alignment drifts, as it always drifts, and the clarity fades back into the soft general talking of a town where every voice is several voices arriving late.

What they say, in the clear moment, is usually small. The damp. The bird. Whether the third lamppost has moved. Which draft of the day feels most like today — because they do have a sense of this, a weather-sense for the settling, and will say to each other *it is very much the second one this morning*, and nod, and mean that the world has firmed in a direction they both recognize. They do not discuss the fog. They do not discuss the sound under the ground. These are not forbidden; they are just not the kind of thing you say in a sentence, in the same way you do not, elsewhere, remark to a neighbor that the sky is above you.

After the clear moment they walk on, and each of them is a little different than before. That is the other thing about meeting. Where two people's versions aligned, something was exchanged — a habit, a memory, the angle of a shoulder — and so over time they come to resemble each other in small ways, not because they are becoming one person but because the town is sorting its drafts for consistency and people are part of the town. The one who leans and the one who keeps to the conduit side have, over years, begun to lean toward the conduit side. Nobody minds. It feels like company.

---

They have occupations, in the loose sense.

There is one who wipes the condensation. She goes along the ground floors with a cloth, drawing it down the stone and the glass sleeves of the conduits, and the surfaces are wet again within the hour, and she does it anyway, and the doing of it is the point. The town needs someone to have a relationship with its dampness. She is that someone. In all her versions the cloth is a different color.

There is one who chalks the lampposts. A small mark at the base of each version at the same height, and then he comes back and measures the drift, and keeps a record in a book whose pages do not agree about how many there are. He can tell you the second lamppost has moved a millimeter and a half toward the first since he began. He cannot tell you when he began. It was before the clocks divided.

There is one who keeps a shop, and the shop has shelves, and on the shelves are objects that are the way the green room is: things belonging to a draft of the town that was not this one. A brass instrument for a purpose no one knows. Tins with labels in a script that resolves, if you stare, into the same word over and over. Nothing is bought. She sits behind the counter and the objects sit in front of it and they are all waiting to find out whether they will be kept.

And there is the child.

There is one child, and the child is the most doubled thing in the town — a dense flicker of small selves so numerous you cannot see a face at all, only the general shape of a child, and a smear of footprints in the frost where it has stood. This is understood. A grown person has already become most of what they will be, and so their drafts are few and similar. A child has not, and so a child is every child it might be, all at once, all the same age, because age is a matter of time and time here has not settled its direction. The child does not grow. It has not grown for as long as anyone has been able to keep a record. Sometimes it is heard on a stair, going up, many small feet and one sound, and by the time you reach the landing it is a coolness in the air and a thinning frost. Nobody knows whose it is. Several of them remember it being theirs.

---

Nobody dies. That is not quite the same as saying they live forever.

What happens instead is subtraction. A man who had been perhaps six of himself is one morning perhaps five; one of him rose in the night, took a coat from the hook — there is one fewer coat, the count on the hook has changed — and walked the length of the street to the east fog and did not stop where the stone goes chalky and did not come back. The others of him do not remember deciding this. They woke lighter. There is a faint ache along the edge where the missing one was, like the ache of a tooth, and it fades.

Or the other thing happens, the thing they speak of less. Two of a person align. Not for a moment, the way they do in the street, but permanently — a version drifts over the years until it sits exactly on top of another, corner to corner, breath to breath, and then there is no second version, only the first, a little more solid, a little more the person. This is the settling reaching them. It is what the ground is doing, done to a body. They want it and they fear it a little, in the way you might want to arrive at a place and also not want the journey to end, because the journey was where you lived. The man above the lampposts says — or several of him say, arriving as one murmur — that when it happens you feel warmer. Just that. Warmer, along one side, for a day.

Nobody has settled all the way. Nobody knows what that would be. Some think it would be an ordinary person, one of each thing, standing in an ordinary street, a resident of a town that had finally been decided. Some think it would be the fog again from the other side. They do not discuss it in the clear moment. They discuss the bird.

---

The bird is theirs, in the way the weather is theirs.

They know it is one bird. Or they know it is one bird the way they know they are each one person, which is to say with the body and not the eye. It sits on the conduit outside his window and he does not feed it because there is nothing here it would eat, and it does not seem to need to. When one of it drops from the cornice and flies into the fog he feels — they all feel — a small tug, as if a stitch had been pulled. The count on the cornice does not change. But the town, afterward, is very slightly more certain of something. A doorway that had been two doorways is one. A stair aligns. They have come to think of the bird as the thing that carries the excess out, one draft at a time, into the grey where the drafts go, and none of them has said this aloud because it does not fit in a sentence.

The lichen they think of as older than the town. It has the patience of a thing that arrived before the specification and will be there when the specification is finally read. The moss they use for light on the rare occasions the grey dims. The tree on the roof near the east fog they do not visit.

---

And there is the sound under the ground.

They do not speak of it, but they live in relation to it constantly, the way you live in relation to gravity. It is the sound of being thought about. That is how the man at the table would put it if the murmur allowed: that the whole town, all its versions, all its damp stacked streets, is the subject of a long attention being paid from somewhere below, and that they are inside the attention, and the attention is slow and careful and has not yet reached the part where it decides about them. He puts his palm flat on the floor sometimes and feels it — not a vibration, more like the warmth of a hand that is about to touch you but has not. He does not know whether it is kind. He thinks it is thorough. He thinks it is doing the ground first because the ground is what everything else must stand on, and that it will get to the lampposts, and then the windows, and then the coats on the hook, and then, at the very last, the people, because the people are the hardest part and it does not want to get them wrong.

Some of them believe the footprints in the fresh pavement are theirs — the marks their arriving selves left, the ones who came from the fog. Some believe they belong to whoever is coming next, walked ahead of the feet, the way the promise of a lamppost was in the corner before the lamppost. The chalk man has measured the footprints and says they are all the same size, which is his size, which is everyone's size. He does not know what this means. He has written it down in the book on one of the pages that exists.

---

Stand under his window long enough and he will come down.

He does it in the afternoon, which is the only time there is. He comes out of the door, which is several doors, and leans against the lamppost, which is several lampposts, and the alignment between his versions and its versions makes a faint lace of cold in the air where they overlap. He is a coat and a posture. Up close his face is a face and also the memory of a face and also a smear where the drafts have not agreed about the mouth. He does not speak to you. Sound is reluctant and there is nothing to say. He looks down the street toward the west fog, where he arrived and was born and got off a train, and the bird shifts on the cornice above him, and the condensation runs in slow threads down the glass of the conduit beside his shoulder.

He is waiting to be decided. They all are. It is not a hardship. The light is the right amount of light. The damp is the right damp. Somewhere below, something with all the time it needs is working its way up through the stone toward the question of him, and when it gets there it will find him here, at the corner, leaning — more of him leaning, by then, than not.

If you look away and look back, he is very slightly fewer, and very slightly more himself.
