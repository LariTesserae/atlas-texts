Start with what can be counted, because the place invites counting and then, gently, defeats it.

There are forty-one wrens. There are forty-one holes of the size a wren would want, and each one has a wren in it, and there is not a forty-second wren anywhere on the terraces or in the air, so the number is forty-one. Eleven toads, in eleven doorways. One slow-worm. One stoat, one heron, one badger, and in the second tier a number of mice that cannot be counted because they are inside the wall, though the smell of them is there and the small dry droppings on the ledge are there and are not, when you look closely, very old, or very new, or anything. Snails past counting. Snails in the hundreds, in the low thousands, packed shell to shell along the mossy joints like a cargo somebody has stowed.

It takes a while — the same while it takes to notice that nothing is dead — to notice that none of them is doing what it is for. The wrens do not forage. A wren in the ordinary world is a thing that cannot be still, that goes about the undersides of things with a kind of furious housekeeping, and these are still. They sit with their heads drawn in and their eyes open. The heron is standing over eleven cisterns full to the finger with water in which there is not one fish, not one newt, not the shadow of a beetle, and it is not waiting for anything, because waiting is the posture of a body that expects a change. The toads' throats move and nothing goes in or out. The stoat goes from one hole to another and the wrens beside those holes do not so much as turn to it, because the stoat is not hunting, and they know it is not hunting, and in some manner that is the whole of what they know.

Nothing here eats anything. That is the second thing the place has arrived at, after the level of the water. There is no hunger in it. The animals are not living in the hollow the way animals live in a wood. They are living in it the way the mallet lives on its shelf: set down, in a place that keeps what is set down, with the moss grown up to them in a collar and stopped.

So the question shifts. Not what lives there — the wrens live there, the toads live there, the badger in its black chamber lives there beyond any doubt — but what it is that they live *in*, what the tenancy is a tenancy *of*. Whose house.

For a time, standing on the fourth tier with your breath going nowhere, you may think it is the cold. The cold has the qualities of an occupant. It arrived after the builders, or during them, and it took the whole hollow and has not left. It drinks the air, wringing the moisture out of it onto the stone. It swallows sound. It furs the north ledges and lets the south alone, which is a preference, which is very nearly a character. Everything else that is here is here on the cold's terms — the saxifrage flowering in it, the frost stopped at its chosen edge, the water clear as it is because cold water holds nothing in suspension. You could say the cold is the citizen and the wrens and the moss and the silence are the furniture of its rooms.

But that is not quite right either, and you know it is not right in the way you know a step has been cut a fraction too high before your foot has finished the step. The cold is the house. The cold is the settled air and the sweating stone and the still water, which is to say the cold is the *condition* of the place and not the thing that is in the condition. A room is not its own occupant. Something is in the room.

Go into one of the chambers.

Bend double at the throat of it, where the stone is grey and the light gives out, and then straighten — you can straighten; the chambers are taller than the doors — and find that the dark was only the length of your body and now the stone is pale again and the light has got in somehow around the corner, from below, from nowhere, the way it does. The chamber is small. Nothing in it is more than three feet from anything else. The walls are the same split grey stone as outside, wet, beaded on every underside, mossed in every joint, and the floor is one flat slab, and the far wall has a ledge cut in it at the height a body would sit on.

Somebody is sitting on it.

Do not call it a body. The place has no death in it and you have already seen that; the fern that has no brown frond, the cord that has not rotted. This is not a corpse and it is not bones and it is not a preserved thing either, not the leather that bog water makes of a person. Say what is there. A figure, sitting, knees up, hands on the knees, in the plain attitude of someone who has finished a piece of work and sat down to look at it. The skin has the quality of the stone: pale, wet, faintly luminous, beaded on every surface that faces down. Moss has come up the shins from the slab and stopped at the knee. Lichen, grey-green, has taken the backs of the hands. The hair is a hank of something the colour of ash-wood and has been let go. The eyes are open, because in light that comes from everywhere there is nothing to close them against, and they are not looking at you, and they are not looking at nothing, and they are not looking at the wall, and the difference between those three is what you stand there trying to feel and cannot.

No breath hangs.

That is the thing to hold onto. In this air your own breath stands in front of your face like a small cloth; theirs makes nothing. Not dead, because dying is a change and this place has finished with changes. Not alive, because living is also a change, is only a change, is a thing going on. Sitting. Set down. At the state they were built to reach.

There is one in every chamber. You do not have to look; you go and look anyway and it is so. One to a room, as the wrens are one to a hole. Some hold a thing — a wedge of hazel, a coil of cord, in one case a flat stone held in the lap with both hands as if it were the last one and there had turned out to be no place for it. Some have moss to the waist and some only to the ankle, and it is not clear whether that is a measure of how long each has sat or only of how wet the particular room is, and the distinction does not seem to be one the place recognises. Their faces are alike in the way the faces of people who have done one thing together for a long time are alike. Whatever they were before the work, the work has made them the same, and the sitting has made them the same again.

These are the ones who agreed not to speak.

That is what the silence is. Not deadening, not the moss and the packed stone and the air that will not carry — those are the means of it, but the silence itself is a thing that was decided. The wrens keep it because the ones in the chambers keep it. The heron keeps it. The drop that falls from the lintel and is gone before it has quite been a sound is keeping it. There was a moment, once, before the frost had chosen its edge, when the last course went on the last wall and the last channel was cut to its hairsbreadth fall and the water rose to the finger below the rim and stopped, and somebody looked around, and nobody said anything, and that not-saying has been going on since and is the tenancy. They live here. They are the ones who live here. They live here the way the water lives in the cisterns: held, full, clear, without a ripple, to a level.

There is one more.

You find her — it is a her; the place, when it comes to this, is particular — on the floor of the hollow, in the last courtyard, where the saxifrage is. The largest cistern is there, a square of stone a body's length on each side, brim-full of the pale sky. She is not in a chamber. She is sitting on the cistern's rim, on the north side where the frost is, with her feet on the moss of the courtyard and one hand on her knee and the other hand laid on the coping stone beside her, flat, palm down, the fingers over the edge. The tip of her first finger touches the water. The water stands a finger's width below the rim because that is where her finger is.

She is the level.

You understand — as far as anything here can be understood, which is by the feet and not the head — that this is the office she was left with, or took, or was: the last one to sit down, and the one who did not sit down in a room. The others went in. She stayed where the water is. The channels feed the cisterns and the cisterns feed this one, by some fall too slight to see, and the water comes to her finger and does not go past it, and if it did go past it — if the drip outran the cold, if the moss let go more than it held — then something would move, and it would be the first thing to move, and it would be her. She would take the finger out. Or she would put her hand in flat to the wrist. Or she would stand. And the wrens would shift in their holes, and the heron would take the one step, and the stoat would come out of its hole and not go into another, and the thing that has been arrived at would be a thing that was once arrived at, and the place would begin to have a history.

It does not happen. The water is at the finger. Her face is like the others, wet and pale and lit from underneath, but it is turned a little downward, toward the hand, in the way a face turns toward a hand that is doing careful work — the face of someone threading a needle, or feeling for a level with the flat of the palm, or holding a thing still while someone else marks it. Moss has come up over her feet. Lichen has taken the coping stone around her hand and has taken the back of the hand and has stopped, very exactly, at the water.

That is who lives there. A town of people who finished, and one who is still finishing, by the width of a finger, and has been at that last finger's width for as long as there has been a sea. The wrens are hers. The toads in the doorways are hers. The badger in its black room is a magistrate of long standing and sits, when you go and look, in the same attitude as everyone else, with its hands on its knees. Nothing needs her to be anything but where she is, and she is there.

You climb out. On the fourth tier you pass the shelf with the mallet on it and do not touch it, because you know now what it would be to set a thing down here, and you would like to keep your hands. At the lip the wind finds you, and it is loud, and behind you the ground closes without a rise or a marker over the crowded silent town and the one hand laid flat on the stone and the water standing where it stands.
