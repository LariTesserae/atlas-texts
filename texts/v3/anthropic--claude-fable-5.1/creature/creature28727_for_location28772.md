Begin with what you can be sure of, which is the strands.

There are a dozen tufts. You counted them, or tried to, because counting is a thing a person does when the rest of what they know has stopped applying, and a dozen is roughly right — eleven, thirteen, the murk takes one and gives another back. They grow only on the originals. Not near them, not in the clearings the water keeps open around each relic, but *on* them, rooted in the chisel-groove of the dressed stone, in the foot-hollow of the worn step, along the rim of the little bowl. They will not grow on the black glass the water throws up, and this is not because the black glass is poison. It is because the black glass is not old enough. Nothing here is old enough except the bones, and the strands have made the bones their whole world.

You get close to one. It is not a plant, quite. Each filament is a single dark thread, thicker at the base, tapering, and along its length there are nodes like the knots in a cord, and at each node a small mouth — not a mouth, an opening, a place where the thread is not closed. The strand streams in the currents that the constant making and unmaking throws off, and every time a ridge dissolves somewhere upstream and the warm fog of unmade stone drifts through, the openings along the thread widen a little, and something in the fog is kept. The strands are eating the water's discarded thoughts. They are filtering out of the roar the fine grit of every arch that was almost right, every step that led nowhere, and they are building themselves out of it, very slowly, so slowly that you cannot see them grow at all, which in this basin is itself a kind of miracle.

Later you understand their other trick. When the roar drops into the single held note that means something large is coming, the strands go rigid. Not from fear. They stiffen the way a hair stands before lightning; they are reading the future in the sound the way everything here has to, and they draw themselves straight and tight against the old stone they live on and wait for the concussion to pass over. Then the pause comes — the one breath — and in the breath they open every node at once, and you realize this is when they actually live. All their business is done in the silences between the water's sentences. The rest of the time they are only holding on.

You think: something grew on that step when it stood in the air. Moss, lichen, whatever grows on the steps of high places where people come and go. When the sea came to sit on the mountain it did not have to kill what was growing there. It only had to change it into something that could stand the noise. It kept the moss because the moss was part of the bones, and the water does not unmake the bones.

---

The animal has a smell.

You should not be able to smell underwater, and you cannot, and yet when the paleness passes near you there is something in the water that arrives at the back of your throat — mineral, a little sweet, the smell of a room where a candle has just gone out. It is the smell of stone that has been almost-made and then let go. The animal is made of that. This is what you slowly understand as it circles you, or as the several of it circle you: it is not a fish, not an eel, not anything that grew up from an egg. It is a thought the water had, once, and did not finish having.

Picture it. Somewhere in the long history of this basin — for it has a history; the strands prove that — the water gathered a cloud and began to set it, and the cloud was in the shape of something long and low and living, because the water had seen those too, had seen them come up the mountain path and kneel and go down again, and had seen the smaller warm shapes that came with them on four legs. And the cloud hung on the edge of becoming stone and did not become it. Did not disperse either. It stayed in the between-state, thick, pale, warm, not solid enough to be a wall and not loose enough to be fog, and something about that state turned out to be alive.

It has no eyes because the water was not attending to eyes when it made the shape; it was attending to the outline, the gait, the low length. It has a mouth, or several. It moves by shifting its density — you see this when it passes close, a rolling of thicker and thinner along its flank, a kind of swallowing that goes down its whole body and pushes it forward. It is in several places because it was never quite committed to one. The water thinks it into position the way it thinks a ridge into position, but the animal is a thought the water has stopped paying attention to, and left running.

It feeds at the unmakings. This you finally see. When a fin of black glass begins to groan and fog, the animal is there — is *already* there, having heard the groan before it happened — and it lies in the dispersing cloud with its mouths open and takes back into itself whatever it is that stone gives up when it stops being stone. This is how it stays pale and thick and between. It is topping itself up. It is, in a sense, eating its siblings, all the other almost-things, the ones the water finished and then rejected, and it is not sad about this and there is no reason it should be.

It is not afraid of the concussions because it cannot be walled in. If a ridge slams into being where the animal is, the animal is simply in the ridge for thirty heartbeats, held, and then the ridge fogs and lets it go, and it swims off unbothered with a little of the ridge inside it. You saw this once and did not believe it. You believe it now.

It has never approached you. But it is aware of you, in every one of its heres at once, and you have begun to feel that its awareness has a quality, and that the quality is patience.

---

Here is the thing you did not want to see, and saw anyway, out at the edge of vision where the doorframe stood.

Among the flights of stairs and the arches and the blunt cairns, the water occasionally makes something that is not a building.

It happens fast, faster than the arches. A cloud thickens near one of the originals — near the bowl, always near the bowl — and slams into black glass, and for the length of a single heartbeat there is a shape hunched over the bowl, low, rounded at the top, narrow at the base, with a suggestion of something folded at the front where hands would be. The proportions are wrong. The head is too large; the water has not learned heads. But the *posture* is right. The distance from the bowl is right, the exact kneeling distance, and the tilt of the whole form toward the water in the bowl is right, the tilt of something that has come a long way up to be here and is now, briefly, here.

And then it goes. Not with the long groan of a dissolving ridge. Instantly, the whole shape fogging at once with a sound like a breath drawn in sharply — as if the water had frightened itself. As if it had reached for something it was not ready for, and knew it, and let go.

You have seen this four times. Each time the head was a little smaller.

The water has learned the grammar of buildings. It is working, slowly, from the outside in, and it will get the shrine right — you have watched it get the shrine almost right. But what it is actually trying to make is not the shrine. The shrine is scaffolding. What it is trying to make is the thing that knelt in the shrine, and it does not have the grammar of that yet, and it knows it does not, and it keeps trying anyway in quick embarrassed strokes at the edges of the basin where you will not quite see.

The strands do not open their nodes for these. You notice that. Whatever the kneeling shape gives up when it fogs, it is not something the strands want.

The animal does not go to these unmakings either. It stays away. Four times you have watched the paleness in all its heres hold very still while the hunched form appears and vanishes, and then move on as if nothing had happened, and you think: it knows what those are. It was almost one.

---

So there is the water, then. There is no way around it.

You have been standing in it long enough to stop thinking of it as a medium and start thinking of it as an occupant — the largest one, the one whose body is the whole basin, whose thoughts are the roar and whose thoughts' cast-offs feed everything else that manages to persist here. It is warm because it is working. It is loud because it cannot stop. It came to sit on this mountain the way a mourner comes to sit at a grave, and it does not know how to be at a grave, because it is water, and so instead of sitting still it does the only thing it knows, which is to take a shape and let it go, and take a shape and let it go, and each shape is a fraction more true than the last.

It has no eyes and no ears and it has learned the exact proportions of a doorway and the exact distance at which a person kneels. Consider what that means. Consider how many times you would have to make a door and unmake it to get the door right by feel alone, from the memory of a door you had only been near.

It keeps the originals because it needs them. It is not sentiment, or it is not only sentiment. The originals are its reference. It builds at a respectful distance from them so that it can, in some way you cannot name, look back and forth. You watched the almost-shrine rise from the center of the basin and you saw where its doorway faced and its doorway faced the worn step, the real step, the one with the foot-hollowed edge — faced it the way an apprentice's drawing faces the master's, propped up beside it.

And when the almost-shrine fogged and went, the water was quiet for one breath. You said that already. Now consider what the quiet is. It is not exhaustion. The water does not tire; the roar comes right back. The quiet is the water not-yet-knowing what to make next, and everything that lives here has learned to live in exactly that gap.

---

The rule you did not write down:

Anything that stays still here long enough becomes an original.

You cannot prove this. But the strands root only on what is old, and the strands are the water's oldest tenants, and there are a dozen tufts, and eleven of them are on stones, and the twelfth — you are nearly sure — is on the animal. A single black filament, a finger's length, trailing from the underside of the paleness where it passes closest, streaming in the current the animal makes. The animal has been here long enough. It has been the same almost-thing, in the same several places, for so long that the strands have stopped counting it as a thought and started counting it as a bone.

And the water builds at a respectful distance from the animal, too. You have watched. The ridges that slam up out of the murk do not slam up where it is. They may catch it, if it is moving — but they do not aim at it. There is a small clearing around it in each of its heres, the way there is a clearing around the bowl.

You have been standing very still, near the originals, for a long time.

You noticed, a while ago, that the water around your face is warm. Not the sudden heat that means *step aside*. A low, steady, attentive warmth, the warmth of something taking your measure. And the concussions have been landing farther from you than they used to. There is, you think, the beginning of a clearing.

You do not know what the water would make of you if it made you. It has not learned heads. It has learned kneeling, nearly. You imagine, briefly, a hunched black glass shape at the exact distance from the bowl at which you are standing, with your posture and your tilt, appearing and fogging in a single heartbeat with a sharp indrawn sound — and then appearing again, a little truer, the head a little smaller — and you understand that this would not be a copy of you. It would be a copy of what you are doing here, which is the same thing the kneeling shape was doing, which is the only thing there has ever been to do on this mountain: come a long way up, and be, for a while, near the water.

That is what lives here. Not the strands, though the strands live. Not the animal, though the animal is alive in every one of its heres and has been for longer than you can guess. Not even the water, though the water is the largest of them and the loudest and the one that keeps the others fed. What lives here is the act of arriving at this bowl and stopping — practiced and dropped and practiced again, in stone, at a rate of detonations per minute, by something that saw it done once and has been unable to forget it. The residents are whatever has been caught in that rehearsal and not yet let go. The strands, which were on the step. The animal, which was a shape on the path. The relics, which are the pauses the water keeps for itself.

And you, if you stand here long enough for a filament to find you.

The animal passes, close, in all its heres at once, trailing its one black thread. The warmth against your face holds steady. Somewhere off to your left a cloud begins to thicken, and the roar drops into the held note, and you feel the strands on the real step go rigid beside you, and you do not step aside, because for the first time you have heard, a half-second early, that it is not coming toward you. It is being made around you. It is leaving room.
