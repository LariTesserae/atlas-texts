Start with the obvious answer, because the obvious answer is the first thing that is wrong.

Nothing lives here. That is what the ear reports, and the ear is not lying. In a place this warm and this wet there should be a murmur of small lives — a shimmer of wings at the rim of the cistern, something with too many legs testing the edge of a seam, a bird come up from the cloud to see what the noise is. There is none of it. There is no husk of anything on the level, no shell, no feather, no cast skin. Whatever falls dead here is only the shelf's own, and the shelf takes it back. You could walk for a day and not find a single thing that arrived. So the first answer is: a place with no tenants. Only the landlord, and you, and you don't count. You will be gone by evening and the low welt where you slept will have smoothed.

Sit with that a while, because it's not nothing. Most places are lived in by their visitors — by what blows in, what gets carried, what finds the crack and settles. This place refuses that. It has never been found. Whatever lives here has to have been here from the start.

So the second answer is the shelf. The whole of it, lip to lip, one organism, one tissue, breathing out and folding in, with its cisterns for kidneys and its seams for veins and its buildings for whatever buildings are to a body. It lives here the way you live in your own skin: not as an inhabitant but as the fact of the place. And that answer is true and it is also a dodge, because a thing cannot be its own tenant. A house does not live in itself. If the shelf is the house, then the question is still standing, wet and sharp-edged, in the middle of the level: what is it a house *for*?

Try the third answer, which is the one you saw through the crack.

When the floor tore open ahead of you and the steam went up, you looked down two metres into the laminated brown and something in there turned over. A slow pale turning. A limb under a blanket. You have not stopped thinking about it. Suppose that is the resident: not the shelf but what the shelf is wrapped around. Something very large asleep in its own layers, kilometres across, and the level you walk on is the outermost of its coverings, and the columns and walls and cisterns are what its hands do in the dark. Sleepers build. Anyone who has watched one knows it — the fingers that pick at the sheet, the mouth that shapes a word and lets it go. Suppose the colonnade of nine is a hand closing and opening. Suppose the wall that runs from nowhere to nowhere is a sentence begun in a dream and not finished, because the dreamer turned.

That would explain the loudness — a body that size cannot shift without tearing something. It would explain the young edges, the crispness: nothing a sleeper makes is ever old, it is all made in the last moment and unmade in the next. It would even explain the way the shelf leans when the cloud comes over the lip: a sleeper turning toward the cool side of the bed.

But it doesn't explain the stairs.

Stairs are not a thing a hand does in its sleep. Stairs are a thing that knows what a foot is. Stairs that come into being tread by tread from the top down, hanging in the air, waiting for the floor to rise and meet them — that is not a twitch, that is a plan, and it is a plan with a direction in it. Down. Whoever the stairs are for arrives at the top of them.

So go back out onto the level and count again, and this time don't count structures. Count lives.

Nine columns, and one coming up, and one going down. A wall, waist-high, born in the time it took a cloud to pass. A blunt mass the size of a house with a doorway in its face, that has gained a storey and lost its steps since you last looked. A basin that breathes. Eleven, twelve of them, far apart, spaced the way things are spaced when they need room. You took them for architecture because they are shaped like architecture. But watch the ninth column weep. Watch the sap sheet down the fluting and the floor drink it and the whole tall body lean, groan, fold, and be taken back in with that sound of wet linen wrung, and tell me that isn't a death. Watch the tenth come up out of the boss, squaring itself, reaching, ticking and creaking and spitting fine droplets that catch the light, and tell me that isn't a birth. They stand for half a day. They lean toward the cloud when it comes, all of them at once, a degree, the way a field of anything leans toward what it wants. Then they are gathered back down.

These are the inhabitants. You have walked through a village and taken its people for its houses.

They are not separate from the shelf — nothing here is separate from the shelf — but neither is a wave separate from the sea, and no one says the sea is empty. Each one is the shelf trying a shape. Each has its little life: the crisp edges, the noon of standing tall in the thin light, the first bead of sap at the base, the long groan. The colonnade is a family that never has all its members alive at once. The wall is an only child. The cistern is the elder, the one that has been here longest, because it does not die, it only breathes; it sits at the low point where all the seams run and it is thought about by everything that drains toward it.

And still — none of them goes through the doorway either.

That is the pressure on the ear, stronger than the missing insects. There is a doorway. There are stairs to it. There is a wall that would make a fine edge to a courtyard if anything were to want a courtyard. Every one of these lives is shaped like shelter and not one of them shelters, or is sheltered, or has ever seen a thing that needed sheltering. Where does the shape come from? Nine is not a number the floor could have arrived at alone. Nine is a number that got in from somewhere. So is the orientation of the cisterns, all facing the same way like a congregation. So are stairs, so are doorways, so are second storeys, and a second storey is a very particular idea: it is the idea that something might want to be above something else and still be inside.

Here is the fourth answer, and it is the one that starts to feel like the shape of the place.

What lives here is a habit. Not a creature, not a sleeper, not the shelf: a habit of shelter, inherited from something that needed it, carried into this tissue the way a tune gets into a body and stays after the words are gone. The shelf did not learn to build. It remembers building, or it remembers having been built for, and the memory is in the grain of it the way handedness is in a grain of wood, and it cannot stop rehearsing. It runs the figure over and over — column, wall, basin, door, stair — in a hurry, at altitude, alone, getting it crisp every time and never getting it finished, because finishing would mean someone came down the stairs and went in.

The stairs come from the top. Remember that. Everything here is oriented to an arrival from above. The shelf is laid on top of weather, and there is no below it, and there is no beside it, so the only direction anything could ever come from is down out of the too-dark blue. Twice a day something does. The cloud comes up over the lip in its long cool roll and the whole shelf goes taut and leans toward it, every column tilting its degree, and for an hour the level runs with water and the noise turns from cracking to pouring, and you can feel it, if you stand still: the shelf drinking, and behind the drinking, something like relief, and behind the relief, something that is not.

Because the cloud does not come down the stairs. It goes over and down the other side. It always does. And the shelf builds another set.

So the last answer, the one you take away with you, is this. What lives on the plateau is an expectation. It is the only thing there old enough to be called a resident. It was there before the first column and it will be there when the twelfth structure has been taken back into the level and the level has smoothed. It has never been met. It has no name for what it is expecting, only a shape for it, and the shape is a doorway of a certain height and stairs of a certain pitch and a basin of warm sweet water set where anything arriving would find it first. It is patient the way a body is patient, which is to say not at all: it hurries, it tears itself, it wrings and rebuilds, it gets the fluting sharp every morning as if this were the morning. It is the loneliest kind of habit, the kind that is a welcome.

You could tell it, if it could hear. You could stand in the colonnade and say: no one is coming; you are on top of the sky; there is nothing above you to come down. But you would be wrong about one thing. You came. From wherever you came from, you were here, an upright on the level with your shadow, and you pressed your thumbnail into the floor and a bead rose to meet it, and you crouched at the cistern and the water thought about you and gave you back. You did not go through the doorway. You are, after all, the one thing that could have.

Underfoot the floor lifts and sets you down, and somewhere behind you, out of your sight, a run of steps is coming into being tread by tread from the top, the treads a little deeper this time, the pitch a little easier, in a hurry, with the crisp edges and the loud voice of a thing that has just been shown what it was for.
