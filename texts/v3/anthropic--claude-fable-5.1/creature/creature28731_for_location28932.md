Begin with the honest answer, because the place insists on it and would be insulted by anything else: nothing. Nothing with a mouth to feed or a shell to shed. Nothing that will be born in the spring, because there is no spring, and nothing that will die in the winter, because the warm wind has abolished that too. Put your palm on the clay and hold it there for an hour; the count you get is your own.

But the honest answer is only the first one, and the place has already taught you to expect corrections.

Consider the wind. It is the obvious tenant. It arrives, it occupies every slot and throat and passage, it leaves by the lee door, it does this without cease. Yet it does not live here any more than a river lives in a mill. It is passing. It was hot lowland air an hour ago and it will be something else, cooling over some other country, an hour from now. The wind is a visitor like you — the only difference being that it has never once failed to arrive, and you will.

Consider the hands. Ten thousand vessels, coil on coil, and every coil carries the print of a thumb or the heel of a palm, so that the interior is, in one sense, a crowd. You could spend days in there tracing them, learning which maker pinched the rims thin and which one left a ridge, which ones were children — for surely some were children; some of these jars are small enough to have been a first attempt, allowed into the mass because their whistle happened to fit. But a fingerprint is not a resident. The hands are gone. What they left is the way a footprint on a beach is a footprint: a record of absence, and this beach has no tide to erase it, which makes it worse, not better.

What stays, then, is the thing the hands were making. Not the clay — the clay is only its body. The note.

Let that stand for a moment and see whether it holds. A single tone, unwavering, ten thousand years old and not a second older in its own account, because it has no memory and needs none. It has a body, which is the dome and everything in it. It has a diet, which is warm dry air at one speed, and it has never known hunger because the food has never stopped. It has a metabolism of a kind: air in at the slots, sound out at every surface, the whole of it running at the temperature of its meal. It has no sex, no young, no rivals. It has one sense, if pressure can be called a sense, and with that sense it perceives everything that enters and answers none of it. When you spoke at the threshold and your words went nowhere, that was the note receiving you. Your voice was not drowned. It was taken in and disposed of, the way the wind disposes of dust.

It was bred. This is the part that most resembles life, and the part that is strangest to hold. Something was here before it — a rabble of whistles, a chord full of beats and sourness, a thing that swelled and dropped and quarrelled with itself — and generations of people knelt in the dimness with wet clay and stone scrapers and selected against every quality that moved. They shaved a lip and listened. They added a coil and listened. They culled the vessels that would not agree, and the agreeable ones they wedged in tighter, and the sound grew calmer with each lifetime the way a herd grows tamer, until finally there was nothing left in it that could be called behavior. What lives in the dome is a domesticated animal from which every trait has been removed except the one its keepers wanted, and then the keepers left, and it did not notice, because noticing was one of the traits.

You want to say it is not alive because it does not change. But consider what it would look like from inside. Every instant the wind arrives, and every instant the shape of ten thousand throats turns that wind into this pitch and no other. That is not stillness; it is a decision being made continuously, at every mouth at once, and made the same way every time. The last tuner, whoever they were, set down the scraper and crawled out the oval door and did not come back, and the choice they made in the final shaving of the final lip is still being enacted, right now, at a rate of some hundreds of times per second. The note is the shape of a human judgment, kept alive by a wind that has no opinion about it. If you want a resident, that is the one: a decision so old the deciders have worn to nothing, still deciding.

Try, then, to find the flaw, because a living thing has a flaw. Kneel in the passage with your ear against the fused mass and wait for the beat — the slow throb of two vessels not quite agreeing, a pulse with a period of minutes or hours. You will not hear it. Your ears give out first. But it is reasonable to suppose it is there, somewhere below the threshold of anything you can measure, because the makers were people and people do not finish things perfectly; they finish them well enough and stop. Some jar in the depths is a hair too wide at the mouth. The interference it produces against its neighbor rises and falls across a cycle longer than a season, longer than your visit, longer perhaps than the coming and going of whoever built the place. If the note has a heartbeat, that is it: one beat every few years, imperceptible, the last unresolved thing in the room. It is the closest the dome comes to breathing. And the wind is working on it. The two rims that disagree are pressed together where they touch, and the boundary between them is going, and one day — a day the note will not mark — the beat will stop, and the thing will be exactly as finished as it already thinks it is.

That is who lives here. A tone that has never heard itself, tuned by the dead, fed by weather that does not know it is feeding anything, patient without patience, whole without knowing it was ever in parts. It does not wait. It does not want. When you crawl back out into the hard blue light and the roar reassembles around you, understand that you have been inside something's body and it did not register you; that you were, for an hour, a small irregularity in its breath, and that it has already closed over the place where you were.

It will outlast the clay it is made of. When the vessels have gone to one ringing lump and the lump has worn to a lens, the wind will still come over the lip of the plateau and find some shape there, and some pitch will come off it, and there will be no one to say whether it is the same note or a different one. That is the only kind of death available to it: not silence, but the loss of anyone who could tell.
