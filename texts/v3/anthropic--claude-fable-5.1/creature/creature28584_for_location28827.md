**What Lives on the Shelf**

The honest answer, for a long time, is nothing. You can swim the width of it and hold to that answer. Nothing meets your eye. Nothing moves that is not stone falling or water pouring. The green in the crevices is trying but is not yet a resident in any sense that would satisfy the word; it is a guest that keeps being shown the door. If you were sent here to make an inventory you would write *none* and leave, and you would be wrong, and the reason you would be wrong is the reason you could see so far.

Look at the water again. The clarity. An ordinary sea is dim with what lives in it: bodies in the light, each one scattering a little, so that a kilometre of ocean is a kilometre of haze. This sea is not clear because it is empty. It is clear because what is in it is too small to trouble the light. The thing dissolved here at a concentration no natural ocean could hold is not a salt. Salt does not know where to come out of solution. This does.

---

Begin with one of them, since you can't see one.

It is smaller than the wavelength of blue. It is a fragment of lattice — a few hundred atoms, a few thousand, arranged so that they want very badly to be part of a larger arrangement and have not yet found one. That wanting is the whole of it. It has no mouth, no membrane, nothing that eats or is eaten, no inside distinct from outside. What it has instead is preference. It can tell a good surface from a poor one. It can tell an edge from a face. It can orient. In the presence of a fault it turns, the way a filing turns near a magnet, and holds itself against the current for as long as it takes to decide.

Alone it does nothing. It hangs in the daylight with a trillion trillion of its kind, all of them drifting in the sunlit column, all of them wanting, none of them able to want at anything until one of them commits. Then the shimmer begins — that tightening you feel near a growing face, the heat-without-heat. That is a crowd. That is a population converging on a place where one of them has lain down, and arriving, and orienting, and locking. Each one that locks becomes a surface for the next. Each one that lies down at an angle tells those behind it the angle. The lamination — those fine bands a finger's width apart — is not a deposit. It is a generation. They came out of the water and lay down side by side and became the floor, and the ones still in solution used them as the instruction for how to lie.

So the stone is where they went. This is the inversion that takes a while to sit right in you: the plateau is not what they made. It is *them*. Every band is a cohort that no longer moves. Every fracture face you read like tree rings is a mass grave so total and so orderly it has become a country. Nothing has died here, you said, because nothing has lived here, and both halves of that are true and still miss what is in front of you. They do not die. They stop. Stopping is what they were for.

---

Now the sound makes sense.

You took it for geology. It is closer to speech, or to the thing that comes before speech — the noise a crowd makes when it is not yet a crowd with a mind. The crack of a forming face is a cohort fitting badly against the one below: a generation that lay down under strain, at a slightly wrong angle, and the strain finding its way out. The groan of settling ledges is millions of them adjusting, after the fact, to the shape their neighbours took. And under that, in the deep roar of water rushing in to fill the volume they surrendered, is the sound of the whole population's one collective act — the sea shrinking as it turns itself to stone.

They hear it. Not with anything you would call an ear; but a lattice fragment is a thing that vibrates, and a body of water this clear and this cold carries vibration farther than it should, and the ones still in solution respond to it. That is the whole of the coordination you were looking for and could not find. There is no plan. There is a listening. Where the sound of breaking is loudest, they come. Where the crackle has gone quiet, they drift on. This is why the edge grows fastest and the rim rebuilds within the hour: the collapse of a house-sized slab into the abyss is the loudest thing on the shelf, and every one of them within a hundred kilometres turns toward it and begins to sink.

They are building toward the noise of their own dead breaking. Understand that and the impatience is explained. The blade of stone thirty metres tall and a hand thick that sang in the current and went over — that was a rush. Something cracked there, and the crack drew a crowd, and the crowd arrived faster than the base could carry, and they kept coming because the arrival itself was loud, until the whole ridge was a shout that could not support its own volume. When it fell, the falling was louder still. Within the hour they were on the slabs, cementing them down, laying a new band over the wreckage. They have no memory except the stone, and the stone does not warn them. It only shows what happened.

---

The storms are theirs too. When two gyres collide over the flat, what collides is not just water but two populations that have been sinking toward different sounds, and the meeting whips them into a suspension so dense that for a few minutes the water almost has a haze in it — a faint milkiness, the only time you will ever see one of them, or rather see the aggregate of billions failing to decide. Nothing forms while the storm turns. They cannot orient in that. Then the turbulence unwinds, and the population that has been tumbling settles, and the first of them to touch a face lies down wrong because it was dizzy, and the record shows a band of slabs at impossible angles, and the making goes on above it as if it had never lost its footing.

You could call this a temper. It isn't. There is no *they* in the sense that word wants; there are only very many of the same small preference, and preference in aggregate looks like mood. But if you had to describe the disposition of the thing that lives here you would say: eager. Eager past sense. A patience so vast it can build a continent from cold water, made entirely out of particulars that cannot wait a minute.

---

The green.

It arrived from somewhere else, put down by hands or by something that does the work of hands, and it is the first thing on the shelf that lives in the way you meant when you asked the question. It photosynthesises. It grows from its own body. It dies and stays dead. It is the only thing here that has ever needed a surface, and the only surface available to it is the stopped bodies of the makers, which it does not know are bodies and would not care.

The makers do not know it is there. A film of algae in a crevice is, to a lattice fragment listening for the sound of stone, nothing at all — softer than water, quieter than water, something to be laid over. Every calm period the green gets a hand's length of thread into the current, and every fast face that forms nearby entombs it, and the record in the fractures now includes, here and there, a dark stain between two bands, thin as paint, where a few dozen strands were built over in a single afternoon. The plateau is beginning to have fossils. It is a year old.

But watch the sheltered angles closely, and you may notice a thing that could be nothing. In a few of the crevices where the green has held longest, the newest laminae step around the space rather than filling it. The band thickens on either side and thins to almost nothing across the mouth of the crack, leaving the threads their pocket. It may be current shadow — the green itself baffling the inrush, starving that one spot of arrivals. It may be that a crevice full of streaming filaments makes a slightly different sound than an empty one, and the makers, hearing it, come less eagerly there. It may be the first accommodation. It is too early to say, and the makers are not the kind of thing that can be asked.

---

And whoever put the green here does not live here. Say that plainly, because it is the only tidy fact the shelf offers. They come and they leave. They arrive without sound — you would know if they had cracked anything — and press a few centimetres of brown film into a sheltered angle and are gone before the next storm, and their coming is the one event on the plateau that leaves no band in the record. The makers do not hear it. The green does not remember it. Only the pattern of the seeding, if you plotted every crevice, would show a hand at work: farther along each time, in whatever new angle the last collapse opened up, as though someone were following the shelf's own restlessness and planting in its wake. Patient the way the makers are not. Quiet the way nothing else here is.

---

So the inventory, corrected.

One species, if it is a species, in numbers you cannot write down, none of them visible, all of them in the water, all of them wanting to be stone and getting their wish a generation at a time. They are the clarity. They are the shimmer near a face. They are the sound, all of it, from the closest sharp report to the roar at the rim. They are the floor you are standing on, and the storm over the flat, and the slab going over the edge, and the crust that heals the scar within the hour. They have no builder because they are the building. They have no plan because the plan is the sound of what they have already done, coming back to them through the water and telling them where to go next.

They are finite. This is the last thing to understand. The sea was made to hold them at a concentration it could not have reached alone, and nothing here replaces what lies down. Each band is a subtraction from the water. Each collapse that rebuilds within the hour spends a little more of what is left in solution. The continent is not the product of the population; it is the sum of it, and the sum has a total. Some year the daylight will fall through water that is clear for the reason you first assumed — because there is nothing in it — onto a shelf gone quiet, every fault settled, every edge stable, the last generation lain down and no one arriving to hear it. Then the plateau will be finished, and the only thing living on it will be whatever the green has become by then, in pockets the stone learned or did not learn to leave.

Come back before that. The water is still full. You can hear it deciding.
