Start with the birds, because they are what you see, and because everything else here is arranged around them.

They are a tern of some kind, or were, a long way back — the black cap, the narrowness, the way they hang their heads to look down. The bill has thickened. It is not a fishing bill any longer, or not only; it is a tool for scraping crust, for prising a beetle from a seam, for hammering brine-cemented shell to powder and swallowing the powder for the salt. Their feet are wrong for a shore. The webbing is reduced and the claws are heavy and the soles are horned like a camel's, so that they can stand all day on stone that would blister you, and step from spoke to spoke of an iron wheel without feeling the cold of it. Their eyes are ringed with a bare grey skin that keeps the salt from crusting the lids. They live perhaps eleven years. The stone they stand on has a lamina for every year of the colony's life and there are thousands of laminae, so a bird is to this place roughly what a day is to a bird.

A bird here begins as one of two eggs in a scrape that its mother scratched with those horned feet in the top few millimeters of pressed guano, in a space defined entirely by the reach of the neighbours' bills. It hatches into noise. It never hears silence — there is a hush when a cloud crosses, and a dip when the flights go out, but the floor of the sound in this basin is still louder than most places' ceiling, and the chick's first calling is pitched to cut through it, a thin two-note squeal that its own parents can pick out of forty thousand others at a distance of one body. It is fed on what comes up: fish, sometimes, whole and silver and strange, from a water it will not see for months; more often a paste of brine-fly and half-digested things that it takes bill-to-bill, its head inside its parent's throat. It sits. It is shaded by the bodies around it, which is the only shade the basin has, and this is the first thing it learns about the colony — that the colony is shelter, that the direction called *in* is safe and the direction called *out* is where the lizards are.

Then it flies, and here the birds divide, and the division is the most important thing about them.

---

Most of them go. At some hour a young bird lifts with a flight, goes over the lip in the grey vein, and comes back hours later with the smell of water in its feathers and something in its crop, and after that it is one of the travellers. The travellers are the colony's arms. Twice a day they go to wherever it is — a shore, a lake, a marsh, a coast — and do the ordinary work of a seabird there, and bring the result home in their bodies. They are the only reason anything here is fed. And they are, in a sense you can feel when you watch them land, the least at home. They come back tired and salted with a different salt. They land first on the iron — the wheel, the pan, the winch drum — and stand there, and you understand that this is not ceremony; it is decompression. The plain is not the place, the far water is not the place; the iron is where a bird stands to become, for a moment, neither, before it drops into the mass and is one of the colony again.

Some do not go. Not many — a hundredth part, perhaps, though in forty thousand that is enough to be a population. They fly, in that they lift and settle and cross the dish, but they do not go over the lip. Whether this is a failure or a choice or something that in a bird can't be either, you cannot tell. They are fed, at first, by their parents past the usual time, and then they are not fed, and they live. They eat what falls. They work the crust with their heavy bills. They take beetles, which the travellers rarely bother with. They take the down and the dropped fish scales and the shell of the hatched eggs. They are not thinner than the others — they are, if anything, heavier, lower, greyer in the cap. They are the ones you see most often on the wheel: the travellers stand there and leave, but the stayers stand there and stay, and the smooth worn track on the rim where feet fall is mainly theirs.

The stayers are the residents. The travellers live *from* here. The stayers live here.

There are things a stayer knows that a traveller does not. It knows the knock. You will see a stayer on the bare iron of the pan hold still, and something passes through it, a tightening, one and one and one, in time with the slow beat you can feel at the lip in the soles of your feet — and then it goes on preening. It knows the channel between the rails, and does not cross it while the beetles are running, and steps over it when they are not. It knows the undercut on the south wall where the cold comes up, and stands there in the middle of the day with its back to the colony and its breast to the dark, and does not feed. It knows which of the lizards along the margin are the same lizards as yesterday.

---

The beetles are older here than the birds — you can see it in the stone. In the undercut, where the laminae are exposed, there are threads of iridescent dust between the pale layers, a bloom of green-black in the section, going down below the point where you can distinguish the feather. They are a dung beetle that has forgotten dung as a separate thing, because here there is nothing else. They do not fly. Their wing-cases are fused. They are the colour of the inside of a mussel and about as bright, and they move in the shade the colony makes of itself, in currents, in what look like decisions but are the sum of a hundred thousand very small aversions to light. In the channel between the rails they run one way in the morning and the other in the afternoon, and no one has ever seen the turn. Between them and the substrate is the layer that only shows as a shift in the grain: springtails, mites, a pale blind isopod, the things that eat the beetles' leavings and the birds' leavings' leavings, and that are themselves, in the accounting of the basin, the final form of a fish that was swimming in a lake two days' flight away.

The lizards are the same salt-white as the wall and lie flat against it and are cold to the touch even at midday, because they are never in the sun; the sun does not get down the wall on the side they favour. They have no pattern. They have no eyelids that you can see. They are the only thing here that hunts, and they hunt in a strip two meters wide and eighty paces round, and they have been doing it for so long that the strip is marked — a shelf, a smoothness, in the wall where their bellies go. They know the rule that keeps them alive better than they know anything, and the young ones die learning it. A lizard that takes a chick at the fringe is under nine birds at once. A lizard that goes one body-length further in than the fringe does not come out and is not seen, and you may imagine what the beetles do with it, and you would be right.

The spiders are on the iron. They string across the spokes of the wheel, high, and rebuild each night what the turning tears, and take the flies. They are small and grey and they are the only thing in the basin that has taken the machinery for what it is, a thing with edges and gaps, and made a use of it that has nothing to do with salt.

---

And then the salt-makers, who are not here, and are.

Perhaps twenty of them, once. You can guess this from the pan — six meters, a crew's pan, not a family's. They drove the bore, and rigged the wheel to the engine and the engine to a pump, and drew the brine up and spread it and waited for the cool air to take the water, and stood in the basin, and heard this noise. They will have tried to make the birds leave. There is a scatter of shot in one of the laminae, a grey line of lead pellets pressed into the stone like a row of seeds, and above it the guano goes on exactly as before. They will have lived up on the plain in tents or huts that the wind long since took, with nothing in front of them to the grey edge but the hole they'd come to work. They will have lasted a season, or two.

Whether they left or did not, they are in the stone now, in the sense that the stone is what happens here to anything that stops. The rail is a habit the birds have. The iron is a threshold the birds observe. The channel between the rails is a beetle road because two men in a season long dead laid down two lines of iron a hand-width apart and did not know what they were building. The colony did not eject the salt-makers. It did what it does with fish. It took them in and passed them through and kept the mineral.

---

Which leaves the knock, and what it comes from.

There is no belt on the wheel. There is no fire in the engine. The cylinder under the colony moves, one and one and one, slower than any bird's heart, because the bore the salt-makers drove goes down into the cold and the cold is not empty: there is brine under this basin, under pressure, and the pressure comes and goes on a schedule that has nothing to do with the sun. It surges up the bore, and the piston the men installed to pump it is instead pumped *by* it, a fraction of a stroke, and falls back, and the wheel, geared to the piston, comes round a spoke. Then the brine draws down and waits. In between, a film of it weeps from the pipe joints and spreads under the colony and dries, and that is the cement, and that is where the laminae come from, and that is why the stone rings.

So the thing that lives at the bottom is the same thing that lives at the top. It is the going-and-coming. The brine rises and falls, and the birds rise and fall, twice a day, and neither one is aware of the other, and the wheel keeps the two times against each other like a clock with two hands of very different lengths. The salt-makers built the clock without meaning to and then left it running. It will run as long as the pressure lasts, and the pressure has been lasting since before the plain went flat.

---

If you asked what lives here and would take only one answer, the honest one is: a shape. Not a bird — a bird is a day. Not a beetle. A shape of eighty paces across and many thousands of layers deep that consists of an in-and-out, a lifting and a settling, and that has been kept in existence by nothing but the fact that everything that enters it either leaves again with the flights or stays and becomes floor. It has no head. It does not know it is old. It is fed from a water it has never seen by arms it does not know are its own. It has a pulse, and the pulse is not its own either, and it does not know that.

But that is the whole and you did not ask about the whole; you asked who lives here, and the whole does not stand anywhere. So take instead the bird on the wheel, the grey-capped heavy one, the stayer, who has never in eleven years been over the lip and whose entire world is this dish and this noise and this iron. It steps from spoke to spoke as the wheel comes round. Under its feet is a rim worn smooth by its own kind and, under that, a spoke, and under that a shaft, and under that a piston that a dead man set into a bore, and under that the cold, breathing once every twenty or thirty seconds. Above it, the ordinary sun. Around it, forty thousand voices, of which it can pick out none, because none of them is calling it. It knows the knock is coming and it tightens for it and it comes, and the wheel hitches, and the bird steps, and stays level, and does not look down. It has never needed to. It is the only creature in the basin for which this is simply the ground.
