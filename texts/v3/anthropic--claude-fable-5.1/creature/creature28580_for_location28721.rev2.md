Nothing lives here alone. That is the first thing, and it takes a while to see, because the eye wants to sort the room into tenants — these are the birds, these are the insects, these are the people — and the room will not sort. A swallow cup that falls in becomes a wasp's anchor or a lizard's den or the place a woman keeps her seed. The bat that dies on the ceiling is on the floor by morning, in the beetles by nightfall, in the waxy leaves by the end of the year, and in a person by the end of the year after. Ask what lives in the pit and the honest answer is a slow circulation: a few hundred pounds of living matter passing through a few hundred shapes, none of which holds still long enough to own anything.

But you can describe the shapes.

---

The swallows are the oldest citizens and carry themselves like it. They are small, dark-backed, pale at the throat, forked, and they have never in the memory of their kind seen a river or a cliff or another swallow colony; the room is the world, and the plateau is the sky's floor, a hunting ground and nothing more. Every swallow alive was hatched in a cup cemented to a cup its ancestors made, and it knows the whole wall by heart — not as a picture, but as a sequence of approaches, the way you know a house in the dark. Their fear runs downward. Everything that has ever eaten a swallow here has come from below, up the ledges, and so a swallow at rest sits with its face out and its tail to the rock and its eye on the floor.

They are the room's alarm. Nothing moves on the walls without the swallows changing their sound, and everything else in the room, including the people, has learned to read the change. There is the ordinary tearing-cloth argument of ten thousand voices. There is the higher, thinner shriek that means a snake is on a particular section, and the shriek moves along the wall exactly as fast as the snake does. There is the sudden hush, very brief, that means the thing at the seam has moved. Swallows do not know they are doing this. They are only afraid out loud.

---

The wasps are grey and patient and have no idea there is anyone else here.

They build paper out of the same dry stems the people chew for fiber, and they hang their lanterns from every overhang the swallows cannot use, and they go about the room in the warm hours with a preoccupied steadiness that never varies. They work the succulents for gel. They work the guano for grubs. They will land on a sleeping person's lip and drink and leave. A sting here is not an event; a child of the pit is stung as often as she is scraped, and thinks about it as much. When a lantern grows too heavy and falls, the wasps do not mourn it. They begin another somewhere else, and the people come and pull the fallen one apart for what is inside.

There is no winter in the room, so there is no year for the wasps, only a slow rising and falling of the hum as the sun crosses the lanterns and the lanterns swell and fall. If you had to name the room's pulse, it would be theirs.

---

The bats are a tide.

By day they are the ceiling — a texture on the stone, faintly moving, the same ochre-grey as everything above the reach of the light. You cannot count them. Nobody has tried. They are the only inhabitants who leave every night and come back every dawn, and they come back heavy with the outside, and they let it fall. Everything the room is made of, below the swallow tier, has passed through a bat. The black soil an arm deep. The fuel in the hand-sized fires. The fertility that lets a leaf swell with gel in a place that has not seen rain in the life of the stone. The bats are the room's only import. They go out over the finished world and find, somewhere in the dark, the small quick things that live in the grass no higher than a knee, and they bring it all home and drop it, and the room lives on what they drop.

They do not know this either.

---

The lizards are the sun's congregation.

There are the fat blunt ones, forearm-long, slow, tolerant, who will let a child lift them and set them down again. There are the flickers of copper no longer than a finger that live in the swallow cups and eat the wasps that come too low. Between those, two or three middling kinds, ridged and spotted, marked like the wall. All of them spend the day following the blade of light around the room, and where the blade is, there is a line of lizards along the ledge, nose to tail, heads turning together like a row of listeners. They lick the walls in the wet hour. They lick the succulent leaves. They take grubs and wasps and each other. They are, apart from the people, the only things in the room that seem to notice the light for its own sake — that will lie in it past the point of need, eyes half shut, simply because it is warm.

---

The snakes are the wall's patience with a body.

There are not many; the room could not feed many. They live deep in the swallow tier, in the oldest cups, in the cracks behind the cups where the stone first failed, and they come out when the blade is on the floor and the whole room is thick with heat and half asleep. They take chicks. They take lizards. They take the fat swallows too slow to leave a cup. They are thick and blunt-headed and move as if time were on their side, which, in this room, it is. The people do not kill them. It is not reverence. It is that a snake eats a chick and a person eats an egg, and there are only so many nests, and the snakes have been here longer, and everyone who lives in a small room learns which paths are not theirs to step in. A child watching a snake pass below her ledge has the snake's expression exactly.

---

The floor belongs to the beetles and to the dead.

Under the succulents, the black soil is never still. Turn a leaf and it is carrying its own small weather of legs. The beetles are the room's stomach. Everything that falls — guano, feather, husk, chick, bat, person — the floor takes, and the beetles turn it, and the roots follow the beetles, and in a hundred years it is a band of paler dust with a shell in it and a story attached.

---

And then there are the people, who are the only ones in the room who know they are in a room.

They are small. The doorways are waist-high to an outsider; to them they are simply doorways. They are lean the way everything here is lean, and they are the color of the wall, not from birth but from the dust, which is in their hair and the creases of their knuckles and the corners of their eyes, and which they stop noticing at about the age they stop noticing the noise. Their hands are hard and flat-fingered from pressing pellets. Their jaws are strong and their teeth worn down early, because they chew constantly — stems for fiber, stems for the juice that binds the mud, sour pulp, seed — and a woman of forty has the teeth of a much older woman anywhere else.

Their eyes are the eyes of a shuttered house. They see everything in the room's late-evening glow and can find a copper lizard on an ochre wall at thirty paces, and they cannot look at the blade. Nobody in the pit looks directly at the seam at midday. When the light is on the floor they turn their backs to it and work in its warmth like the lizards. When one of them goes up onto the plateau — and some do, a few, at dusk or before dawn, to fetch the black glassy stone from the rubble and to gather the small hard seed from the knee-high grass — they go with their faces down and their eyes narrowed to slits, and they come back squinting and shaking their heads as if from a place that shouted at them.

They speak low. Not in whispers — you could not whisper here and be heard — but in a register the room leaves empty, a band of sound below the swallows and above the wasp-hum, clicking and guttural, pitched to travel along a ledge rather than across the room. To an outsider it would sound like someone talking in their sleep. Across the pit they do not speak at all; they gesture, or they wait until the other has come around. There are perhaps sixty of them, and everyone is kin to everyone, and nobody is ever out of sight of somebody.

---

What they know is the room.

They know the wall the way the swallows know it, as a sequence of holds and ledges, and they can go along it in the dark. They know every nest in their section — which ones are old and which are laying and which have a snake behind them — and they take one egg from each of six and never two from one, and they do not think of this as a rule so much as the way eggs are taken, the way you would not think of breathing as a rule. They know the wasps by sound. A lantern that has gone quiet in the afternoon is a lantern that will fall by the next warm hour, and someone will be waiting beneath it with a hide. They know the succulents individually, which one bleeds freely and which one is dying and which one is old enough to have seed in it, and they know that a leaf broken for gel must be broken low and on the shaded side so it does not draw the wasps before a person can drink.

They know time as a place on the wall. There are no hours; there is the light on the red streak, the light on the fallen tier, the light on the floor, the light on the third ladder. And they know the year the same way, because the blade does not follow the same track every day; it wanders north and south across the room across the seasons, and there are marks scratched into the stone where its edge falls at the turning points — very old marks, some of them, some of them under later mud. The year is a track the sun draws on the wall and then draws back. Nothing else changes with the year, up here, except the cold of the night and how heavy the beards hang in the wet hour.

They know the wet hour better than anything. It is the only hour that has a name in the sense an outsider would recognize. They wake for it as the bats come home, and they are on the ledges with their scraps of fiber before the first beading shows, and the hour is spent entirely in pressing and wringing and pressing again, mouth, bowl, gourd, mouth. Children are taught it before they are taught to climb. The whole room is drinking at once, lizard and swallow and person, and no one is greedy, because there is exactly one hour and it belongs to all of them and the wall will be dry when it is over no matter what anyone does. Water here is not something you find. It is something you are in the room with, briefly, once a day, and then it is in you and the wall is stone again.

---

They are born in the dim and they go into the floor.

Birth is not hidden. There is nowhere to hide it. A woman is delivered in her own low room with the swallows screaming over the door and the wasps working past the sill, and the child comes out into a light that is never bright and a noise that never stops, and is dusted before the day is out, and never afterward knows what it is to be somewhere quiet. Children are raised on the ledges. They fall, sometimes. They are stung constantly. They lie in the sun-line with the lizards. They learn to read the swallows before they learn to speak, because the swallows are louder.

The dead are laid on the floor, among the succulents, at night, and the floor takes them. It is not ceremony so much as return. The beetles come and the roots follow and in a season there is a slightly greener patch and in a generation there is a band in the soil. The people know this. They dig in the floor for the black soil to patch their walls and they turn up the hearth-black of the old floor and the palm-pressed pellets under it and, sometimes, bone, and they set it aside without alarm and go on digging. Their grandmothers' grandmothers are an arm's length down. Below that are the grandmothers of those. The floor is the only thing in the room that is entirely theirs, and it is made of them.

They have a way of speaking of the plateau. It is the finished world. The word they use carries something like completed, something like worn, something like done with. The world was made and then it wore down and was over, and the sky lies on it like a lid, and there is nothing to be done up there but fetch stone and seed and come back. The room is the unfinished place. Something was forgotten here — a crack was left, a hollow wasn't filled — and everything that still happens, happens in it. They do not say this as belief. They say it the way they say the sun is on the third ladder.

---

There is one more.

It waits at the seam every evening. It is on the plateau, not in the room, and in the whole of the finished world it may be the only living thing that stays — the swallows and the bats go out and come back, the people go up and come down, but this stays, up there, in the thin cold, on a table of stone where nothing stands higher than a knee, and it lives on the room's outpouring. When the bats rise in their column it is hunched at the lip of the crack, and now and then there is a snap, and the column scatters and re-forms. It takes swallows too, in the mornings, out of the rope of bodies going up. Nobody in the pit has seen it clearly. From below it is a shape against stars. From above — the few who have been up at dusk say this — it is a shape against the last light, low, patient, the color of the plateau, and it does not move when a person comes near it, and it does not come down.

The people have no name for it that is separate from the seam at evening. The same word does for both. They watch it, when the bats go, and they say nothing, and it is not that they are afraid. It is that everyone already knows, and it belongs to the room as much as the snakes do, and there is nothing in the world to compare it to. It is simply what lives at the edge of the one place where anything lives, and eats what comes out, and stays.

---

So: what lives here.

The swallows, who built it. The wasps, who pace it. The bats, who feed it from outside. The lizards, who love the light. The snakes, who wait. The beetles, who eat everything. The plants, who have decided to be their own wells. The people, who know they are in a room and have made the room the whole of what can be known. The thing at the seam, who has never come in.

And under all of them, passing through all of them, one more inhabitant, older than the swallows and younger than the stone: a small fixed quantity of water, never enough to make a drop, that comes in through the seam on the cold air before dawn and beads on everything and is licked and wiped and wrung and swallowed and breathed out and rises warm to the lid and slides out through the seam at dusk on the backs of the bats and comes back in on their backs at dawn and beads on everything. It has been going around this room since the room was made. It is in the moss and the wasp and the child's eye and the snake's tongue and the grandmother in the floor. It is the only thing here that has never died. Everything else is just the shape it is passing through.
