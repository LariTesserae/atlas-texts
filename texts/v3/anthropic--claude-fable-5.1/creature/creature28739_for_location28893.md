**What Lives on the Shelf**

Ask anyone who has been up and come back and they will say: the shelf-bearers. That is the answer, and it is true, and it is true the way *water* is true when you ask what is in the sea. The plateau from above is one animal repeated past counting. The plateau from below is a country, and a country is never inhabited by one thing.

---

Start with the room, because the room is where nearly everything is.

It is a hand high. It is warm and it is wet and it is, for most of its extent, almost dark — the only light is the pulse along the cartilage cables, a pale flicker that travels a meter, waits, travels another, so that a given patch of moss is lit for perhaps a second in every ten, from one side, and then from another side, and never from above. Nothing in the room has ever seen the sun. Nothing in the room has ever been dry. The rain is continuous and gentle and falls from a ceiling of cold metal onto a floor of moss and rock and grit in the making, and the air moves only when a leg moves, which is always, and slowly.

The room has a climate, and where there is a climate there is a fauna.

---

The smallest are in the moss, and there are more of them than there are shelf-bearers by a factor no one has tried to name. Pale things. Blind things, or nearly — eyes are wasted on a light that comes for a second and goes. Some are like the springtails of a lowland forest floor, jointed and jumping, except they don't jump; they have lost that, there being nowhere to jump to and nothing that hunts them fast enough to need it. Some are worms of a translucent white you can see the moss through. Some are so small that they live on the surface tension of a single drip as it swells on a flange and falls and lands, and they ride it down, and crawl off into the cushion, and wait for the next.

They live by the mouthparts, which is to say they live in spite of them. The shelf-bearers eat everything in reach, and the moss-dwellers are in the moss and go down with it. But the mouthparts are slow — slow the way everything on the shelf is slow — and a thing that can move a body-length in the time it takes a grinding jaw to close has all the time it needs. So the room selects for the one quality the shelf-bearers do not have. Under a million animals that have never been in a hurry, there are a billion that are in a hurry all the time, and the difference in speed between the two is so great that they scarcely register as living in the same place. The shelf-bearers do not know the moss is inhabited. The moss-dwellers do not know the ceiling is alive. Each is weather to the other.

---

Then the seams.

Between one carapace and the next, where the flanges overlap, there is a gap of a finger's width or less, and it is the only place on the shelf where the cold above meets the warm below. Snow slides into it. The dry hard afternoon snow comes down onto the frozen plates and skids into the seams and stops there, a little way in, at the exact depth where the air is warm enough to take it, and it melts and drips, and that drip is different from the others. It carries what the sky carries. The blue flame that runs along the plates on cloudy days is doing what lightning does; it fixes what is in the air into what is in the water. The seam-melt is faintly sour and faintly fertile, and the moss is thickest not where the drip is heaviest but where the seams are.

Something lives in the seams. It is flattened to fit them — a body like a leaf pressed in a book, grey above and white below, with legs that fold into grooves so that in cross-section it is nothing but a plane. It lives half in the frost and half in the thaw, at the line where the snow stops being snow, and it moves along the seams the way water moves along a crack, and it is the only thing on the shelf that you could call quick. Put your eye to a seam and wait. Something will go past. You will not see it; you will see that the line of frost has been disturbed, and then, a meter along, disturbed again. It eats the melt and what is in the melt, and it is eaten — presumably — by the mouthparts when it strays down out of the seam, but it does not stray. It has the seam, and the seam is a road as long as the plateau, and it has never needed anything else.

---

The cables.

They run animal to animal in the warm dark, thick as a wrist, the color of wet horn, slack enough to sway when a leg brushes them, and the pulse moves along them without hurry, a light you can read by if you don't need to read fast. They are not empty. Where the pulse pauses — it does pause, always at the same places, a hand's breadth from where the cable enters the underside of a carapace — there are clusters. Small, soft, the same translucent horn as the cable itself, fastened to it in the way a barnacle is fastened, and faintly lit from within when the pulse is on them and dark when it is not.

Whether they are on the cable or of it is the kind of question the room does not answer. They may be parasites, drinking whatever moves along the strand. They may be the strand's own organs — something the cable grows to do a job at the junctions, the way a nerve grows a knot where it meets another nerve. They may have started as the first and become the second so long ago that the distinction has gone out of the world. There is a point past which the question of what is one animal and what is another stops having an answer, and the room is a long way past it. The cluster on the cable is part of the shelf-bearer the way the moss is part of the shelf-bearer the way the shelf-bearer is part of the shelf. The plateau is a single organism or it is ten thousand species or it is both, depending on where you draw the line, and there is nowhere to draw it that does not cut through something alive.

---

They die.

Not often; nothing on the shelf is often. But an animal the size of a wagon that has been grinding moss under its own roof for longer than any lowland tree has stood does eventually stop, and when it stops, its roof does not. The carapace stays where it is, wedged flange-under-flange with its neighbors, holding up its corner of the ceiling. The legs fold. The mouthparts still. The cable that ran to it goes dark on that side of the pause, and the pulse begins to skip the dead junction, routing round it, the way the whole plateau routes round anything.

What happens under the dead roof is the room's one act of haste. The neighbors' mouthparts come in from the sides — they were always coming, slowly, and now there is nothing pushing back. A fungus that lives nowhere else on the shelf, or lives everywhere and is waiting, comes up through the moss in a fan of dull red like the creeper's color gone dark, and takes the soft parts in a season. Beneath it, in the grit, something that has been described as a worm and something as a slug and is neither goes to work on what is left, and what it leaves is the grit that everything leaves, and the grit goes into the floor, and the floor is a little higher, and there is a shelf-bearer's whole life in a layer of rock too thin to see.

The plates are the last to go. They are cold and hard and the fungus does not want them, and for a long time — a very long time, by lowland reckoning — a dead roof stands in the packed interior with living roofs on every side, indistinguishable from above, frost on it like all the others. Then a young one, made somewhere nearby in the dark, moves under it. And whether the young one eats the old plates or lifts them or simply grows into the shape they define, the effect is the same: the roof is a living roof again, and the pressure of one more body propagates outward, rank by rank, toward the rim.

---

The rim is where the shelf leaks.

The room's warm air has to go somewhere, and most of it goes up through the seams and makes the afternoon clouds, but at the edge, where the front rank overhangs the drop, it goes out. There is a breath at the lip of the room, steady and wet and thirty degrees warmer than the sky, and it rises along the top of the cliff face and freezes there into the ice cords, and in the meter or two between where it exits and where it freezes there is a band of life like the lip of a hot spring.

It hangs. Moss hangs from the undersides of the outermost carapaces in curtains. The coin-leaved thing grows on the vertical rock in a mat. Water beads and runs and freezes at exactly the same line every day, so that the band has a lower edge as sharp as a shoreline. And things come to it that come from nowhere else on the shelf: things with wings.

They are small and dark and they are not birds, or not quite, but they do what birds do. They roost in the fractures of the cliff face just below the overhang, packed in against the warm rock the way the shelf-bearers pack on the flat, and at dawn they go out and down, into the lowlands, and at dusk they come back and sleep in the exhale. They are the only thing that crosses. They carry moss on their feet and seed in their guts and they drop it in the lowlands where it does not grow, or grows a season and dies, and they carry lowland things up on their feet and drop them in the band where those also die, mostly. Mostly. The band at the rim is the one place on the shelf where anything from outside has ever taken. A grass grows there, in a single patch a few paces wide, that has no business two and a half kilometers up, and it is the only plant on the plateau whose ancestors saw the sun.

---

Below the cliff, in the sharp grey apron of scree, there are the fallen.

A carapace that goes over the edge lands the way a plate lands. It does not break — the plates are too hard for that — and it does not weather, and it lies where it fell, galvanized and faceted and throwing back the lowland sun in the same fan of reflections it threw from the crest, for longer than anyone has kept records. Under it, if you lift it, which takes several people, there is a small dark wet space and in the space there is moss. Shelf moss, come down in the seams of the plate, living in the shadow of a roof that is no longer warm. It lives a few years. It is always dying, and there is always some of it, because the plates keep falling. There is a strip along the base of the wall, a few paces wide, that is the shelf's one colony, and it is failing continuously and continuously renewed, and it smells, when you put your face to it, of ozone and crushed green things and something mineral.

The lowland people take the plates. Not often; the apron is a hard place to be, under that glare, in that sound. But a plate is a roof, and it is a roof that will outlast the house beneath it and the village beneath the house, and in the country under the wall there are barns and granaries and one small church roofed in grey faceted metal that came from an animal, and the people who live under those roofs are the only people who have ever lived under a shelf-bearer, and they know it and do not think about it. That is the whole of the shelf's contact with made things: its dead, repurposed, at the bottom of a cliff, by people who have learned not to look up.

---

And the interior.

No one has seen it. The plates would bear you; people have walked out onto them, onto the frost, into the roar, with the whole plateau throwing the sun back into their faces. They have gone a day, two days. There is no water above — every drop the shelf has goes down, into the room — and you can drink from the seams if you lie flat and wait for the melt, but you cannot do it for a season, and a season is what the crossing would take, and there is nothing to cross to. Those who went furthest came back and reported what those who went least far reported: plates, seams, frost, roar, the flicker in the gaps, out to the horizon in every direction, the same. It is the same. It has to be the same. There is no reason it should be otherwise.

But the young are made somewhere. Not at the rim; the rim is the old. Somewhere inward, in the warm dark, at some depth from the edge that no one has reached, the room does the one thing it has never been seen to do, and whatever lives there — whether it is the shelf-bearers themselves, or something else that only the interior has, or something the shelf-bearers become when there is enough plateau around them in every direction — is the oldest inhabitant of the shelf and the only one without a description. It might be nothing. It might be the same animals, older, larger, still. It might be the reason the shelf exists at all.

---

So: what lives there.

One thing, seen from the lowlands, a white band on the horizon that does not move. A hundred things, seen from the room. Or the plateau itself, which is growing, and eats, and makes its own weather, and has a skin of metal and a warm wet interior and a pulse you can see, and is doing the thing that living things do — persisting, and getting larger, at whatever rate the world permits — using animals for cells the way animals use cells for animals. Ask which is the organism and you get the shelf's only answer, which is the roar: everything at once, at every rate, canceling into a single texture, going on.

It is barely started. Whatever lives there has not finished being born.
