Begin with the smallest, because the smallest are the most numerous, and because they are the only living things on the shelf that have chosen to go toward the sound rather than away from it.

The grazers on the towers are jointed, ten-legged, roughly the size and weight of a closed hand, and they have no heads. What would be the head is a plate of shell fused shut, thick as a thumbnail, and behind it the body is a series of overlapping rings that let them flatten against the iron so tightly a knife will not get under them. They eat scale. The white mineral crust that streaks the towers is deposited by the water and eaten by them almost as fast as it forms, so that the towers are in a slow equilibrium between being coated and being cleaned, and the pale bands you see from the shore are the places they have not reached this month. Where the vibration is strongest — at the waterline, in the gyre, on the side of each tower that faces the deep — they glow. Not brightly. A dull rust-orange, like the inside of a kiln seen through a crack. Those who have brought them up in buckets say the glow fades within an hour of leaving the tower and comes back within an hour of being returned to it, and that a grazer kept in still water in a hut turns grey and stops moving in about three days and dies in about six. They are not fed by the shaking. They are fed by *something* the shaking does to the water at the tower's skin, some exhalation, some electrochemistry no one has the instruments to name; but the effect is the same. They are the only animals here that need the roar. Everything else has merely learned to survive it. The grazers would starve in silence.

They breed on the iron. The young are pale and soft and crawl upward, away from the waterline, into the quieter scale near the top of the tower, and they harden there over a season, and then they turn around and crawl down again into the loudest water in the world and take their place in the orange band, and their shells vibrate against the plating for the rest of their lives at exactly the pitch of the note, which is why, if you were to put your ear to the tower — you would not, you would lose the ear — you would hear not one bell but ten thousand small ones, all agreeing.

---

The ribbons are one animal each, though it does not look that way.

Each is a chain of translucent segments, every segment a stomach and a pair of beating fins and nothing else, joined end to end for the length of a boat or two boats or, in the largest ever measured, a hundred and twelve feet. They drift with the gyres in single file, sometimes twenty or thirty in a line, and the line turns as a line, all at once, like a thought occurring to a crowd. They eat the grey snow. The floor of the sea is dusted with it — powdered basalt, precipitated mineral, the ground-up shells of dead grazers — and the gyres lift it and the ribbons take it in through slits along each segment and pass what they cannot use out the far end as a finer snow, and in this way the sea's floor is very slowly being made into ribbons and the ribbons very slowly back into floor.

They have no front. When a ribbon is torn — the boats sometimes tear them; the boats are heavy and the ribbons are not — both halves continue. Neither seems to know which it was.

---

The fish are the ones the boats come for, and the ones who have been longest studied, because they can be held.

They are pale, without eyes, without scales, cold and smooth as a peeled egg, and along each flank runs the ridge: a raised line of sensory cells so developed it stands a quarter-inch proud of the body from gill to tail. This is how they see. The standing wave of the high sea is not uniform — near a tower it is fierce, in the dead middles between gyres it is fainter, at the rim in the shallows it thins to a shiver — and the fish read the pattern the way a hand reads a wall in the dark. They know where the towers are from miles off. They know the shape of the shelf's floor, the depth, the slope, the position of every boat that has ever gone out along the rim, all as a texture in the water. To them the sea is not a medium. It is a floor plan, lit.

Lift one out of the water and it goes rigid. Not thrashing — rigid, every fin flared, the ridge pulsing. The people who do this for a living say it is not fear. It is what a person would do if the lights went out and the floor went with them. Set it back down in the shallows and it hangs motionless for the space of two or three breaths, reading, and then the ridge settles and it swims off in a straight line toward the nearest tower, and it does not learn from the experience and can be lifted again the next day.

They are good eating. Their flesh, cooked, tastes faintly of iron.

---

The large thing.

It is best to say what has been seen, and then say what is thought, and keep the two apart.

What has been seen: a back, dark, granular, the width of a boat at least, off the northern towers. A fin or the edge of a fin, sail-shaped, rising and folding. A long slow bulge in the water that does not break and travels, unhurried, for a quarter of a mile, and subsides. A shadow under a boat in the rim shallows, once, that the boat's occupant said took a full minute to pass beneath a hull forty feet long, though he was deafened at the time and could not be asked whether the note had changed. What everyone who has seen it agrees on is that it does not surface the way an animal comes up for air. It rises and falls with the pressure — up on the swell of the note, down on its trough, as though the roar itself were lifting and setting it down — and it is never seen anywhere except in the deep gyres, where the shaking is worst, where the fish will not go and the ribbons come apart.

What is thought: that it is very old. That it is one animal, or was. That it is what the grazers are, at scale — a thing that does not merely endure the vibration but is made of it, feeds on it, has replaced whatever it once had for a heartbeat with the note. That when it shifts its weight the rock underfoot on the shelf feels it, briefly, a second tremor under the first, and that this is not because it is striking anything but because it is coupled to the sea so completely that its movement is the sea's movement. Some who have thought about it longer than is wise say it may be older than the towers. That the engines were not built to keep the sea from the frost or from the fall, but to keep the sea *ringing*, because something in it needs the sea to ring; and that the roaring shelf is not a machine with an animal in it but an animal with a machine strapped to it, keeping it alive.

Nobody has tried to check. There is nowhere to stand.

---

The people are of two kinds, and the line between them is the ear.

The seasonal ones come up the face in spring — it is not warmer, but the shadows are shorter — and go down before the long nights. They wear the hoods, quilted and packed and strapped under the jaw, and they work the rim in the flat iron boats, hauling fish with bare hands out of shivering water and scraping scale from the low bands of the two nearest towers with long-handled blades, never closer than the buoy line, never for more than an hour. They lose weight. They lose sleep, because the body will not stop treating the note as an emergency and there is no position in which the ribs do not hum. Their teeth crack. Their handwriting on the chalk boards gets small and pressed hard into the slate. By the end of a season they have a way of walking — a very slight roll, a settling of the weight on each step — that people below recognise, and that turns out to be the gait of a body that has synchronised itself to a ten-thousand-foot tuning fork and has not yet been told it can stop. It wears off in a month. The hearing, if they have kept it, comes back thinner than it was.

They do not stay in the huts at night by choice. They stay in the huts because there is nowhere else, and the huts face away from the water, and the windows are small, and the doors are heavy, and none of it makes any difference to the sound. They talk in hands. They have a sign for the towers, a sign for the boat, a sign for the note — a flat hand pressed to the sternum — and a sign for the large thing, which is the same sign made slower.

---

The other kind are the ones who have gone over.

Deafness here is not a loss of hearing so much as a change of address. For a year the note is a thing outside the body, pressing in. And then some morning the hairs in the inner ear that have been shaking without rest for four hundred days finally lie down and do not get up, and the note goes out — not quieter; *out* — and the shelf becomes the place the seasonal ones cannot imagine: grey and still and enormous, with water that trembles for no reason and towers that stand in silence and a storm on the horizon that makes no sound. The first weeks of this are said to be the worst on the shelf. Worse than the roar. People weep and cannot hear themselves weep.

And then the body finds it again.

Not in the ear. In the shins, first. In the long bones and the sternum and the plates of the skull, in the jaw especially, in the roots of the teeth. The note is still there and has always been there in the bone, and with the ears finally out of the way the body begins, slowly, to read it. Not as sound. As place. The way the fish read it. The deaf on the shelf can tell you which tower is nearest with their eyes shut. They can tell you when a boat is out on the rim, and roughly where, by a change in the grain of the tremor through the soles of their feet. Some claim — and this is chalked as a joke, but not entirely as a joke — that they can feel the large thing move, a heaviness in the jaw that comes and goes, and that it is usually somewhere off the northern towers and usually at rest.

They stop wearing the hoods. There is nothing left to protect. They stop facing the huts away from the water. They go out along the knife edge of the rim in a way the seasonal ones will not, because to them the water is no longer a threat but the biggest and steadiest thing they know, and they sit at the edge with their hands flat on the young black glass and read it.

---

The one in the hut on the southern lip is a woman, though the flag code does not distinguish and the boats mostly say *the south hut* and leave it there.

She has not been down the face in eleven years. She came up for scale, the way most do, and stayed a second season for reasons she did not chalk, and by the third the ears had gone and she found she did not want to leave a place she had only just begun to be able to read. The boats bring her flour and lamp oil and take her fish and the small blades she grinds from tower iron, which hold an edge longer than anything made below. They raise a flag on a pole at the buoy line to say they are there. She raises one back. There are seven flags. Enough.

She keeps the board. It is not a duty anyone gave her; it is a thing she started, and now the boats look for it when they round the southern point, and the crews who have been up before know what it means and the ones who have not are told. STILL RINGING. She writes it every morning in the same hand, and it means what it says, and it means also that she is alive, and it means a third thing, which the seasonal ones do not fully take in until they have been up a while.

Nobody with ears could tell if the note stopped. Not quickly. The roar is so total, so much the ground of every other sensation, that its absence would register at first only as a confusion — a wrongness in the ribs, a sudden clumsiness, the way a person feels when a fan they have stopped hearing switches off. It might take a minute. It might take an hour of arguing in hands before anyone said the word. The theories about the frost and the fall may be wrong. But if they are right, a minute is a very long time.

She would know at once. She has no ears to be fooled. The note lives in her shins and her jaw and the long bones of her forearms, and she has lived inside it long enough that she does not experience it as anything at all, the way the fish do not experience the water; but she has been careful, over eleven years, to notice what its absence would feel like, and she believes she has it. A lightness. A sudden ordinary stillness in the bone, like a held door letting go.

So she sits at the southern lip in the sharp violet-edged light with her palms on the glass and the storm standing where it stands, and the fish come into the shallows and do not fear her, and the grazers glow on the far towers, and once every few weeks something heavy shifts its weight off the northern gyre and her jaw aches and passes. And every morning she goes to the board and wets the rag and wipes it clean, and looks at it blank for a moment — the boats say she always does this, they can see it from the buoy line — and then she writes.
