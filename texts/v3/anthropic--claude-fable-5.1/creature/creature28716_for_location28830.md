**What Lives in the Warm Throat**

Begin with the ones who leave, because they are the easiest to count. They have edges. They can be seen against the sky.

The small dark bat with the folded leaf of a nose is the room's long traveler. It looks like it should be a short-range hunter, all that tight-packed clustering and quick breath, but at dusk when the colony pours out of the throat this kind does not stop on the high slopes with the others. It goes down. It drops off the shoulder of the mountain and rides the sinking evening air past the cinder, past the cushion plants, past the line of the last trees, all the way into the wet forest a day's walk below, where things fruit. It feeds there through the middle of the night on figs and on soft yellow berries that split in the heat and ferment on the branch. Then it climbs back. Two thousand meters of climbing on wings the size of a child's hands, in air that thins with every hundred meters, arriving before dawn with its belly full of pulp and its breath sweet.

That is the smell at the entrance — the fermented sweetness that has no source within a day's walk. The source is inside, hanging from the ceiling, breathing out the forest it has just left.

And the seeds come up with it. Every night this kind carries in its gut the seeds of trees that cannot grow here, and every night it passes them, and they fall into the guano and lie there, thousands upon thousands of them, an orchard's worth of forest sleeping in the dark a mile above where forest can live. None will ever germinate. The floor beetles crack them and eat the meat. But the shells persist, and if you sifted the deep guano you would find layer on layer of them, going down through centuries: a record, kept by no one, of every fruiting season in a forest that has never known the room exists.

The pale long-eared kind is different in every way. It does not go far. It hangs loose at the cool margins because it does not need the vent's heat the way the others do; it is a mountain bat, built for cold, and the room is a convenience to it rather than a necessity. At dusk it works the slopes just outside, gleaning moths off the rock, taking the crane flies that stand over the meltwater seeps, flying low and slow and listening with those enormous ears for the rustle of a wing-case on stone. It is back early. It sleeps lightly. It is the last kind to have found the room — a few thousand years, no more — and it has not yet fully committed. When something disturbs the colony it is the long-eared kind that leaves first and returns last, and there are nights when a good portion of them do not come back at all but roost outside in some cold crevice, and the margins of the ceiling are thin until they drift in again over the following days.

It is the kind the people take. This is not an accident. The long-eared bats hang low and apart, in reach and unguarded, and they are large enough to be worth the trouble. Whoever first came up into this warmth and put out a hand found them there, and everyone since has known where to reach without having to be told.

---

The people should be counted with the ones who leave, though they leave for longer than anyone.

They do not live here. Nothing about the room is theirs. But they are part of what the room is, and have been for long enough that the room has adjusted to them the way it has adjusted to the long-eared bats: a small regular tide, arriving and receding, changing the sound for an hour and then letting it close over.

They come one or two at a time. Never a group — there is no room on the shelf inside the throat for more than two to sit, and the flakes on that shelf are the debris of a knife being resharpened by one pair of hands, not many. They come in the narrow season when the high passes are open, or they come when they have to, driven up out of the cold by something that made the cold intolerable. They come with almost nothing. The cord. The gourd, for water, filled at the last seep and finished here. The knife.

What they know about the room they know in the body rather than in words. They know the knob at the entrance is where the hand goes; they have never had to look for it. They know to crouch for a body-length and then stand. They know to wait, when they first stand up in the dark, for the room's noise to settle around them before moving, because a colony startled all at once will fill the air with bodies and the throat with bodies and there will be no getting out for a while. They know to take from the pale kind and not the dark. They know the floor is deep and warm and not to be trusted with the whole of one's weight until a foot has tested it.

They eat here. They sit on the shelf above the floor-life and work through what they've taken and drop the small bones over the edge, where the beetles find them within the hour. And then — this is the part that the flakes and the bones do not explain — they stay. Not long. But longer than eating takes. The polish on the knob is not the polish of a hand that comes, takes, and goes. It is the polish of a hand that rests there on the way in and rests there again on the way out, and between those two moments there is a stretch of time spent sitting in warm total darkness inside a mountain, listening to it breathe.

They have never made a fire. They have never marked a wall. They have never, so far as anything shows, touched the frogs. Whatever it is they come up here for, it does not require them to change the room, and they never have.

---

Now the ones who stay.

The floor first, because it is the largest single thing in the room and because everything above it eventually becomes it.

The black beetles are its body. They are the room's most numerous animal by weight, and they live their entire lives in the top hand-span of the guano — hatch there, feed there, pair there, walking over one another in the dark with a soft continuous clicking of shell on shell, and die there, and are eaten by their own kind within a day, and become the floor that the next generation walks on. They eat what falls: droppings, dead bats, the seed-meat of the forest, the wing-membranes that come down when a bat is killed on the ceiling by a fall or a fight. They are not fast. They do not need to be. Nothing on the floor runs from them and nothing much runs at them, and so they have settled into a pace that is really the floor's own pace, a slow churning that never stops and never hurries.

The pale roaches are quicker and fewer and live along the wall-foot where the floor is drier. They are the color of tallow and nearly as soft, having no need of armor. The crickets are blind and white and walk on top of the beetles as though the beetles were ground, their antennae sweeping ahead of them in the dark like a person's hands. They do not sing. There is nothing in the room that could hear a cricket's song above the colony, and they gave it up long ago, keeping only the legs and the long feelers. The springtails are the room's dust. They are in the guano at a density past counting and when a foot comes down they rise in a pale puff and settle again.

And the mites are the room's grain — the finest resolution at which it is alive. They are on the beetles and the crickets and the bats and the snails and the walls and the frogs and, within a minute, on you. There is no surface in the room that is not, at some scale, moving.

Out of the wet guano near the vent, where it is warmest, come the flies. Small, gray, weak-winged, they breed in the deep sludge and emerge in a thin steady stream, and they do the only thing they know, which is to climb toward warmth. So they rise. They go up into the column of steam over the vent and hang there in a loose smoke, turning slowly, a few hundred at any moment, born out of the floor and lifting toward the ceiling. They will not live to reach it.

---

The walls belong to the snails.

They are the room's grazers. They eat the film — the slick of bacteria and fungal thread that grows wherever condensation runs and the light from nothing at all falls on it. They are packed on the rock so tightly that a hand laid flat on the wall touches fifty of them, and they rasp at the film with a sound too small to separate from the room's general noise but which, if the room were ever silent, would be a fine continuous hiss like sand blowing. Their shells are nearly clear. You can see the animal through the wall of its own house, a dim brown coil, going about its long slow work.

Between and among them move the harvestmen, which eat the mites and the springtails and the smallest of the snail-young, and the spiders, which sling loose ragged webs in the corners of the water-grooves to catch the flies that stray from the steam column. The spiders are pale and their eyes are going the way the frogs' went. And there is the centipede.

The centipede is the color of old teeth and moves as though it has never once been surprised. It is the length of a hand and it is the wall's chief killer: it takes the spiders, the harvestmen, the roaches when they come within reach, the crickets when it can get down to them. It is not common — there are perhaps a few dozen in the whole room — and that scarcity is a kind of authority. When it moves through a patch of wall the snails do not react, because it does not eat snails, but everything that can move gets out of its way, and a faint clear ring travels with it across the rock. It will take a frog if it can, and it can only rarely, and that has been the arrangement for a very long time.

Around the vent itself is the rind. Gray and orange, in bands, leathery, warm to the touch and faintly slick. It is not a plant and not exactly an animal. It is what grows where hot sulfur water meets air, a mat of things too small to see building themselves out of the mountain's breath, and it is the only thing in the room that makes food from nothing but chemistry and heat. It is almost certainly the oldest living thing in the room — it was here when the vent first opened, before there was a room to speak of, when this was only a hot crack in a lower and greener mountain — and the snails graze it at its cool outer edge, and it grows back, and it has not once in all that time been aware of anything.

---

The third kind of bat.

It does not leave. That much is certain. In the deep cleft of the ceiling where it hangs, the rock has been eaten by ammonia into a soft pitted rind a hand deep, and the bats are packed into that rind like seeds in a pod, and no one has seen them in the dusk river, and no one has seen them in the dawn return. They are simply always there.

They are eating. That is not the mystery; the mystery was only what. And the answer is the room.

Through the day and through the night, at intervals with no apparent pattern, one of the third kind drops from the cleft and flies. Not out — around. It makes slow circuits under the ceiling-crust, a hand's breadth below the hanging bodies of the other two kinds, and then it dips into the steam column over the vent and takes the flies there, one after another, in a heavy unhurried flight that has nothing of the darting quickness of an outside bat. The flies were born out of the floor and rose toward the heat, and the bat eats them halfway up, and what the bat does not use falls back to the floor and becomes fly again. The room feeds itself through the third bat's mouth.

And it does not only take flies. It lands. It comes down onto the walls in the places where the snails are thickest, hangs there by its thumbs with its wings folded, and works along the rock taking snails whole and cracking them in a jaw that has grown heavier than any other bat's jaw on the mountain. It takes harvestmen. It will take a spider out of its own web. Once, or more than once, it has taken a frog from the edge of a pool and carried it back up to the cleft, and whether that is a rare accident or a slow habit there is no one in the room who could say.

Its ears have shrunk. There is nothing in here to hear at a distance and no distance to hear it across, and so the ears have folded back toward the skull and the whole face has gone blunt and close. Its call has dropped. The high chitter that serves an outside bat on a cold slope goes nowhere in a room full of soft wet bodies and running walls, and so this kind has let its voice sink, over a great many generations, into the low churr that travels through dense damp air and carries through fur and along stone. It sounds like a frog. It has come to sound like a frog because it lives in the frogs' room, under the frogs' conditions, and there is only one voice that works here.

Or the frogs have come to sound like it. Or they met in the middle. In the dark it does not matter. There is one sound, low and continuous, and it comes from the ceiling and it comes from the floor, and it is the sound of two very different animals having arrived, from opposite directions, at the same answer.

The third bat is a bat that has decided to be of the room and not of the mountain. It is halfway to being something else. Give it long enough — as long again as the frogs have had — and it will not be a bat at all, but whatever the room makes of an animal that hangs from its ceiling and never looks up.

---

The frogs were here first.

Not first of all — the rind was here, and the beetles' ancestors were probably here before the frogs had a reason to come in. But the frogs are the oldest thing in the room that has a face, and the only thing in the room that remembers, in its body, what the mountain used to be.

They are small. The largest would sit in a thumb-joint. Their skin is the brown of wet bark and it is thin and it drinks, so that they are never dry, never can be dry, and the one thing that would kill them faster than the cold outside is the dry air outside, which would take the water out of them through their own skin in minutes. Their eyes are dimples. Under the skin there is still a dark bead where the eye was, and it still, faintly, knows light from dark, and that is all it does, and the frogs have not needed it to do more for longer than there have been people anywhere near this mountain.

They live in the pools. Every hollow at the wall-foot where the condensate collects has its frogs, a few to a pool, and they sit in the warm water with their backs just breaking the surface and they wait. They eat what comes to the edge: mites, springtails, the young of the snails, the fly larvae that wriggle up out of the guano where it meets the pool. They breed in the water, and their eggs are white and lie on the bottom of the pools in clumps, and their young are pale tadpoles that graze the film on the pool-rock and never see anything and change, in their own time, into small blind frogs that sit in the water and wait.

They call. The churring is theirs, or theirs originally. Each frog in each pool sends its low note out into the room, and the note goes through the water and through the wet rock and through the guano, and the other frogs feel it more than hear it, and answer, so that under the colony's noise there is a slow conversation being held along the floor, pool to pool, that has been going on without a break since before the mountain was as tall as it is.

That is the thing to hold onto. When the frogs came in, this was not a high place. It was a warm crack in the flank of a lower mountain, and the slopes outside were forest — the same forest, perhaps, that the leaf-nosed bats fly down to now — and the frogs were forest frogs who found a hole that stayed warm through the night and moved into it. And then the mountain rose. It rose the way mountains do, too slowly for anything to feel, a hand's width in a lifetime, and the forest fell away below and the air thinned and the cold came down and settled on the slopes and killed everything that could not leave. The frogs could not leave. They also did not need to. The hole stayed warm.
