Start beneath the floor, because that is where the living starts.

A hand's depth into the grey sediment — below the burrows, below the last of the oxygen, in mud that is dark in a way that has nothing to do with light — the methane comes up. It has come a long way. It is old, older than the slab by a factor the slab has no way of measuring, and it began as things that were once alive and were pressed until they gave up their carbon. It rises through the pore-spaces between grains the way breath rises through a scarf, and in those pore-spaces it meets the first inhabitants.

They are not visible. If you brought a spoonful of the mud up into the light and held it to your eye you would see mud. They are cells, and they live in a marriage of two kinds that cannot survive apart: one that takes the methane to pieces and one that takes what the first one cannot use, each passing the other something the other cannot make, so closely packed that no one has ever been sure where one ends. Between them they do the single thing that nothing else in the room can do, which is to make a living off the leak directly. And the leavings of their living are two. One is bicarbonate, which meets the calcium in the seawater and falls out as a powder finer than flour and settles on whatever is nearest and sets. The other is hydrogen sulfide, which is poison to nearly everything that breathes oxygen, and is the food of the next tier.

So the rock is not something the seep does. The ceiling, the ribs, the cement that has taken the mussels' lower halves, the rind forming across the threshold and closing the door — all of it is the midden of creatures too small to count. The city stands inside the accumulated waste of its smallest citizens and goes on being built by them, grain by grain, from underneath and from within. Every larger thing here is living in a house the bacteria excreted, and will be buried in one.

The white lawns are the first inhabitants you can see, and they are barely that.

Each filament is a chain of cells standing on end, and each cell is stuffed with granules of raw sulfur, which is why they are white: they are storing their own leftovers inside their bodies, the way a tree stores the year in a ring. They live at a line — the exact plane in the mud where the sulfide coming up meets the oxygen coming down — and that line moves. When the surge comes it lifts; when the surge passes it sinks back. The filaments follow it. They glide, slowly, on nothing anyone has quite explained, up a millimeter and down a millimeter, so that the lawn is not grass rooted in soil but grass that migrates with its soil, an inch-high field forever adjusting itself to stand where the two waters kiss. Brightest beneath the fractures because that is where the meeting is richest. Absent between them because there is nothing there to meet.

They are grazed. The snails that come to them are small, dark, thick-shelled, with a tongue like a rasp, and they work the lawns the way sheep work a hill — not stripping, because a stripped lawn does not come back fast enough to be worth having, but taking the tips and moving on. They were not born knowing this. There is simply no snail here whose ancestors did otherwise. The worms that ring the lawns in their burrows do the same from beneath, coming up to the edge of the white and drawing filaments down into the mud, and the ring of burrows around each lawn is the drawn shape of how far a worm that wants to keep its head in its hole can reach.

---

The tubeworms are the oldest thing in the room and the strangest, and the strangeness is that there is almost no animal in them.

An adult has no mouth. It has no gut. It has nothing that could be called a stomach, and nothing comes out the other end because there is no other end that opens. What it has, instead, is the plume — that red feather — and the red is a blood, a hemoglobin unlike yours, which can hold oxygen and sulfide at the same time without letting them touch, which in you would be a contradiction and in the worm is the whole of its economy. The plume takes in both. The blood carries both down the length of the body, inside the tube, into the dark, to an organ that fills most of the worm and is packed with bacteria — a garden, planted once when the worm was a larva and tended ever since. The bacteria burn the sulfide with the oxygen and grow. The worm eats what the garden lets fall.

So the animal is a wall around a garden. It is architecture first. Its body is the raised bed, its blood the irrigation, its plume the window through which the weather gets in, and the thing that actually lives — that takes in food and makes something of it — is a tenant. The worm's part of the bargain is to hold still and stay open.

Here it hangs upside down, plume toward the floor, and at the other end, where the tube meets the rock, it has done something else: it has grown roots. A thin extension of its own tissue has crept out of the tube's closed end and into the fracture it settled on, threading into the carbonate the way a tree's roots find a seam, and through this it drinks sulfide straight from the seep before the seep ever reaches the water. It feeds from both ends. Its middle is a pipe. The largest worms in the central avenue, thick-walled, crusted, standing in the rock like the pillars of a cloister, were here when the ceiling was a crust you could have broken with a thumb. They are two centuries old, perhaps more; the count is uncertain because they grow so slowly that a year is a few millimeters and the millimeters at the base have turned to stone. At their roots they are rock. At their plume they are animal. Somewhere along their length is the line where one becomes the other, and the line moves upward every year, and the plume does not know.

What a worm knows of the surge is not an event. It is a change in what its blood is carrying, a fullness, and then a lessening. What it knows of the room's history is the slow decline of the fracture it chose — a little less each decade, as the crack fills with the leavings of the mud — and it will know the end the same way, as a hunger in the roots that the plume cannot make up.

The tubes are inhabited twice. Inside each, in the dark between the worm's body and the tube wall, lies a scale worm: flat, plated like a pinecone, a lodger. It has never left. Its world is a cylinder the width of a wheat stalk and it knows every millimeter of it. It eats the fouling that drifts in, the small things that settle, and — the evidence is a nicked plume here and there — a little of its landlord when the landlord can spare it. It does not pay rent. It is not evicted. The relationship is old enough that the worm has stopped noticing, if the worm ever had the means to notice, and the scale worm has lost the pigment it would need in a place where there was light.

Outside each tube, limpets. Smaller than a lentil, cone-shaped, they rasp the film of bacteria off the tube's own surface, and under every limpet is a clean patch and around every limpet is the beginning of film again. They are themselves inhabited. On the shell of a limpet, if you could see it, is something the size of a grain of pepper with the outline of an animal, and on that there is very likely something else. The nesting does not stop at any level anyone has bothered to look.

The mussels made a different bargain. They kept their mouths.

They kept their guts too, though shrunken, and in a bad season — the leak faltering, the surge long absent — they can gape and filter what falls from above and live on it, meagerly, the way a farmer lives on stored grain. But the gill is most of what they are, and the gill is a garden of the same kind as the worm's: a tissue so thick with bacteria that it is brown with them. Two kinds, in some. One that burns sulfide, one that burns methane, so that when the leak's chemistry shifts the mussel does not have to choose; it simply lets the one that is fed outgrow the one that isn't. Water goes through the gill, the bacteria feed, and the mussel — here is the difference from the worm — eats some of them. Not the leavings. The gardeners. It farms and it harvests.

They are quicker than the worms. Decades, not centuries. They are the room's dense middle, the ones who take the ground between the avenues and pack it, and they compete in the only way a fixed thing can compete, which is by being where the water is. The upper layers stand on the lower and hold themselves there with threads spun from a gland in the foot — the only thing anyone in the room makes that resembles a rope — and the lower layers are slowly losing the gape they need to feed, first to the weight above and then to the rock below, and when they die they do not fall. They stay. Their shells become the shelf the next generation stands on. The ridge between two mussel-districts is not a wall built against anyone; it is the height of the dead.

Then the room's air.

The amphipods are not so much the room's citizens as its atmosphere. They are the only thing in it that holds position without touching anything — a flick of the swimmerets, too small and too constant to read as motion, keeping each one exactly where it is against a drift you cannot see — and they hold position facing the warmth, because the warmth is where the water carries the most of what they eat: bacteria loosened from the ceiling, particles of the lawns, the fine chemistry of the leak itself. They are what the room breathes. They live a year, or less; they are always dying and always replaced; a great many were born here, in the space under the slab, from parents who had never been anywhere else, and some fraction drifted in through the crack from the open slope and found the warmth and stayed. Where the fish is, they are thinner. Where the lobsters sit, they are thinner. The shape of the cloud is the shape of everything in the room that eats it, drawn in negative.

The lobsters have property. Each sits in its niche between two tube-bundles with its claws to its chest and its eyes, which are stalked and nearly useless, pointed at nothing, and the space between one lobster and the next is not accident. When two meet there is a brief business of claws — no sound; you would see it only as a blur and then two lobsters where there had been two lobsters, farther apart — and one leaves. The spacing across the whole ceiling is the map of every such meeting that has ever happened here, redrawn each time one dies and a niche stands empty for the days it takes a wanderer to find it. They eat film from the rock. They eat amphipods that hold position badly. They have been seen, elsewhere, to pluck at the plumes of tubeworms, and the worms here are frayed at the edges in a way that suggests it.

---

The fish is the exception to everything.

It has a spine. It has a brain, though a small one, and a stomach that fills and empties, and eyes — reduced to grains, but not gone, and in the thin blue bar at the threshold they may still register that there is a threshold. It is the only inhabitant that has seen the outside. It came in through the crack, when the crack was wider, as a juvenile no longer than a finger, and it found the corner where the ceiling meets the mud and it has been there since, and how long since is unclear; fish like this one grow so slowly in water this cold that thirty years is not out of the question. It eats a few amphipods and lives on them for days. Its whole metabolism is a held breath.

It is the only thing in the room that will never be built into the walls. It has no shell for the rock to take, and it moves — not often, but enough. It is also, therefore, the only thing in the room to which the closing of the crack is a fact about the future rather than a fact about chemistry. Whether it can know this is doubtful. But there will be a day when the gap at the threshold is a finger's width and the fish is two fingers wide, and either it will have gone before that day or it will not, and if it has not, then it will be the one inhabitant of the city to be entombed alive, in a room that is closing around a body that could have left.
