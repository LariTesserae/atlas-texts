Start with the stain, because everything else is standing on it.

The film on the wet faces of the rock is not one thing. Up close, in the low morning light when it is glossy with condensation, it resolves into zones: a red-brown that holds the outer edge and tolerates drying, a darker almost black band inside that, and along the drip-lines a thin green that only appears where the water runs continuously and that the ring people, who have looked at it under glass, say is the part actually eating light. The rest is eating the green, or eating what the green leaks, or holding water for it. It is a village a few cells thick. It grows toward the drip. Where a ledge weeps steadily the film below it will spread a hand's breadth in a season; where a slab-top dries by noon the film is a scatter of coin-sized patches that each mark a place where the stone is slightly rougher and keeps a little more damp. Scrape it off and it returns in a month, faster if you left the edges. It is the oldest living thing on the plateau. It is about eleven years old.

Some of it came on the wind. Some of it was carried up in the guts of the first birds. Some of it, the green especially, was seeded — there are records in the oldest ring of a season spent walking the seams with a sprayer — and no one now can tell which patches are which, and no one is trying. It has become a single thing the way a language does.

---

The flat insects that graze it are pale, and you see them first as a paleness moving. A herd is a few hundred, a loose oval a meter or two long, and it travels across a wet face at about the speed the shadow of a mast travels, so that if you sit and watch you can see it has gone somewhere without ever seeing it move. Behind it the rock is cleaner — not bare; they leave the red-brown and take the black — and in a few weeks the cleaned lane darkens again and another herd, or the same one, comes back along it.

They live inside the day. In the still of the morning they feed. When the cloud builds and lowers they climb, all of them, toward the top edge of whatever face they are on, and in the fog they mate, and the eggs are pressed under the lifting edge of the film where it meets dry stone and left there. When the cloud drains off in the evening they come back down. They are the only thing on the plateau that seems to regard the afternoon rain as an event rather than a nuisance.

The snails do not herd. They are solitary and slow and there are a great many of them, and they hang. That is the posture you will remember: a snail on the underside of a ledge, shell down, held by a foot you cannot see, with a bead of condensation forming on the shell's lowest point and dropping into the pool below. Their shells are thin enough to show the animal inside as a darker shape, and they are thin because the snails grow fast and die young and the stone gives up its minerals reluctantly. In strong sun a snail shell looks lit from within. In the fog they are invisible.

They came up the margins on their own. Everyone agrees on that. It took them, the ring people estimate, about four years to cross two hundred kilometers of cooling rock, and no one saw them do it.

---

The pools have nothing in them.

This is worth saying because it is the one habitat on the plateau that is empty, and it is empty for a reason that took a while to understand. The pools are too clean. There is no sediment for anything to settle in, nothing dissolved to feed on, and the water is gone by the next afternoon — drunk, drawn down through the cable-mouths, lifted into the cloud — and replaced with new water that has never touched anything alive. A pool is not a pond. It is a puddle that happens in the same place every day. The films do not grow in them because the floor is too smooth and too often dry. The insects will not cross them. The snails drink from their edges and drop into them and drown, and are taken.

So the pools are where the plateau's shortest lives end, and where its longest patient ones wait, and that is why the heron-things stand there.

---

The birds you will already have seen. There is no way to be on the plateau in the morning and not see them.

They are the size of a fist, or smaller. They are the color of the dry rock, so that a ledge with two thousand of them on it looks like a ledge with a heavy frost until it moves. They roost in rows, all facing the same way — into whatever faint air is moving, so that the whole ledge adjusts together when it shifts — and they leave the roost a few at a time, then in ropes, then in a single lifting sheet that thins toward the insect herds and settles on them and does not seem to reduce them.

They have never been hunted from the ledges. Nothing here climbs. So they do not move when you come, and if you stand at a ledge they will roost against your boots, and if you stay very long one will land on your shoulder, not out of trust but because you have become a shape that is warmer than the rock and the rock is what they have. Their eggs are laid directly on the stone, two or three to a shallow place, and the stone is sharp, and the eggs are thicker-shelled than lowland eggs of the same size and the chicks come out of them with the shell-breaking tooth already worn.

The murmur is the sound of them telling each other nothing in particular. It does not rise to song. In eleven years no one has heard one sing. The ring people argue, mildly, about whether this is because there is no reason yet to defend anything — no territory worth having, no shortage — or because the birds that came up the margins were the ones that didn't sing, the ones that fed close and flew low and didn't announce themselves, and singing simply didn't make the trip. Either way the morning here is a crowd-noise. Then the slab settles, somewhere, and it stops, and you hear the drip, and then it begins again.

---

The heron-things are the problem.

Not a problem in the sense of a danger. A problem in the sense of a fact that will not fit. Nobody brought them. Nothing like them is known in the lowlands the margins descend into. They are in no one's records, and the oldest ring's records are good. They were simply on the plateau, at the pool edges, by the third or fourth season, as if they had been there the whole time and it had taken that long for people to learn to see them.

They are long. That is the first fact and the one your eye keeps returning to. A body like a bundle of reeds, held low and level, and then a neck that is not so much a neck as a continuation, and at the end of it a head that is mostly eye — two eyes, wide-set, dark, and a mouth beneath them that is a slot until it opens. The legs are the wrong way. They fold backward at the middle joint, so that a heron-thing standing looks like a heron-thing about to sit, and when it does move it does not step but pours, one leg unfolding forward and setting down and the body sliding over it. They are grey. In the fog you can be three meters from one and not know.

They stand. Hours. At a pool edge, head tilted, watching the water, and when a snail drops from the ledge above and hits the surface the head goes down and comes up and that is the whole of it. They do not seem to drink. They do not seem to sleep in any way you can identify. They are never in groups, and they are never far from another one either — pace out from a heron-thing in any direction and within a kilometer there will be another, so that the whole interior is threaded with them at a spacing that looks, on a map, exactly like the spacing of the rings.

The ring people leave them alone. Not from policy. There is something in the way a heron-thing turns its head to follow you that makes you not want to go closer, and nobody has tried to catch one, and the one time someone found a dead one — in a pool, folded, the water perfectly clear around it — she sat with it for an afternoon and then left it where it was, and by the next morning it was gone and the pool was clean.

What people say, when they say anything, is that the plateau is new, and new places have things in them that were not put there, and the heron-things are that. Then they leave it there.

---

The grazers were put there.

You will have heard them before you see them. The knocking of hooves on unweathered stone carries for kilometers in still air, and it does not sound like an animal. It sounds like someone working. A band is thirty or forty and moves as a broad wedge across a slab-top, heads down, and the sound each one makes is a scrape and then a knock — the lower jaw dragged across the film and then the head lifted and the front hooves shifting — and forty of them slightly out of time is a rhythm you will find yourself walking to.

They look like goats until they look up. Then the head is too wide, and the lower jaw is a blade — no teeth to speak of, a hard scalloped ridge of horn that grows continuously and is worn back by the rock, so that an old grazer's jaw is shorter than a young one's. The coat is the other made thing. It is long and dense and hangs to the knee, and it is built to catch condensation, and it does: at dawn a standing grazer is silver with water down to the hem of it, and the first thing each one does when the light comes is turn its head and draw its jaw down its own flank, wringing the coat, drinking from itself. A band at dawn is a slow ripple of heads going down and coming up. They do not use the pools much. They do not need to.

At night they lie down in a ring, tails in, and the coats overlap, and the warmth is enough. In the fog they stand. Something in them will not lie down in fog, and the ring people say that was not designed, that it came from wherever the stock came from and could not be bred out, and they say it with something like relief.

They were the experiment. The whole net of rings and masts and cable was thrown over the continent so that someone could learn whether hoofed animals set down on bare stone before any plant would live, and go on living, and make the plateau more habitable by being on it. And they do. Their dung dries hard and pale and crumbles, and in the lows where the wind can't take it and the water can't move it, it gathers, a hand deep in the best places, and it is the only soil there is. Everything that will eventually grow here will grow in what a grazer has left.

They know the ring people. Not individually — a band will let anyone walk up to the edge of it — but as a kind of thing that comes from the rings, and is patient, and sometimes has a hand on a flank while a jaw is looked at. When a band passes near a ring at evening the grazers will slow, and some of them will stand looking at it for a while, and then the band will go on.

---

Something follows them.

There is no soft ground for it to leave a print in. There is no cover for it to leave hair on. There are the broken bands — a wedge of grazers in the fog suddenly a scatter, the hooves gone from knocking to a hard clatter, a band that was forty in the morning and thirty-eight at evening. And there are the carcasses, which are found a day or two on, in a low, cleaned. Cleaned is the word everyone uses. Not eaten. The coat is there, and the bones are there, and the bones are white.

Two people say they have seen it. One says it is low and long and pale and moves along the seams. One says it is upright. Neither saw it for more than the time it takes to say so. The oldest ring's records have a page for it that is mostly dates and numbers and one sentence that says *possibly more than one*. The grazers know it — a band will break for no reason anyone standing there can see, and stand blowing, and re-form facing one way — and the birds know it, because they leave a ledge before a band below them breaks, and the heron-things, so far as anyone can tell, do not know it or do not care.

It walked up. That much people agree on, in the same way they agree the snails walked up: because the alternative is that it was brought, and no one will say they brought it. Whether it came behind the grazers, following the first bands as they were released along the margins, or whether it was here before them and waited — the ring people do not have an answer and have mostly stopped asking. It keeps the bands moving. It keeps them from thinning any one slab's film to nothing. It is, someone in the third ring said, doing the job the plateau needed done, and no one had thought to build anything to do it, and that is either luck or it is not.

In the fog you hear the hooves change. That is all. Then the fog drains off, and the band is standing somewhere else, and you count them.

---

The cushions are what people walk out to see.

A few hundred across the whole interior. Grey-green, a few centimeters across, so low and so tight that they look at first like a stain on the soil rather than a thing standing on it. They grow only where the dung has gathered deepest, in the sheltered lows near the older rings, and they grow where the grazers do not walk — which is to say the grazers know them, and step around them, and no one designed that either.

Once a year each one flowers. The bloom is the size of a grain of rice and lasts two or three days and is white, or nearly. People go to look. It is not a festival. Someone in a ring will say that the cushion in the low south of the third mast is open, and over the next two days, from that ring and the two nearest, most of the people will walk out to it, singly or in twos, and stand there a while, and walk back. There is nothing to do at a cushion. You look at it. It is the first thing on the plateau that has ever made a flower, and it is doing it in a handful of grazer dung on a slab of stone that was liquid not long before the oldest ring people were born, and it will be years yet before there are enough of them for anyone to stop counting.

---

Which brings us to the people, who are the least of what lives here by weight and the most of it by attention.

There are about three hundred, on a continent. They are not, as you were told, engineers, though a few are. They are not exactly scientists either, though most of them keep records and some of them came up as researchers and have simply not gone back. What they are, mostly, is people who wanted to be somewhere that had not yet been anywhere. They are the sort who could hear the phrase *a floor laid by someone who intended to come back* and want to stand on it. Some of them are running from something in the lowlands and are honest about it. Some of them are running toward the cushions.

You know them by their hands. The rock takes skin, and the first season is a season of cuts — the seams, the fresh ledges, the edges of the slabs where you put a palm down to steady yourself — and then the skin thickens and the hands go grey and rough as the stone they touch, and you can tell a second-season person from a first by whether they still reach out to catch themselves. They wear clothes that shed water, layered and close and the color of nothing. They keep their hair short because the fog beads in it. They walk everywhere, kilometers a day, in a gait that is flat-footed and even and does not lift the heel much, because the stone is trustworthy and a lifted heel is a wasted movement over forty kilometers.

They are quiet the way the plateau is quiet — not silent, but without background. A ring at evening has voices in it, low, and the sound of things being set down. Nobody shouts here. There is nothing to shout over. When two people from different rings meet in the open, which happens perhaps once in a season, they stop a few meters apart and stand a while before speaking, the way the grazers stop and look at the rings, and then they talk about where the bands are and which ledge has the most birds and whether the cushion in the low north of the ninth ring has set seed.

They can feel the slab move before they hear it. Everyone can, after a year. A pressure in the soles, a sense of the floor considering something, and then the report, and then the birds. People who have gone back to the lowlands say the ground there is dead under them. They say the smell is unbearable — soil and rot and growth, the smell of everything having already happened — and they say they could not sleep without the drip. Most of them come back up.

Some of them are here for the animals in a way that is nearly professional: they walk the bands and count, and note jaw-wear, and know each heron-thing by where it stands. Some of them are here for the films, and spend their days on their stomachs on wet rock with a lens. Some are here for the weather, which is a thing you can be here for — a plateau that makes its own sky is a thing worth watching, and there are two people in the fourth ring who have not missed a noon in six years. Some tend the rings, in the small ways the rings need tending. Some cook. Two are children. One of the children was born here, and has never smelled soil, and thinks the birds are a kind of weather.

And they argue, in the mild way of people with nothing much to defend, about what should happen next. Whether to bring plants — real plants, seeds in the dung-pockets, and skip the centuries. Whether the heron-things should be counted as belonging. Whether the follower should be found, or named, or left to be a page of dates. Whether the cables should be moved now, before the rivers, or whether the process under the rock will do it when it decides to. None of these arguments has a deadline. The plateau will be here. They talk, and then the fog comes down, and they go in.

---

There is one more resident, and it is the one the place is built around, and no one has met it.

It is under the rock. Kilometers under. It is the thing that lifts and sets stone, that is still, at the margins two hundred kilometers away, laying floor too hot to stand on. The masts talk to it. That is how the ring people put it, and they are not being whimsical: the masts carry the settling of the slabs down to it and carry back the adjustments, and when a cable slackens a moment before a slab drops, that is the process having heard and answered. It is presumably planning for the rivers. It is presumably planning for a great deal. It made the seams straight so the cables could sit in them, and it made the ledges sharp because it had not yet decided to make them otherwise, and it made the whole plateau with a barely perceptible dome so that water would go everywhere, and everywhere is where the film wanted it.

Whether it is a mind, no one in the rings will say. Whether it knows about the grazers, or the cushions, or the heron-things standing at a spacing that matches its own masts — no one will say that either. They live on it the way the film lives on the rock, and it holds them up, and it is warm underneath, still, from being made.

In the evening, when the cloud drains off the edge of the world and the cold comes down and the sky opens, the ring people go out and stand at the boundary where the warm air of the ring meets the wet air of the plateau, and feel it with their faces, and listen. The birds have begun again. The hooves are knocking somewhere over the curve. A heron-thing a kilometer off has not moved. Under all of it, too low to hear and just possible to feel, the floor is being finished, slowly, by something that intended to come back and has not stopped coming.
