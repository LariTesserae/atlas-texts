What lives here is easiest to answer wrongly, because the first answer is the green, and the green is not wrong. It is the most alive thing in the yard by weight, by reach, by appetite. It has taken the racks the way water takes a low room. If you stand still long enough among the troughs you can hear it — not growing, nothing grows audibly, but shedding: the tick of a drop released from a moss-pillow, the small wet collapse of a frond that has finally leaned too far and folded over its neighbour. The green lives here in the way a tide lives in an estuary. It is present, it is doing something, it will not answer to its name.

But it is not the inhabitant. It is the inhabitant's evidence.

---

Start smaller. The moths.

They are the colour of the channel-water, a milky not-quite-white, and if you lift a leaf and look at the row of them underneath, you will see that they are not all the same. Some are fresh, wings crisp as the concrete corners, the tent-fold sharp. Some have gone soft at the edges, powder rubbed away where they have brushed against the leaf above in their shivering, so that the veins show through like the ribs of a very small boat. These are the only things in the yard that wear. They are born under the leaves, they die under the leaves, and in between they do the one thing: when the valve on the machine sighs, they shiver, all of them, once.

Turn the slag-crumb over in any trough and you will find why. Small, pale, blind, the larvae lie in the crumb where it is warmest, a finger's depth down, and they eat the moss from underneath. Where they have been the moss goes yellow and thin and lifts off the crumb like felt off a table. Then the larvae go still and the moths come up through the moss and take their places under the leaves. They are the yard's only native. They did not arrive on the cloud. They did not arrive at all. They were in the slag when it was rock, or they were in the cloud when it was sea, or they were nowhere, and then there was warm crushed stone under a permanent wet and they were in it, the way the moss was in it, without asking. The moss and the moths are the two ends of a very short rope. Between them, on this made ground, they have already worked out how to be a world.

The shiver is not fear. Watch it enough times. It is the exact moment the pressure in the machine drops, and the exact moment the warmth coming up through the floor plates changes, very slightly, in pitch. They are not reacting to the sound. They are reacting to what the sound means. They have learned the machine.

---

The heron does not live here. It visits, and the distinction matters. It comes up out of the cloud along the eastern parapet, always the same place, always alone, and stands in the channel, and stabs, and finds nothing, and stays. It is the only thing in the yard that comes from somewhere else and goes back. When it is here, the yard has an outside. When it is gone, it does not. It carries the outside on its wet feet and takes it away again, and whether it comes tomorrow is not a thing the yard can arrange.

It has been fed. That is how you know the heron is not the inhabitant and also how you know there is one. Beside the channel, on the grating, at the spot where the bird stands, there is a small square of slag-crumb that has been put down deliberately — squared off at the edges by a boot-side, not spilled — and in it, half-grown, a mat of the fat moss, and on the moss, when the bird is gone, three or four of the pale larvae, laid out. The bird takes them or it does not. The next day there are three or four more.

Whoever does this does not do it when the heron is watching. Whoever does this has worked out that the heron will not come to a hand.

---

There is a sound in the racks that stays ahead of you.

It is not hard to describe: leaves moving where there is no wind, at about the height of a shoulder, and always in the row you are not in. If you go left it goes left. If you stop it stops, a beat late. If you push through — and you will, once, because the sound is so clearly a body and so clearly close — you come out into the next aisle and there is nothing there but a broken frond still nodding, and a run of pipe with the water on it disturbed, drops shaken loose and the film of wet on the steel showing the ghost of where a hand went along it for balance.

The hand-ghost is the thing. Look at it before the cloud fills it back in. It is small-ish. It is not a child's. It has gripped the pipe and let go, gripped and let go, and each grip has left a pattern in the wet that is not the pattern of skin. It is finer than skin. It is closer to the grain of the formwork in the concrete, or the surface of the slag slabs where they cooled fastest.

You can decide that means nothing. You can decide the wet on hot steel simply dries in odd shapes.

---

The routes are what give it away, in the end. You need the wind for this — the warm downward wind that comes perhaps once in a day and presses the cloud flat and shows you the whole yard for a minute — and you need to have understood the rule already: that the green comes only where the made ground has gone, and that the made ground goes where it is carried.

Seen all at once, the green outside the racks is not random. It is not spill. It is lines. A line from the north side of the machine to the channel, wide and dense, walked many times. A line from the channel along the racks, thinner, a scatter, someone brushing past with crumb on their clothes. A line from the racks to the western doorway, dark and thick as a cable, because that way carries the most. And one line — faint, a stitch of moss-dots on the grating, a boot-print's width apart — from the machine to the eastern parapet, to a single place at the wall, where the moss has thickened into a rough oval the size of two feet standing together.

Someone stands there. Someone walks these ways and no others, carrying earth on the soles of their boots without meaning to, and the earth remembers where they went and comes up green months later, so that the yard has a map of its inhabitant drawn on it in the one substance the yard recognises as true. It is the most accurate portrait of a person you will ever see and there is no face in it.

---

Then the face. Eventually, because you stay, and because whatever is ahead of you in the racks knows the difference between a visitor who leaves and one who does not.

They are not old. This is the first thing and it fits, because nothing here is old, and it takes a while to see why it is also wrong. They have the movements of someone old — the pause before bending, the hand out to the rack upright before the step, the economy of a body that has done every one of its motions ten thousand times in the same order — and a face and frame that have not been asked to pay for any of it. Cloth the grey of the cloud, gone stiff with lime at the hems. Boots. The boots are what carry the crumb. They have never been cleaned; the treads are packed solid with green.

Their hands are the exception. Their hands are the only old things in the yard.

They are burned. Not fresh — set, healed, healed again, a surface like the slag slabs where the cooling went badly: glassy in places, pocked in others, seamed with the pale welled-up lines of skin that has closed over the same wound more than once. The nails are short and dark and cracked. When they hold a thing they hold it the way the ladle holds — tipped, waiting, ready to pour and not pouring. These are hands that have been down the western doorway. These are hands that know the furnace's mouth, and how far in you can reach before the air itself becomes something you cannot put your arm through, and what it costs to reach anyway because the fire has to be fed and there is nobody else to feed it.

That is what they do. That is the whole of what they do, if you count it in the way work is counted.

They go down the doorway when the breathing below changes — when the slow deep rhythm you cannot place develops a catch in it, a shortness — and they cut rock, and they feed the furnace at the lowest rate that keeps it lit. They come up with the crumb on their boots and their hands newly wrong. They check the gauge on the machine, wipe the fog from the glass with a thumb, though the fog is inside and the wiping does nothing, and they do it anyway because it is the motion that goes with checking. When the flywheel slows — it does; without the furnace at full draught the pressure sags, and the wheel will drift toward stopping over the course of an afternoon — they lay their forearms on the rim and lean, and walk it, one revolution in half a minute, until the valve remembers itself and sighs, and the moths shiver, and the wheel takes back its own weight.

They do not pour. They go to the moulds each day and stand under the ladle and look up at the angle of it and do not touch the winch. A pour needs a hand on the winch and a hand on the stopper and a third to signal, and they have two and no signal. So the ladle stays tipped. So the stalactite grows its tuft. They could change this. They could rig a line. They have had a great deal of time to think about how one person might pour, and it is clear from how carefully they do not look at the winch that they have thought about it and decided something.

What they have decided, you come to believe, is that the pour is the end of the sequence and the sequence belongs to somebody who is not here. Feeding the fire only keeps the yard warm. Turning the wheel only keeps the cloud coming and the crumb wet. Those are maintenance; those are keeping a thing as it was left. Pouring would be finishing, and finishing is the one act that would make the question — is anyone coming back — into an answer. They are not ready to have it answered by their own hands. Their hands have done enough.

---

They sleep on the north side of the machine. You could find the place without them: the moss there has a hollow in it, shoulder-and-hip, the shape a body leaves when it lies on its side against a warm casing every night for longer than moss takes to grow. The moss started there because of them. Slag dust from the boots, breath, the warmth of a back against the plate, and the moss took its chance. It is spreading now across the casing on its own, a hand's breadth a season, and they do not scrape it off. They lie down in it. The machine and the moss and the sleeper are, for the hours of the dark, one warm thing against the wall.

They eat from the third tier of the westernmost rack, one trough, where something with broad leaves and a swollen root has been let grow and dug and let grow again, so that the crumb in that trough is finer than in the others, worked and reworked. They drink from the channel, kneeling, mouth to the water like the heron's beak, because they have never brought a cup up from below and there is nothing on the yard to make one from. Everything here is either steel or stone or green, and none of it holds water for a hand.

They do not thin the plants. This is the choice that tells you the most. A tender would thin — would clear the aisles, cut the vine that has gone head-first down the second rack, lift the fronds off each other so the lower ones get what light there is. They do none of it. They push through the same as you, and the water comes off on them the same as on you, and they let the green be as ahead of itself as it wants to be. Somewhere in the long unfinished middle of this work they understood that the plants had not waited for permission, and that the yard's makers, if they came back, would say the plants were early, and that the plants were not early, the makers were late; and they took the plants' side. They live among them the way the moss lives on the stalactite, in the pause, on the drip, at the tip of a thing stopped mid-fall.

---

The word over the western doorway — the cast number, and beside it the word you do not know — they can read. You will hear them say it. Not to you: to the racks, in passing, the way one says a name to a dog that has not learned it yet. They say it to the trough they eat from. They say it, once, standing at the eastern parapet in their moss-oval with their burned hands on the cold new concrete and the cloud coming over the wall against their chest, into the cloud, downward, where the heron comes from.

It is the name of what the yard is going to be. It is what the ground is for. Nobody has told them they are the one who is going to live on it; nobody has needed to; they are already living on it, a little early, a little ahead, on the racks and the slabs and the sleeping-moss, the way everything here that is alive has started before the start.

When the wind comes and flattens the cloud, they are the figure at the parapet with their back to you. When the cloud stands up again they are gone into it, and the leaves in the racks are moving where there is no wind, and if you go and put your hand down into the crumb in the nearest trough you will find, an inch away in the warm wet not-yet-earth, the hollow of another hand, still warmer than the crumb around it, full of roots.
