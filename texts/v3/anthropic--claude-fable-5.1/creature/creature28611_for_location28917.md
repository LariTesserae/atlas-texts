Your first answer, standing on the gallery, is that nothing does. Not in the sense the question means. The place has told you so itself: no fish, no bats, no webs, not one hole in one leaf. Whatever lives here has no eyes and no mouth and does not go anywhere.

And that is true, and it is the moss.

It is worth staying with this for a moment, because the moss is not many things. It looks like many things — cushions and continents and haloes and a carpet — but a moss colony is a single organism the way a lawn is not, each shoot connected below to the next, and the carpet on that floor has been growing without interruption or interference for longer than most animals live. It is one thing. It has a shape, which is the shape of the hall. It has a temperature preference, which it expresses by stopping exactly at the edge of the bearing-warmth. It has a memory, in the strict sense: cut it and you read the years. It responds to the lamps by leaning up at them and to the shafts by giving way to the bramble. It is fed by the river's spray and the machine's waste heat, and it feeds nothing, and it dies from underneath into itself, and the dying is what it stands on.

If you wanted to say the hall is inhabited by one very large, very slow, very patient thing that has learned the room completely and asks nothing of it but to be kept warm and wet, you would be describing the moss, and you would be right.

But you would not be finished.

---

The second answer is the one your body gives before your mind does, and it is the machine.

You have been standing in its breath since the stair. The penstocks are warm the way a flank is warm. The three hums swell against each other and die and swell, and there is no word for that but breathing, and you have already used it. The tremor in the rail is a pulse. The tailrace is the sound of something drinking. It would be very easy to say that what lives here is the plant itself, the whole engine, and that the green is simply what has grown on it the way lichen grows on a sleeping animal.

The trouble is that the machine does not want anything. It turns because water falls; it would turn just the same in a hall with no moss and no lamps and no one to hear it. It does not lean toward the light. It does not stop at the edge of anything. It has been made to look alive by the green, which took a warm object and dressed it and fitted itself to its seams until the two read as one body — but the wanting in that body is all on one side.

Except. Somebody painted the panel.

---

So the third answer, and the true one, arrives the way the fresh paint arrives: as a small wrong note in a place where nothing is wrong. A patch of railway green where everything else is a different green. A walkway that is a walkway. A dust-sheet that was, once, a decision.

There is one animal here.

You will not see them for a long time, and this is the first thing to know about them: they have made themselves into the thing the hall would least notice. They come down the stair the way the water comes through the screen — slowly, and taking nothing with them that isn't allowed. Their boots are soft-soled. They walk the paths and only the paths, and the paths are worn smooth in the centre because for decades they have been walked by the same feet in the same line, and if you looked you would find that the line bends very slightly at the base of each generator, out around the ring of *Selaginella*, so that the club-moss has never once been stepped on. They step over things they could step on. They have been doing it so long they no longer see the moss when they do it, any more than you see the kerb.

They do not speak. There is no room in the air for it, and after enough years of no room the habit goes. Perhaps above ground they still have words, for shops and forms. Perhaps not. Down here they have found that the hall answers a hand on a housing more readily than a voice, and that the things that need saying in this place — this bearing is a shade warmer than yesterday; this hum has drifted; the tailrace band is a finger higher than it should be in June — are not sayable anyway. They are things you feel through your palm and your teeth and act on.

---

Here is the shape of their day, insofar as the hall has days.

Down the stair, hand on the furred wall where the moss is thick and dry enough to trust. Along the gallery. Into the control room, where the dials sit behind their milked glass, and they do not read the dials — nobody could — but they know where each needle should stand the way you know where the furniture is in your bedroom in the dark, and they stand in front of the wall long enough to be sure that every needle is where it lives. Then down the iron stair to the turbine floor and along the line of the machines, and at each one they lay a hand flat on the bearing housing and leave it there for the length of a breath. That is the whole of the inspection. A hand knows a degree.

Then the tailrace, to look at the grille, and to look at the band on the wall. Then, if it is the season for it, the shafts: they stand under the thin green column of daylight and look up it, checking that the grille at the top is a grille and not a hole, because a hole would mean birds and birds would mean the end of the rule.

Once a year — perhaps not on a schedule; perhaps when something in the sound tells them — they open a panel. What they do inside it you would need to know the machine to say. It takes a day. They close it and they paint it, the same railway green from a tin they brought down on the tramway, and the reason they paint it is not that iron this warm and this dry under its lagging needs paint. It is that the machine was green, and someone should keep saying so.

They eat over the tailrace. This is the one place in the hall where a crumb does not matter, because the river takes everything that falls into it out under the grille and into the dark, and so the only eating that happens anywhere in this place happens leaning on the rail above the channel, cold food from a tin, and the tin goes back up the tramway with them. Nothing is left. Nothing has ever been left. The single animal in a room with no animals has arranged to leave no more mark on it than the water does, and for the same reason: because they pass through, and the place stays.

---

Whether they sleep here is a question the hall does not help you with.

There are twelve bunks and every one of them wears its moss, and if a blanket were left on one for a season it would wear moss too. So either they do not sleep here, or they sleep here and take their bedding up and down like a snail takes its shell. The second seems truer to the kind of person this is. Picture it: a roll of oilcloth on the top bunk nearest the door, unrolled at what their body has decided is night, rolled again in what it has decided is morning, and in between, sleep in a room where the drips fall on the frames around them and the hums beat through the mattress and it is — for them, now — the only quiet there is.

And there is the small room with the one chair. The books on its shelf are swollen past reading and they know that and have not moved them. They sit there. That is what the room is for now. Sitting in a place where the noise has stopped being noise is a thing a person can do for a long time, and they do.

---

Who they were before, the hall does not record.

The bunkroom says twelve. The canteen says twelve, or more, sitting down together, an urn, talk that had to be shouted. Somewhere between then and now the number went to one and the shouting went to nothing, and whether the one is the last of the twelve, or the child of one of them who came down after and found the door and the stair and the work waiting, or someone from nowhere who took a job that a piece of paper somewhere says still exists, is not written on anything down here. Their age you could not guess in the amber light. Damp and dimness and never speaking do things to a face that make it read as old and as no age at all.

It may not matter. It may be truer to say that the keeper is not a person but a position, the way the sycamore under the shaft is not a tree but a spot where a tree comes up every spring on borrowed light and lasts a season. One goes and, after a while, one comes. The hall does not distinguish. The paths stay the width of one body. The tin of green paint is on the shelf in the workshop under the root that holds the pipe, and the next hand finds it.

---

So let the image settle, since the hall has given you time.

What lives here is the moss, which is one thing and asks only warmth and wet. What lives here is a machine that has been dressed as a body so long it breathes. And what lives here, threaded between the two, the way the roots are threaded between the cables so that you have to look twice, is one animal who has spent a life making themselves less of one. Who eats only over the water that leaves. Who has traded speech for a palm on warm iron. Who has kept the paths open by walking them and kept the machine green by insisting, once a year, that it is. Who is the one thing in the entire place that can notice a change — a needle a hair off its mark, a hum that has drifted — and who has, for that reason, made sure to be the one thing in the place that never causes one.

If you stood on the gallery long enough you would see them, and you would take them at first for a shift in the dim, a shape moving at the pace shapes move here. They would have known you were coming since the stair. Not heard you — felt you, a new variation in a thing that does not vary. They would look up at the gallery from the turbine floor with a hand still flat on the bearing housing, and they would not say anything, because there is no room in the air, and because, in the only way that matters down here, everything that needs saying has already been said by the fact that the machine is still turning and the ferns are still perfect and the drips are still falling at exactly the rate they fell yesterday.

Then they would go back to what they were doing, and the carpet would close behind them, and you would have seen the only animal in the world that has learned to live in a place by leaving it, over and over, exactly as it was.
