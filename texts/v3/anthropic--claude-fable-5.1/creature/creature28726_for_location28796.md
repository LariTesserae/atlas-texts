They are not, most of them, from anywhere in particular. That is the first thing. The lorry brings up people the plains have finished with in some way that is never asked about — men and women who have run out of a marriage or a debt or a talent, or who simply found that they did not like being spoken to and were relieved to hear of a place where speech had been abolished by weather. The recruiter in the plains town at the foot of the switchback does not advertise. Word passes. It attracts a certain type and the Pan takes that type and makes it more so.

The first year is spent going deaf and learning to use the hands. New workers are put on the chipping crews, where the work is simple and the noise is worst, and by the time they have hammered their way through their first season of crust they have stopped trying to talk and started to sign. The sign language of the Pan is a working language; it was made by fitters and it is mostly about valves. It has more words for kinds of pressure than most spoken languages have for kinds of weather. It has a word for each of the pump houses and each of the towers and each of the long aqueducts, and it has a word — a flat hand laid against the breastbone, held there — for the sound, which is also the word for *here*, and for *now*, and by long extension for *yes*. When a fitter agrees with you on the Pan she puts her hand to her chest as if swearing something, and what she is saying is: I am inside it too.

---

Their bodies are changed by the place in small ways that accumulate.

The skin softens. Nothing dries; the hands go pale and wrinkled as if the whole body were always just out of a bath, and then, at the places where the mineral water beads and evaporates over and over — the knuckles, the backs of the wrists, the rim of the ear — it does the opposite, and forms a fine white ridge of lime that will not wash off and that the older workers no longer try to remove. Fingernails grow ribbed. Hair stiffens and lightens and after a decade stands off the head in a dull chalky brush. Boots become stone within a season, a smooth pale hoof with a foot inside it, and are replaced not when they wear through — they do not wear through — but when they grow too heavy to lift.

They walk in a particular way, flat-footed and slow, feeling for the lip of each basin through the sole, because a person who trips on the Pan cannot call out. They stand in a particular way too, slightly braced, as if against a wind, because the ground hums and the body learns to absorb it. On the rare visits down to the plains they are known by this stance and by the way they shout, never having heard their own voices go flat and enormous over the years. They are known too by how they look at a quiet room — with something that is not quite fear but is adjacent to it, the way a person who has lived beside the sea looks at a lake.

They lose the top of hearing first. Birdsong, if there were birds. The hiss of a kettle, which is a joke they make in sign. Then, over years, the middle. What is left at the end is the roar, and the roar is not heard so much as inhabited, and inside it, faintly, the low tones — footfall through the floor, the thump of an engine changing stroke, a fist on a door. They can feel a lorry arrive from a quarter-mile away. They cannot hear their own names.

---

There are four kinds of people on the Pan, and the divisions are as fixed as anything there.

The house-fitters are stationary. Each pump house has its crew, a dozen or twenty, and a fitter will attach to one house and stay with it for a working life, sleeping in the dormitory block bolted to its side, learning the particular chord of its engines so completely that a change of a fraction in the note will bring her out of sleep and down the ladder with a spanner before she has fully understood why. They are proprietary about their houses. The Fourth House crew consider the Fourth House to be the true centre of the works and everyone else to be an outpost; the Ninth House crew think the same about the Ninth. They meet perhaps twice a year and regard one another as foreigners.

The chipping crews move. There are three of them, each a dozen strong, and they walk the Pan on a circuit that takes eleven years, hammering the crust off pipe and pier and stair and valve, sleeping in whatever house they have reached, moving on. They are the closest thing the plateau has to a travelling class, and they are treated as such — given the best of the food when they arrive, asked for news with the hands, resented a little once they have been in the house a week. A chipper sees every part of the Pan once a decade and so is the only sort of person who can say, with any authority, what has changed: this pier has thickened, that basin has spilled over into a new one, the stairs to the south tower are a ramp now where eleven years ago they were still stairs. They have a word for this, the slow difference, and it is the same as their word for *home*.

The signallers live on the towers. One to a tower, in a glazed cabin at the top — glass kept clear by daily chipping, since a signaller who cannot see is nothing — with a lamp and a rack of coloured flags and a stove. They talk. It is the strangest fact of the place: that in a city where speech is impossible the signallers conduct, nightly, the longest conversations on the plateau, in lamp-code across a mile of steam, with a person they have never stood beside. The code was made for flow rates and engine states. It has been bent, over generations, to carry everything else. There are signallers on the south edge who have exchanged lamps every night for twenty years with the signaller on the next tower and could not pick that person out of a room of three. When one of them dies, the tower goes dark for a night, and the next one over sends the same short sequence into the mist every hour until morning, and then stops, and takes up with the replacement as if nothing had occurred, because what else is there to do.

And there is the road — the lorry driver and the two or three who work the depot at the top of the switchback, who spend half of each month in the roar and half in the silence and belong fully to neither. They are the only people on the Pan who still speak aloud out of habit, and the only ones below who hold their hand to their chest to say yes.

---

They eat in silence in long rooms with thick walls. The food is what will keep in the damp — grain, salt fish, hard cheese that goes soft in a week, coffee — and it is eaten quickly, and afterwards there is the rest of the shift's dark to fill.

They carve. Wood is useless on the Pan, swelling and rotting in the wet, but there is limestone everywhere, chipped off the works by the ton, and the workers have taken to it. In the dormitories, on shelves above the stoves where the air is driest, stand small pale figures — engines, towers, the outline of a particular house, hands making particular signs. They cannot be kept outside; anything left out is recoated within the year, and a carving set on a windowsill will be a smooth pale lump by the time the chippers come round again. Some are set out deliberately for this reason. A fitter who has finished her time will leave a carving on the roof of her house before she takes the lorry down, and it will be taken by the stone, and become part of the house, and the next chipping crew will feel a bump under the hammer and go round it.

They play a card game whose rules nobody on the plains would recognise, with cards that have swelled to the thickness of biscuits and are held together by habit. They drink. They sleep a great deal, because sleep is the only place the roar becomes background rather than foreground, and even there it does not leave — the workers of the Pan dream in the noise, and the dreams have a floor to them, a boom under everything.

They pair off, sometimes. Not often, and rarely for long; the shifts do not allow for it and the houses are far apart. When it happens it is conducted almost entirely by hand and face and the pressure of a body in the hum, and the people involved seem, to outsiders, hardly to notice one another — a glance across the long room, the flat palm to the chest.

---

There are children. Not many, and the works do not encourage it, but the lorry cannot take down a woman who does not want to go and by the time some of them have decided, the child has arrived, and is a child of the Pan.

They are born into the roar and hear it before they hear anything else. Most of them are deaf in the ordinary sense by the age of five and have never particularly missed what they never had. They sign before they walk. They do not speak, or only rarely, in a mutter copied from the adults without understanding what the mouth is doing. What is remarkable about them — what the fitters notice and do not much discuss — is that they hear the structure. A child of the Pan can stand in fog at a junction of two aqueducts and point, without hesitation, to each of the houses by the note they are making, and can tell you the month by the chord. They hear the year. Some of them say they can hear the deep works, under the three hundred feet of drawings and the word *warm* — a slow note under the slow note, they say, and put their hand to their chest, and the adults let it go.

When they are grown they mostly stay. Those who do go down come back within a year or two, thinner, and take the first shift they are offered.

---

The old are a fifth kind, and there are more of them than there should be.

The rule is that a worker who has done twenty years is taken down on the lorry, with a pension, to the plains. Many go. A good number do not, and the works have never had the heart or the means to force it, and so there has grown up around the Seventh House, on the western edge where the falls are lowest and the noise is least, a population of people who have finished and not left. They do light work — the glass in the signal cabins, the kitchens, the sorting of the depot — and they live in the oldest dormitory block on the Pan, whose windows are all stone and whose doorway has narrowed with lime until they go in and out of it sideways.

They cannot go down. That is the plain fact of them. They have tried, some of them, and lain awake in the enormous silence of the plains for a week or a month and come back up on the next lorry, looking, the driver says, like people rescued from drowning. The Pan is in the skull by then and the skull needs the Pan around it to make sense of what it is hearing. They are the ones who say the note is dropping. They stand in the evenings on the west edge with their hands on the pier of the great western aqueduct, feeling it, and they agree with one another — slower now, deeper — and disagree about everything else, most of all about what the pumps are for. One of them will tell you, in sign, that the works were built to drain the Pan and keep the plains from flood. Another will tell you the opposite: that the water was pumped up, that the plains were once dry and the Pan is a reservoir for them, and that the falls are the leak. A third will tell you both are true and that the direction reverses with the century. They are the only people on the plateau who still think the question matters, and they have made no progress on it in forty years, and they are content.

---

The dead cannot be buried. The ground is crust for as far down as anyone has dug and it heals faster than a grave can be cut. So they are laid out. On the north side, under the range, where the water comes up heaviest with lime, there is a set of terraces the workers call the Rows, and the dead are carried there and set down in the shallow warm basins, on their backs, and left. The water does the rest. In a month there is a pale shell; in a year, a smooth low shape with a face still faintly in it; in a decade, a swelling in the floor of a basin that the water runs over and spills from into the next. The Rows are the one part of the Pan the chipping crews do not chip. They are walked once a year, in autumn when the flow drops and the shapes are clearest, by whoever wishes to walk them, and there is no ceremony, and nobody says anything, and the sound goes on.

---

That is who lives there. There are the others — the swifts that hang in the spray and nest in the roar and hear none of it; the red crustaceans in their billions, blind in the warm felt; the algae, which was there before the works and will be there after and is the true population of the place, a mat of living colour on which a few thousand people have set up housekeeping; the eels, once in a few years, on the intake grate, flexing, eyeless, from a depth nobody has drawn. The children say they can hear them. The old say the note is dropping. The fitters say nothing and put a hand to the chest.

The honest answer, if one were pressed for it, is that what lives on the Pan is the sound, and everyone else attends it. It was there when the first engineers came up the switchback with their drawings, in the falls if not yet in the engines, and it will be there when the last house has filled with stone and the lorry has stopped coming. In the meantime it is fed coal and cable and people, and it holds its note, and lowers it, by a fraction, by a fraction, and the people who live inside it hold their hands against their breastbones and say *here* and *now* and *yes*, which are the same word, and which are all, in the end, that anyone up there has to say.
