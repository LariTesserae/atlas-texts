Begin with the mistake you will make, because it is the correct first mistake: you will think nothing lives here. You will stand on the pale stone under the violet sky and see the groves as furniture, as features, as the plateau's vegetation, and you will look past them for the inhabitants, for the thing with a face that this place belongs to. There is no such thing. But that is not the same as saying the place is empty. The plateau is inhabited from horizon to horizon. It is simply inhabited by things that do not move at the speed at which you are used to recognizing life.

So start again. Start with the groves, and stay with them longer than feels reasonable, because they are the ones who live here, and everything else lives on their terms.

---

A grove is one thing. This is difficult to hold in the mind, standing at the leaf-wall with your hand on a cool paddle, because it looks like a thicket—looks like a hundred trees grown up together and crowded—but the trunks are not individuals. They are the places where a single organism has decided, for now, to hold itself up. Under the flour the roots meet and merge and the wood is continuous; the amber that moves in one trunk moves in all of them; a leaf broken on the eastern face is felt, slowly, on the western. If you could see it whole, root and crown, you would see something less like a forest than like a single vast animal lying down, its back to the sky, holding very still.

It is holding still because it is paying attention. That is the closest available word. The grove has, in effect, one sense, and that sense is the sun: where it is, how hard it lands, what angle it makes with each face of the canopy. Every paddle is an organ of that sense. The grove reads the sky continuously and adjusts continuously, and the adjustment is so slow that it has no visible present tense—only a past you can measure by the tilt of the leaves and a future you can infer from it. It is not thinking about the sun. It is doing what a thought does, without the thinker: gathering a fact from the world and answering it with a change in itself. That answer, accumulated across a day, turns the canopy. Accumulated across a year, it leans the whole grove. Accumulated across a life, it writes the lane.

What the grove is made of is not water and never was, and this matters more than it seems. Water is quick. It evaporates; it freezes; it carries rot; it must be found and kept and defended. The amber is none of those things. It does not leave. A grove loses almost nothing to the air, and what it makes it keeps, and so a grove is a closed account, an economy that runs almost entirely on sunlight and stone and time. This is why they are lush past reason in a place that has never been wet: the lushness is not spent on anything. Nothing here rots. A leaf that fails does not fall and decay; it hardens where it hangs, and the grove, over years, draws the amber back out of it and lets the husk go to the flour, dry and weightless, or seals it into a trunk. Push far enough in and look at the ribbing on an old trunk and you will find it is not ribbing. It is layers. It is the shapes of leaves and shoots and other things, folded in, lacquered over, kept.

What the grove knows, it knows about light and cold and itself. But it also knows, in some way, about the others. The pollen that goes up in the afternoon gold smoke is more than seed. It carries the grove's substance, a trace of its particular amber, and when it settles on another grove's outstretched paddles the receiving grove takes it in, and some of what it takes in is information—which grove, from which direction, how far. Across a hundred kilometers the groves are faintly, constantly in touch. Not speaking. Tasting one another on the wind. And they read the stone: a grove drifting into a lane another grove has swept clean, decades ago, moves differently there, roots taking hold in a pattern already made. The plateau is written on, and the groves are the only ones who can read it, and what it says is *someone passed this way*.

Then, rarely, two of them meet.

You would need centuries to watch it, so watch it in the stone instead: two lanes converging, then one broad smoothed place where they overlapped, then two lanes leaving. What happened there took perhaps sixty years. The leaf-walls touched and stopped being walls. Roots that had been draped over the same plate for a decade found each other and did what roots do. Trunks were shared—a shoot sent up from one grove's root, in the confusion, fed by the other's amber, belonging to both. For a while there was a single grove twice the size, two attentions to the same sun, and the amber of each ran into the other, and each tasted the other from the inside. And then the sun moved on, as it does, and they moved with it, and because they had come from different directions they left in different directions, and they had to decide what was whose. Some of that decision was made by geometry: this trunk is on that side, it goes with that grove. Some of it was not. A grove coming out the far side of a crossing carries trunks it did not have going in, and lacks some it did, and its amber is not quite its own for a generation. It is not the same grove. But there was never a same grove. There was a shape that an argument had arrived at, and the argument has been joined by another voice, and the shape has changed, and it goes on.

This is why the word *individual* does not quite hold here, and why *who* does not quite hold. A grove is closer to weather than to a creature—a pattern that has learned to keep itself going, that persists without a center, that can be split or joined without dying. It is the most alive thing on the plateau and it has no self to lose. It has only its direction, and its slowness, and the sun.

---

Inside the groves live the climbers, and they are the second kind of thing here, and they are the first that would seem to you like animals.

You will not see one well. They are the color of the trunks, and they move in the dark fur of filaments near the wood, and they are slow—not grove-slow, but slow the way a hand is slow when it does not want to be noticed. Their bodies are long and low and jointed in more places than a body ought to be, so that they do not walk along a trunk so much as pour along it, each segment finding its own hold. Their eyes are small and set in shadow and do not like the light. If you stand still long enough in the submerged green you will begin to see them the way you see stars in the corner of your vision: a shape that resolves as movement just as you look at it directly, and then is only bark.

What they are doing is tending. Not in the way a farmer tends; in the way a body's small cells tend the body. Where a leaf has broken and the amber has sealed the wound, a climber will come to it, eventually, and eat the seal—the thickened, exposed amber gone hard and dull—and leave the wound clean for the grove to close properly from beneath. Where a husk hangs that the grove has finished with, they take it down. Where the fine filament-fur has grown across a gap it should not, they clear it. They eat the grove's waste and the grove's mistakes, and what they get in return is everything: the amber they consume is their blood and their water and their food and their warmth. They have never been outside. They are born in the fur and die in the fur and their bodies, when they die, do not rot—nothing rots—but harden and are sealed and are drawn into the wood, and if you look very closely at the layered trunks you will find, among the leaf-shapes, other shapes, long and many-jointed, kept.

Whether they belong to the grove or the grove to them is a question with no edge to grab. When groves cross, the climbers cross too, and some stay behind in the wrong grove, and the grove takes them as it takes everything. There are probably no two groves whose climbers are strangers. There is probably, across the whole plateau, one population of them, mixed and remixed at every crossing for longer than the lanes go back, so that the climbers are the one thing here that is continuous across all the groves—the grove's hands, borrowed, traveling.

They make a sound. It is not the leaf-tick. It is softer and rarer, a dry brief friction, and you will hear it only in the hour after dusk when the canopy has closed and the whole grove has gone dark and still, and then, from somewhere above, one climber shifting its weight against another. They rest in contact. Whatever passes between them then, it is not much, and it is enough.

---

Out on the stone, once in a long while: a grazer.

You have already seen one, or seen the idea of one—tall, narrow, pale, folding. Look longer. The pallor is not coloration so much as a kind of translucency; in the flat light of morning you can see the shadow of its bones. The neck is long because the head must reach the leaf-wall without the body coming near enough to be shaded, and the legs fold in so many places because at night, when the stone goes cold enough to ache, the animal lowers itself onto the flour, joint by joint, until it lies folded into a shape almost exactly the size and color of a stone plate, and there is no way to tell it from one until morning, when it unfolds, and stands, and the plate has legs.

They are always alone. This is not shyness. There is nothing on the plateau to fear, and a grazer standing at a leaf-wall will not move away when approached; it will keep eating with the same slow deliberate care and look at you, if it looks at all, with a small dark eye that has no particular expression. They are alone because there is no reason for two of them to occupy the same grove at the same time. Each grove, on its outer face, produces only so much that can be taken without harm, and the grazer knows the measure—knows it as surely as the grove knows the sun—and takes exactly that and no more, and then walks in a straight line to the next dark smudge on the horizon. Two at one grove would be one too many. So there are not two.

Their mouths come away glossy because the amber is what they drink. It moves in them as it moves in the groves; they have no water either. What they cannot use they leave in the flour, dry and small, and it is worth kneeling to look at what is in it, because the answer is: seed. The grove's pollen falls wasted into the flour by the ton and lies there, and almost none of it ever begins anything; but a grazer, eating the outermost paddles, takes in pollen with the leaf, and carries it out onto the bare stone in a body that keeps it dark and warm and moist with amber, and sets it down kilometers from any grove, in a small dry packet of everything it needs. Follow a grazer's straight-line track across the plateau, the prints that will last a century, and here and there beside it you will find a shoot the size of a finger, black-green, alone on the pale stone, with a lane behind it not yet long enough to see. Most of them fail. The ones that do not are the plateau's new groves, and every one of them began at the end of a grazer's stride.

So the grazer is not a parasite and not a pet. It is the grove's gait. The grove walks at the speed of the sun's yearly wander; the grazer walks at a pace you could match; and between them they cover the plateau. The grove could not seed the far side of the tableland on wind alone in ten thousand years. On foot, it has already done it.

You asked about the occasion on which two grazers meet. It is the crossing. When two groves pass through one another and are for sixty years a single grove, the grazers who attend each arrive at the same leaf-wall, and there are, for the first time in perhaps a generation, two. What happens then happens where it cannot be watched—inside the doubled grove, in the submerged green, among trunks that belong to both. The young are born small and pale and folded, and they do not go out onto the stone at first. They stay in, and eat from the inside where the leaves are softest, and the climbers clear around them, and the grove closes over them at dusk. Some of them, it is thought, never leave. Some of them, when the groves finally part and there is a wall between them again, are on the far side of it, and go out into a lane that is not the one their parent walked, and that is how the grazers, too, are one population, mixed at every crossing, continuous across the whole tableland.

---

There is a fourth kind of thing, and you will not see it at all, because it is in the flour.

The wind ripples the flour, and the ripples look like a seabed, and you have taken them for wind. Look more closely, in the last light, when the rising day-wind has laid down and the flour is still. Some of the ripples are still moving. Not far—a millimeter in an hour, a hand's breadth in a season—but moving, and not with the wind, and not toward the sun. They are moving toward pollen.

Whatever lives in the flour is small past seeing and pale past the flour's own paleness and eats the gold smoke that the groves send up and lose. Of every hundred grains that fall wasted onto the stone, ninety-nine go to this—to a slow tide in the dust that finds them and takes them apart and moves on. It is the reason the flour is not, after all these ages, thick with pollen: the flour is clean because the flour is eating. And what it leaves behind is finer than what it found—the flour itself, or part of it, is what these things have made of stone and pollen and time. The surface you walk on is a body. The print you leave that lasts a century lasts because the body has not yet closed over it.

The grazers' dry packets fall into this, and the shoots that survive do so partly because the flour around them has been fed and is, in some very slow sense, willing. And when a grove passes and its roots let go on the shaded side, they leave behind a lane of husk and old amber, and the flour follows it. The lanes are not swept clean by wind alone. They are grazed.

---

Take stock again, then, standing on the bare stone at noon under the sun that lands.

The groves, who are one thing each and drift with the year and taste one another on the wind and pass through one another and come out changed. The climbers, who are the groves' hands and have never been outside and are kept, in the end, in the wood. The grazers, who are the groves' feet and are alone by arithmetic and are the reason there are groves at all past the horizon. The flour, which is alive at a depth and a speed that make the groves look hasty, and which is the ground everything else stands on and the ground everything else becomes.

Four kinds of life, and not one of them is a *who*. Nothing here has a name for itself, or a self to name. Nothing here has ever made anything, because making is what you do when the world will not hold still long enough to be lived in directly, and here it does. There is no one at the center of the plateau. There is a system that has found its state: the groves following the sun, the grazers following the groves, the flour following the groves' waste, the climbers keeping the groves clean, the pollen going up gold and coming down and being eaten or carried or, once in a thousand years, becoming something new with a lane behind it.

And yet if you had to say who lives here—if the question insisted, if you needed to point—you would point at a grove. Not because it is the largest, though it is, nor because everything else depends on it, though everything does. You would point at it because of all the things here, it is the one that most plainly *decides*. Every day it reads the sky and answers. Every year the answer accumulates into a lean. Every century the lean becomes a lane. It has one sense and one desire and no interior, and it has been choosing the sun, continuously, for longer than the stone it stands on has had its present surface, and the record of that choosing is the only history the plateau has.

Something with no mind, that has spent every moment of an unimaginable life paying attention to exactly one thing and turning itself toward it. That is what lives here. It is not thinking. It is not awake. But it is not indifferent either—it is, in the most literal sense available, *inclined*—and at dusk, when the leaves close one by one and the plateau fills with that slow textured ticking from everywhere and nowhere, it is hard not to hear it as something settling, contented, at the end of a day it has spent, as it spends every day, facing the light.
