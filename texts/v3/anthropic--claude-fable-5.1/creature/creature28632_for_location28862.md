Start with what can be counted, because that is what the place offers first.

The amphipods live here the way dust lives in a house. There are unthinkable numbers of them and no one of them lives long; a single animal is hatched in the lee of a block, blows across two terraces in its life, eats the grey film and each other, and is eaten in turn by something that will itself be nothing in a year. They are not inhabitants. They are a condition of the water, like the silt. The small fish are the same: a shoal is a thing that persists, but the fish in it do not, and the shoal itself is only a habit the water has, a shape it keeps pouring into and out of. The waders and the divers on the surface are older, individual, recognizable — a bird can be known by a notch in its wing for eleven or twelve years — but they are not of the place either. They came over the rim on the wind, and their bones, when they die, lie on the top terrace and are picked clean and are ground to nothing in the chop within a season. Nothing of them enters the stone.

The pebble-fish come closer. Each one holds a length of terrace for its whole life and will not leave it, and there are lines of them along every wall that have been unbroken, parent to child, for longer than the birds have had wings. They know the paving. A pebble-fish put down on a terrace it does not know will search, visibly, for the joint it was born beside. If the place has a people, in the sense of animals who belong to a particular stone and will die on it, these are the nearest thing. But they inherit only a habit. They do not remember. Each morning's courtyard is the first courtyard that has ever been built.

So none of that is the answer. Look at the frogs.

They are wrong for the place. They are too big for what there is to eat, and too slow to catch it, and they do not eat, or not often — a frog on a step in the shallows may be watched for a month and never open its mouth. They are the color of the blocks and their skin hangs off them in the same slack folds that the terraces make in the fog, and when they move it is not the way a frog moves but the way a thing moves that has forgotten it can. They are always facing the same way: toward the middle of the basin, toward the cistern mouths. They sit on the steps like something waiting to go down.

And now and then one does. It slides off the lip of a terrace and lets the night cold take it — the dense curtain that forms along the wall and pours down the stairs — and it goes with it, not swimming, riding the falling water down into the grey where the light stops. It does not come back. The others do not look.

Below the light, the shafts.

The square wells that open in the terraces do not end. They step down inside themselves — cistern within cistern, each floor a lid with a smaller mouth in it — and they connect. Below the paved valley floor there is a second architecture, the one the first was built to close over: galleries, wide as the causeways and lower, running beneath the whole bowl in a plan that has the same straightness and the same indifference to any purpose an animal on two legs could have had. The water in them does not move with the wind. It moves with something else — a very slow tide, in and out through the shaft mouths on a rhythm of days, and this is what turns the cisterns over and sends the bubbles up. The bubbles are breath. Not many. Not often.

What breathes them was here before the water, and made the stone.

It is difficult to hold as a single image, because it is not built to be seen whole and the water will not let you. What is met, in the galleries, is a wall that is not a wall — a face of something pale as the undersides of blocks, the same grey-white, the same slack and pitted surface, that when the silt clears a little is seen to be breathing, if a movement completed in the time it takes a cold current to pass can be called breathing. Follow it and it goes on. It fills the gallery as the amphipods fill a hollow, packed to the sides, and where the gallery turns it turns, and where a shaft descends it has a limb down the shaft. The limbs end in hands. That is the thing the eye refuses and then accepts. They are not a frog's hands, exactly, but they are what a frog's hands were going to be, or once were: long, splayed, webbed only to the first joint, with pads on the fingertips worn smooth as the stair-treads above. The fingers are as thick as a body. Two of them rest on a block that has not been laid yet.

Because it is still building. This is what the deep knock is. Down here the blocks that were left ready — split along the grain, dressed, stacked along the gallery walls when the water rose — are being set, one at a time, and one at a time means a lifetime of birds for the finger to close and a lifetime more for the block to move. The pebble-fish rebuild every dawn and are never different. This rebuilds once in a thousand years and the plan is never the same twice, because each stone set below changes what the water does above: shuts a current, opens a shaft, sends the cold down a different stair. The invisible weather of the basin is the weather of a house being finished, very slowly, from underneath.

It does not eat. Or it eats the way the stone would eat if stone were alive: the film on the undersides of things, the fall of dead amphipods into the shafts, the cold itself, drawn in and let out. Its blood is nearly the temperature of the water and the water is nearly the temperature of ice, and so it lives at the pace of ice — not frozen, not stopped, but so close to stopped that a century is what a season is to a frog. This is how it lasted. This is how the whole highland was rewritten by hand: not by many hands moving quickly, but by these, few, moving at a rate that outlasted the mountain's objections. The builders had no tools that survive because their tools were their fingers, and their fingers are still here.

Whether there is one of it or several cannot be settled from inside. The galleries all have it in them. Where two limbs meet, it is not clear they belong to the same body, and in the largest chamber under the center of the bowl — the one the causeways, on the surface, point at from all sides and never reach — there is a mass that may be a torso and may be several animals lying against each other in the cold as frogs do, for the little warmth. The frogs on the steps are what it makes. Not children in any sense that has a season in it; they simply appear in the shallows, small and soft and folded and already old-looking, and they sit facing the shafts for a decade or a lifetime, and one by one they go down when the cold comes and are added. Whether they are added to it or become it, or simply lie beside it in the dark and slow until they are indistinguishable, is the same question asked three ways.

It knows the water is there. That is the last thing. It was not drowned by accident and it is not waiting for the wind to win. The bowl was made for the water and the water came, and the work went under as it was meant to, and the rim was set at exactly the height where the surface would stop and the wind would begin its useless combing. The chop on top, the roar that the ear gives up on — that is the weather it arranged so that the place would never be still, so that the silt would never settle, so that nothing green would ever take hold and nothing warm would ever stay. It likes the cold. It came from the cold, before anything on this plateau had a stem or a wing, and it built a house where the cold could pour down the walls every night forever, and it went inside, and it is still setting the last stones.

Above it, the amphipods glitter and are gone. The fish empty a cistern and fill it. A pebble is set down, and knocked loose, and rolls two steps. And under all of it, in the grey, a finger the size of a man closes another hair's breadth on a block that has been waiting a thousand years to be laid, and the whole valley hears the knock and drinks it, and does not change.
