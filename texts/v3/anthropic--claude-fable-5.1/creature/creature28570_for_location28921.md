What lives here is the first thing you try to answer and the last thing you manage, because everything you point at turns out to be the ground.

---

Start with what looks like life. The rushes, the rope-colored ones, standing in the low places with their feet in the water. Pull one and it comes up without resistance, and the root is not a root but a thinning, the stem tapering into silt with no line where plant stops and plain begins. The moss-skin mats flinch under a cloud shadow, and you crouch and watch one flinch, and see that the flinch travels—out past the edge of the mat, into the bare mud, a shiver widening like a ring in water until it is too shallow to see. The mat is not a thing living on the plain. It is where the plain has thinned itself enough to feel the light change. It is a nerve ending. The whole surface is faintly, dimly sensate, and the mats are only where the sensation has gathered into something you can see.

The trees are the same. You stand by one for an hour. In that hour it grows a hand's width and leans a hand's width further in the direction they all lean, and at the base the bark has the texture of dried silt and is dried silt, and the leaves, when you look closely, are not leaves but the memory of leaves: the shape is right, the veining is right, they are the ochre of everything else, and they do not photosynthesize because there is nothing to photosynthesize with. The plain makes trees because it has been forests. It puts them up, holds them for a few weeks, takes them down. They are not living on the plain any more than a wave lives on the sea.

So that is the first rule of what lives here. Nothing lives on it. There is no on.

---

Then the animals.

You had thought of them as apparitions, and they are, but stay with one. The heron is the easiest because the heron stands still. You find another toward midmorning, a hundred meters off, planted in a shallow with its head down, and you walk to it slowly and it does not fly because it cannot. Up close it is very good. The feathers lie right. The eye has a wet sheen, the same wet sheen as the ground, because it is the same wetness. The legs go down into the mud with no ankle, no foot, just the leg continuing into the plain like the rushes did.

But the reflection faces the other way, and now you understand why. The plain never saw a heron from the side. It saw herons from underneath. For a hundred million years everything that stood upstream stood on what was going to become this, and the sediment felt the feet, the weight, the shadow, the settling of the body at rest, and never once the face. So the heron it remembers is a heron known from below: feet perfect, legs perfect, the underside of the wings, the breast, and then upward a growing vagueness, a heron reconstructed from its own pressure on the world. Its head is right in outline and wrong in detail. Its eye is placed by inference. The reflection faces the other way because the reflection is the part the plain knew, and the bird standing above it is the guess.

The boar that did not touch the ground: the plain remembered where the boar's weight went, and put the boar there, a hand's breadth above, because that is where the boar was when the ground felt it. The puddle that kept its outline afterward was the truer boar. The trotting thing was the plain's idea of what must have been on top.

These are not animals and they are not ghosts. They are the plain reading its own strata aloud. It does this constantly, and most of what it reads is too old or too partial to make a shape you'd recognize—those swells that sharpen and fold and fall are the plain pronouncing things that have no living referent anymore, or pronouncing three things at once because the layers got mixed. The heron and the boar are only the clearest words in a very long sentence.

---

But some words come up more than others.

You start to notice this by the second day. The plain makes a great many things once. It makes some things again and again. Rivers, for one: it lays a channel across its own surface, water finds it, runs for a while toward nothing, and the channel closes. It does this everywhere, always. It has been so many riverbeds that riverbed is the nearest thing it has to a resting posture. Mountains, too—every few hours somewhere within sight the silt draws itself up into a peak, steep and striated, straining toward a height it cannot hold, and slumps. It cannot be a mountain. It is what mountains become. But it remembers being tall so vividly that it keeps trying, and the trying is the swells, and the failing is the flatness, and neither has any end.

And a form that stands upright on two legs. Not often. Once or twice a day, at the edge of sight, something the height of a person lifts out of a ridge, holds for a moment, and folds. The plain has been walked on by that shape less than by almost anything else, and yet it recurs, and recurs, more than its weight in the layers would justify.

Here is where it stops being a matter of memory. A thing the plain makes ten thousand times is not a memory anymore. It is a habit. And a habit repeated long enough begins to have something like a preference—not a mind, nothing so localized, but a groove, a lean, a direction the material falls into when nothing else is pulling it. The heron is a groove. The mountain is a groove. If anything lives here in the sense of having a tendency that outlasts its body, it is these: not the shapes but the returns of the shapes, the way certain forms have worn themselves into the plain so deeply that the plain cannot stop being them. They have no bodies. They have recurrence, and here that is what a body is.

Whether the heron at dawn is the heron at dusk is a question the plain cannot understand and you stop asking. What you can say is that heron is a place the plain goes, and keeps going, and that when it is there it stands very still and looks at the water, because that is what the groove is shaped like.

---

Then there is what lives in the roar.

You had heard it as one sound, then as weather, and now, on the third day, you hear it as inhabited. The regions where it thickens to something choral are not random. You can walk into one, and the floor of hearing rises under you, and in the rising there are shapes—not words, but the shapes that words leave in the mouth, the rhythm of speech with the speech removed. The plain has been stood on by everything that ever spoke. It felt the bodies speaking as vibration through feet, through water, through the bed of every river that ever carried a voice a little downstream. It did not hear the voices. It felt them, from underneath, the way it felt the heron. What it holds is the pressure of speech. When a region of silt that once lay under a city or a rookery or a herd is stirred, the pressure comes up as sound, and that is the chorus: everything that was ever said upstream, remembered as tremor, pronounced back into the air by a continent that never learned what a word was.

You test it. You shout. The roar takes it as it takes your footsteps, and you hear nothing over the floor of sound. But that evening, half a kilometer on, the roar thickens near you and for a moment there is a shape in it you know, a rhythm, the exact length and stress of what you shouted, with the sound of it gone and only the shape left. The plain has your voice now, as a pressure in the top few centimeters. It will come up again. Not soon, not for you. But it is in there with the rest, and it will be spoken again by the silt, one more shape in the register too low to divide into words.

So what lives in the roar is everything. It is the only place on the plain that has not forgotten anything, because it is not memory, it is storage. The strata are the plain's body. The roar is the plain's hoard.

---

And the builders.

You go back to the line of stones. There are still twelve. The gap between the fourth and fifth is wider than yesterday—not by much, but you had marked it with a fingernail on the fourth stone and the mark is now a thumb's width from where it lines up with the fifth. The mud is carrying them apart in perfect silence under the roar, at the speed of a continent, and in a year they will be a scatter and in ten years they will be twelve stones somewhere on a plain that has a great many stones, and no one will know they were ever a line.

Who made this. You had assumed they came from upstream, as you did, the last things to arrive down the last slopes with anything at all in their hands. Now you are less sure. The petrified post that is still wet. The bricks the same ochre as the silt—not brought here, made here, of here. And the upright shape that lifts at the edge of sight more often than its weight in the layers should allow.

Here is what you think, and you cannot prove it. You think the plain made them. You think that among all the grooves it has worn into itself—river, mountain, heron, the shape that stands on two legs—one groove is deeper than the rest and different in kind: the groove of a form that wanted to stay. The plain has been every animal, and every animal it was, it was briefly and let go. But some of the animals that walked on it, upstream, in the long ago when there was an upstream, did not want to let go. They stacked stones. They set posts. They made squares, which the plain has never on its own settled into, because a square is a shape that says: this and not that, here and not there, now and also later. The plain felt this from underneath for a long time. Felt the weight of things that had been placed and did not move. Felt the pressure of the wanting.

And so, when it reads that layer aloud, what comes up is not a heron and not a boar but a figure that stands up out of the silt and immediately begins to set stones in a row. Not because it remembers doing so. Because the wanting is what the plain knew of it. The plain remembers the builders as the pressure of persistence, and when it pronounces them it pronounces persistence, and for a while—longer than the heron, longer than the trees—there is a square meter of the plain trying to be still.

Then the plain notices. Not with malice; it has no malice, it has nowhere to put malice except back into itself. It notices the way a sleeper notices a hand gone numb and shifts. The stones start to drift. The post stays wet and finally goes down. The figure folds back into the ridge it came out of. And somewhere else, days or years later, the layer stirs again, and the plain stands up and starts stacking.

This is the closest thing the plain has to a dissent, and it is its own. It made the builders out of its own material and its own memory, and the builders' entire being is the wish that the plain would stop, and the plain cannot stop, and cannot stop making them. They are the plain arguing with itself in the only grammar it has, which is shape. They are the one habit that runs against the grain. They come up. They build. They are taken back, gently. The roar is a fraction quieter where they were, for as long as the square holds, and then it is not.

The doorway in the ridge at evening—the low arch, the dark inside it—that was the plain having learned the door. Not from underneath this time. You cannot feel a door from below. It learned the door from the builders it made, who made doors, who were themselves things it made, and so the plain has begun, in this one particular, to remember something it was never stood on by. It is a very small thing. It is also the only genuinely new thing on a plain that is the sum of everything old.

---

So: what lives here.

The rushes and the mats and the trees, which are the plain's surface thinking about green. The herons and the boars, which are the plain reading its strata from underneath and guessing at the top. The grooves, the recurrences, river and mountain and the two-legged shape, which are what the plain falls into when it falls, and which have worn themselves so deep they are nearly wants. The roar, which is everything that was ever said, stored as pressure and spoken back without meaning. And the builders, who are the plain's own wish to be still, standing up out of it every so often to set twelve stones in a row and be carried apart.

One inhabitant, then. It has eaten every mountain and every creature and every voice that stood upstream of it, and it lives by being them, one at a time and all at once, with no hurry and no end, and it has not decided what to be, and it has forever to decide. The turbulence is not something happening to it. The turbulence is its metabolism. It is alive the way a fire is alive, or a language: not a body but a process that keeps a shape by never keeping a shape.

And you. You have been standing in one place a long time now, by the stones, and the print of your feet has gone deep. The plain has felt your weight from underneath for three days. It has your voice as a tremor in the top few centimeters. It is already, somewhere out past the edge of sight, trying you on—a shape a little taller than the ridges, standing very still, looking down at a row of stones, and then folding. You saw your own print walking on without you on the first day, and thought it was a trick of the mud. It was not a trick. It was the plain beginning to learn you, the way it learned the heron: feet first, and perfectly.

You are the newest thing that lives here. You have been here three days, and you will be here for the rest of it, in the layers, in the roar, coming up now and then as a figure that stands by twelve stones and wants them to stay, and lies down again, and the water stands in the place where you were and shines.
