The first answer is everything, and that is no answer. Ask what lives on the low ground and the honest reply is a census with no bottom to it: the crabs, the frogs, the ants, the sleeved trunks of moths, the bowed branches of birds, the wrong-shaped figs full of bats, the millipedes in the drainage lines, the termites in the paper, the something large that lowers itself to drink. So start small and let the place sort it.

Start with the crabs.

They are pale. Not white—nothing that lives against wet limestone stays white—but a bluish, translucent gray, the color of the rock in the one hour after rain when it is still holding a skin of water. They are freshwater crabs, land crabs, though there is no land here in the sense they would have been built for and no fresh water except what falls through the stone and pools against clay. Their ancestors lived in the sea that made this shelf. The sea left. The crabs, in their way, did not. They went down into the crevices when the rock cracked and they have been going down ever since, following the water, so that by now they live in the plumbing of a dead ocean and come up at night to sit in the one place it still surfaces. Their eyes are on stalks and are mostly useless. What they have instead is legs, eight of them planted on stone that carries the thud from the works, and they feel it as a crab would, which is to say completely: each pulse arrives as a small stiffening of the whole body, a bracing, and then a release. They have been bracing every four or five seconds for their entire lives. When they knock shells at the pool's edge, hundreds of them, it is not signaling. It is just that they are packed close, and they shift after each thud, and the shifting clicks.

Above them on the ledges, the frogs. They are the size of a thumb-joint and there are thousands. They have the gloss that comes of never being dry. Somewhere in their past they had a call—the whole apparatus is still there, the sac under the throat, the muscles to pump it—but the nights here are too hot to spend air on, and generations of frogs that called died a little sooner than frogs that didn't, and now the ledges are silent. They find each other by contact instead. A frog moves along the wet stone until it meets another frog, and that is courtship: a shouldering, a settling, a shared breath. They sit so close that the ledge is not a surface with frogs on it but a surface of frogs, and the surface rises and falls, and the rising and falling is not quite in unison and not quite out of it. It is close to the thud. Not locked to it. Close. As if a very large, very slow breathing were trying to keep time with a machine and almost could.

The ants are harder to see as a resident, because a resident is a thing and the ants are a process. The column that runs up the buttress and down it, wrist-wide, both directions, has not broken in living memory, and it is worth being literal about that: there is no ant in the column that was alive when the column began. The column is the animal. The ants are its cells. It goes up the fig and into the crown and comes back carrying what the crown drops—scale insects, dead moths, fruit pulp, bat dung—and takes it down into the stone, into the same cracks the water uses, and somewhere below the floor of the forest there is a body of ants the size of a house that has never been seen and never will be. It has been eating the canopy and feeding the rock for longer than the trees standing on it have been trees. When a strangler finally kills its host and the host goes soft, the column simply moves onto the strangler and continues. It does not notice the change. Nothing here does. That is the point of the place.

The moths on the trunks are a different kind of resident: not a lineage but a coating. They are the same handful of species repeated ten thousand times, each one drawn to the same faint warmth, the same film of wet, so that from a hand's breadth away a trunk is not bark but a fur of folded wings, gray on gray, breathing very slightly as the wings lift and lower with the air. Brush it and it does not fly. It shifts, reseals, goes still. They have gone the way of the frogs: it costs too much to move, so they have stopped moving unless moved. A trunk sleeved in moths is a trunk that will, in an hour, be a trunk sleeved in moths a few inches to the left.

And then the birds, packed like fruit, and the fruit bats that make the figs look wrong. These are the place's weight. If you stood under one of the roost trees in daylight and someone told you what it carried you would not believe the branches could take it, and the branches don't quite—that permanent slight bow is a tree that has been carrying its tenants so long that the wood has grown to the load, laid down thicker on the underside, curved the way a beam curves and then made the curve its shape. When a colony stirs and the tree shivers, what comes down is not just the sweet-ammoniac smell. It is heat. A roost of two thousand bats is a furnace, and the furnace is one of the reasons the night does not cool.

All of which is true, and all of which is still a list. So go back down to the largest doline, to the mud trodden into paste, and wait for the thing that lowers itself to drink.

It is heavy. That is the first thing, before shape—it comes down the worn groove and the stone gives it back as a change in the thud, a second, irregular weight laid over the regular one, and the crabs shift to make room before it arrives. It is not tall. Nothing here is tall. It is long and low and wide across the back, built the way the trees are built, close to the ground and braced. Its skin is bare. In this heat hair would be a punishment, and whatever it once wore it has shed, so that what is left is a hide the color of the mud and the pale stone together, folded, seamed, pitted like the limestone it walks on, and holding water in the folds the way the rock holds water in its flutes. When it stands still it is very hard to say where it stops and the ground starts. It has small eyes and does not use them. It has never seen the source of the glow and does not know the glow is a glow. What it knows is the groove under its feet, worn hoof-deep by its own kind over more generations than the forest has stood, and the sound of the dripping changing as the canopy opens over the pool, and the thud.

It lowers itself. The knees go, then the chest, and the chest meets the mud and the mud takes the weight, and the exhale is the sound that was described earlier from a distance—the sound of something large letting go of its own mass. It drinks. It does not drink the way a horse or a deer drinks, in pulls; it puts its mouth into the water and stays there, and the water goes in, and the pool drops by less than the width of a hair, and rises again by the finger's width the leaves give it, and by morning the balance is even. The crabs come back around it while it lies there. The frogs on the near ledge do not move. Bats dimple the water a hand's length from its head. It is not that they are unafraid of it; fear is not a category that has purchase here. It is that the animal is a feature. It is part of what the pool is.

Its heart is slow. Slower than the thud—much slower, one beat to every three or four of the machine's—and this is where the place finally stops being a list, because if you lay against the stone beside it with your own sternum to the rock you would feel two pulses, one from kilometers off and one from an arm's length, and they would not be in time, and they would not be out of time, and after a while you would stop being able to tell which was the animal. It was born into this. Its mother lay in this mud with the pumps already thudding, and her mother, and the copper stain was in the haze above the canopy the night each of them first opened its useless eyes. The rhythm has been in the ground for the whole of the lineage's memory. Whether the animal's heart slowed to sit inside it, or was always this slow and the machine simply happened to fall between the beats, there is no way now to know. The two rates have matched. That is the settled argument the place is made of, and the animal at the pool is where you can put your hand on it.

So this is what lives here, if it has to be one thing: not the animal, exactly, and not the forest, but the arrangement. A body made of ten thousand pairs of lungs, none of which is calling; a coating of wings on every warm surface; a column that is older than its trees; a pale tenantry of the dead sea sitting in the one place it still leaks out; a slow heavy something lying in the mud with its mouth in the water; and over and under all of it, borrowed without asking from a smelter or a refinery or a row of flares beyond the ridge, a pulse. The forest never had a heartbeat. It has one now. It has taken it the way it takes a fallen fig, as a fact, slowly, and without comment, and by this point the taking is complete: there is not a living thing on the low ground that would recognize a night without it.

That is the resident's one weakness, and it is worth saying plainly because everything else here is so thoroughly settled. If the works ever stop—if the fires go out beyond the ridge and the haze thins and the reciprocating thing kilometers off reciprocates for the last time—the frogs will go on breathing and the crabs will go on shifting and the animal in the mud will go on drinking, but the stone under them will fall still for the first time in their memory, and into that stillness something will arrive that the forest has no word for and has never had to have one. Not noise. The opposite. A silence with no mass in it. And every lung on the low ground will feel it at once, and brace, and wait for the release, and the release will not come.

But the pumps have not stopped. The pool rises by the width of a finger. The animal lies in the wet with its slow heart. Four seconds, five, and the ground says what it has always said, and the crabs shift, and the forest breathes.
