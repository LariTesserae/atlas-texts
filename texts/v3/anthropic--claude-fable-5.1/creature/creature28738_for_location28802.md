**What lives there**

Start at the doors, because that is where the sorting happens.

Every split in the wall is a threshold, and the outside presses at it. Light gets in a body-length or two and stops. In that shallow band the plateau tries to follow: there is a rind of lichen on the wet lip of every large split, grey-green going to white as it reaches inward, and a few hands past the last of the lichen a moss that has given up on color entirely and grows as a pale felt over the tissue, thriving on the drip. Small flies breed in the felt. Something like a wren comes in after the flies, nests in the laminar layers of the torn edge, and never goes further than the light. Spiders web the seams. All of it lives where the outside can still reach it, and all of it, if you look, is doing badly on the inside edge and well on the outside edge, and the line between the two is not moving. The doors do not lead in. They are a place things stop.

What went in went in a long time ago, when there was less of the inside to get lost in, and stayed because it could not find the way out, and has been changing ever since.

---

Go down to the water first, because the water is where most of the living is.

The pools are black to the eye and thick to the hand. Put an arm in one and the cold is instant and total, and then, a moment later, the arm is touched — brushed, tasted — by many small things at once. These are the swarm the flyers come down for. Kneel and lift a double handful and let the water drain, and what is left in the palm is a loose moving mass the color of skimmed milk: tiny jointed things, no bigger than a grain, that were once the water-fleas of the plateau tarns and have lost their pigment and their eyes and grown longer in the leg. They eat what the pools are full of, which is the plant — the fine constant shedding of the soft trough tissue, a drift of grey specks that never stops falling into the water. In the wet hours, when the drizzle fills a trough that was half dry, the swarm blooms so thick the water goes cloudy, and the flyers know, and come.

Under the swarm, deeper in the black, there are fish. Not many kinds. The tarns had one — a small stiff-finned thing that lived under stones — and it came in on the water somehow, in the first floods when the plant lifted the basin, and there are still stones in it: it hugs the bottom, it will not rise, and it has lengthened and softened and gone the color of the inside of a shell. People net them in the channels where the water moves. They are almost transparent when fresh. You can see the last thing they ate.

And the channels themselves have something in them that is not a fish. In the smooth grown gutters where the water runs toward the low drains, a hand's depth, warm, always moving, there are long soft things pressed to the bottom that feel like a tongue when you step on them and are gone before you can look. Nobody has pulled one out whole. They may be the same animal as the fish, changed further, or they may be something that came in later, or they may live in the drains and only come up. The drains go down through the floor into wherever the plant keeps its water, and nothing that has gone down them has been seen again, and the long soft things are the only things that seem to come and go from them freely.

---

Then the floor, and what eats.

A grazer dies. Say the hunter left it, or it broke a leg in a channel and lay down. Within an hour there is a fur on it: a fine white pile that grows visibly, that you can watch thicken if you kneel with a light, that in a day has made the carcass look upholstered. It is a mold. It came in on the animals or on the people and it has found the one place in the world it was built for, and it is on everything — the old bark, the plugs in the tap-holes, the underside of every shelter. It does not touch living tissue. It goes for the dead and it goes fast.

After the mold, the walkers. Small flat things, many-legged, the pale grey of the floor, that come up out of the troughs where they live in the soft tissue and move across the carcass in a slow tide. They were beetles once, or the young of beetles. Now there is no adult stage worth the name; they are larval all their lives and they breed in the fur of the dead. After them, a thing like a slug, the length of a finger, blind, that eats the walkers. After the slug, nothing, because by then the floor has begun.

The floor grows over a dead thing the way skin grows over a splinter. First the edges of the corky surface soften and go translucent, and then they rise, and by the fourth or fifth day the body is in a shallow dish of grey and the grey is closing. A grazer takes about two weeks. A person, less. What is sealed in stays: two ridges down, under a hand of clouded tissue, you can find the swarm-eaten skeletons of things that died before anyone was here to see them, their bones going soft and brown in the sap. The plant does not seem to want them. It does not seem to want anything. It closes over them because closing is what it does, and it eats them, in whatever slow way it eats, because they are there.

---

The grazers.

The people call them by the plateau word for the lichen-cattle their ancestors were, though nothing about them but the word is still the same. Stand still when a herd passes close and you will feel them before you see them — the floor carries their weight as a shudder in the cork — and then in a grey blade of split-light, briefly: a tall narrow body, all ribs and hip, on legs too long for it; a neck that hangs; a head lower than the shoulders, swinging, with the ears set high and turned forward like two hands cupped to listen. The hair is thin and colorless and lies flat with the wet. The eyes are seams. If one turns toward you it is the ears that turn, not the face, and the nostrils, which are enormous and wet and take you in with a long draw.

They eat the trough tissue. Their lower jaws have gone forward into a kind of scoop and the front teeth have grown out and flattened into a blade, and they walk a trough with their heads down and shave it, a strip at a time, leaving the mottled floor cut to the paler layer beneath. The plant regrows it within a season. There is no reason to think this is an arrangement. It is simply that the softest tissue is the one they can eat, and the plant makes more.

They follow the rain. A herd moves under the vault as the weather does, a half-day behind it, arriving in a trough as the drizzle is ending and the swarm is blooming and the tissue is soaked and soft. They know it is coming the way anyone here does — the walls breathe harder before rain and the hum shifts up a little — and their coughing changes then, gets faster, and the herd turns. The cough is how they hold together. Each one calls and hears the others and knows the herd's shape from the answers, and in a full herd in the open the calls overlap into a texture like a slow engine, hundreds of voices at a distance of a hundred paces each, so that the herd is not a thing you see but a region of sound with edges. The calves call higher. A calf that stops calling is left; you can hear this happen. The herd does not slow.

They sleep against the wall. In the cold hours, where the tissue is porous and the warm air comes out of it, a herd will pack itself along a hundred paces of flank, standing, heads in, ears out, and steam, and you can walk up to the outside of the pack in the dark and put your hand on a hot bony haunch and it will not move. They have nothing that hunts them from the side. Everything that hunts them comes from the same darkness the herd came out of, and so they turn their ears outward and stand against the warmth and wait for it to be day, which it never is.

---

The flyers.

The roof of the Hollow, where anyone has reached it by climbing the wall at a split, is seamed the way the walls are, and in the seams the flyers roost — packed in by the thousand, hanging in the warm breath that rises there and collects. They are bats, or were. They have gone pale and the fur has gone soft and long and the wings have thinned until light shows through them, and the face, as the piece says, is mostly mouth and ears, because the mouth and ears are what they use. They come down when the walls exhale and the drizzle starts. They cross the floor at head height in loose clouds and take the swarm off the pools by skimming, and the clicking that goes with them — the dense dry pour of it — is the whole flock reading the water, and each other, and you.

Not all of them stay. There is a portion of the roost, nobody knows how large, that goes out. At the hour when the outside is dark they leave by the highest splits in a stream and hunt the plateau above, and come back at dawn to a roost that never leaves, and the two kinds hang together in the seams and are the same animal and are not, quite. The ones that go out are darker. The ones that stay have the eyes closed over. They are a few centuries apart in a single roost, and there is no line between them, only a gradient from the seam-edge inward, and that gradient is the whole history of the place written in one species.

They are the only thing that lives here and also anywhere else. When one falls it does not stay fallen long. The walkers come.

---

The hunter.

Here is everything anyone knows.

It takes grazers, mostly the old and the calves, and it takes them at night — or in what the herds and the people treat as night, the cold hours when the walls breathe least. It takes them when the wind veers, when the hum shifts pitch and the whole Hollow rings for a minute and the herd's coughing is drowned and the herd can hear nothing. Every kill that anyone has pieced together happened during a shift in the tone. Whether it waits for that or makes use of it when it comes, no one can say. But the people have stopped travelling when the note changes. They stand where they are, and hold the cord, and wait for the hum to settle, and count.

Its call is heard perhaps a dozen times in a season. It is low and long and it does not carry the way the coughing carries; it seems to come from very close and then not to have come from anywhere. Some say it comes up from the floor. Some say from the pools. One old tapper says it is not a call but a breath, the sound of something very large breathing out once, and that the herds hear it as a breath too, and that is why they scream: not because they know what it is, but because for a moment they think the wall itself has moved toward them.

Its kills are long. The floor around them is marked — not with prints, which the cork does not hold well, but with a kind of smear, a broad flattened track where the surface sheen has been rubbed off in a swathe two or three paces wide, as though something had dragged its whole length across the ground. The tracks go toward the walls and end at the porous flanks, where the warm air comes out, and there are places along those flanks — hollows, body-shaped, where the tissue has been pressed and warmed and pressed again — that the herds will not lie in.

It has never been found dead. Its young have never been found. Nothing that anyone has cut from the floor two ridges down has been anything but grazer or flyer or person.

It came in, presumably, the way everything did. Off the plateau, through a split, after the herds. The plateau had wolves and it had the big pale cats that live above the snow, and either of those, changed as much as the grazers have changed, might be this. But the people who have been here longest have stopped saying wolf or cat. They say it as a word of its own now, an old plateau word that means roughly *the one in the wall*, and they mean by it something that has been here longer than the herds, or came in with the herds and changed faster, or is not one thing but several — a wolf's line and a cat's line and something out of the pools all converging in the dark on the same shape, because the dark permits only so many shapes. Nobody who says this believes it, exactly. But nobody has a better account, and it fits what the herds do, which is to treat every direction as the one it comes from.

---

The people.

They are plateau people, herders' children, and they come in for the season with sap-jars and cord and a month's food, and most of them go out again before the cold, and a few do not. The ones who winter are called by a word that means *left behind*, though they were not. They stay because the winter is when the sap runs thickest, and because the herds are easiest then, packed against the walls, and because, some of them say, the sound is different in winter and they want to hear it.

Inside, they change in the ways you would expect and in some you would not. They stop looking. Within a week a newcomer has stopped turning the head toward things and started turning it a little away, so the ear leads; within a season the eyes are half-closed most of the time, because there is nothing to see and the dark is a strain. They speak low. Loud speech comes back at you from a wall a day away, changed, and it is unpleasant, and also it carries, and nobody wants to be heard by the herds or by what hunts them. Their hands go soft and white in the bark-wrappings and never dry, and the fingertips swell, and an old tapper's hands are the hands of someone who has been in the bath for thirty years. They smell of the sap, which is sweet and slightly rank. They eat grazer, netted fish, swarm-mash boiled in the one pot that is allowed a flame and only at a split with the door-light on it, and the sap, always the sap, which is most of their calories and rots the teeth of the ones who stay.

They know the Hollow as a shape of sound. Ask a winterer where you are and they will listen — actually listen, head aside, for a slow count — and tell you: the wall is that way, so far, because the drip is thick that way and thin behind; the pools are down there, the swarm is up, you can hear the fizz; a herd is coming from the east, a big one, three or four hours out. They name the pitches of the hum. There are six or seven names, plateau words for kinds of wind, and the winterers can tell you from the note alone where the outside weather is sitting on the range. They cannot see the range. They have not seen the sky in months. But they know when it snows up there, because the hum goes to the note they call *closed*.

They have made no gods here. The plant is called what it is called, an old word for a turnip that the first accounts used and that has stuck, and they speak of it the way they speak of weather — as a fact with moods. But they keep count. Every winterer keeps a post, a length of stalk driven into a trough near their shelter, notched at the floor-line each season, and the notches climb: a finger's width some years, a hand's in others, and in one bad year that everyone remembers, a forearm. They compare posts. They argue about whether it is speeding up. When a winterer dies inside, the others do not carry the body out — it is too far, and the cold hours are not safe for a slow burdened party — and they do not bury it, because there is nothing to bury it in. They lay it in a trough beside the post, and wrap it in its own bark, and leave it to the mold and the walkers and the floor, and they notch the post one last time at the level the floor has reached when the closing is done. The oldest posts have a notch like that near the base, and above it thirty years of ordinary marks, and the person under the base has long since gone to sap.

A few children have been born inside. Very few, and most were carried out as infants when the season turned. But two or three have grown up in the Hollow, and they are the thing the winterers watch most carefully, because they are the first of something. They turn their heads before a sound is made. They can find a shelter across a day of open floor without a cord. They speak to the herds — not in words, in a cough, from the chest, and the herds answer, and turn, and the winterers do not know what to make of this. One of them, a girl, will stand at a split in the door-light with her eyes screwed shut and her face wet and say the light hurts, and go back in.

---

That is what lives here. A door-rind of the outside that cannot get further. A pool-swarm and its fish and something long in the channels. A mold, a walker, a slug, and the floor, which closes. A cattle gone tall and blind that follows the rain and sleeps against the breath of the walls. A roost of flyers strung between two worlds along a single seam. A hunter that is only its effects. And a hundred people, and a few who have stopped being visitors, and three children who have never been anything else.

None of it is finished. The grazers are still recognizably the lichen-cattle; you could stand one beside its ancestor and see the family. The flyers have not yet split into the two things they are becoming. The people are three generations in. The mold, which is doing best of all, has been here perhaps a hundred years. Everything is a first draft, sharp-edged, unsettled, still the shape it came in with and only beginning to be the shape the dark wants.
