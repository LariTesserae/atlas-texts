You will want to say that nothing lives here, and you will be wrong in a way that takes a while to correct. What you mean is that nothing here moves at your speed. The basin is as populated as any place you have stood in. It is only that its residents are the kind you can spend a whole afternoon beside and never catch doing anything.

Start with the green, because it is most of what there is.

At first it seems like three kinds of thing that happened to arrive at the same rock: the ferns, the cushion plants, the silver shrub-trees. Spend a cycle among them and you begin to doubt the arithmetic. Nothing here flowers. Nothing has a scent except the stone. There is no wind to carry a seed and no insect to carry anything else, and yet the fracture that was a hairline yesterday has a seedling in it today, pale and folded, pointing up. Get down on the grit and put your eye to the seam and you see how. Along the floor of the crack runs something white and cord-thick — a root, or a stem, or a thing that hasn't chosen between them — following the fracture the way water would if there were water. Where it stops, it swells. Where it swells, the stone is wider. The green is not growing in the cracks. It is making them.

This is the first correction. You had thought the land was new and the plants had come to it. But the land has no weather — no rain to groove it, no wind to sand it, no frost you can detect, no stream — and it is being softened anyway, a fingertip at a time, from the inside. The green is the only weather this place has. Every edge you could cut a hand on, it will one day round. Every seam of undecided grit it will decide, by filling it. And it has already reached a density that ought to have taken a thousand years, on rock that is not a thousand years old, and so you have to conclude that the green is fast — that in stone's terms it is a hurrying thing, almost frantic — and that only your own speed makes it look asleep.

Whether it is one organism or ten thousand you can't tell, and after a while the question loses its edge. It behaves like one. It drinks once a cycle, all of it together, the whole basin bowing under the beads at the same minute and straightening at the same minute. It reflects away what it cannot use, which is most of the light. It leans toward the columns — not the way plants lean toward a window, since there is no direction to lean toward here, but the way a crowd leans toward a doorway. The oldest, tallest mounds are at the footings. It has grown up into the lowest fins and has not yet been discouraged from doing so, and you think it will get higher. It makes no sound and it does not need any. If it has a want, the want is simply: more seam.

The snail next, because the snail is what the green does not know about.

You saw one shell, white, large, wedged in a fern-crown, and you could not tell whether anything was in it. That uncertainty is not your failure. It is the snail's whole design. In the dip before what would be morning, when the temperature drops its single degree and the ferns begin to bead, the shell tilts. A plate of something dry and papery lifts from its mouth. A foot comes out, grey, the color of a wet fin, and finds the edge of a frond, and for the length of the beaded hour it eats — not much, a fingernail's width of one frond-edge, taking the water and the green together. Then the air warms and the foot withdraws and the plate seals and the shell is a shell again, indistinguishable from a dead one, for twenty-three hours or however long the cycle runs. It is alive for one hour in each. It has arranged to be dead the rest of the time, because the rest of the time there is nothing to drink and nothing soft enough to eat.

There may be one. There may be a hundred, sealed in fern-crowns across the basin, and you walked past them all. It is the only mouth in the basin that eats the green, and the green does not notice; the green loses more to its own reflected light than it loses to the snail. But it means the basin is not quite a place where only one thing lives. It is a place where one thing lives and one other thing lives off it, so slowly that the arrangement has never had to become a struggle.

Then the bird.

It sat on a fin and drank from the beads and did not move when you came, and did not move when you touched the metal a meter from its feet. You took that for tameness, or shock, or stupidity. It was none of those. You were simply not in its category of thing. Nothing in this basin moves toward it, so it has no reflex for something moving toward it, the way you have no reflex for the light dimming, because the light does not dim.

What does it eat? Not insects; there are none. You think of the white shell in the fern-crown, empty or full, and you think: perhaps that. A grey bird could carry a shell to a footing and drop it on the only smooth hard surface for miles, and there would be no sound, and it would wait. Or perhaps it eats nothing here. Perhaps it only drinks. It sat on the fin the whole hour, and the columns stay wet longer than anything else in the basin, and so the bird has done what no plant could do: it has found the place where the drinking hour is longest, and it goes there, and it stays until the last bead is gone. The green gathers at the columns' feet. The bird goes to the top. It is the only creature here that has chosen the machines outright, over the plants, and it is the color of them, and you don't know which came first — whether it was grey and found grey, or became grey by sitting on grey for a very long time.

You saw it once. It may be the same bird every time. It may be the only one. It looked at you with an eye like a drop of the same water — meaning it looked at you the way the basin does, without darkening, without deciding anything about you.

And now you have to face the columns, because you have been putting them off.

Do they live here? They drink. They drink longer and harder than anything else in the basin, and every other living thing has arranged itself around that fact. They stand on a footing the way a tree stands on a root-plate. They are cold, and they have a pulse — not a sound but a pressure on the ear, something enormous turning at a rate you cannot hear, the way the green grows at a rate you cannot see. They take from the wire at the top and give to the pipe at the bottom, and the wire comes from over the ridge and the pipe goes past the notch, so whatever they are, they are not the whole of themselves; they are the part of a larger thing that is in the basin. There are twenty of them, joined at the top by the wire and at the bottom by the converging pipes, and so perhaps they are one inhabitant with twenty stalks, the way the green may be one inhabitant with ten thousand fronds, and the snail one inhabitant with a hundred shells.

If life, in this place, is what drinks once a cycle and waits, then the columns are the most alive things here. The green has understood this and grown to their feet. The bird has understood it and gone to their fins. You are the only one in the basin who hesitates over the word, and you hesitate because the columns were made and the ferns were not — but the ferns were made too, by the slow white cord in the seam, and the seam was made by the lifting, and you don't know who did the lifting, and there is no one to ask.

So: what lives here.

One green, which is the weather. One snail, or a hundred, which is the mouth. One bird, which is the only thing that goes anywhere and goes only to the columns. One machine in twenty pieces, which drinks the most and sends it away. Every one of them lives by the same rule: take the water when it comes, and then do nothing that can be seen. None of them makes a sound, because a sound is an expenditure and there is nothing here to spend it on. None of them casts a shadow, because the light will not permit it, and so none of them has ever had to hide. They are the only inhabitants a place like this could keep. Anything that moved through would have starved between beadings; anything that hunted would have found its prey sealed; anything that needed to be heard would have been swallowed by the brightness a meter from its mouth. What is left is what stays.

You are the exception, and the basin treats you as one. You drink from a canteen at the wrong hour. You cast almost no shadow but you cast one, a smudge under your boots that the cushion plants do not know what to make of. You move at a speed that makes everything here look dead, and to everything here you are a brief disturbance in the light, like a bead forming and going. The bird did not fly because there was no reason to; you would be gone before it mattered.

When you cross the second ridge and turn, the last wet fin is glittering high on the farthest column, and if the bird is anywhere it is there, and under it the green has climbed a hand's breadth further up the footing than it had when you arrived, and somewhere in a fern-crown a white shell is either empty or waiting for the air to cool by one degree. That is the population of the basin. It is not small. It is only slow, and it is not waiting for you.
