---

The fish first, because the fish is the only thing here that has a front.

It is pale to the point of being a rumor of a fish, a forearm's length of something between white and no color, and it has no eyes, which in a room made entirely of light seems less like a loss than a decision. It hangs in the turning water at about the depth of your knee. It does not swim so much as correct. The pool rocks and it leans into the rock; the pool hurries toward the center and it lets itself be carried almost to the rim of the black and then, with one movement of the tail, is not there anymore, is a body-length back toward the edge, holding.

When the level drops and the drain sounds its low note, the fish goes still. It doesn't flee. It waits exactly where it is, as if it knows that the pull only lasts as long as the note, and the note is never long. When the level comes back — from nowhere, filling — it resumes correcting.

Cave fish live a long time. Slow metabolisms, cold water, nothing to eat and nothing to run from. This water is not cold and the fish is not slow, and you have no idea how old it is. Older than the fern, certainly, which is torn down and regrown every few weeks. Old enough that its whole life has been this pool and its whole life has been the size of one refusal, repeated: not the center. Everything else here is being thrown or is hanging on. The fish is doing a third thing. It is declining.

---

The midges, second. In the pause they are visible — a haze of dark points in the middle of the air, thousands, all drifting the same way, the way the water in the pool drifts, slower than the wind and in the same direction as the wind. They are not flying. When you watch one, it falls. All of them are falling, very gently, out of a sky that has stopped holding them up.

Then the wind catches and they are gone. Not blown to a wall — gone, distributed, become part of the white. You understand that in the roar they are everywhere in the room at once, one to every few hundred droplets, and that a room can be full of a thing and show none of it. There is a sound, in the seconds after the wind resumes, that you have been hearing as part of the roar and now hear separately: a thin, very high hiss, like wet silk. It is wings. It is thousands of wings taking hold of the air again at the same moment.

Where do they lay eggs? On what? The walls are scoured. The pool never sits still. The fern is three fronds. The most likely answer is the one that makes the least sense: they breed in the air. They live their whole lives without touching anything, and when the wind drops they fall, and if the wind stays down long enough they will reach the pool, and the fish will eat them, and you have never seen the wind stay down that long. The midges have made a bet on the storm's reliability. So far the storm is good for it.

So: three ways to live here. Hold — the green-black skin, the fern behind its lip, the orange crust ringing the scallops. Refuse — the fish. Ride — the midges. Each of them has found the one gesture that the wind permits and has become nothing but that gesture.

There is a fourth way, and it is not one the fish or the fern or the midges could take, because the fourth way is to be the wind.

---

You have been standing in it an hour, calling it weather.

Go back over the rules. The wind turns one way and does not reverse. Weather reverses; weather comes from somewhere and goes somewhere and turns around when the pressure changes. Hearts don't reverse. Guts don't. Things that circulate inside a body go one way because the body was built that way, and they keep going because stopping is the other word for dying.

The pattern is unstable. A machine is regular; that is what a machine is for. This stutters. It leans against one wall for a minute — "throws a fit," you said, and you were right to reach for a word that belongs to something with a temper. It gathers itself. It lets the mist fall and hangs there, grey and quiet, for a few seconds, and then resumes with a slap, the way a sleeper who has stopped breathing resumes with a snort. You have been calling those pauses the storm faltering. Call them breaths and see if anything stops fitting.

The heat has no source. The rock is not warm. The water is not warm. The air is warm and the air is at blood temperature, not a little above, not a little below, but exactly the temperature at which your skin stops being able to find the edge between itself and what surrounds it. That is not the temperature of a greenhouse or a hot spring or a machine. Only one class of thing in the world keeps itself at that temperature against a cold surround for years without a sun and without a fire, and it does it by being alive and by burning, slowly and everywhere, whatever it eats.

And the light. What glows is the water in the air. Nothing else. The walls are stone. The pool is dark. The droplets are lit from inside, flat and white, and the more of them there are and the harder they are being thrown the brighter the room. Things that are alive give off heat. Some give off light. This one gives off both, and gives them off from its blood, and its blood is the water it has torn from the walls, and it puts that water back on the walls again, which is why the walls weep and the pool refills from nowhere. The pool is not filled from an inlet. It is filled from the room. It is a mouth, and the drain is a throat, and the low note is swallowing.

---

Now the room itself reads differently. You called it a threaded bore and said something had been turning inside it for a very long time. Yes. But the room was not carved to hold the storm. The storm wore the room. It sat down in the dark in whatever cavity the water had left it and it turned, and it kept turning, and the limestone gave way to the shape of that turning the way a chair gives way over decades to the shape of whoever sits in it. The flutes and scallops are not architecture. They are the animal's outline. You are standing inside a shell, and the shell was made by wear, not by building, and it is exactly and only the size of the thing that wears it.

Look at the passage behind you. A black hole in white paper. The light stops there. Not fades — stops, at the lip, as sharply as light stops at the edge of a body. The animal does not go down the passage. It is not interested in the passage, or it cannot fit, or the passage is cold and it will not enter what is cold. Whichever: that edge is a skin. On this side of it, warmth and roar and light. On that side, rock.

The fish lives in it the way a fish would live in the belly of something large enough that the belly is the world. So do the midges. So does the fern. None of them has seen the storm. There is nothing to see. There is only the wind, which is its motion, and the warmth, which is its temperature, and the light, which is its blood in the air, and none of those is a thing you can stand outside of and look at. You can only stand inside it and be wet.

---

Then the railing.

You have your hand on it. It is dry. Everything the storm touches, it wets — it has wet the bolts into rust-stains a meter long, it has wet the treads into chutes, it has wet the writing off the plates until letters and wind-scallops are the same. It has had the same long time with the railing that it has had with everything else, and it has not touched it. A single grey rod, perfect, and along the whole of its length a hand's width of still, dry air.

The warmth in it is not the room's warmth. It is smaller and closer. Like a hand that was holding it a moment ago, you thought, and you should keep that, because it is the most exact thing you have thought.

There are two ways to take this and you don't have to choose yet.

One: the people who cut the stair came up it to stand where you are standing. They held the rail. They came often enough, over long enough, that the storm — which had already learned where the flutes should go and how deep the pool should be and how long to hold its breath — learned where the hands go. And so it goes around the rail. It leaves a channel of quiet along that one rod the way you would leave a chair pulled out at a table. The warmth is the warmth of something kept ready.

Two: the storm has not gotten to the rail yet. Everything else it has worn to its own shape, and this it is still considering. The warmth is the warmth of attention — the feeling on the back of your neck of being looked at, except that it is in your palm, and what is looking has no eyes and no front and is the entire room.

The door on the stair has filled with white stone. The room behind it closed slowly, you said, the way a tree grows around a nail. A tree grows around a nail because the nail is in it. Something was in that room. The deposit is what the storm leaves when it decides to keep a thing where it is. You do not need to know what it kept.

---

So. What lives here.

Something that has no name because nobody has been in a position to name it — you cannot name a thing you can only be inside of. It is roughly the size of a small cathedral and exactly the size of the room, because the room is the shape it has worn into the dark by being itself for a very long time. It is warm-blooded and its blood is water and its blood shines. It breathes irregularly. It turns one way. It drinks from the pool at its own foot and gives back what it drinks. It does not hunt, because there is nothing here to hunt; it does not rest, because resting is the thing it cannot do. It eats something you can't see — the difference between wet and dry, between up and down, between the warm inside and the cold rock, whatever gradient a thing this size can lean on and take a little from.
