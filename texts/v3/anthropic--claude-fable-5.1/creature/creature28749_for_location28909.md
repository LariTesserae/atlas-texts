**The Ones Who Live on the Shelf**

Begin with what you can see, because there is so little of it.

---

The grazers first. You will want a name for them and the shelf does not offer one, so call them the followers, since that is the whole of what they do.

Up close — and you will only ever see one up close by accident, by having stood still long enough that it forgets you are a separate thing from the moss — a follower is less an animal than a piece of terrain that has decided to move. The felt on its back is not fur in the way you know fur. It is a mat of fine black filaments, each one hollow, each one full of the animal's own stale breath, so that the coat is a blanket of warmed air held in a thousand thousand tubes. Frost forms on the outside of it and does not get through. Under the felt the skin is thick and pale and almost hairless, and it is hot to the touch in the way that a stone left in a fire is hot, dry and steady and slow to give. You will not touch it. But if you did, that is what you would feel.

The feet are the strange part. They are broad, as you'd expect, splayed to spread the animal's weight over the mat — but the underside of each foot is bare, and soft, and threaded with vessels that lie close under the surface. The follower does not walk on its feet so much as listen with them. The pressure in the deep stone rises long before the slab lifts, and the rise carries with it a change in the temperature of the rock a fathom down, a shift of a fraction of a degree that travels up through the moss and into the pads of the feet. The herd feels the slab beginning to warm before there is any warmth to feel. That is how they are walking before the *tock*. It is not foresight. It is a kind of hearing done with the soles.

They are not clever. There is nothing on the shelf that requires cleverness. What they have instead is patience of a depth that has no analogue in warm places, where things happen. A follower will stand chest-deep in the mat for a whole dark day, chewing, with its head down and its red eyes half-lidded, and in that time it will have moved perhaps the length of its own body, and it will have done exactly what it needed to do. The herd is not a herd in the sense of a crowd. It is a string, a line of beasts spaced along a seam so that each one is just at the edge of the next one's sight, and the line can be a mile long, and the only thing holding it together is that every animal on it is following the same slow change in the ground. When the warmth turns — when the shuttle of pressure crosses from one slab to the next and takes an angle — the head of the line turns with it and the rest follow, one by one, a bend traveling down the string over hours like a wave going down a rope.

The young are born onto warm moss, in the shallows, where the mat is knee-deep and the frost is sublimating off it in curls. They are small and dark and wet and they steam. Their eyes are already lit, though dimly — a red so faint it might be the memory of a red — and their first act is to put their feet down and feel for the slab. If it is lifting they stay. If it has begun to settle the mother is already moving and the young thing follows without ever having been taught to, because the ground beneath it has gone quiet and the ground ahead has not.

The old ones fall behind. That is all that happens. The string moves at the pace of the warmth and the warmth does not slow for anyone, and an old follower with cold in its joints simply arrives at each seam a little later than the one ahead of it, and then later still, until one cycle it is standing on a slab that has settled while the herd is a mile off on one that has begun to lift. It does not seem to distress them. The old one stops chewing. It stands with its feet in the cooling mat and its eyes go from red to something darker, and the frost comes down on it and does not melt, and in a day or two it is a shape in the moss, and in ten it is a ridge. The dead do not fall. They are too broad-footed and too stable. They freeze standing, and the moss grows up around their legs, and when the warmth comes round again forty days later the seam beneath them opens and they go down.

---

Inside the moss there are the twitchers.

You will never see one whole. The mat is too dense and they are too quick, and they have no reason to come up. What you see is the surface of the black plates shivering in a line, as if something the size of a forearm had run along beneath them, and then stillness. Sometimes two lines cross. Sometimes a patch the size of a table quivers all at once and goes still and you understand that something down there has caught something else.

If you dig — and the moss resists, closing over your hands like a fist — you will find that the mat is not solid. It is a lattice. The leathery plates are only the outermost layer, and beneath them the stems are thick and hollow and branch and rejoin in a labyrinth that goes all the way down to the stone, and the labyrinth is warm, warmer than the surface, because it holds the slab's heat the way the followers' felt holds their breath. This is where the twitchers live. They are long, and jointed, and blind in the true sense — no eyes, not even the vestige of eyes — and they are white, the white of things that have never needed to be any color. They eat the moss from the inside. They eat each other. They lay their eggs in the stems and the eggs hatch into smaller versions of themselves and those eat their way outward, and the moss, as far as anyone can tell, is not harmed by any of it. It may be that the tunneling is how the mat breathes. It may be that the moss grew the twitchers the way it grew everything else here, to keep its own inside open.

They do not follow the warmth. They cannot; they are too slow over distance and too small to keep heat in their bodies on the cold slabs. Instead they wait. When a slab settles and the mat above it goes cold the twitchers in that mat go still, and stay still, curled in the hollow stems, for the thirty-odd days it takes the shuttle to come back around. They are not dead in that time. They are not quite alive. When the warmth returns they unfold and go back to eating as if no time had passed, and for them, perhaps, none has.

---

The things in the fissures.

Every slab has seams at its edges, and every seam has places where it opens wider than a hand, and those are the fissures — the black throats the description mentions, rounded smooth by ages of warm breath. When a slab lifts the fissures around it exhale. The air that comes up is wet and warm and smells of deep stone, and it rises in a column that you can feel on your face from ten strides off, and in that column, if you are close and if you are looking, there will be hangers.

They come up with the air. You do not see them emerge; they are simply there, at the mouth of the fissure and then above it, long and pale and slender as ribbons, hanging in the updraft with their limbs spread. They have four limbs, or six, or the number changes depending on how you count, and each limb is fringed with something that is not quite fin and not quite feather and that ripples in the warm column and holds them aloft. They do not seem to have heads. They have a thickening at one end where the limbs join and a long trailing tail at the other that hangs straight down into the fissure and does not come out. It is possible the tail is an anchor. It is possible they are not separate creatures at all but the reaching arms of something that lives in the seams below, and that what you are seeing spread in the warm air is a single animal the size of a slab putting its hands up into the cold to feel what is there.

They eat the frost-fall. That much is clear. When the followers breathe and the glitter of frozen breath comes down, the hangers are beneath it, fringes spread, and the glitter settles on them and vanishes. When the twitchers' eggs are washed out of the mat by meltwater and carried down the seams, the hangers are there. They are not predators in any way that involves pursuit. They are filters. The shelf pushes warmth and water up out of the stone, and everything living on the shelf casts something off into that upflow, and the hangers hold themselves open in it and take what comes.

When the slab settles they go down. Slowly, deliberately, folding their limbs, sinking into the black throat of the fissure feet-first — if the tail is the feet — until the last pale ripple of fringe disappears and the mouth of the seam is only a hole in the moss with cold air standing in it. Where they go is not known. The fissures are deep. Whatever is at the bottom of them is warm, always, and has never been seen.

---

The gliders.

You will have decided they are birds, because they are pale and they pass overhead, and you will be wrong in almost every particular except the passing.

A glider is a membrane. That is the whole of it. There is a body, if you want to call it that, a dark knot the size of two fists hung in the center of a sheet of something translucent and stiff and veined, and the sheet is as wide as you are tall and rimed always with frost, so that when it catches the horizon's bruise-light it looks like a pane of ice with a stone at its heart. It has no wings because it is a wing. It has no eyes because it does not need to see; it needs to feel the warm air rising and it does that with every square inch of itself, and it can find a lifting slab from the far side of the plain by the shape of the updraft alone.

They ride the columns. A glider will sit at the mouth of a fissure, folded, for the whole cold phase — days, weeks — motionless as a fallen leaf, and when the slab begins to lift and the fissure begins to breathe, the sheet unfolds and rises straight up into the warm air, turning slowly, and hangs there a hundred feet over the moss, and waits. It is waiting for the next column. When the pressure crosses to the adjoining slab and *that* slab's fissures begin to breathe, the glider tips and slides down the cold air between, losing height the whole way, and arrives at the new column with just enough left to be caught by it and lifted again. It follows the warmth the way the herds do, but from above, and it never touches the ground except to wait.

What it eats is anything the updraft carries that the hangers do not catch first. Frost-glitter. Spores from the moss. The smaller twitchers, when the mat is disturbed. And there is a thing they do to the followers that is not quite predation: a glider will settle onto the back of a grazing beast, in the dark, spreading itself over the warm felt like a blanket, and rest there for an hour, drawing heat up out of the animal into its own cold membrane, and then lift away. The follower does not seem to notice. It has heat to spare. Everything on the shelf that lives outside the moss is either a thing that holds warmth or a thing that borrows it, and the gliders are borrowers, and they have never had to learn to ask.

---

And the hunters.

The description says *presumably*, and the caution is right. You will not see them. In the entire time you are on the shelf you will not see a kill, or the aftermath of a kill, or anything that looks like a creature built for killing. And yet the followers have the alertness of prey, and the string of a herd is never allowed to break, and when a young one wanders more than a few body-lengths from the seam the nearest adult goes to fetch it with something that is almost haste.

So think about where you would be, if you were the thing they feared. Not in the moss; the twitchers own it and it is too dense for anything large. Not in the air; the gliders are membranes and could not close a jaw if they had one. Not in the fissures; the hangers are there and whatever the hangers are, they eat by holding still.

That leaves the cold slabs.

A follower will not walk on a settled slab if it can avoid it. The string bends around them, hugging the seams where the warmth is. And when one of the old ones falls behind and stops on a slab that has gone dark, and freezes standing in the mat, it lies there — the description says — until the warmth comes round and the seam opens and it goes down. But that is forty days. And a body the size of an ox, frozen solid, does not go down a seam a hand's breadth wide unless something has taken it apart first.

Nothing has been seen doing this. But the cold slabs are very large and very dark, and the frost comes down thick, and a thing that could live on a settled slab — a thing whose warmth came not from the ground but from what it ate, and that could go a full cycle between meals — would have every reason to be the color of frost and to lie still under it. The followers do not fear the dark. They live in it. What they fear is the *quiet* — the places where the ground has stopped humming, where the soles of the feet feel nothing, where a slab has gone silent and something in the silence is waiting for the warmth to bring the string of them past.

You feel it too, standing at the edge of a settled slab. Not a presence exactly. An absence with weight. The moss there is stiff and the frost on it is undisturbed and there is no reason to think anything is there at all, and you find you have stepped back onto the warm side of the seam without deciding to.

---

There is one more.

You will be a long time on the shelf before it occurs to you, and when it does it will come not as a thought but as a change in how the place feels under your knees.

The moss is not a bed for the others to live on. It is the thing that lives here. The followers are how it moves heat from slab to slab on the down-cycle, fur and hoof and glowing breath. The twitchers are how it breathes. The hangers are how it reaches up into the cold and tastes what falls. The gliders are how it carries its spores across the seams it cannot cross itself, riding the very breath it exhales. And the hunters — if there are hunters — are how it clears the cold slabs of what has stopped and returns it, in pieces small enough for the seams, to the warm dark where the roots are. The dead go down. The warm air that comes up smells, for a little while, of something that is not stone. That is not an accident and it is not a horror. It is digestion.

Nothing on the shelf has a name, or a language, or a use for either. Nothing shouts. But if you lie on the mat long enough, with your face against the leathery plates and the frost gathering on the back of your neck, you can feel the hum come up through the bones of your cheek, and you can feel that the hum is not quite even. It has a texture. It rises in one place and falls in another and there is a pattern in the rise and fall that repeats every forty days almost exactly, and the moss is thicker along the route the pattern takes and thinner where it takes longest to return, and it is *tending* that route. Not with intention. There is no one here to intend. But in the way a heart tends its own rhythm, in the way a body keeps itself at a temperature without knowing that it does — the shelf is inhabited, singly and entirely, by something that grew a whole fauna as a way of holding still.

It has been doing this for longer than there have been followers. The followers are recent. The gliders are recent. The hunters on the cold slabs, if they exist, are the newest thing of all — a solution the moss arrived at when the old ones began to freeze standing and the seams were too narrow for what fell.

It will go on. That is the last thing to understand about who lives here. The warmth comes round. The slabs lift and settle. The string bends and the membranes rise and the pale limbs open in the breath from the deep and close again, and every one of them is a limb of the same slow dark thing, and the thing is not waiting for anything, and it is not going anywhere, and it does not know you are here.

*Tock.*

The frost comes down on all of it alike.
