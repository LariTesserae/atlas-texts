Start with what doesn't live here, because the line is so clean.

The city has its followers — the animals that come after people into every seam and cellar — and on the top dozen steps you find their evidence in the usual way: a cockroach shell on its back, the papery husk of a moth under the last surface lamp, a grey smear of guano on the handrail. Then the heat comes up to meet you and the evidence stops. Not thins. Stops. Below a certain step there is no rat-smell, no insect, no pigeon feather blown down on the draught, as though the warmth or the roar or the presence of the ribs were a shoreline the ordinary opportunists of the underground will not cross. Whatever lives in the chamber did not come down from the city. It came from the other direction, or it was here already, or it has been here so long that the question of arrival has stopped meaning anything.

And the warmth — the warmth of a room many people have just left — is not the warmth of people. That's the first thing to say about the inhabitants. There is no smell of anyone. There is no trace of the crowd the temperature implies. Whatever is warm here is warm on its own account, and what lives alongside it has arranged itself around that warmth the way a village arranges itself around a well.

You have to lower your expectations of speed and scale. Nothing here is quick, and very little is large, and most of it you will mistake for mineral before you know it for alive.

The crusts are alive, to begin with. Not the white, which is only what the water leaves behind, but the ochre fringes around the drains are a slow community of something that eats dissolved iron and excretes the colour, laying itself down in rings that record the pool's high-water marks going back years. The violet is something else, something that grows on top of the ochre in a thin gloss like the skin on cooling milk, and when you touch it it is slick and comes away and re-forms behind your finger over the course of an hour. In the drip-cups — the hollows worn into the floor by centuries of the same falling drop — there are grazers. Translucent, the size of a grain of rice, with a single muscular foot and no shell, they move across the violet film at a rate you can only confirm by looking away and looking back. When the cups overflow they ride the overflow into the channels and are carried, apparently without objection, to the next cup down, and so make their way across the room over lifetimes, in the direction the water goes, which is the only direction they know.

The moss is next, and the moss is stranger than you thought.

It isn't one thing. The dark felted pads at the rib-feet, when you look with your face almost on the floor, resolve into a dense weave of at least three: something filamentous and rust-coloured that does the structural work; something in the filaments' shade that is almost black and beaded, like fish roe; and, on the surface, a grey fur so fine it registers as texture rather than growth. They live together and you can't tell which is host. What they do — what you can see them doing, if you wait through a rain-hour — is breathe out. The pads are dry because they refuse water; when the general patter comes down from the ceiling and everything else in the room beads and streams, the pads darken for a while and then, visibly, steam. A thin exhalation lifts off them into the warm air and carries with it the sweetish vegetal smell you couldn't place on the stairs. That smell is theirs. It is the smell of the moss giving back what it doesn't want, and it rises, because everything warm here rises, up the cool face of the rib into the dark.

Their placement — that cultivated regularity, always at the foot of a rib, always on the pool-facing side — turns out to have a simple author, but you won't understand that until later.

The pale shoots at the far end of the long pool are one plant. You work this out by following the cracks. The floor is a mosaic of abandoned surfaces, each poured over the last, and every crack in the topmost layer at that end of the room has something threaded along it: a pale root, thinner than a shoelace, running for yards, branching where the cracks branch. The shoots are where the root has found daylight, so to speak — where the crack is wide enough to let it stand. There are perhaps forty of them above ground and the network beneath must be the size of a tennis court, all of it lying in the fissures of the old floor, all of it within a hand's breadth of whatever is under that floor making the pulse. The flicker at the tips comes with the pulse, but you stay long enough to begin to doubt the order of events. It may be a fraction before. It may be that the plant is not lit by the beat but announces it, the way a dog gets up before the car turns into the street. Their tips, when you look closely, have a dew on them that the rest of the room doesn't — a clear bead, one per shoot, that reforms after it is taken.

Because it is taken. That is what the animal comes for.

You saw it once: low, long, unhurried, crossing the far shore. It comes to the shoots at intervals you can't yet find a pattern in, and it stands among them with its face down, and each time it goes away the beads are gone from the tips it stood beside. You get closer to it, over time, because it does not seem to mind. It has no eyes you can find. What it has instead is a face made almost entirely of whiskers — not a moustache but a whole muzzle of them, long, pale, held slightly forward, trembling continuously in the moving air — and a skin like wet slate, hairless, with a bloom on it in the grey-green light. Its feet are broad and soft and splay when they take weight, and its gait is the flat side-to-side gait of an animal that has never needed to run and has spent its whole life on a surface that might not hold. It weighs, you'd guess, about what a cat weighs, and is longer, and moves its head very little.

It navigates by the room. That's the thing you come to understand watching it. After every pulse it stops — wherever it is, mid-stride — and stays still for a second or two, whiskers forward, and then goes on. It is listening to the echo of the pulse come back off the walls and the pools and the ribs and reading the shape of the chamber from it, the way you might glance up to check where you are. The room is an instrument and the animal has learned to hear its own position in the chord. Its routes are worn into the floor: paths of slightly polished mosaic between the pools, passing each rib close on the pool-facing side, at a distance from the rib exactly equal to the width of its own body.

And there is the moss explained. The pads grow where the animal brushes. It carries the rust filaments on its flanks, has done for as long as there have been animals like it walking these routes, and every rib-foot it rubs past on its way to the shoots receives a little of what it carries, on the side it passes, at the height of its shoulder. The regularity is its regularity. The garden is a byproduct of its habit.

There are, you think, three of them. You have seen one. You have heard two, moving at different distances during the same pulse-interval. The room could feed more but it doesn't seem to need to. They are old — the one you know has a pale seam of scar down one flank where the skin healed shiny, and a deliberateness in the way it lowers itself into the drain-cups to sleep that suggests joints that have been used for a long time. It sleeps in the drains. The crusted mouths of the floor-drains are the warmest places in the room, warmer than the moss even, and just wide enough to hold an animal curled, and you find the one you know there in what you've decided to call the morning, its whiskers folded back, breathing slow, the eleven-second beat going through the crust beneath it and, you suspect, through it.

Its diet is what is slow enough to be caught by something slow. The dew from the shoots. The rice-grain grazers, licked from the cups in passing — you watch it do this, an unhurried lap along the rim of a hollow, and count the grazers before and after. And the things in the pools.

The pools have their own life and it keeps the water's hours. During the rain-hour, when the drips thicken into a patter and the channels run and the pools rise, something comes up out of the drains to meet the new water: pale, soft-bodied, the length of a finger-joint, with a fan of antennae longer than the body that it holds spread and motionless in the current like a hand held out to feel for rain. They don't swim so much as position themselves. Twenty or thirty to a pool at the height of the flood, all facing upstream toward whichever channel is feeding it, taking what comes. When the ribs draw and the pulse comes and the water begins to go back down, they go back down with it, into the drains, out of sight, and you don't see them again until the next rain.

The larger thing in the water you never see at all. You hear the plash — once, twice more over the days you're there — always from the parts of the room you'd misjudged as dry floor, where the pools are deeper than they look and the grey-green shows you a surface and not what's under it. Something goes into that water and stays under long enough that you stop waiting. It may be a fourth animal, one that has taken the pools instead of the paths. It may be something the animals are related to and would not recognise. You crouch at the edge once and hold your hand over the surface and it is warmer than the air, and you don't put your hand in.

The fliers live up where the metal-sliding sound seemed to come from, and they are the only fast things in the room, and their speed is borrowed from the heat.

The chamber breathes upward. Warm floor, cool ribs; the air rises through the middle of the room and sinks down the faces of the ribs, a slow convection you can find with a wet finger, and the fliers ride it. They're smaller than you thought from the flap — the size of your two hands together — with a leathery, greyish wing that catches the grey-green along its leading edge, and bodies that are warm to a degree you can feel when one passes near your face on a downdraught. They roost on the high walkway brackets and the lip of the housing, on the undersides, among the stalactites, where the cool descending air keeps them dry. They eat the air. Whatever rises from the moss-pads in that sweet exhalation — spores, or the fine grey fur shed on the updraught, or the small things that live in the fur and are carried up with it — the fliers turn and turn through the warm central column with their mouths open and take it.

Their cries are the room's. That short falling note you heard is exactly as long as the interval before the first echo returns off the far wall, so that the cry and its answer never overlap, and they call — you check this over many pulses — only in the second or two after each beat, when the echo of the pulse has died and the room is, for a moment, at its clearest. They have cut their voices to the chamber's dimensions. Or the chamber, over long enough, has accepted only the voices that fit. There are maybe a dozen. They are the only inhabitants that ever seem to hurry, and even they hurry in circles.
