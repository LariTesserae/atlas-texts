Start with the ones you cannot see, because they are the ones who made the place worth seeing.

The builders are the color of things that have never needed color. Pale — not white, exactly, but the translucent no-color of a fingernail, so that in the rare instance when one is carried up into the light you can see the dark thread of what it last ate running through it. They are soft. That is the first surprise. A creature that has laid down a hundred miles of stone-hard gallery is itself as yielding as a seed that has just split. Their heads are a little too large for their bodies and their bodies are a little too short for their legs and their eyes, where they have eyes at all, are two dim smudges that register only the difference between dark and less dark, which is a distinction they almost never need to make. They live by touch and by taste. Their antennae are always moving, and what they are doing with them is reading — reading the wall, reading each other, reading the thin scent-lines laid down along the gallery floors by everyone who has passed before, so that a forager coming home in total darkness is walking on a road made of the smell of her sisters' feet.

They are not one animal. They are several animals wearing one name. The most numerous are the workers, and they are the ones described above — small, soft, sexless, tireless in the way that a heartbeat is tireless, which is to say without the concept of rest. They chew the rock. That alone should stop you: they take the basalt, which nothing else here can touch, and they work its surface with jaws that are tipped in something harder than anything a soft body should be able to grow, and they carry the powder away a mouthful at a time to be mixed with the pulp of dead succulent and the glue of their own bodies and laid, still damp, on a rising wall. Then they go back. They are the reason the rock has cracks with white in them — not entirely, but partly. They have been mining the salt seams since before there was a forest to shade them.

Above the workers, if above is the word, are the soldiers, who are built differently and look it. Their heads are enormous, armored, a dark honey-brown that is the only real pigment the species allows itself, and they are mostly jaw. Some cannot close their mouths enough to feed themselves and are fed, mouth to mouth, by the workers they defend. Some have no jaws at all in the usual sense but a long hard snout through which they can fire a bead of sticky, bitter resin at whatever has broken the wall. When a gallery is breached — by a lizard's claw, by a falling arm of green, by a bird working at a vent — the soldiers come first, and they do not come to fight so much as to fill the gap with their own bodies, heads outward, and hold it while the workers behind them seal the hole. Often they are sealed out. This is not a tragedy to anyone involved. A soldier is a wall that can walk to where a wall is needed.

And there is the one at the center, though *center* is a courtesy, since the city has no center. Deep in the base of each great tower, where the temperature never changes by more than a degree, there is a chamber that was built around a body and has been rebuilt, larger, as the body grew. She is immense by the standards of her children — the length of a hand, perhaps, a swollen, gleaming, segmented thing whose legs have long since become vestigial ornaments at the front of a body that is otherwise an egg-producing organ with a head attached. She cannot move. She has not moved in years. She is groomed, fed, cooled by a constant attendance of workers who fan her with their wings-that-are-not-wings and carry her eggs away as they emerge, one every few seconds, day and night, for the length of a life that may be longer than the lizards', longer than the birds', longer than some of the columns that stand above her. Beside her, always, is a male, much smaller, whose only occupation is to be near her. Neither of them has ever seen the light or the green or the sky. Neither of them knows there is a forest. They are the reason there is a forest.

Because the plants, too, live here, and *live* is the right word, and they live in an arrangement with the builders that neither party agreed to and that neither could now survive without.

The columns are older than the towers that climb them. Some of them are older than the oldest tower by a long way — they are the first colonists, the ones whose ancestors' seeds arrived on the cooled rock in the gut of some passing bird before anything else had dared, and sprouted in the crack, and put down a root that found no soil and so simply spread, flat, and held on. They are not fast. A column the thickness of a body has been standing for two hundred years, and its rings, if you cut it, would not be rings but a fine waxy gradient of slightly-more and slightly-less, the record of a life spent counting mornings by dew. They breathe at night. In the heat of the day they close every pore they have and stand shut like a held breath; when the dark comes and the air cools and grows a little heavier, they open, and drink what there is to drink out of the air itself, and swell — you can hear it, that creak — and this is when the builders' collars of earth do their most important work, because the collar holds the night's coolness against the column's skin until noon, and the column sheds into the collar's darkness, and the shed skin feeds the city, and the city's exhalation, warm and damp from the bodies of a million workers, rises up inside the collar and gives the column a second dew. They are each other's weather.

The rosettes are different. The rosettes are ambitious. They arrived later and they arrived intending to take, and they have taken — you can see where a rosette has settled against the base of a column and begun, over decades, to lean on it, to shade its lower skin, to send those flat gray root-hands crawling up onto the column's flanks and drink from the same night the column is drinking. Some columns have died this way, slowly, and the rosette that killed one sits now in the middle of a ring of standing dead wood with the air of something that has finished a meal. The builders do not take sides. They quarry the dead column. They build a collar on the rosette. The forest is a slow war, and the city is the one that always wins, because it is the one that always eats what falls.

The vines belong to no one. They are the forest's only real travelers — the coin-leaved loops that go from column to column are a single plant, sometimes, running for the length of a street, rooted at one end in a crack that has been rooted in for longer than the plant has existed and drawing from every column it touches a little, a very little, through fine pale filaments that have gone into the host's skin so gently that the host has not noticed. When a column dies the vine lets go and reaches for the next. When a fire comes the vine burns first and fastest, and its ash is the first thing the builders carry.

Now the tenants.

The lizards first, because they are the most honest about what they are doing. They are long, flat, gray-tan — the exact color of cured wall, and they got that color by living against cured wall for a thousand generations, so that a lizard on a gallery is a bump in the gallery until it isn't. They do not hunt. They wait. A lizard will lie across the mouth of a vent for a whole day with its jaw resting on the lip and its eyes half-shut, and when a forager finally surfaces — because foragers must surface, to reach the dropped arms and the litter — the lizard's head moves once and the forager is gone. The lizard's eyes do not open any further. It swallows. It waits. In this way a lizard eats perhaps forty workers in a day, and the city, which produces forty thousand, has never noticed a lizard exists. Their young hatch in the abandoned upper chambers, twenty at a time, no larger than a fingernail, already the color of wall, and most of them are eaten by the birds within the week.

The birds are less honest. They are small and dun and they move through the vents as if the vents had been made for them, and in a sense they have been — a tower's upper chambers, once the builders abandon them and let them dry, are precisely the size and shape and darkness a small bird's egg wants, and there are thousands of such chambers in any given street. The birds do not sing. They have no need to; they are never far enough apart to call to. They eat soldiers, mostly, which they take at the wall-breaches the lizards make, hammering through the honey-brown heads with short thick bills and leaving the jaws behind, so that the ground beneath a bird's favorite tower is scattered with tiny dark pincers like the shed teeth of something much larger. They also eat the lizards' young. They also, once a year, in the long season, eat the flowers of the columns — the columns flower once and briefly, at night, opening huge waxy white cups at their very summits for a single dark stretch of hours, and the birds are there when the cups open and drink what is offered and carry away on their dun breasts a yellow dust that they leave, without knowing it, on the next column, and this is how there come to be new columns, still, on rock that has never had rain.

The beetles are their own order of life and would prefer not to be discussed. They are black, and heavy, and slow, and armored to a degree that seems excessive until you understand what eats them, which is nothing. They walk the buttresses in processions because the buttresses are the only surface in the forest that is warm enough at dawn and cool enough at noon, and they eat the dried spit that the builders leave on the outsides of new walls, licking the city as it grows. Their larvae live inside the dead columns and take a decade to become anything. They are the forest's memory, if it has one: a beetle in a procession may be forty years old and may have walked the same buttress ten thousand times, and if the buttress is quarried and rebuilt elsewhere the beetle walks to the new place without hesitation, as if it had been told.

The thing with the long ears is a grazer and the only one. It is the size of a small dog and shaped like a rabbit that has given up on speed — heavy-bodied, short-legged, with hind feet wide as a hand and soled in horn to take the basalt without cutting. Its ears are long because the ears are how it cools; they are veined like leaves and it holds them up into any moving air. It eats the rosettes. It eats them from the outside in, one fat leaf at a time, spending a week on a single leaf, and the rosette regrows the leaf, and the grazer moves to the next, and by the time it has gone around the rosette the first leaf is back. It never drinks. Everything it needs is in the leaf. It lives its whole life within sight of the rosette it was born under, and when it dies — usually of the hawk, sometimes of the thing in the crack — the rosette's roots find the body within a day.

The thing in the crack is the reason the grazer stays close to the rosette. It is long, longer than seems possible, thin as a rope and the dull slate of shadowed basalt, and it lives in the cracks the way water would live in them if there were water. It does not come out to hunt. It waits in the seam beneath the litter for a footfall to pass over it, and then it is up and around the leg and pulling down, and the crack is deep enough, in most places, that whatever it pulls does not come back. It is cold-bodied and slow-metabolized and eats perhaps six times a year. It has no venom and no need of one. It is the only thing in the forest that can go under the city — it travels the seams beneath the galleries for miles, and the builders, who fill every hole they find, have never managed to fill the ones it uses, because it is always in them.

The hawk is not a resident. The hawk is a visitor who has stopped visiting anywhere else. It sits on the highest chimney because the chimney is the only place from which the forest can be seen as a forest, and it sits there for hours, and what it sees is the moment when a grazer's ears go up into the air on the far side of a rosette a mile away. Its fall from the chimney is silent because it has learned to fall through the green without touching it, folding itself into the shape of the gap between two columns. It nests nowhere. It sleeps on the chimney. Its droppings, white and salt-rich, run down the chimney's flank and the builders come out at night and take them, grain by grain, to strengthen the chimney the hawk sits on.

And there are others, smaller, less certain, that the description of a place like this tends to leave out because they are hard to see and harder to be sure of.

There is something that lives in the white salt at the bottom of the cracks and is not the long thing. It is small — a mite, a springing speck — and it is the only creature here that eats the rock's sweat directly, and its shed skins are what give the salt its faint iridescence at certain angles of the afternoon.

There is a moth, gray, that comes out for the columns' flowering and at no other time, and for the rest of the year is a pupa in the dry litter, so that the forest has a night-creature that exists for two nights a year and spends the other three hundred and sixty-some being a small hard shell that the builders have learned not to carry.

There is a fungus — pale, threadlike, growing only in the deepest galleries where the builders' exhalation keeps the air damp enough — that the builders farm, deliberately, on beds of chewed succulent, and that is the actual food of the city, the thing all the foraging is for. The workers cannot digest the succulent skin they gather. The fungus can. The fungus cannot exist outside the galleries. Neither party has ever seen the other and neither would survive a season alone, and this partnership is older than the towers, older than the columns, older than this rock; it came here in the gut of the first founding pair that flew in on the wind and dropped and dug, and everything above it — the streets, the collars, the chimneys — is the fungus's house.

And there are the ones who arrive after the fire. When the lightning takes a stretch of litter and the slow burn goes through and the packed green is briefly opened to the sky, there is a day, sometimes two, when the ash is still warm and the builders are still out on it, exposed, and the towers are scorched and their vents are open, and into that gap comes a wave of things that live nowhere in the forest and everywhere at its edges — quick red ants that carry off the pale workers by the hundred, a black-and-yellow wasp that lays in the soldiers, a small hooked bird that is not dun and does sing, once, a long descending note, and is gone the next morning. They do not stay. The forest closes. But they are as much a part of what lives here as the lizards are, because the fire is as much a part of the forest as the dew is, and something has to live on the fire.

Stand still long enough and the sense of it comes: that nothing here is living alone. The column is drinking the city's breath. The city is eating the column's skin. The lizard is a bump in the wall the city built. The bird is nesting in the rooms the city left. The beetle is licking the city's spit. The hawk is feeding the chimney it sits on. The fungus is the reason for the whole vast quarrying labor, and the fungus is a thread in a room that no light has ever entered. The salt in the cracks is being eaten by something. The dead arm on the ground is a hundred mouths. The grazer is a leaf that walks. The long thing is the crack made animal. And the queen — the queens, one to a tower, a hundred thousand of them in a hundred thousand sealed chambers across the whole warm shield of rock — lie in the dark producing, every few seconds, one more small soft pale life that will chew rock it cannot see, on behalf of a garden it will never taste, under a green it will never know is green.

It is not an ecosystem, exactly, though it is that. It is closer to a single very slow, very large animal that has not yet noticed it is one. Its bones are the towers. Its skin is the green. Its blood is the air that goes one way by day and the other by night. It eats sunlight and lightning and the fresh mineral of a floor that was liquid, geologically speaking, a moment ago. It has no eyes. It has never needed them. It is spread across the whole low warm shield to a horizon that is a haze, and it is not finished, and it is not ruined, and it does not know that anywhere there is rain.
