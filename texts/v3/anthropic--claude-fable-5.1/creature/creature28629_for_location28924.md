**Who lives in the Weeping Gallery**

Start with what is easiest to count, because nothing here is easy to count.

The bats are the roof. Small — a body no longer than a thumb, wings that fold to the size of a folded letter — and brown in the way that wet bark is brown, darkening toward the wing-roots. Their faces are the pale part. In the amber light from the knee-strip, a face on the ceiling catches like a thumbnail: a short muzzle, a wide mouth, ears set forward and cupped. They are not the long-eared sort. Whatever their ancestors listened for, these have stopped listening for it; their ears, if you could compare them with a cousin's from outside, are shorter, thicker at the base, as if the whole organ had been asked to do less and had quietly agreed. They hang in the dry stripes between drip-lines, and the stripes are dry because they hang there — the heat of a few hundred bodies keeps the wall above them from beading — so that the bats have made, without meaning to, the exact conditions they require. The pewter surface under each stripe is worn to a soft shine by generations of the same feet gripping the same seams. They are in constant small motion, and the motion is the way they know one another. A bat arriving at the roost lands among its neighbors and is touched, sniffed, shouldered; it does not announce itself, and nothing announces itself to it. Their echolocation, which they still have, is used for the grille and the sky and the insects outside. Inside the gallery they navigate at a range of about a hand's width, by whisker and by the warmth of the bodies they are about to join. They are, in the roar, a colony that has replaced hearing with touch, and the ceiling is the result: a single continuous surface of grooming and settling, rippling from one end to the other whenever something shifts, like wind moving across a field of one crop.

Below them and toward the light are the swifts. If the bats are the roof, the swifts are the eaves. Their nests are cups of mud from the deposit and their own spit, gray-black, shelled hard, built along the mineral lips in ranks so tight the cups have merged into a single crenellated ledge, and new cups are built on the ruins of old ones so the ledge is thickest at its oldest end. The birds are dark, with a paler throat, and outside the grille they are all voice — the shriek of a swift at speed is one of the few sounds that can be heard in the gallery at all, arriving in the room as a thin bright scratch on the roar. They stop at the slats. A swift crosses the grille and closes its mouth, and inside it communicates by the angle of its body in the nest-mouth and by a wide-open silent gape that the chicks answer with their own. The chicks are fed in silence and grow up in silence and learn, at the moment they first fall out into the cloud and catch themselves, that they have a voice. It is possible the young ones scream on that first flight from surprise.

The frogs are the wet zone, which is most of the walls. They are not one kind. There are at least three: something small and dark with a copper stripe down the spine that lives in the running water and is almost impossible to see when it is still; something the size of a fist, warty, pewter-colored like the wall, that sits in the mineral cups with only its eyes and the ridge of its back above water; and a middle sort, slick and black with a throat that flushes pale when it calls. The calling is the thing to watch. A frog in the trough of the roar will inflate its throat and hold it, trembling, for a full second, and you will hear — if you are close, if you are lucky, if you are holding your breath — a tick so high and so thin it seems to come from inside your own ear. Frogs elsewhere call in the range where frogs have always called, because that is where the roar is not. These have gone up. They have gone up over generations until their calls ride in the one narrow band the wall leaves empty, and the females, who hear in that band and no other, come to the sound across a room in which every other frequency is a wall of noise. Their eggs are laid in the mineral cups. Look down into one of the dissolved bowls under an overhang and you will see them: a clear jelly, a dozen black points, and in older cups the black points have become tadpoles that hang in warm metallic water and eat the alga film off the crust and grow, and the water in which they grow is water the wall wept a week ago. The cups overflow in the afternoon when the roar climbs and the weeping thickens, and this is how the tadpoles move — carried down from cup to cup along the flowstone in a slow pewter cascade, a nursery that is also a staircase.

The geckos take the crusts between the frogs' cups, and they are the only inhabitants that look at you. Pale, almost translucent, with a spine of darker blotches and toes spread like drops of wax, they hold to the overhangs and the vertical faces and move only in short flat runs. Their eyes are enormous, as gecko eyes are, and here they have grown larger. In a place with one slab of gray light and one band of tired amber, an animal that hunts by sight has had to become mostly eye. They eat what the deposit sends up — the beetles, the small moths, the flies that hatch out of the black soil in humid pulses — and they are eaten by nothing that you can see, though there are fewer of them on the far side, and the far side is where the ringed tails live.

About those. The description you were given hedged — two of them, or one that moves quickly — and standing in the gallery long enough resolves the hedge: there are two, and they are old, and they are a pair or were. Low, long-bodied, gray-brown with a coat that stays sleek because it is always wet, and a tail banded in darker rings that is the only part of them the amber strip shows clearly as they move along the deposit's far edge at the end of a day. Their faces are pointed and their ears are small and laid back, and they walk with a rolling, deliberate gait, nose down, working the surface of the soil for beetles, for a frog that has strayed too far from the wall, for a swift chick that has fallen. They do not hunt by ear. They do not seem to hunt by eye either. They hunt by nose and by the feel of the ground, and they have learned the gallery's tide so exactly that they emerge at the same point in the roar's evening fall each day and return to their hollow in the deep deposit of the far corner at the same point in its morning climb. One of them has a stiffness in the hind leg. The other waits for it. Whether there were ever young — whether there are young now, somewhere in the corner, in a warm bowl of black soil — is not a thing the gallery discloses. It is possible these two are the last, and that after them the frogs will be a little more numerous and the beetles will work the deposit unhindered and nothing else will change. It is possible there is a third, smaller, that has not yet learned to come out at the fall of the sound.

Then the insects, who are not a population but a weather. The midges live in the grille-light and nowhere else, a standing smoke that thickens when cloud comes in and thins when it goes, and their whole lives are lived in a volume of air about the size of a wardrobe. The beetles are black and rounded and slow, a dozen kinds that all look like the same kind, and they are the engine of the floor: they eat what falls and produce the fine soil that everything else stands on. The centipedes are the color of the wall. You will not see one whole; you will see a shine cross a shelf and then be gone under it, and you will understand by the length of the shine that the animal was longer than your hand. The moths rest on the ceiling between the bats, wings open, and they shiver — not in fear, there is nothing here to fear from sound, but because the roar moves through the ceiling material and into their bodies and their wings answer it the way a drumskin answers a note. They have been shivering there so long that a moth at perfect rest in the Weeping Gallery is a moth in constant motion, and you begin to think that stillness is not something this place permits.

Below the insects, below sight, is the true population. The crust is not a mineral deposit. Or it is, but the depositing is done by something. Along every drip-line, in every cup, over every lip of flowstone, there is a film — a dark, wet, gelatinous skin of alga and bacteria and things that are neither — that takes the metals out of the weeping water and lays them down. The crust is thick as a wrist because the film has been thickening it for longer than the structure has records, and the shelves have grown to the shapes they will keep because the film has found, over centuries, the geometry that holds the most water for the longest time. The green-black mat along the amber strip is the same film where it has enough light to be green. Everywhere else it is simply the wet, and you have been touching it since you came in. It is the gallery's oldest resident and its only one that was never a visitor: it did not come in through the grille or the hatch, it was in the water when the water first came through the wall, and the whole flowstone cathedral is its shell.

And the deposit, which the description called the most alive thing in the room, is alive in the way a body is: warm, working, converting. Fungus threads it. Worms move through it — pale, blind, thread-thin — and the beetles' larvae, and the eggs of things that will hatch in the next humid pulse. It breathes ammonia. If you dig into it with a hand you find it layered, darker and denser as you go down, and near the bottom, on the pewter floor, there is a black paste that has stopped being anything in particular and is just the gallery's memory of everything that has ever lived above it.

Which leaves the path. The description mentioned it in passing — ankle-deep in the center, where the traffic keeps it packed — and did not say what traffic. The bats do not walk. The frogs stay to the wet. The ringed tails work the far edge. The path runs from the low hatch to a point beneath the grille and stops there, and it is worn by feet.

Someone comes. Not often, and not for long, but regularly enough that the deposit has not swallowed the path. Sitting at the end of it, where the gray light falls, you can see what they leave: a flat stone the size of two hands, wiped clean of soil, on which the film has not been allowed to grow. Nothing else. No tools. No sign of repair to the wall, no attempt at the amber strip, no marks on the pewter. Whoever this is does not maintain the gallery. They sit on the stone in the grille-light and do whatever one does in a room where speech is provincial. They have, like everything else here, made an arrangement with the noise, and the arrangement is probably the simplest one available: they cannot hear it. Whether the roar took their hearing or whether they came already unable to hear and found in the gallery the one place where that was not a lack — the one room in the whole structure where everything that lives is deaf by circumstance or by inheritance or by choice, and nothing is listened for, and a body is regarded as weather — the gallery does not say. But the frogs do not go quiet when they pass. The geckos do not run. The ringed tail with the bad leg has been seen, at the fall of the sound, to cross the packed center and pause a moment at the stone before going on. That is not nothing. In most places that is a thing that takes years.

So: who lives in the Weeping Gallery. A roof of bats who have traded their ears for their hands. Eaves of swifts who are mute indoors. Three kinds of frog who sing above the roar in a band of silence they had to climb to reach, and raise their young in cups the wall wept for them. Geckos that are mostly eye. Two old animals with ringed tails who may be the last of themselves. A weather of midges and a floor made of beetles and a ceiling full of moths that shiver because stillness here is not a thing that can be had. A film older than the room, building it a shell. A soil that is the memory of all of it. And on a clean stone at the end of a path, sometimes, a person who does not hear and is not heard and has found, in a fold in a wall of a thing that is mostly wall, the one den large enough for that.

They have this in common: none of them is startled by anything. They are what remains when you take away the possibility of being warned. What lives in the gallery is everything that learned to live without a warning, and they live very close together, and very warm, and they do not look up when the cloud comes in.
