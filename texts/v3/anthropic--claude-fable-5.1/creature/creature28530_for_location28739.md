The swifts are the first answer, and the wrong one.

They are what you see, and what you hear, and for most of a day they are most of what the hollow seems to be. But they do not live here. They come in over the rim like water finding the low place, and they take what the bowl gives, and at the moment the light leaves the western wall they are gone, all of them, in a spiral that empties the air in less than a minute. The hollow is a table they eat at. Their lives are in the cracks of the plateau, in the ordinary air, at the ordinary speed.

What lives here is what is left when the column lifts.

---

The trees, then. Four standing and — you will find it eventually, under the moss on the southern slope — one down. The fifth fell a long time ago and is still going back, a mound the length of a boat with ferns rooted along its spine and its heartwood, where a limb has split from it, still pale and tight-grained, not yet soft. Things take a long time to go in the hollow. The trees know this better than anything. They are older than trees of their kind are, elsewhere; they have kept growing because nothing has told them to stop, and each one has become a settlement rather than a plant, a warm damp country stacked up in the air.

The country has residents. The frogs the colour of tea are born in a bromeliad cup and, most of them, die in the same cup; the pond they know is a hand's-breadth across and a thumb deep, and the sky above it is the underside of a leaf. In the fog hour, when everything drips, a few of them climb. They go up the wet leaf and over the rim and down the outside and across a span of bark to the next cup, and this crossing — twenty centimetres, in the mist, with the swifts screaming overhead — is the largest thing that will ever happen to them. The beetle that lives in the forks lives nowhere else, and never has: the whole of its kind, every individual that has ever been or will be, is inside forty paces of stone. The bats roost in the deep shade and are the hollow's second shift. The spiders string the gaps between the trees in the evening and take the silk down at dawn, because the fog would bead on it and bring it to the floor. These live here. Their whole world is the bowl.

But they live in the trees, and in the air, and in the cups. They live in the hollow the way anything lives anywhere. It is only the place that is strange, not them. They are quick. They are ordinary. The hollow has not made them; it has only sheltered them.

---

Under the trees, the black algae. This is the oldest thing.

It was here before the trees, because the trees stand on it — their roots went down through it and it closed around them. It grows on the stone that never dries, a film so thin and so dark that you take it for the stone's own colour until you touch it and your fingers come away slick. It does not grow toward anything. It does not grow, that you could see, at all. But it is thicker under the fallen tree than beside it, and it has begun, very slightly, to climb the lowest ring of moss on the trunks, and in the seams in the eastern wall, between the white layers of bone, there are black layers too, thin as a fingernail, one above another, going down as far as the bone goes and then farther.

If patience were a living thing it would be this. But it is hard to say the algae *lives* in the hollow. It is closer to say it *is* the hollow — the floor's own skin, the record the bowl keeps of itself. You could not say what it wants. It does not seem to want.

---

Then the floor. Bare warm stone, the description says, and it is nearly true.

But the plateau has no loose stones. That is the thing you understand slowly, standing at the rim. A country rubbed this flat has nothing left to break off; there is grit, and there is bedrock, and there is nothing between. And yet on the floor of the hollow, between the trees, on the bare warm slope, there are stones. Low. Rounded. Seven of them, or eight — one is half under the fallen trunk and might be a root. Each about the size of a person crouched down with their head tucked in. The colour of the floor exactly, because they wear the same grit the floor wears, drifted down from the rim over the same long time.

You notice them because they are wrong, and then you stop noticing them, and then, in the afternoon, when the shadow of the northern tree has moved across the floor, you see that one of them is not where the shadow's edge said it was.

---

It is easier to say what they are not than what they are. They are not tortoises, though that is the shape your mind reaches for first. Nothing so distinct as a shell: the back is a broad dome of something between hide and rind, ridged lengthwise, and the grit sits in the ridges and lichen has begun to colour the grit on the older ones. No head that you could point to when they are at rest. Low along the front edge, where the dome meets the stone, there is a fold. There are eyes, small and set very low, but they are shut most of the time, or lidded with dust, and it is a while before you are sure of them.

They do one thing, and they do it at dawn.

When the cold has pooled and the first sun touches the eastern wall and the fog stands knee-deep on the floor, the ridges on their backs part a little. That is the drinking. The mist beads in the opened ridges and runs in. And at the same time the fold at the front lowers to the stone and opens into a lip — broad, soft, grey-pink underneath and startlingly clean — and the lip goes down to the floor and begins, unhurriedly, to travel.

The fog brings things down. Moths with their wings beaded shut. Spiders whose lines it has broken. Petals from the flowers that open in the dark, and the insects that were in them. Whatever the night dropped and the damp finished. All of it lies on the floor in the hour before the sun climbs, and the lip goes over it and takes it, the way a tide would, without haste, without missing anything. By the time the fog has burned off the floor around each of them is clean to the stone.

That is why the floor is bare. They keep it.

Except for the bones. The lip comes to a fallen swift — there is always at least one; the slow air kills a few every day — and stops, and lifts a little, and goes around. They take nothing that was ever warm. Whether this is a rule they follow or a thing they cannot digest or something else entirely, there is no way to know. The bones stay. The grit covers them.

---

Their slowness is not the ring's slowness. The ring makes a leaf fall a breath late; that is all. It would not slow a heart.

Their slowness is their own, and the hollow taught it. Think of what the bowl is, from the floor. Nothing comes down the walls to hunt — nothing that could, would; the slope is long, and the thick air is a trap for anything that flies. And every dawn, without fail, for longer than there have been swifts on the plateau to find the place, the fog has stood on the floor and set the night's dead down on it. Food arrives. Danger does not. Speed cost something and bought nothing, and at some point, so far back that the ancestors of these were probably something quite different and quite quick, they set speed down and did not pick it up again. They have been putting it down ever since. Lay a hand on the flank of one — it will let you; it does not have a response to hands — and you can feel the heart, and the interval between beats is long enough that you count it twice to be sure.

They do not sleep, or they do not do anything else. In the heat of the day, under the full roar of the swifts, they sit. In the cold, some of them move, and the moving has a direction: by night the ones on the eastern side are closer to the pale pieces in the wall than they were at noon. A flank against the smooth stuff that lichen will not grow on. Whether it is the hum they want, or some faint warmth in it that is neither warm nor cold, or only that the ground beneath the pieces is a little more level, you cannot say. They lean there. In the morning they have leaned away again, an arm's length, and the fog comes, and the lip goes down.

---

The oldest has moss on it.

Moss grows against the ring pieces and not onto them. On this one it has come up out of the grit in the ridges and taken hold, a grey-green scurf along the spine, and there are seedlings — two — that have found enough in the moss to start, and will not go far, but have started. It has not moved in the days you have watched. You would say it was dead. At dawn its ridges part and the mist runs in.

At the other end there is a small one. Beside the largest, on the northern slope: a fistful, gritless, darker than the stone, its ridges sharp and its lip quick — quick as a tortoise's, which in the hollow is a kind of frenzy. It moves in the heat of the day, when the others do not. It has not learned yet. You watch it cross a hand's-width of bare rock in the time it takes the shadow to move the same distance, and you understand that this is a childhood, and that the whole of its life will be spent slowing down, and that it will get there.

They do not leave. Not because the ring holds them — nothing holds them — but because the fog is here and only here, and they are made for the fog; and because the walls go up, and up is the one direction they never had a reason to learn. The rim is forty paces away and might as well be the moon.

---

In the eastern wall, among the thin white seams of swift bone and the black hairlines of the algae, there are other layers. Thicker, and rarer — one every so often, going down. Grey, and rounded at the edges where the wall has cracked through them. The grit on top of each is the hollow's own grit, the same grit they wear in life, so that when one of them finally stops and lies where it stopped, nothing much changes about it for a long time. The fog beads on it. The lip of a neighbour comes to it and lifts, and goes around. The seedlings in its moss grow, and fail, and the grit comes down grain by grain from the rim, a breath late, and closes over.

---

Near sunset, when the swifts are loudest and the air itself is ringing and you are about to go, one of them — not the oldest, one of the middle ones, a stone among stones — lowers the fold at its front to the floor and lets the lip out a hand's-width in your direction, and holds it there.

It is the gesture it makes toward things that were never warm and might be food. It holds it for a while. Then the lip draws in, and the fold closes, and it is a stone again.

You were considered, and set aside, and that is the whole of your dealings with the only thing that truly lives here — the only thing in the bowl that lives at the bowl's pace, in the bowl's time, and has no other. Everything else is passing through at speed. The swifts by the day, the moths by the night, the frogs in a season, the trees in an age. The ring hums and is not alive. The algae keeps the record and does not want. Between them, on the warm bare stone, these sit, and drink the morning, and clean the floor, and lean at night against the thing that made the place, and when the plateau has worn away to nothing and the hollow stands alone above the plain, full of mist and trees and birds and humming, they will be on the floor of it, and they will not have noticed that anything happened.
