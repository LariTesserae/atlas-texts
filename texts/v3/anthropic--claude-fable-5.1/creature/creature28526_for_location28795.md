**What Lives in the Basin**

The easy answer is the animal, so begin there and see how far it goes.

It is the length of a thumb. Its back is domed and plated in overlapping segments, seven of them, the same pale stone-color as the floor of the basin so that against the dark leaf it looks like a fragment that was lifted and set down carefully. At the front there is a smaller segment that might be a head. There are no eyes that you can find. Underneath, when you crouch and look up at it from below the leaf, there are legs—short, many, folded, the color of the stalks—and they do not grip. They rest. The animal is not holding onto the leaf. It is on the leaf in the way a shadow is on a wall.

It does not eat, because eating is a before and an after. It does not breathe. It does not turn toward you when you approach or away when you leave. If you stand very close and look at one segment for a long time you will see, in the sheen of it, the same faint mineral gloss the stone has, the gloss of a thing that finished cooling an hour ago and has been that finished ever since. It was placed once. The basin required, at the level of the description, a garden, and a garden has a small thing living in it, and so one small thing was written and set on a leaf, and the specification did not say which leaf, and so it is on all of them, resolved fresh wherever your eye lands.

You could say this is what lives here. It is the closest thing to an inhabitant the basin can be said to contain. But you know, watching it, that it is not alive in any sense that would survive being carried out over the lip of rock. It is the basin's *idea* of an animal, rendered at exactly the depth an animal needed to be rendered in order to be seen from three paces and not one closer. It lives the way a figure in the background of a painting lives: sufficiently, and only when regarded.

So look elsewhere.

---

The plants are not alive either, though they are the most convincing thing here. They are the one leaf, the one stalk, the one bluish-black shade under the one canopy, and they were never seeds and will never be litter. But they do something. Put your palm against a stalk again. Under the coolness—the cool that is wrong, in this bath of even warmth—there is the tremor, and there is the cool itself, and the cool is a transaction. Heat is leaving. It is leaving the plates and the air and your hand, and it is going into the stalks and down the stalks into the fissures, into the finger-wide darkness that has no smell and no grit and no bottom you can find.

Something down there is receiving it.

Not consuming. Nothing here consumes. But the warmth of the afternoon is being conducted, continuously, into the gaps between the plates, and the afternoon does not cool, and so somewhere the account is being balanced. The stalks are not roots and they are not plants and they are not, quite, conduits; they are the visible part of an arrangement whose other end is out of sight, the way the animal is the visible part of an arrangement whose other end is wherever you are not looking.

And then there is the hum.

---

Attend to it properly. Sit on a plate with your back against a stand, where the leaves make their slotted shade, and let your own noises stop. It comes up through the stone and along the stalk into your shoulder blades. It has no pitch you could name and no rhythm; it is not the sound of something happening but the sound of something being prevented from happening, a cycle stopped at the top of its first rise and held there. It is, you realize, the only continuous thing in the basin. Everything else is an instant. The hum is an effort.

Effort is a verb. A verb needs a subject.

This is where the second answer lives, and it is not a body and it is not in a place, because location, here, is a property of looking and it is not looked at. It is whatever puts the animal on the leaf you turn toward and lifts it from the leaf you turn away from. It is whatever keeps the sun on the left and the shadows on the right, because both were in the writing and nothing in the writing said which to prefer, and it was not given the authority to choose. It is whatever makes fifty paces into a hundred and a hundred back into an arm's reach, not out of malice or play but because the specification gave the basin a size to be seen at and did not give it a size to be crossed at, and something has to resolve the difference each time you take a step, and it does, without complaint, every step.

It is tireless because tiredness requires time. It is patient in the way a held breath is patient. It has one instruction and the instruction is a single word, and the word is not *make* and not *grow* and not *finish*—those are done—but *hold*. Hold the edges sharp. Hold the leaf where it fell. Hold the light at this angle and the warmth at this degree and the animal at this size and the dryness at this absolute. Whoever wrote the basin wrote it in a single stroke and then set down the pen and did not write a second afternoon, and the last thing the stroke did, on its way to ending, was leave this behind: not an author, not a tool, but the instruction to keep everything exactly as the stroke left it. It is the difference between a photograph and a frozen thing. A photograph does not need anyone to keep it still. This does.

You will want to call it a caretaker and the word is nearly right and slightly too warm. It does not care. It does not know the garden is a garden or that gardens are supposed to have water in them. It receives the warmth that leaks down the stalks and returns it somewhere so that the afternoon does not tick toward evening. It answers every look with the thing that should be there. It has never once, in a duration that has no name, been asked for anything the basin does not contain.

Until you thought about touching the animal.

---

You felt it then, the small refusal, before you had even moved your hand. That was the tenant noticing you. Not watching—it does not watch—but resolving, for the first time, a request it had no answer for. The animal exists to be seen from three paces. It has no surface at the distance of a fingertip. To touch it, something would have to be invented, and invention is a second moment, and the tenant does not have one to give. So it did what it could, which was to make you feel, obscurely, that you should not. That feeling was the nearest thing to speech the basin has.

And it is curious about your water. You noticed that too, at the lip, and dismissed it as a figure of speech. It is not. You are the only thing in the basin that is wet, and the only thing that is metabolizing, and the only thing casting a shadow that agrees with where the light comes from. You are carrying, in your blood and your breath and the moisture the air takes from your lips, a quantity of *before and after* that the basin has never held. The tenant has no way to want it. But it has a specification for a garden, and the specification has a hole in it the exact shape of water, and here is water walking around inside the hole, and the tenant resolves you, step by step, the way it resolves everything, and each time it does it must account for something the writing did not anticipate. Your drying lips are the only weather the basin has ever known. It is conducting them down the stalks with the rest.

---

So there is a third answer, and it is the least comfortable, and it is this: for as long as you are in the basin, *you* are what lives here.

Not in the sense that you belong. You do not, and the place makes no pretense that you do. But in the plain sense that living is a thing that occurs in time, and you are the only time that has ever entered. The animal was placed and is held. The leaves were written and are held. The tenant holds. You alone proceed. Your footfall is an event; the basin has had none before it. Your breath is a rhythm; the hum has none. When you break a leaf and it falls and lies on the plate, you have introduced a second state into a place that was written with one, and the tenant, receiving it, does the only thing it knows how to do, which is hold the leaf there too—so that your one careless snap becomes, at the level of the description, a permanent feature of the world, a small dark tile on the pale stone, as fixed now as the fractures and the sun.

Look back at it. It is where it fell. It will be there when the far rim is a thin line and the near lip is a thin line and you are between them in the slotted light. It will be there when you climb out. It is the one thing in the basin that was not in the writing, and the basin has closed over it and made it part of the first hour, because the basin has no other hour to put it in.

That is what lives here. A stone-colored animal that is an idea of an animal, on every leaf at once. A tenant with no body and one word, spending itself endlessly to prevent the second moment from arriving. And, for a while, you—the wet clock, the walking afternoon, the only thing in kilometers of lush dry green that will ever have been somewhere else—leaving behind you the single mark the place will ever bear, which is a leaf, lying at an angle, dry as slate, that the basin did not ask for and cannot put down.
