Begin with the small things, because they are the easiest to be sure of.

The snails come up with the fog. You will not see them arrive; you look at a pipe casing in the grey before dawn and it is bare, and you look again and there are a dozen of them, shells no thicker than a fingernail paring, bodies you can see the light through, each one set at the lower edge of a bead of condensation and drinking it. They are not eating the plant. The plant does not seem to be eaten by anything much. They drink, and when the sun clears the rim and the beads start to shrink they turn, unhurried, and go down the casing and into the mat at its base, and the mat takes them without a mark. Where they spend the day nobody has found out. Nobody has dug for them. Digging is not something that is done here.

The beetle is darker and slower and lives in the dead layers, the spongy ones a hand's depth down, and it is the one creature in the hollow that does eat: the long-dead stem, the parts gone soft. You see it when a path is cut back, a dull black shape the length of a thumb joint that does not scatter when exposed but simply continues, at its own pace, into the nearest shadow. The path cutter knows it and does not trouble it. Between the beetle and the retting troughs, that is all the decay the hollow permits, and it permits it only underneath.

The cat is a story. It is told once, by whoever you happen to ask, in nearly the same words each time: there was a cat, it came down with the handcart, it lived in the pump house for the warmth, it would not walk on the liverwort, it was old when it came and it was here for some years and then it was not. Nobody says where it went. Nobody speculates. The story ends there, and it ends without regret, and you understand after hearing it twice that its function is not to remember the cat but to mark that the hollow has been, once, a place where a cat lived, and is not one now.

What lives in the roots you will not see. You may feel it. Standing very still on a kept path with the engine's knock coming up through your feet, you can sometimes feel, between strokes, a second and slower movement in the mat, not a tremor but a settling, as if something a long way down had shifted its weight. The people call it the mat working. They do not say what works it. Once, in a place where a path had been let go for a season and then cut back, the cutter turned over a shoot that had come up already stripped of its bark, cleanly, in a spiral. He looked at it for a while and laid it on the path with the rest.

---

The people you have to count more than once.

You will make it seven, walking the hollow in a morning, and then eight, and then, doing it again in the afternoon and finding the figure on the north rim gone and the ladder against the pump house empty, six. Ask and you will be told eleven, or nine, or told that it depends. It does depend. They are not hiding and they are not being counted; there is simply no vantage from which all of them are visible at once, and they do not gather. The buildings hold one or two each. Some hold nobody and are kept anyway, the walls re-pleached in their turn, the roofs knotted, the floors trodden glossy by whoever passes through to do it.

They are dressed in the fibre, all of them, the coarse grey-green cloth that comes off the press, and it is never dry. Nothing here is ever dry. The cloth hangs a little heavy and darkens at the shoulders where the eave-drip catches it, and they have stopped noticing this in the way you stop noticing your own weight. Their skin has gone the way skin goes in water. In the mornings their fingertips are pruned. Their hair lies flat. Their faces have the particular quality of things kept in green light: not pale exactly, but uncoloured, as though they had been drawn and not yet filled in. The old ones are very old. It is hard to say whether there are any young ones or only smaller old ones.

They walk as the mat has taught them to walk, flat-footed, the weight spread, without hurry, because the liverwort is slick and the club moss springs back at the ankle and anyone who moves quickly here goes down. You can pick out a newcomer by the way they lift their knees. The people do not lift their knees. They slide their feet an inch above the surface and set them down whole, and the mat receives them, and they are gone along the path before you have registered that they were moving.

They speak very little, and quietly. The mat soaks up a raised voice and gives back nothing, so that shouting here has the quality of shouting into a pillow, foolish and faintly obscene, and nobody does it. What needs saying is said at arm's length. Most of what needs saying is not said at all but done, and read. The nets on the north rim have been shaken and rehung; therefore someone was there before the mist lifted; therefore the water in the cistern will be up by noon. The beam has been trimmed; the cut shoots are on the path by the pump house door; therefore the engine tender has been up the ladder this morning and the engine is, in her judgement, well. A path that has been let close by a finger's width is a message too, and a sharper one. Nobody sends it lightly.

---

The engine tender is a woman, this generation, and she sleeps in the pump house on a shelf of woven stem that the warmth of the cylinder keeps from ever quite sweating. She does not hear the engine anymore. She hears its changes. A knock that comes a half-beat late will bring her off the shelf before she is awake; a hiss that thins at the end of the intake will have her at the valve chest with a file before she has decided to go. She has a vocabulary for these things, twenty or thirty words, and they are not shared with the others because the others do not need them. The others need the knock to keep coming.

Her hands are the only hands in the hollow with calluses from iron. She files the burls that serve as cams, and she does it the way a person pares a nail, a little at a time and often, so that the shape is never lost and never quite finished. She oils the crosshead pin, which is a knot, with something pressed from the retting waste. She keeps the ash at the base of the chimney. She trims the beam.

The beam's shoots are pale and soft and come up along its top edge in the warm months in a line like the hairs on a forearm, and she takes them off with a curved blade every few days and lays them on the path, where they are trodden in. Except that once, some years ago, she did not lay one on the path. She set it into the mat on the south side of the pump house, in a place where the drip from the eaves would reach it, and it took. It is a tree now, or the beginning of one: a trunk you could not yet close both hands around, straight, still barked, with the same pale shoots coming up along its upper edge, which she also trims. Nobody has said what it is for. Nobody has asked. The present beam is sound and will be sound, in her estimate, for longer than she will. But the tree is there, in the wet, growing at the rate the hollow allows, and when you stand between it and the pump house and look from one to the other, you understand what is being kept.

The net tender is the one who sees the dry country. Every morning before the mist lifts he is on the rim, and every morning the mist lifts around him and there it is: the flat brown reach of it going off under a sky so much larger than the hollow's that it takes a moment to recalibrate. He looks at it. He does not look at it long. Then he unhooks the sheets, one after another, heavy and dark with what they have caught, and shakes each one into the trough that feeds the standpipe, and hooks it back, angled a degree or two differently if the wind has come round, and goes down. His shoulders are stooped from the shaking. His face is the only one in the hollow with any weather in it, and the weather is all on one side.

The retting is done by two people who smell of it and no longer know that they do. The troughs stink, sweetly, of stem gone soft in warm water, and the two who tend them stand in it to the knee, turning the bundles, testing the fibre with a thumbnail for the moment it will come free. They work within reach of each other, which is unusual here, and they do not speak either. When the fibre is ready they comb it out on a board of pleached stem gone hard as bone, and twist it, and take it to the press, and from the press it goes to a loom in the next building where an old person you will never see working sits behind a wall and makes it into sheets. The sheets last a season. They have always lasted a season. The retting pair have the hands of people who have never in their lives held anything dry, and when they come out of the troughs at evening their legs are the colour of the liverwort.

The path cutter is the one who decides where the hollow is. He does not put it this way. He would say the paths are where the paths are cut. But it is his blade that keeps them, and where he does not walk the way closes at a finger's width a day, and where he walks a little wider for a season the way is a little wider, and there are two buildings on the west side that the paths no longer reach because in his predecessor's time they stopped being walked to, and the doors are still there, green and knotted shut, and the roofs still lift and settle with the day's warmth, and nobody goes in. He lays the cut stems on the path behind him and they are trodden in by the next person to pass, and this is how the floor of the hollow is made: out of the edges of itself, laid down and walked on.

---

People come down. Not often, and not in numbers. The handcart goes up the rim with the surplus fibre and comes back with iron for the rails, and salt, and sometimes a person walking behind it who has, for reasons that are the dry country's business, decided to walk behind it. Some of them stay a night in the green light and the drip and go back up. Some stay a season. A few stop going up to the rim at all, and their knees stop lifting, and their fingertips are pruned in the mornings, and one day you count them.

Nobody is born here that anyone will confirm. There is a child, or there is something that is smaller than the others and moves the same way; you see it once, at the retting house, laying cut stem on a path with the flat deliberate care of someone who has been shown how exactly once. You do not see it again. Whether it came down with the handcart or came from the hollow itself is not a question that has an answer you would be given.

Nobody dies here that anyone will discuss, either, but the mat is deep, and nobody has reached the bottom of it, and it closes over whatever is laid on it in a season. This is understood. It is understood in the same way the engine is understood, the iron giving way to wood where the iron wore out, until it can no longer be determined what was built and what was grown. The people who tend the hollow are in it, is the plain fact, some of them a long way down, and the paths are cut where they have always been cut, and the beetle does its slow work in the dead layers, and nobody finds this troubling. The two have reached terms.

---

It is at about this point, if you have stayed long enough, that you begin to suspect you have been describing the wrong inhabitant.

The plant does not divide into plants. The walls can no longer be told apart; neither can the mat, nor the roofs, nor the casings on the pipes, nor the great trunk-stack of the chimney. It leans toward the light as one thing. It breathes out the mist as one thing, in the two hours before dawn, from every surface at once. It closed the gap in the rusted pipe as one thing, the way a wound closes, from all sides. It has grown around the engine so completely — the beam that is its own timber, the cams that are its own burls, the pin that is its own knot — that the engine can be understood as an organ of it, a heart it acquired or built or was built around, lifting the water that keeps it alive in a country that would otherwise dry it to nothing in a year.

And the people, in this light, are what it grows to keep the heart running. It grows them the way the beam grows shoots: a few at a time, along its edges, pale and soft and in need of trimming. It clothes them in its fibre. It feeds them on its water and whatever comes down with the handcart. It teaches them to walk on it without falling and to speak in voices it can absorb. It takes them back into the mat when they are done, at a finger's width a day. The nets catch the water that runs to the engine that lifts the water that grows the stems that are retted into the nets, and somewhere in that closed loop, turning it, are eleven or nine or six figures who do not call to each other across the distances because they do not need to, because they are not, in this light, separate.

This is too neat, and you will know it is too neat, and the thing that breaks it is the net tender on the rim in the morning, looking at the dry country. He looks at it. Nothing the plant grows for its own use would look at that. And the engine tender's tree, which nobody has a use for yet, set into the mat by a hand that was thinking about a time after itself. And the cat story, told in the same words, ending without regret but ending, kept. And the two doors on the west side, green and knotted shut, that somebody chose to stop walking to.

The people could leave. Some do. The plant could not, and without the paths cut and the beam trimmed and the burls filed and the nets shaken, the knock would come a half-beat late one morning and then later, and then not at all, and the mist would still rise but nothing would lift the water to meet it, and the dry country would come down over the rim in the slow way of dry country. The plant knows this, if a plant knows anything. The people know it. Neither is the other's organ. Neither is the other's master. They have reached terms — three parties now, iron and stem and the flat-footed figures between them — and the terms are the hollow, and the hollow is what lives here.

---

At dusk the engine tender comes out of the pump house and stands with her back against the frame, where the iron is warm, and puts her hand flat on the casing. She is not checking anything. She does this every evening. The knock comes up through her palm and through the soles of her feet at the same instant, and the difference between the two — the tiny lag as the sound travels the mat — is a thing she could tell you about in one of her thirty words, if you asked, if you were the kind of person she would answer.

Across the hollow the path cutter has laid his last stems and is standing very still on a kept path, feeling for the mat working under the engine's beat. On the north rim there is nobody now; the nets are hung and the dry country is going dark. In the retting house the water in the troughs is the temperature of the air, exactly. Something in the roots shifts its weight. The steam thread stands straight up from the chimney and is lit, and then is not.

Once every four or five seconds. Everything here is inside that.
