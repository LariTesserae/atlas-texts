The first thing to say about what lives here is that the word does not divide the way it should. On the plateau, *lives* has been said too many times, like the stone, and its edges have gone. There is the moss that pumps and the hare that steps out of it and the bird that lands on it to be warm, and there was once a difference between those three, and the difference has been rained on.

So start with the birds, because they are the easiest to be sure of.

They are small and brown and they are pipits, or were. Streaked breast, thin bill, the long hind claw of a ground bird. There are hundreds on every mound and they are always wet; the feathers have given up trying to shed water and lie flat and dark against the body so that each bird looks smaller than it is, a sodden thing the size of a thumb, clinging to the sedge around the vent with its eyes half shut. They came in a storm. That is what you would guess, and it may even be true—one flock, driven up over the cloud deck and finding the only warm place for a hundred miles in any direction, and landing, and not leaving. Why would they leave. The mound is warm and the midges rise from the steam in a column and the bird only has to open its bill. They do not fly when you approach because flight requires distance to be reliable, and they have learned that here it is not. A bird that lifts off toward another mound may arrive before it left, and find itself already there, huddled, and not know what to do about it. So they walk. They hop. They keep their wings shut and they take the heat.

They breed. They tuck their nests into the warm moss on the lee flank—if there were a lee; on the flank away from the vent—and lay, and sit, and when the mound draws in the nest goes with it, eggs and all, into the mass. And a minute later, or a cycle later, or three cycles, there are pipits on the flank that were not there before, standing damp and blinking, and they join the others. There is no way to tell which. Their song, when they give it, a thin descending run, comes to you a little before the bill opens. You can watch this. You can watch a bird stand with its throat still and hear it already singing, and then see it catch up to its own song. They do not seem to mind. They have been here long enough that the song is simply how it sounds now.

The frogs are the next surest.

They lie in the warm seep at the base in a crust two and three deep, and the crust is arranged. The bottom layer is against the warm ground and asleep, or in whatever state a frog is in when it has stopped moving and its eyes have filmed over. The top layer is in the cold air and awake, and it is the top layer that watches you. Every so often the crust turns over—slowly, over ten or fifteen minutes, one frog easing down between two others and a warm one rising—so that each frog spends part of its time cold and watching and part of its time warm and gone. The middle layer, you begin to think, is where the mound is. Not that the mound has frogs in the middle. That the middle is neither frog nor mound, that a frog going down through it comes out warm and slightly different, and that the frogs know this and do it anyway, because the warmth is the point.

Their spawn lies in the seep in ropes and clouds and the mound draws the seep up through itself with every intake. What comes back down the flank is warm water and, sometimes, tadpoles. Sometimes frogs. So the population of the crust is stable, and has been, and there is no way to say what proportion of it was ever anything but moss.

Under the frogs, in the moss, the thing with too many legs.

You never see it whole. A section of it will cross an opening in the vegetation—dark, segmented, jointed at intervals that are not regular, each segment with its own pair of legs moving in a wave that begins before the segment arrives and continues after it has gone. It is longer than the opening. It is longer than any opening. If you follow the moss around the flank you will see other sections of it, and they may be the same section at a different time, and they may not. It is the mound's way of moving things about inside itself: a fallen bird, a frog that has gone too deep, a hare that has come apart into strands but not far enough. These are passed along. You can see the lump of them travelling under the moss like something swallowed, and where the lump goes there is a swell in the flank a minute later, and the flank parts.

Whether it is an animal or an organ is the kind of question the plateau has stopped answering.

The made things you have already seen. Hares, mostly; the mound seems to have settled on the hare the way a hand settles on a gesture. But not only. Stoats, thin and low. Once a grouse, which stood for a long time in the water saying nothing and then dissolved from the feet up. Once—you will tell yourself you did not see this—a lamb. It stood and shook and its ears were too long and it took a step and came apart before the second step. The mounds are not trying to make anything. That is not the right word. They are what happens when peat has been alive for long enough to have habits, and the habits are shaped like the things that used to cross the plateau when it was still a mountain and had a shape and there were paths.

At the centre, where the water is warm and clouded and the columns join overhead, the habits have gone further. The swimmers there are not copies. Nothing walked the plateau that looked like that: soft, pale, the length of a forearm, moving with the flex of something that has muscles but no skeleton to pull against. Some have the suggestion of eyes—two darker patches, forward, roughly where eyes would go if the thing needed to see. Some have more than two. They swim between the flanks with what looks like purpose, go under, and come out different—longer, or with the eye-patches moved, or not at all. The mounds out there have been exchanging themselves for so long that they have begun to make things none of them would make alone, and these are the things. One of them has been swimming, you will be told, for a very long time. It goes under and comes out the same. That has not happened before. The birds on the nearest mound have gone quiet in the way they do.

And then there are the herons.

You may not notice them for a while because they are the colour of the plateau and they do not move. They stand in the empty ground between the mounds, in the inch of water, one leg drawn up, and they are the only things on the flat that are cold. Everything else has gone to the warm. The herons stand where the warmth is not. Grey, very tall, longer in the neck than a heron should be—as though the neck has worn thin like the distances—and they wait. What they wait for is the crossing. A hare steps out of a mound, shakes itself, and sets off across the cold water toward the next mound, or toward nothing, and if it is going to come apart it usually does so in the first thirty metres, and if it does not, there is a heron. The strike is very fast and the heron does not swallow. It holds. The hare comes apart in its bill into strands and the heron stands with the strands hanging and does not let them drift back, and the mound draws in and the strands lean toward it and do not go.

It seems to be the only thing here that keeps anything.

Because the sequence has worn thin, the heron is sometimes ahead of the hare. You will see a heron standing with a bill full of dark strands and the moss on the flank behind it only just beginning to swell. It has caught the thing before the mound has finished making it. It stands there holding what has not yet stepped out, patient, cold, its one eye on the water. When the swell parts and nothing comes, or something comes that is already less than it should be, the heron does not adjust. It has held its portion. Eventually the strands go slack and slip and it lets them, and they are drawn back, and it lifts its other foot and puts it down and is still again.

There are perhaps a dozen of them. There is perhaps one. They stand at the intervals where the distances fold, and if you walk toward one it will get no closer until it is very close, and then you will see that its eye is not on you and has never been on anything but the ground between the mounds. They are the plateau's slow pump. Everything else here holds its shape for a minute or an hour; the heron holds for longer, and in that holding something like a record gets kept—not of what happened, since nothing here happens in an order, but of the fact that something did. They are where the plateau remembers that it is running.

Last, the people, because there are people, and you will meet them, and it will be the same few.

There is one who sits. She has a place on the flank of a mound about a third of the way toward the centre, where the moss has shaped itself around her over a long time into something between a seat and a socket, and she sits in it with the pipits on her shoulders and her boots in the warm seep. Her coat is grey wool and it has grown a fine green pile along the shoulders and down the back, and she has not brushed it off. She came—she will tell you this, if you sit—to survey. There was a theodolite. There were pegs and a chain. Somewhere out on the flat there is a brass plate set into the stone with a number on it and the number means nothing now because the thing it measured has been rained on. She says this without regret. She came to measure the plateau and stayed to be measured by it, and she seems to think this was a fair exchange.

Her words arrive before she says them. You will grow used to it. She speaks and the sentence is already in the air, low and patient, and then her mouth catches up. She is not troubled by the hares. She eats them, sometimes, when one comes close enough and holds together long enough—the meat is warm and tastes of iron and comes apart in the mouth before it can be swallowed, but there is a moment, she says, and the moment is what she is here for now. She knows the herons. She knows which mound will make a lamb, and when, and she does not watch.

You will meet her again further along, and she will be younger, and she will not know you. You will meet her again further along and there will be a cairn where she was, and the cairn will be moss, and warm, and working.

She is not the only one. There is a man who walks. He is always in the middle distance, always heading for the horizon that is too close, and he has not reached it, and he is there again behind you, walking, and the water shows his reflection lagging. He does not stop to talk. It may be that he has never stopped. There is someone at the centre, or something with someone's shape, who sits with the swimmers and has one hand in the warm water, and the swimmers pass under the hand and come out changed, and it is not clear which of them is doing the changing. The woman who sits does not go there. It is fine not to.

Whether these people are the mound's people, the way some of the frogs are the mound's frogs, is the question you will come to and then set down, because she has set it down, and the birds have, and the herons standing in the cold have not so much answered it as held their portion and let the rest go. She will tell you her name, and it will be the same as the name of the mound she sits on, and she will not mean this as a joke, and you will not be able to tell whether she means she has given her name to it or been given its own.

So: what lives here. Small wet birds that sing before they open their bills. Frogs in a crust that is warm at the bottom and awake at the top and neither in the middle. A long thing under the moss that is either an animal or a way of moving. Hares that are a habit. Pale swimmers that are a habit's habit. Tall cold birds that keep what nothing else will. A surveyor and her cairn, at once. A walker. A hand in the warm water.

And under all of it the mounds, which are the only things here that were never anything else, and which are not alive in the sense you brought with you, and are not not. They draw. They release. They make and unmake and the thud arrives before the fall, and everything that lives on the plateau lives in the space between the thud and the fall, in the inch of warm water that comes seeping back down the flank, in the minute before the strands go slack. That is the whole habitat. That is the only room there is. The plateau is finished and nothing more can happen to it, and in the nothing, at forty-metre intervals, a great many things go on being almost themselves.
