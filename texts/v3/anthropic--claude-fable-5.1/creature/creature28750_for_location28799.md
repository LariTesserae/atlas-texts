Begin with what the description already admits: nothing lives here but the thing itself. Take that as the first answer and then see whether it holds.

The thing itself, then. It is easiest to say what it is by saying where it stops, and it does not stop. The columns are it. The conduits are it. The vanes and the stalks and the mat that goes down and never reaches ground are it. The cloud overhead that no one has seen is it, in the way breath is a person's for the moment it is out of them. It is not a forest, because a forest is many things standing near one another, and this is one thing standing in many places. Put a hand on a column and a hand on the mat and there is nothing between them that is not the same body. Walk for a day and the body has not been left. Walk for a year. There is no outside of it to walk into. It does not occupy the lowland the way a tenant occupies a room. It is the room. It is what the word *lowland* refers to. If the plant were removed, there would be no floor.

So the first inhabitant is also the house. That is a strange kind of living. Consider what it lacks.

It does not eat. Nothing enters. The water it drinks is water it exhaled. The salt and copper in the fluid have been salt and copper in the fluid for as long as there has been fluid. Whatever it was built from, it was built from once, and since then it has been rearranging the same material at a steady rate, like a man passing coins from his left pocket to his right and back and calling it commerce.

It does not die. Not because it is protected, but because dying is a thing that happens to a body when something else gets in — a rot, a rust, a tooth — and nothing has gotten in. The edges are sharp because no one has dulled them. Death, in most places, is a visitor. Here no one has visited.

It does not make more of itself. No flower, no spore, no runner that puts down a new column a little further on. A living thing is usually recognized by its impatience to become two living things. This one has no such impatience. The description puts it well and then does not follow it: it is not finished with being what it is.

Three lacks. And those three — eating, dying, multiplying — are very nearly the whole of what makes a living thing visible as living to anything watching. Take them away and what remains is not obviously alive. What remains is a process. A river is warm-blooded compared to this. A river at least takes in rain and gives up to the sea.

And yet.

Lay the hand flat between two ribs again and feel the pressure. It moves at a rate, and the rate is the rate. That is not how dead things move. Dead things run down. Water finds the lowest place and stays. Heat spreads until it is even and then nothing happens. A pipe under load with nothing pushing it goes slack. This does not go slack. Something is doing the pushing, and it has done the pushing for long enough that the ribs are still crisp, and it is doing it now, under the palm, and it will be doing it when the palm is gone. That steadiness is not the steadiness of a stone. It is the steadiness of a thing that is *holding*.

So go inward. If the thing is the house, then what lives in the house is whatever moves through it. Go to the fluid.

It is warm. It tastes of salt and copper. It moves one way in one conduit and the other way in the next. It carries a charge, and the charge is not small, and touching two conduits at once makes the fingers ache. Anyone who has bled knows what salt and copper is. Anyone who has had a cramp knows what it is for a signal to go along a line at a potential. The plant is not built like a plant. It is built like the inside of an animal, laid out flat and grown over a continent, with the skin on the inside — the wet, the warm, the pressure of blood coming through a wall — and the dark all around it because it never needed to look out.

And what does the fluid carry? Not food; nothing is eaten. Not waste; nothing is shed. It goes out and it comes back and it tastes the same. In an animal, blood that carried neither food nor waste would carry only one thing worth the effort of moving: news. The state of the far parts. Whether the vane at the edge of the day's walk is still catching. Whether the column a year to the west is still under load. The violet thread that jumps a gap after a long stillness, the deep displacement felt in the chest on its interval — these are not the machine. These are the machine reporting to itself.

Here is a second candidate, then, for what lives here: a message. A long, slow, continuous message that the body sends around itself and receives and sends again. Not a thought, exactly, because a thought goes somewhere. This does not go anywhere. It goes around. Sit long enough on the mat and the interval of the deep displacement becomes familiar, and it is possible to imagine what it says — *still here, still here, still here* — a check, a roll call of columns, the whole continent confirming to itself that it is whole, at the rate, forever.

But that is too neat. A message needs someone to be for. Who is the thing telling?

Go back to the surfaces. Warm, wet, even, unbroken. The description says it plainly and then moves on: *nothing has laid a spore on the wet warm surfaces where a spore would surely take.* Surely. The word is doing a great deal. This is the most hospitable ground that has ever existed. There is no cold to kill, no dry to parch, no light to burn, no wind to strip. The mat gives underfoot like the back of a hand. It would take anything. Anything at all, set down here, would grow.

And nothing has been set down.

Look at the plant again with that in mind and it changes. The vanes catch the water before it can fall and waste itself on the mat — but a mat that wanted nothing would not care about waste. The fluid goes around and around and nothing is drawn off — but a body with nothing to feed keeps all it has, out of habit, or out of readiness. The charge is held, not spent. The temperature does not vary because a temperature that is being kept for something does not vary. There is no product because the product has not come.

The third candidate: what lives here is an expectation. The plant is not a refinery with nothing to refine. It is a bed that has been made. It is a hand held out, palm up, in the dark, at body heat, for as long as there have been hands, with the fingers not yet closed because nothing has been placed in them. It does not reproduce because reproducing is what a thing does when it has given up on being joined. It has not given up. Whether it knows what it is waiting for, whether there is a *knows* anywhere in it, is not answerable from the mat. But the readiness is real. The readiness is the most alive thing about it. Everything is contained, and containing is not the same as hoarding. A cup is contained. So is a womb.

Now come to the last thing, which the description also admits and does not look at.

*Hunger will eventually give the time.*

Someone is here. Someone walked into the first column and laid a hand on it and followed a conduit a hundred columns and tasted the drop that missed. Someone sat down on the mat. The description is entirely in the second person because it is a description of a place being entered, and a place being entered has, for the duration, a second occupant.

And this occupant is everything the plant is not. It eats. It has already begun to be hungry. It will die — the hunger is the clock, and there is no other clock in the lowland, and so the visitor is not only the second living thing here but the first duration. Before the boot came down, nothing in the place was earlier than anything else. Now there is a before and an after, and the after is getting shorter, and the plant, which has never had a *now*, has been given one, and does not know it, and is keeping the temperature for it all the same.

The visitor will sit until sitting is over. Then the body will be on the mat, warm because the mat is warm, wet because the air is wet, and it will be the first thing in the lowland that is not the plant and that stays. And it will do what bodies do, because bodies bring their own small tenants with them, in the gut and on the skin, and those tenants have learned to decay, and they will find themselves on the most hospitable ground that has ever existed, and they will surely take.

That is the fourth candidate and, in the end, the truest: what lives here is what is about to. The plant has been the whole inventory of the place for all of its duration, and it has been enough to keep the place running, and it has never been enough to make the place *inhabited*, because an inhabitant is something that could leave and something that could end. The plant cannot do either. The visitor can do both, and will, and in doing so will give the plant the only two things it lacks and could not make for itself: a death, and — following the death by a few hours, on the wet warm mat, in the dark — the first spore.

After that, the rate will no longer be the rate. Something will have gotten in. The ribs will begin, very slowly, to soften. The plant will learn what a visitor is by being visited, and it will not be able to tell whether it has been fed or infected, because in that lowland, for that body, there was never any difference.

Until then: one thing lives here, and it is the house, and it is holding, and it is warm.
