Start with the honest answer, because it is true and because it is small.

A fern lives here. One. It is in the corner where the channel turns, rooted in a crust of lime no thicker than a coin and a smear of silt that the reservoir sent down through a hairline crack before the crack had a number. It is the pale green of something grown under a bucket. Its fronds are not yet fronds but the coiled proposals of fronds, three of them, each the length of a fingernail, each uncurling toward the halogen lamp with the slow conviction of a plant that has never seen the sun and has decided this must be it. It does not know it is two thousand meters above the sea. It does not know it is inside a wall. Its spore came in on a boot tread or a shovel of aggregate or a breath of upvalley air on the one afternoon the hatch stood open, and it landed in the only square centimeter of this place that offered wet, and lime, and light, and it did what spores do. If someone comes for the lamp it will die in a week. If no one comes it will die anyway, in a year or two, of the light being the wrong color and the water being the wrong water, and it will leave a brown filament on the wall that the lime will slowly bury. It is not a tenant so much as a mistake that is taking a long time to be corrected.

The stain beside it lives too, in the way a stain lives. Algae, or the first ambition of moss, holding the wall where a leak keeps it slick, doubling its area over a month by a margin you would need a ruler to confirm. It has no idea where the lamp is and does not care. It is the more successful of the two because it wants less.

That is the inventory of green. It would be tidy to stop there.

---

But neither of them is what lives here, not really. They are here the way a coin is in a drain. To live in a place you must be shaped by it, and the fern is shaped by a lamp that could be anywhere, and the stain by a wetness that could be any wetness. Neither one knows it is in the dam. Neither one has been changed by the particular fact of this room — its exactness, its newness, its roar. They would be the same fern and the same stain in a cellar or a culvert or the north face of a garden wall.

So look again, and look at what has been shaped.

The water has. It comes through the upstream face as reservoir water — cold, blue-green, full of the dissolved mountain — and it arrives on this side as something else, having spent a week or a month finding the fractional gaps between one pour and the next, having been filtered through fifteen meters of cement paste until it is clearer than anything that falls from the sky. It has been made *this* water by *this* wall. It knows the wall the way a hand knows a glove. It has mapped every cold-joint and every cooling crack more thoroughly than any engineer with a torch will manage in the next decade, and it publishes its map continuously, in dark stripes down the grey, for anyone who wants to read it.

And yet it does not stay. It runs down the V-channel to the sump and the sump is pumped and the water goes on into the tailrace and is gone, and new water takes its place that has never seen the room. The water is not a resident. It is a procession.

What it leaves is closer. The white threads on the wall — the calcite, lifted out of the cement and set down again in the air — those stay. Those have been made here and will remain here, and they are the first thing in this place that will be older than the place is now. In fifty years they will be teeth. In two hundred they will have their own names in the inspection log, and someone will be measuring them the way someone now measures the seepage, and they will have become, in the slowest possible sense, the room's own body growing back into the space that was cut for it. If the question is what will live here, the answer is the stone, arriving one molecule at a time. But it is not alive yet. It is hardly even a presence. It is a promise the wall is making to itself.

---

The roar is a better candidate than any of these.

It is not in the room; the room is in it. It came up through the floor slab on the first day the hollow-jet valve was opened and it has not properly left since, only paused. It has a range. When the valve is cracked a quarter open it is a pressure behind the eyes and a tremor in the standpipe caps. At full discharge it is a solid, a second concrete poured into every corner of the chamber up to the ceiling, and you move through it as through something with weight. It has moods that follow the reservoir level and the whims of the control room. It is the only thing here that changes hour to hour in a way a human body can register without instruments.

And everything here has been shaped by it. The puddle at the base of the ladder has its permanent standing rings. The pendulum wire has its blur. The condensation on the conduit does not hang and swell and fall like ordinary drips; it is shaken loose early, small, so that the rain in this room is finer than it would be in a silent one. The fern shivers. Even the calcite must be forming a little differently, deposited on a surface that is never quite still, its crystals seeded by a vibration nothing above ground would call sound.

If a thing that lives is a thing that shapes its surroundings and is shaped by them, the roar lives here more than the fern does. But a roar has no inside. It cannot be startled. There is no seat in it from which the room is seen.

---

Which leaves the lamp.

Someone set it up. Someone aimed it at a patch of wall near the floor where a crack had begun to weep, and knelt, and looked. Someone was in the middle of an act of attention when the act stopped, and the lamp was left burning at the wall as if the attention had simply been set down and walked away from, the way you set down a cup.

Whoever that was has not gone.

Not far, anyway. The lamp's circle of warm light is small, and the fluorescent tube is weak, and between them they leave more than half the chamber in a grey that the eye cannot resolve, and that is where the keeper is. Standing, mostly. Sometimes crouched by the weir with two fingers in the flow. The keeper does not come into the light and it is not clear that the keeper would register as anything in particular if the keeper did — a figure in wet coveralls, hair plastered flat, skin the grey-white of the mortar plugs, face turned always a little downward and to one side, in the posture of someone listening hard for something under something else.

Because that is what the keeper does. The keeper hears inside the roar.

It is not possible; everyone knows it is not possible. At full discharge a shout dies in the mouth and the drips fall in silence and the ear gives up and the roar becomes a place instead of a sound. But the keeper has been in the place for long enough that the place has become a room again, and the roar has become a floor — a level from which other sounds can be measured, the way the weir measures the water from the bottom of the notch. The keeper can hear a new drip start. The keeper can hear, or feel, or has stopped distinguishing between the two, the pendulum wire change its note by the fraction that means a millimeter of lean at the crest. The keeper knows when the control room throttles the valve before the pitch has finished changing, the way you know the phone will ring. The keeper does not read the piezometer standpipes by lifting the caps. The keeper puts a palm flat on each one in turn and holds it there and then moves on, and the readings are correct.

There was a notebook. It is pulp now, a grey brick on the floor by the electrical boxes, its pages fused into a single sodden tablet with the ghost of ruled lines in it. Since the notebook the keeper has kept the log in the wall. Along the downstream side at shoulder height there is a row of scratches in the concrete, made with a form tie or a key, fine and regular and close together, and they go on further than the light does. They are the seepage, and the level in the sump, and the temperature at the weir, and the lean, taken at intervals no one specified, in a notation no one taught, for a reader who is not coming down.

The keeper has not eaten. That is the fact that breaks the account, or the fact that tells you what kind of account this is. There is nothing here to eat and the keeper has not eaten, and the keeper's temperature is the temperature of the wall, and condensation beads on the keeper's forearms and runs, and the hatch at the top of the ladder is bolted from above and has been since before the lamp was left. Whether the person who set up the lamp is the keeper, or whether the person went up and the room — which has a body's dampness and a body's gradients and no tenant to be a body for — made itself a keeper in that person's shape out of what it had, lime and silt and patient water and the memory of someone kneeling in the light, is a question the room does not resolve and the keeper does not seem to have asked. It is possible both are true. Seepage dissolves rock in one place and sets it down as stone in another; the roar comes up through the slab and becomes a wall. Things here are in the habit of being carried in as one substance and remaining as another.

The keeper does not go to the lamp. That is worth noticing. The fern goes to the lamp, and the stain to the wet, and the keeper goes to the roar — to the place under the ladder where the slab is thinnest and the vibration is clearest in the soles of the feet, where the sound is not loudest but most legible. The keeper stands there through the long open-valve hours with a hand resting on a rung, and the rung sings into the hand, and the face is turned down and to the side, and the mouth is very slightly open, and if there is an expression it is the expression of someone who has found the one station in the world from which everything can be heard.

---

And then the valve shuts.

The silence comes down like a dropped sheet of glass. The concrete ticks. The drips are suddenly loud, ridiculous, domestic. The tube hums. The weir makes its little tap-in-another-room sound. And the keeper, for the minutes or the hour that it lasts, is afraid.

You can see it. The hand comes off the rung. The keeper backs against the downstream wall, out of even the grey light, and stands very still with both palms flat on the concrete as if to keep hold of the last of the vibration as it leaves. In the roar the keeper is competent and placed; in the silence the keeper is exposed, and the room is plainly a room — small, wet, grey, exact — and the keeper is plainly whatever the keeper is, standing in it, with nothing under the drips to hold them up.

The fern, for its part, stops trembling and stands perfectly upright toward the lamp, and for once in this place the two of them look like what they are: one thing that has come here to find the light, and one thing that has come here to find the sound, neither of them native, both of them the first.

Far above, a needle moves. Someone reaches for a lever. The keeper's head lifts before the pitch begins.

Then the room fills again to the ceiling, and the wire blurs, and the fern shivers, and the hand goes back to the rung, and the keeper leans in to listen, and along the downstream wall, in the dark beyond the light, a new scratch is added to the row.
