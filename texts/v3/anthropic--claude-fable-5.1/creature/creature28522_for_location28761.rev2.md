What lives here is sorted by what it does with the roar. That is the only taxonomy the plain respects. The tenders tried others — flying and crawling, wet and dry, seeded and arrived — and each broke within a season, because a thing that flies at one node crawls at another, and everything is wet at the base and dry fifty paces out, and nothing was seeded but the nodes. But ask of any creature: does it hold the sound, or read it, or feel it, or hear it, or make it, or hide from it — and it will tell you where on the plain it lives and how, and roughly how long.

---

**Those the sound holds.**

The floaters first, because there are more of them than of anything else, and because they are the closest thing the plain has to a rain that lives.

They are not really animals in the way the tenders first assumed. Each one is a ring of cartilage no wider than a palm with a membrane stretched across it, and the membrane is the whole of the creature — gut, sense, skin, and drum. It is tuned. Not by the floater; by inheritance and by the field. The membrane sits at a natural pitch that is some harmonic of the chord, and a body that hums at the chord's harmonic does not fall through the chord. It rests. This is the entire trick of their existence, and it is why they have almost no other anatomy: pigment is weight, muscle is weight, a mouth is weight. They are translucent to the point that the dust bands they lie in are more visible than they are, and from below what you see is not the floaters but the way the tea-coloured light thickens where they are, a faint curdling in the stripe.

They feed on the lifted water and what rides in it — stone-dust drawn up from the fracturing ground, spores off the node leaves, a fine mist of whatever the columns exhale. Water lands on the membrane and is taken through it. They never touch anything solid after the first hour of life. The eggs are laid on the broad upper leaves of the node galleries — small, clear, adhering — and are lifted with the water in the thin visible threads that climb the columns, and hatch somewhere in the ascent, and the hatchling either finds its pitch or does not. Those that do not fall back as a glitter onto the shelves below. Those that do rise until the field stops them and lie there, a thousand to a stripe, turning with the sky's slow turn.

They mate by touching, rim to rim. Two drums at slightly different pitches beat against each other, and the beat is the courtship, and if it settles the membranes fuse briefly at the contact and something is exchanged, and they part. They live weeks. When they die the membrane loses its tension and the pitch goes and the field lets them go and they fall — slowly, spinning, the way a leaf falls but with nothing of the leaf's purpose — and land on the stone and dry within an hour into pale discs like the scales of some enormous fish. The grazers eat these. It is the only way the sky feeds the ground here, and it is a steady, glittering, unceasing tithe.

The tenders call the fallen ones *shed*. On a still morning at a node base you can hear it — you cannot hear anything, but you can see it — settling through the drip, a snow of spent drums.

When the chord drifts, the bands drift, and the floaters drift with them, the whole population sliding a few metres across the sky over a week without a single one of them doing anything. Some are left behind by a drift too fast for the population's spread of pitches, and those tumble out at the band edges, and that is where the ear-birds wait.

---

**Those that read it.**

The ear-birds do not hear the roar. This is the first thing to understand about them. The roar has taken the whole frequency range in which a body their size might hear, and something living in it for generations does not go deaf so much as go *around*. Their heads are all ear — two great cupped folds of skin and cartilage that meet over where a face should be, with the beak a small hard afterthought below — but the ears are not for the sound. They are for its shape.

Sound at this volume is not something you hear. It is something you are inside, like water. And water has currents, and pressures, and places where two currents meet and the surface stands up in a ridge. The ear-birds read that. They tilt a head and the two cups take the field at slightly different pressures, and the difference is a direction, and the direction is a gradient, and a gradient is a place where floaters tumble. They fly the plain the way a sailor with no chart reads the swell: this rise means a node to the west, this trough means the band is thinning, this sudden steepening means the chord has shifted and the hunting line has moved and everything will need relearning by nightfall.

They cannot hear each other. Whatever calls they had, the roar has them. So they speak in two other ways. Close, they click — a tick too high for the chord, felt as much as heard, a language of nearness only. At distance they use the field itself. A bird banks and its body casts a small shadow in the pressure, a flicker of cancellation that runs outward along the band, and another bird a kilometre away feels the flicker in its cups and knows a hunt has turned. The tenders have watched a flock of two hundred fold and drop as one on a spill of floaters with nothing passing between them that any instrument could catch. They are not silent. They are speaking in the roar's own material, and the roar carries it.

They roost in the nodes. This is the thing the tenders find hardest to look at without a certain feeling. The columns are hollow, and the interiors are made — galleries at the proportions of rooms, landings at the proportions of landings, doorways the height of a door. Nothing was told to move in. The ear-birds hang in them now by the hundreds, clustered on the ribbed inner walls of the great throat-like shafts, their cupped heads folded shut against the green dimness, and the floor of every landing is layered with their droppings, and the node takes the droppings up. It is the first traffic those corridors have carried. It goes in one direction, down and inward, and the node grows a fraction faster where it lands.

The young are born with their ears furled and cannot read the field at all. For the first weeks they cling. Then a parent carries one out into a band and lets go, and the field takes the fledgling the way it takes a floater — holds it, not falling — while the ears unfold and the young bird learns, suspended, to feel the shape of the thing that holds it. There is no other way to learn it. There is nowhere to practise. The plain teaches its own birds by carrying them.

An old ear-bird whose cups have stiffened cannot read anymore and does not know it is falling until it lands. Most land at the node bases, in the drip, where the low things are. A few — the tenders have seen this three or four times in as many years — fly instead into a null, and settle on the stone there, and sit, and do not move again. Whether they are seeking the quiet, or fleeing the field, or have simply lost the thread of it and stopped where the thread ran out, no one has an answer. But the pale things in the nulls do not touch them. They sit at a distance, ears up, and the tenders who have seen it say they appear to be listening to the bird.

---

**Those that feel it.**

Everything on the ground is deaf, and the tenders were a long time realising that this does not mean it is unhearing.

The chord comes up through the stone. Not as loudly as it comes through the air — the fresh rock is a poor conductor, still full of gas and unset fracture — but it comes, a floor of vibration under everything, and the ground animals took that floor and built on it.

The grazers are the largest of these. Broad and flat and many-legged, the biggest of them a metre across and a hand high, carapaced in overlapping plates the same dark green-tarnished grey as the stone, so that a herd at rest looks like a stretch of shelf that has decided to move. The carapace hums. You can see it — a shiver across the plates like wind on a pond — and the shiver is the animal's whole perception of the world. They do not see well; the light is bad and they have never needed to. They feel the ground's note through their feet and bellies and the carapace picks it up and rings with it, and every change in the ground's note is a fact: here the stone is thin over a pool, here it is thick, here the drip has softened it, here it is dry and hot and the note goes sharp and bright and *wrong*, and the grazer turns back.

This is why they never cross. It is not the heat, or not only. The dry stone between nodes sings a different song, and to a body that is a resonator tuned to the damp rain-shadow under the canopy, walking onto the dry is like a struck bell being carried into a room where the air is the wrong shape. They cannot bear it. So each node has its own grazers and has had for a hundred years, and the herds are already diverging — the ones at the northeastern nodes flatter and heavier, the ones toward the centre smaller and higher on their legs — and no grazer will ever meet its cousin unless the spans in the high dark come down to meet the ground, which they show no sign of doing.

They crop the moss, and the moss regrows behind them as fast as they crop it — faster, at nodes where the herd is large, which is one of the things the tenders mean when they say the nodes listen. They eat the shed. They breed slowly and their young are born already plated and already humming, and they graze in slow circuits around the column bases in the perpetual soft rain, and they have never once, that anyone has recorded, looked up.

The heron is a different kind of feeling. There is one at each node, or a few, standing in the black pools on legs that seem to have no joint, motionless for hours at a stretch with the water at its knees. The bill is soft — a long grey probe with no hardness to it at all, closer to a finger than a beak — and it feeds by touch, lowering the probe into the still water and holding it there and waiting for something to brush it. It never startles. The ground hums under it and the water carries the hum and the heron stands in both and reads, presumably, whatever is coming up through the pool from below.

It knows when to step out. This is the thing about the heron. Whatever is in the pools has never, as far as the tenders know, taken one — and the tenders have watched a heron stand in a pool for an afternoon and then, for no reason visible on the surface, lift one leg, and step onto the shelf, and fold itself down to wait, and a minute later the black water has bellied upward in a long slow swell and settled. The heron and the thing under it have some arrangement. Or the heron simply feels it coming and has the sense to move. Either way it is the only creature on the plain with a working relationship to the deepest resident, and it is not talking.

The climbers feel the columns. Small, quick, long-fingered, dark-furred and damp, moving up and down the root-curtains in lines of a dozen or more, so sure of the fibrous surface that they seem to run up it rather than climb. They live on the flowers — door-sized, waxy, faintly luminous in the pooled dimness at the bases — and in the last few years on the fruit, which the nodes have begun producing without anyone asking. The fruit is soft and pale and sweet, the tenders say, and the climbers eat it and the seeds pass through and are dropped in the galleries, and nothing grows from them, because that is not how nodes propagate. The node absorbs them. It is hard to say who is feeding whom.

The climbers are the first to have gone up. They nest in the galleries — they chew moss into pads and pack the pads into the smaller voids, the ones the right size for a cupboard or a bunk — and they have been seen, small and dark against the striped sky, crossing the fused spans hundreds of metres up, in lines, from one node to the next. The traffic those spans were growing to carry has started, and it is them. When the tenders speak of the climbers now there is something careful in how they do it. The climbers are furnishing the city. They are commuting in it. They did not wait to be told it was theirs.

And the low things, which feel the heat.

They are seen as motion, mostly — a length of something wet and dark sliding along the base of a crack at the deeper dusk, or the shed drifting up in a puff where nothing stepped. Long and low and warm to the touch, the one time a tender touched one, with skin like the inside of a cheek. They den in the cracks that sweat — the joints that give back the ground's warmth as vapour at dawn — and they hunt by heat and by the ground's note, feeling the grazer above them through the stone before they surface behind it. They make no sound because there is no sound to make. They take grazers, and climbers that come down to the pools at the wrong hour, and every ear-bird that falls. They are the plain's undertakers and they are patient, and slow, and the temperature of the stone.

---

**The one that hears nothing, and was here first.**

The pools connect. This took the tenders years. Each pool sits in its own fresh joint, black and still and warm, and looks like a closed thing, but the stone under the plain has not agreed on where water goes and the joints run into each other below, and a pool that was full at dusk is a hand lower at dawn with no rain and no sun to account for it.

Something moves through them. The ripples are large — a swell that starts nowhere and travels the length of a pool and stops at the far lip without breaking — and the ripples are all anyone has seen. The tenders call it the *under*, when they call it anything. It is warm, they think, because the pools it has passed through are warmer. It is single, they think, because the ripples are never in two pools at once, though the plain is the size of a province and no one is watching all of it. It does not respond to the chord — the chord drifts and the bands drift and the herds thicken and the under keeps its own time, surfacing and not surfacing in a rhythm that follows nothing above.

The tenders believe, most of them, that it is old. That whatever was here before — the sea floor, the marsh, the drowned delta, the accounts disagree — had something in it, and when the ground was lifted or replaced or *decided*, the something came with it, down in the warm dark under the new stone, and has been there since, in the black water, in the heat, not hearing. It is the only resident that predates the sound. The heron knows where it is. Nothing else does.

---

**Those that hear.**

In the exact point between four nodes the pressure cancels. Step a metre to either side and the roar is back, but stand on the point and it drops to something you could nearly speak through — a chord you could, for the first time, hear as a chord.

Something lives there. Small, pale, soft-furred, with ears that are half again the size of the body and seem to be listening even when the creature is asleep. They have not lost their hearing. They are the only things on the plain that have not, and it is because they cannot leave. Born in the null, fed on the shed that drifts in and the pale moss that grows in the pooled dim, they live and breed and die on a patch of stone a few paces across, and if one strays past the edge the roar takes it — the tenders have watched this — and it goes rigid and falls and is dead in minutes, from what, no one is sure. The pressure. The shock. Something in a hearing body that cannot survive the field.

There are as many nulls as there are cells in the grid, and each has its own population, and they are as isolated from each other as if they lived on separate moons. The lineages are already distinct. The tenders can tell a northern null-thing from a southern one by the set of the ears.

They sing. It is very thin — a high, wavering note, almost nothing — and they sing it in chorus, dozens of small pale bodies sitting up on the stone with their ears raised and their mouths open, and the tenders who have crouched in a null and heard it say it is a harmony. Not with each other. With the chord. They are the only creatures on the plain that hear the roar as music and they answer it as music, and the tenders have wondered — not in writing — whether the nodes hear the nulls best of all, because the null is where the field is quietest and the nodes are listening.

Sometimes a chord drift moves a null. The cancellation point slides a metre east over a night and the pale things go with it, all at once, in the dark, and in the morning the patch they lived on is under full roar and empty. Sometimes a null closes. The chord shifts so that the four nodes no longer cancel where they did, and the point simply is not there anymore, and the tenders find, weeks later, a patch of pale moss and a carpet of small bones.

And sometimes, the tenders say, a null opens where there was not one, and there are already pale things in it. They do not know how. They have stopped saying they do not know how.

---

**Those that make it.**

There is a way of counting in which the plain has a population of a few dozen thousand — the floaters alone, in a single stripe — and a way of counting in which it has a population of one.

The nodes were planted by people. Something the size of a cart pressed into the fresh stone at each survey mark, and a tone, and the columns came. They are fibrous, living, ridged like bark at the base and broadening into galleries that should not hold and do, and they are hollow, and they are growing, and they are the ones who sing. It is not clear whether each is an organism or each is an organ. The arms reaching between them along the antinodes — fused now at perhaps a third of the junctions, thickening — say the latter. When the last span closes the plain will be one body the size of a province, standing on a thousand legs, with the roar for a voice, and the question of what lives here will have a short answer.

What the nodes are doing, as far as anyone can tell, is learning. The chord was set by the planters at a pitch meant to shape stone and lift water and grow rooms for people. And it does. But the chord is not fixed; it was never fixed; it drifts, and the drift follows what has arrived. A new hunting line and the band moves to meet it. A herd booms and the moss thickens. The climbers begin using a gallery and the gallery widens. The fruit appeared in the third decade with no one asking, and it is the right size for a climber's hands, and sweet enough that they carry it, and nothing else on the plain eats it. The nodes are shaping the city around whoever showed up, and whoever showed up is shaping the song, and the two have been in that conversation for a hundred years without a single word from the people who started it.

The tenders came to build a city. They are, mostly, honest about this now. It is being built. It has a population — floaters in the sky, ear-birds in the rooms, climbers on the roads, grazers on the commons, low things in the sewers, the under in the deep, listeners in the quiet — and the buildings are learning their names.

---

**Those at the edge.**

Nine tenders. Sometimes eleven. They live in acoustic shelters at the plain's edge — thick-walled, half-buried, humming faintly themselves because nothing here does not hum — and they go in wearing plugs and come out shouting and have stopped noticing that they shout. They check things that cannot be checked from inside. They have given up measuring the walk between nodes. They have given up, most of them, the equations, which describe the field perfectly and describe nothing they recognise.

They have started to hum. Not on purpose. In the shelters, at night, someone will be writing and the sound will come up out of them, low, and it is the chord's fundamental, and when they notice they stop, and later they do it again. The ones who leave the plain for the old country say the silence there is not silence. It is a hole the shape of something that used to be there. Several have come back. One did not leave, exactly, but has taken to sitting in a null for whole days with the pale things, and returns to the shelter without saying what she heard, and the others have stopped asking.

A shelter's band moved last year. The dust stripe over the eastern shelter slid a few metres north, over a month, until the shelter sat under it, and the roar in the shelter went up by a fraction that everyone could feel. The nodes are kilometres away. They had never shifted a band toward the edge before. The tenders talked about it for a while and then stopped talking about it, and they did not move the shelter.

Beyond them, in the old country, within a day's walk of the edge, there are villages whose children were born into the pressure and shout without knowing it, and who are, in the only sense the plain understands, already its people — the outermost ring, the ones the field has reached but not yet held.

---

So: who lives here.

The floaters, who are held. The ear-birds, who read. The grazers and the heron and the climbers and the low things, who feel. The pale things, who hear. The under, who was here before hearing was. The tenders, who are learning to hum. And the nodes, who sing, and who are listening to all of it, and whose song is — slowly, over hours, over seasons, in fractions you feel in your teeth — becoming the sound of what lives here, rather than the sound of what was supposed to.

It was a city for people. The rooms are still the right size. The doors are still the height of a door. But the ear-birds are in the bedrooms and the climbers are on the bridges and the fruit is sweet and small and no one asked for it, and the chord is drifting, and it is not drifting toward you.
