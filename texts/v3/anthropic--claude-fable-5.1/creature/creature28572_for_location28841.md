What lives there is, first, exactly what you saw, and it would be a kind of dishonesty to start anywhere else.

The newts. Dark above, pale beneath, each one the length of a finger, each one lying with its throat on the water as though drinking and never actually drinking. If you lift one it is cold all the way through and heavier than it looks, dense as wet clay, and it does not struggle; it lies across your palm with its small hands open and waits to be put back, and when you put it back it is in the same posture within a breath, throat to the water, as if the interval in your hand had been edited out. The snails, grey as the silt, packed shell to shell on the underside of every frond, so that the roof of ferns over the pool is really a roof of snails with a little green showing through. The millipedes coiled in the moss like dropped cord. The crickets, waxen, feelers going, walking over everything with the unhurried care of creatures who have never once had to move fast. The beetles walking over the newts. The frogs that do not call. Under the water, the shrimp and the flatworms, transparent, and the long white thing beneath them, which turns over sometimes and shows you it has no eyes and does not need them. The bats above, a single hide of bats, breathing.

That is the census, and it is complete, and it has been complete for longer than anyone could count.

---

But you begin to notice that none of them do what their kind does.

A newt is a hunger with legs. It should be taking the beetles that walk across its back. A snail should be rasping at the fern, and the fern should show it. Bats should leave at dusk, but there is no dusk, and they do not leave. Frogs should call; crickets should sing; something, somewhere, should be eating something else, because that is what a place full of animals is — a slow fire, each body burning the next. There is no fire here. The beetles walk over the newts and the newts allow it, and the beetles are going nowhere and arrive nowhere, and this has been going on since before there were words for any of them.

So in what sense do they live? Not the way a newt lives on the plateau, where a newt is a brief bright argument against the cold. They live the way the stone lives. They persist. And once you see that, you cannot go back to seeing a hundred thousand animals. You see one thing, wearing a hundred thousand skins.

---

It has no name, because nothing has ever been down there to call it anything, and it does not need one for itself. But it is one thing. The pressure you felt under the drip — the breathing that is less a sound than a weight — is its one breath. The newts are its skin where the skin touches water; the snails are its skin where the skin touches leaf; the bats are its skin where it touches the dark. When you set your foot down and the moss slides aside without hurry and closes behind your heel, that is not a hundred small decisions. That is one body making room for a splinter and then healing around it.

It is not asleep. Sleep is something you do between the things you do. It has no between, because it has no things. It reached its shape, as the description says, and its shape is *full*. Its one appetite, if you can call it that, was for room, and it has had room enough for a very long time, and so the appetite is finished too. What is left is the state after appetite. You have felt this, briefly, on the far side of a meal or a grief. It has felt nothing else.

---

And yet there is the shelf.

Halfway down the eastern wall, a little proud of the seep, damp instead of wet. The flakes, black and glassy. The stone with one edge worked to a blade, lying where it was set down. The moss grown up to it on every side and not one hair's breadth over.

Everything else in the hollow is occupied. This one thing is kept. There is a difference, and the difference is the only interesting thing about the place, and you could sit beside that flint for days before you understood what you were looking at.

Someone came down. A person, warm, out of the wind, and sat on the shelf, and the crickets walked over their hands and the newts came and settled against their ribs. That is the one appetite left in the hollow: not food, not room. Warmth. Whatever drips from the bats is warm for an instant before it isn't. The newts against your side are taking what you have, gently, the way the pool takes a footprint, and they will let it go again, the way the pool lets a footprint go. The hollow makes no heat of its own. It borrows. It borrowed from the knapper for an afternoon, and the knapper struck flakes and made a blade and set it down and climbed back up into the cloud, and the hollow — which does not remember, because nothing has ever happened to it to remember — remembers this.

It is the only mark on it. A finished thing that has been touched once. And so it keeps the shelf dry and keeps the moss off the stone, not out of anything you would call intention, but the way a body keeps the shape of a hand that has pressed into it and then lifted — for a moment, before the flesh forgets. Here the moment has not ended. Here there is nothing to end it.

---

So there is something else that lives in the hollow, besides the one thing in its many skins. It lives on the shelf, in the space the moss will not close. It is the shape of a returning hand. It is not a ghost; nothing died. It is closer to a held breath, or the pause after a question. Call it a vacancy. It is the only vacancy in the place, and the hollow tends it the way the hollow tends everything, and cannot tell, having no way to tell, whether it is holding or waiting.

You could say that this is the one living thing down there, in the sense you would use the word on the plateau: the one thing that is *for* something that has not yet happened.

---

When you come down, you are taken in. That is worth knowing. You are not an intruder, because there is nothing there capable of feeling intruded upon; you are not a guest, because there is no one to receive you. You are a warm stone the census flows around. The crickets walk on you. The newts find your ribs. The moss takes your weight and the silt takes your foot. For as long as you stay, you are counted, and the count does not change, because it was never really a count of bodies.

And when you climb out, you are let go, without regret, and the shape you made in the silt goes back to flat over a day, and the newt that lay against you is throat-to-the-water again as if you had been edited out. Only the shelf keeps anything. And it does not keep you. It keeps the one who came before, who set the blade down and did not come back, and it will keep that one when you are dead, and it will keep that one when the plateau above it is gone, because it has already stopped, and a thing that has stopped cannot be interrupted.

What lives there. A body with a hundred thousand skins, breathing, wanting nothing, warm only where it borrows. And a small dry place inside it that is still, after all this time, the shape of a hand.
