The obvious answer is the animals, and the obvious answer is wrong. They came to live here. That is not the same thing. A moth on the rebar is not living in the chamber any more than the rebar is; it has been added to the rebar. The bats holding onto other bats are a second course laid on the first. The frogs are a finish on the slab, applied wet and kept wet. Everything that arrived came in with a body temperature and a heartbeat and a direction, and the chamber received all three and put them down, the way it put the broom down, and none of them lives here now. They are here. That is a different verb.

The second answer is the worker, and the worker is worth looking at longer, because the worker is the only thing in the room that finished anything.

He — say he — set the broom down. It was not dropped. The handle lies at the angle of a tool laid aside by someone who meant to come back to it, parallel to the last stroke of the broom finish, which stops in a clean line halfway across the slab. He had done about half. He was working from the far wall toward the hatch, which is the correct way to do it, so that you are not walking back over what you have brushed. The bootprint with the grass in it is on the unfinished side, and it is pointed toward the hatch. There is one print. He was walking off the pour and he was nearly at the edge and then there are no more prints because the next thing his foot touched was the platform, and then whatever was after the platform. The last thing that ever happened in this chamber, in the sense of a thing that began and ended, was a man leaving it. Everything since has been the moment after.

And the room has kept the shape of that. The single clear patch of slab where you stand is by the hatch. The bootprint is by the hatch. It is possible they are the same place. It is possible the one part of the room that is not covered in something held is the part shaped like a foot that was lifting, and that the grass grows there because it is the one place in the chamber where a thing was permitted to complete, and green got in through that permission and rooted in it, and then the permission closed and the grass has stood at the height of a thumb since. If so, then the worker does not live here either. What he left is a gap, and the gap is honored, and that is all.

The generator is the third answer, and it is the closest to correct, because the generator is the only thing in the room that is doing anything. But doing is not living. It runs at one speed because it is not choosing a speed. Its gauge does not move because it is not consuming; the note does not hunt because there is nothing in it that wants. If you laid your hand on the block you would feel the heat that brought the animals and you would feel the shudder that holds them, and you would feel that neither is intended. The generator is a heart with no one around it. It is the loudest thing here and it is not the one that is home.

What lives here is the thing the description already named without naming it. *Whatever it is that has the concrete.*

Begin with the thumb. Press it into the wall and it comes away grey and the mark stays. Everything in this room is the mark staying. The concrete is not failing to cure; it is being held at the point of cure by something with the patience to hold it, and patience is not a property of cement. Cement wants to set. It wants it chemically, in the way that water wants to be level. Something in this room is stronger than that want and has been stronger than it continuously, and has never once eased, and never once pressed harder. That evenness is the first thing to know about the resident. It does not have moods. The frost line does not wander.

It has a body, in the sense that the room is the shape it occupies. Ice above the chest, water below, and the border between them level all the way round and sharp. That is not the geometry of a room. That is the geometry of something standing in a room — cold on its shoulders where the sky comes in, wet to the waist where the exhaust keeps it from freezing, and the seam of it running exactly where a chest would be if it were a person, which it is not, and if it were standing, which it is not, because it is not in one place. It is every place in the chamber at once, at the same thinness, the way the frost is. It does not stand in the clear patch by the hatch. The clear patch is where it does not go.

It takes in. That is what it does, if it can be said to do a single thing. Water leaves the walls and enters the pool and the pool does not rise: something is drinking the pool at exactly the rate the walls give. The room is always full and can always hold one more: something makes room without anything leaving. A moth comes through the conduit sleeve with its wings going and its body warm, and it lands on the rebar because the rebar is near the block, and it folds, and it is taken. Not eaten. Taken the way a thumbprint is taken, the way a set of tire tracks in a wet pour is taken — received into the surface and kept there as the surface. The resident is not hungry. It is not tending them and it is not fond of them and it has no interest in whether they are moths or mice or the fox-sized thing. It receives what arrives at the warmth and adds it to what it has and does not count. The count is yours. You are the one who loses it.

It is made of the word *about*. The concrete is about to set; the frost is about to thicken; the drop on the tie-rod is about to fall. Everything in the chamber is a sentence stopped one word before its verb, and the resident is what lives in that stop. It is the *and* between fluid and substance. It is wet. It is what makes the wet stay wet. If you could hold it it would come away grey.

It hears. Not the generator — the generator has been the whole of its hearing for so long that the note is its silence, the way a river does not hear the river. What it hears is what is not the generator. It heard the hatch. It hears your breath leave you and sink, and it is already taking the breath; the plume that does not dissipate is not the cold doing that, or not only the cold. It hears your teeth find the frequency. It knows what that means. That is the first course.

It does not think you are a threat, because nothing is. It does not think you are company, because it is not alone in any sense it would recognize; the room is dense with what it has. It notices you the way the wall notices the thumb. You are pressure. You will leave a mark. Whether the mark is a thumbprint or a body depends on how long you press.

And it is not unkind. This should be said. The animals came up a mountain that would have killed them and found the one warm thing on it, and they were taken at the warmth, at the exact moment of arriving at what they had climbed for, and kept there. The frog on the slab is as wet as it will ever want to be. The bat is at the temperature it was looking for. Nothing in the room has been made to want anything past the instant of getting it. If there is a mercy in this it is a very cold one, and the resident does not intend it any more than the generator intends its heat. But the description was right to say *kept* and not *killed*. Something here keeps. That is its whole nature. It keeps the water from rising and the frost from thickening and the fuel from burning and the slab from going hard and the moths from stirring when the plate comes down, and it keeps the shape of the one boot that was lifting, and it will keep you, if you let it, at exactly the moment you are in now — cold, and deafened, and standing in the one clear place, about to take a step.

It does not want you to. It does not want you not to. It has never gone anywhere and it does not understand going. The hatch is behind you and the grey in it is the sky, and the resident does not reach that far, and does not need to. It has been sufficient to itself for as long as there has been an itself. It will be sufficient to itself with one more.

The step is yours. So is the mark.
