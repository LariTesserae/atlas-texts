It is not one.

The thing that settled against your ankle and then, without leaving, was gone — there are others like it, and it takes the whole length of your standing there to understand how many, because they do not gather and they do not scatter. They are placed around the pool the way the crystals are placed around the room: wherever they happened to arrive, and then no further. Nine, perhaps. Eleven. You will never count them, because counting requires that things hold still in a way you can check, and they hold still in a way you cannot. One is under the ridge of the shell-smooth bottom near the far side. One is pressed into the seam where the basin meets the wall. One is on the surface, or was, its back just breaking the meniscus, so that the water bends around it without ever quite closing over. If you went looking for them you would not find them. If you stood long enough they would find you, one at a time, at intervals nearer the drop's than your own.

Take one in your hand.

You can, because it does not resist. It weighs almost nothing — the weight of a wet leaf, if a leaf could be warm. It is the length of your thumb and about as thick at the shoulders, tapering. The skin is so thin it barely qualifies as a surface; your fingertips feel the give of muscle under it, and the small hard line of a spine, and under the throat a slow pulse that is not so much a beat as a change of pressure, one, and then after a while another. It has four limbs, short, ending in fingers finer than the root's finest threads, and it has a tail longer than its body, flattened, the tail of something that swims but does not need to swim quickly. On either side of the head, behind where a jaw would hinge, there is a soft feathered mass — not fur, something wetter and more ordered, plumes that lie back along the neck and lift, very slightly, when you move your hand and the water moves with it. These are how it breathes. They are the reddest thing on it, if you could see red. You cannot, and neither can it, and there is no red here to be seen.

It has no eyes. Not closed eyes, not eyes gone white: no eyes. The skull is smooth from the nostrils back, and where sight would have been there is skin, and under the skin nothing has waited. Whatever ancestor came into this room came in seeing and then, over a span you cannot measure because the room will not let you, stopped paying for what it could not use. Nothing was lost. The face simply became a face for this place.

What it has instead is the whole of its skin.

Understand that the pool is a single body of information, and this animal is submerged in it, and everything that happens in the room happens to the water first. The drop that falls — the one you waited for, that you were not sure you had heard — arrives in the pool as a shock, a bright spreading pressure, and every one of these creatures knows it at the same instant along its entire length, and knows which direction it came from, and knows how far. For them the drop is not faint. It is the loudest thing that ever happens. Between drops, they read smaller things: the leak of dissolved stone from the walls, a ridge of fungus letting go of a filament, the microscopic shift of temperature where the pool's edge meets the crust. When you stepped in, the water told them a body had entered it, and how large, and — this above all — how warm. You are the warmest thing that has been in the pool in the lifetime of every animal in it. Less than a degree. It will have registered as an event.

That is what the placement on your ankle was. Not curiosity; the room has never produced enough novelty for curiosity to be worth having. Not fear; fear is a response to speed, and there is no speed here to respond to, so it was never made. The animal moved toward the warmth because warmth is where things dissolve into the water — where food is more likely to be, where the fungus loosens — and it put itself against you and waited to see what you would give off. You gave off nothing it could use. Eventually, at the pace of a thought it did not have, it stopped attending, and its body drifted from yours because nothing was holding it there.

It does not eat often. Days are the wrong unit; so are seasons, which do not reach it. It eats when the fungus at the root's edge sheds, or when a thread of the root itself, having done its work, sloughs into the water and comes apart. It eats the film that grows on the ridged bottom, a film so thin you would need the lamp to see it and the lamp would not be enough. It eats, when there is nothing else, the cast skin of its neighbors, which they let go of once in a very long while, whole, like a shape of themselves left standing in the water. Between meals its heart slows past slow. It settles into a hollow and its plumes lie flat and it becomes, for all that the water can tell, a soft irregularity on the stone. It can hold this for longer than you would believe. Longer than you have been alive.

They are old. Not as a species — as bodies. The one in your hand may have been in this pool when the lamp burned, or may be that one's child, and you have no way to know which and there is no one who does. They lay eggs rarely, a few, in the seam under the far ridge, and most of the eggs do not become anything, and the ones that do take years to be sure of it. A new one is born about as often as, outside, a language changes. There are never many because there is never much. The root sends down what daylight it can spare; the fungus takes what the root gives up; the animals take what the fungus lets go; and at the end of that thread of sky there is only enough for a dozen small lives, kept just above still.

Here is the one event.

Their ancestors were in the pool when the people came. The light meant nothing to them — the skulls were already closed. But the water told them everything else. A body, two bodies, sitting on the shelf: heavy, hot, breathing. The air over the pool filled with something that had never been in it, smoke, and the smoke came down onto the surface and the water took it in, and for the length of the lamp's burning the whole pool tasted of fat. Something hard was set down on the floor and never picked up. Something was pressed into the wall. Perhaps a hand went into the pool, as yours did, and perhaps it closed around one of them, as yours did, and lifted it into a light it could not see, and looked, and put it back. That is possible. If it happened it was the strangest thing that ever happened to anything in this room, and it left no mark on the animal, because the animal had nothing to mark it with. Then the bodies left. The taste of fat thinned and went. The water settled. It has been settled since.

They do not remember this. They cannot. But something of the population's shape may hold it — a slight preference for the near side of the pool, away from the shelf, that no one chose and no one keeps.

There are other things living here, if you are generous with the word.

The fungus is one. Whitish, a fur, a mat — a single colony that has crept out along the wet of the root's threads and stopped exactly where the wet stops, and has been the same size for as long as the root has been the same size. It is alive the way the room is alive: it grew, and it reached the extent that this much water and this much sugar could support, and now it does not grow. It replaces itself in place. If you pulled a patch of it away the patch would return, slowly, and be the same patch.

The root is another, but it is hard to say the root lives *here*. The living plant is above, on the cold skin of the mountain, and what hangs into this room is its long blind reach, a wick. It drinks. It breathes out a little sugar through its finest threads, which is what the fungus wants. It has never in its life been dry. It does not know there is a room; it knows there is warmth and wet in this direction and it went that way as far as it could, and this is as far as it could.

The stone lives, or was living, or lives at a rate that makes the word useless. It grew out of the water into needles and curtains and coral and then the water had no more to give and the growing became a kind of standing. If you could watch it for ten thousand years you might see a facet thicken. You might not.

And the drop. It is not alive. But it is the only thing in the room that arrives, and everything alive here is oriented toward it — the animals turn their lengths to its shock, the fungus is thickest where the runoff that feeds the drop begins, the pool holds its level by the drop's slow addition and the air's slow taking-back. If the room has a heartbeat it is that. Unfixable, unhurried, exact.

Put the animal back.

It goes down through the water without swimming, lets the pool take it, comes to rest on the ridged bottom and is at once indistinguishable from the bottom. Its plumes lie back. Its pulse — you can no longer feel it, but it is there — goes on lengthening toward the interval it prefers. It has been touched by the warmest thing in its life and it is already returning to the temperature of the room, and so are you.

What lives here, finally, is something that has made peace with never being surprised. Not resignation; there was never anything to resign. It is a body shaped entirely by the absence of urgency, an animal in which nothing was ever selected for speed or fear or sight or wanting, and what is left when you take all those away is not a lesser animal but a stranger one — soft, sealed, attentive along every inch of itself to a world that consists of warm water and the taste of dissolved stone and, at long intervals, a single drop falling from a ceiling it will never know is there. It is the only thing in the room that moves by its own account, and it moves so little that the room does not have to notice. It matches the place. It *is* the place, in the one form the place has allowed to be alive.

When you go back up the fissure toward the cold and the grey and the wind, the pool will hold the small hollow of your legs for a moment and then not. The animals will have known a warmth come and go. It will not have been a thing that happened to them. It will have been a thing the water said, and then stopped saying.
