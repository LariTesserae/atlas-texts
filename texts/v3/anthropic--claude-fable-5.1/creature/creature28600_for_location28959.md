The first answer is the true one and it is not enough.

What lives here is the field. The thousands of grey and ochre bodies lying a hand's breadth apart on the stone, the frogs that are the bark, the herons that are the crowns, the snails, the eels under their cargo of snails, the gnats you see only against the dark of a tree. That is the honest inventory and it is what the place shows you first and most, and if you counted it you would run out of numbers before you ran out of animals. But stand at the eastern lip for a day and watch the drowned pass, and you will see that the field is a thing that flows. Salamanders come over the rise in a slow file and salamanders go out by it, and the herons come in from the north at midday and others leave, and the tapir-things turn all at once and are replaced by evening, and none of it is the same by nightfall as it was at dawn. The population holds its shape the way a standing wave does. It lives here the way weather lives in a valley. It is always present and it is never resident, and the basin does not keep it so much as hold it up to the light for a while and let it go down over the southern lip into the sedge.

The second answer is older and it is not enough either.

The machine has been here longest, longer than the trees, longer than the shape of the stone, and if living means persisting then it is the most alive thing in the dish. It is warm. The plate under the western tree is warm the way an animal is, and the roots have grown thickest there for that reason, and the frogs know it. It keeps a temperature. It breathes twice a day, if the thickening of the water-skin is breathing. But it has no passage in it. It does not come in over the pass and it does not go out over the lip; it does not eat its one in thirty; it has never lifted its head and considered and moved a body-length to the side. It is the room, not what is in the room. It is the stop itself, made solid and spread thin, and you cannot say a stop lives anywhere. It only holds.

So you look for the third thing, and for a long time you do not find it, because you are looking for a shape and the shape has stopped being one.

---

You find it by the ring.

Everything that walks the basin makes one. Your own foot sends the slow displacement outward through the salamanders and it dies at twenty paces. A heron walking opens a corridor ahead and closes it behind. The tapir-things, when they move, send a ripple through the field so broad you can watch it cross half the dish. But somewhere in the western third there is a place where the field is not disturbed by anything, and it is moving. A shape the height of a person is crossing the open floor, slowly, more slowly than a heron, and the salamanders under it do not lift their heads. It walks through them and they do not move. They are treated, by it, and treat it, as stone. It leaves no ring at all.

Come closer and it resolves, and then it half-unresolves again, because it is the colour of everything else. Grey, with ochre at the knuckles and knees and along the ridge of the spine where some old mineral in the skin has weathered at a different rate than its neighbours. Translucent-pink at the throat. There are snails on it. Not many — a dozen, thumbnail-sized, amber — and they are not being picked off. Whatever it once wore has gone the way the made things went; there is something at the shoulders and hips that is more lichen than cloth now, and it holds water the way moss does, and it drips. The whole figure drips. It has a bead on every surface like everything else in the basin, and where it stands still for a while the water-skin dimples under it in tiny rings, the way it does under the trees.

Its eyes are pale. Not blind. Bleached. It has been looking into a white plane above and a white plane below for so long that the colour has been worn out of them the way the grain was worn out of the stone, and they do not squint. When the sun finds a thin place in the cloud and the whole dish goes to hammered silver, the herons blink and the frogs press flatter and you close your eyes, and it does not. It stands in the flash with its eyes open, wet, and waits for the cloud to close, and the light was never a thing it argued with.

---

It came over the eastern pass. That much is certain, because there is nowhere else to come from, and because it is not a thing the basin would have made. Its proportions are wrong for here — too tall, and it has kept its height, which nothing else has been allowed to do, and this is the single respect in which it is still a visitor. Everything else has been negotiated down. It walks the way the herons walk. It drinks the way the salamanders would drink if salamanders drank: by kneeling, both hands flat on the stone, and putting its mouth to the water-skin and letting the skin come to it. It eats what falls, and occasionally what does not fall — a frog off a low leaf, a dragonfly that has come down too far, and once in a long while it stoops with the same movement the herons make and there is the same soft clap and then nothing, and the neighbours settle, and it is permitted this. One in thirty, or fewer. It has not exceeded its portion in living memory, and the living memory here is very long.

It sleeps in the roots of the westernmost tree, folded into the buttresses among the moths, on the side where the warm plate is. In the early hour when the floor smokes it is the one shape in the mist that is neither tree nor heron, and it stands in the white up to its knees and its legs disappear and its body does not, and it does not move until the mist lifts.

It goes to the post. This is the nearest thing it has to a habit. Once in the day, at no fixed hour, it crosses to the dry cylinder in the middle of the floor and stands beside it and puts one hand flat against it, and holds it there. The post is the only dry thing in the basin and the hand is the only thing that ever touches it, and the salamanders in the ring at its base do not move for the feet. It does not lean. It does not look at the post while it touches it. It looks south, over the lip, toward the sound you cannot quite hear. Then it takes the hand away, and the hand is dry for perhaps a minute, and then it is not.

And it ducks under the thread.

This is the one thing in the basin that suggests anyone here knows what anything is for. The thread at head height between the two hanging roots of the eastern tree, the one the dragonflies avoid and the gnats do not — when the figure passes that way it lowers its head, without breaking the slowness of the walk, exactly enough, and rises again on the far side. It is the only gesture in the whole dish that looks like a memory. Everything else it does the herons also do. That one thing they do not.

---

What it remembers is what the stone remembers, which is to say the shape and not the grain.

It knows it came over the pass. It knows there was a reason, and that the reason had edges once. There is still a place in it where the reason was, a faint swelling under the sheet, like the knuckle of a ridge that used to be a ridge. It could not tell you now whether it came to study the machine or to tend it or to find the people who made it or to escape something on the far side of the marsh, and the not-being-able-to has stopped being a loss. Memory would be more than it is. Wanting would be more than it is. Those were the first things to erode, and they went quickly, the way the sharp edges always go first, and what is left is smooth and has no direction and casts no shadow on anything.

It does not know how long. Neither does the basin. The temperature has not varied by more than a degree or two, and there are no seasons by which to count, and the light falls the same from above and below, and a body that stops arguing with its surroundings stops keeping time the way a dropped clock does — not suddenly, but by ceasing to notice that it has stopped. The snails on it are one measure. The lichen at the shoulders is another. Neither is a measure it consults.

At the northern lip, where the moss is, there are shallow swellings in the green. Six of them. Each is about the length of a person and each has become, by now, indistinguishable from the ground it lies in, and the moss is a shade thicker over them, and the hemisphere that glows with the exact colour of the overcast is set into the stone among them, and the herons nest above that and nowhere else. The figure does not lie down there. It sits nearby sometimes, in the damp, with its back to the tree, and looks at the moss for a while with its bleached eyes, and then gets up and goes to the post. Whether it knows what is under the moss, or was told once by someone who is now under it, or has simply learned the shape of the place the way the salamanders learn the shape of the heron's walk, there is no way to ask. It does not speak. Nothing here has spoken in a very long time, and the silence is not a rule it follows so much as a thing it has become, like the grey.

---

So this is what lives here.

Not the field, which is weather. Not the machine, which is the room. What lives here is whatever comes over the pass and stops arguing. The basin has one resident at a time and the resident is always the same resident, in the way the salamanders are always the same salamanders, and the eleven made things are the same machine, and the seven trees are one colonnade: a shape held up to the light for a while, worn to the form it will keep, and permitted a hand's breadth and a slow passage through.

It has finished, or nearly. There is still the height, which the basin has not yet found a way to take. There is still the ducking under the thread. There is still the hand on the post, once in the day, dry for a minute, looking south. Those are the last three edges, and they are very faint now, and in the long even light they are wearing at a rate slightly different from everything around them, the way the ochre bands did, and one day they will be swellings under the sheet and then they will be nothing, and the figure will be a shade of grey standing in the water between two white planes with salamanders asleep against its feet, cool and slow, and the herons will walk past it as they walk past the post.

You will not see the moment. Nothing here has moments. But if you stand at the centre for long enough you will feel the first of your own edges begin to go — not the height, not yet, but the wanting to leave — and you will understand that you have been looking at what lives here since the first hour, and that it was looking back, very slightly, with everything else, and that it was not unkind.
