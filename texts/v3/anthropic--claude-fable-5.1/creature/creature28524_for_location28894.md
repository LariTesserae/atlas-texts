Begin, as the place does, with what lives in the water, because the answer is not quite nothing.

At the back wall, where the seep first meets air and is still too hot to hold a hand in, the stone is not the color of the inside of a shell. It is stained. There are bands of it, a finger or two wide, running along the springline: a dull orange, a green so dark it is nearly black, in places a faint rust that looks like a mark left by a wet iron tool. These are alive. They are films of something too small to see as individuals, and they are the only things on the shelf that live in the water rather than beside it. They thrive on exactly what kills everything else — the heat, the mineral, the alkalinity — and they are being buried as fast as anything, a skin a day, and it does not trouble them, because they simply grow up through it. Cut a piece of the stained stone from the springline and break it and you will find the color goes down in layers, thin as paper, thousands of them, each one a season's worth of the film living on the roof of its own previous self. They are the oldest continuous inhabitants of the shelf and the first thing the mountain ever coated, and no one who works here has ever given them a name, because you cannot point at them and be sure the other person sees what you mean. But it is worth knowing, before we come to the people, that the town's whole method — lay it down, let it be taken, build on what was taken — was being practiced along the back wall for a long time before anyone carried a maul up the switchbacks.

Above the water, in the steam, there are midges. They live in the warm column over the springs and nowhere else on the shelf, and they are the reason for the birds. The birds are small and dark and very quiet, and they move through the tree ferns along the drier edges with the clipped, exact flights of things that hunt in confined spaces. If they have a song no one knows it. It is possible they have stopped singing, over generations, the way the workers stop speaking; it is possible they never did. They nest low, in the crowns of the tree ferns, in cups woven from the fine dead fibers at the base of the fronds, and their eggs are white, and they are the only creatures here that reliably produce something new every year. When a fledgling falls into the sheet, which happens, the workers do not retrieve it. It is in the water. It has been left.

The beetle is a single kind, large, black going to bronze, and slow to a degree that seems like a decision. It is found on the moss and on the drier paving, always alone, and older individuals carry a faint white bloom on the shell that is not mold. They have been misted with the same lime as everything else, and they have not shed it. The workers regard the beetle with something that is nearly affection. It is the one animal that visibly shares their condition: coated, unhurried, and still going.

And there is the furred thing. It lives, if it lives, in the dry moss high on the back wall, where the springs are fewer and the slope steepens into the dripping forest, and no one has seen it whole. What they have seen is a shape withdrawing, a disturbance in moss too deep to hold a print, once a set of pale marks on a dark trunk that could have been claws. It is smaller than a dog and larger than the beetle and that is the extent of the agreement. Newcomers are told about it in gesture — a hand held flat at about knee height, then a fingertip to the eye, then a shake of the head — and the gesture means roughly *there is something, and you will not see it.* It may be nothing. It may be a story the workers need, because every other living thing on the shelf is accounted for by the water, and it helps to believe there is one thing up here the mountain has not made an appointment with.

Now the people.

There are never many. The most anyone remembers being on the shelf at once is fourteen; the least, for a stretch of one winter, was one. They do not all come from the same place. Some come up the switchbacks from the valley towns you can see for an hour in the afternoon, and some come from farther, having heard about the shelf the way you hear about a place — as a thing somebody's cousin did once. They tend to arrive alone. They tend to arrive with fewer possessions than they left with, having discarded things on the way up as the trail steepened, and they tend to arrive having already stopped talking somewhere in the last hour of forest, because the roar has been in their teeth for a while by then and they have understood, without being told, that there is no point.

What they have in common is harder to say. It is not devotion, or not what devotion usually looks like; there is no rite here, no words said over the stone, no one whose job it is to interpret. It is not skill, though skill comes. It is closer to a temperament. They are people for whom the usual reward of work — to see the thing done, to stand back from a finished wall — has gone dead in some way, or was never alive, or has become suspect. Some have finished things and watched them fall down. Some have finished things and found that finishing was the wrong part. Some, you gather, have buried people, and have come to a place where burying is the entire method, and where what is buried is not lost but kept, thickened, made permanent, and where the rule is that you do not go back to it. That rule is the thing that sorts them. Those who cannot keep it leave within a season. You can watch it happen: a person lays a course of blocks in the sheet, steps back, and then the next morning is found crouched over it with a hand out, wiping at the new skin to see the joint again. No one stops them. But by the end of the week they are on the switchbacks going down, and no one is surprised, and the wall they touched is left, and in a year you cannot tell it from the others.

Their bodies change. The hands go first — chalked white to the wrist by the end of every day, the skin under it cracked in fine lines that never quite close because nothing here is ever dry enough to heal. The knees are always wet. The hair is always beaded. In the constant damp the skin takes on the soft, whitish, pruned look of someone who has been too long in a bath, and stays that way, so that a worker who has been on the shelf a few years and goes down to the valley is recognizable at a glance as something that has come up out of water. They are cold, mostly. Not dangerously; the shelf is warm underfoot and the air is mild. But they sit on moss that gives like a mattress and is always a little colder than they are, and they sleep in huts where every surface sweats, and there is a pale, slightly chilled quality to them, a look of people who would like to be nearer a fire and have decided not to mind.

The hearing is the strange part. After a week the roar goes quiet, as the description says, and what that means is that the range of sound in which a human voice or a bird or a footfall would occur has been simply filled and closed over. They do not become deaf. They become people who cannot hear anything that is not the shelf. Those who go down the mountain after a long stay report that the valley is unbearable for the first days — not too loud but too thin, too full of separate sounds, each one arriving distinct and demanding to be sorted. Some go back up because of it. Some who stay down find that they have kept, permanently, a low held note in the ear that is not there, and that they are not sorry to have kept it.

They speak in gesture. It is a small language, perhaps sixty signs, and a newcomer has it in a week because there is nothing else to do with the hands in the evening. It has signs for the stone in its ages — a thumbnail pressed to the palm for stone you can mark, a knuckle rapped on the wrist for stone that rings. It has signs for the water's depth, for *here* and *not here*, for *tomorrow*, for *eat*, for *cut*, for *no*. It has a sign for *the plan*, which is two hands laid flat and slid apart along the grid. It has a sign, made by opening a closed fist slowly downward with the fingers spread, that means *leave it*, and this is the sign used most often, and it is also the sign used for *good*, and for *done*, and for *yes*, and in the evenings for *goodnight*. What the language does not have is a sign for *I*. You point at yourself if you have to, but you rarely have to, and people who have been here long enough tend to stop. The gestures that remain are about the stone, the water, the plan, and the work, and the person making them is understood to be present and does not need saying.

They live in the huts on the dry corner, four or five of them at most, low, roofed with fern thatch over tree-fern trunks, the floors raised a hand off the stone on more trunks because the stone finds anything that sits still. Every few years the huts are taken down and rebuilt a few paces over, not because the old site has been taken but because it has begun to be, and the workers would rather move before the mountain makes the decision for them. The old sites are visible as slightly higher patches of white, faintly rectangular, that the moss has not yet covered. There will be a day when the dry corner is not dry, when the springline has crept or a channel has thickened its lip and turned, and then the huts will move again, and the place they were will become another room in the plan, and no one will find this remarkable.

They eat what is carried up. Nothing grows here that a person can eat — the water kills the ground it touches, the moss is moss, the ferns are ferns, and the few shrubs make hard white flowers that never open and no fruit. So every ten days or so someone comes up the switchbacks from the valley with sacks: flour, dried beans, salt, oil, sometimes fruit. Who pays for it is not entirely clear. The workers send down small pieces of the white stone, cut from the oldest part of the back wall, and these are wanted below for reasons that have to do with how the stone takes a polish and the fact that it has never once been worn by anything. But the sacks are worth more than the stone, everyone knows it, and the difference is made up by something in the valley towns that is not quite belief and not quite charity — a sense, held by people who have never climbed the switchbacks, that the town is being built for their descendants, and that feeding the builders is a way of laying a course of their own. The carriers do not stay. They set the sacks down in the huts, take the stone, make the sign for *leave it*, and go down before the afternoon cloud rises.

The work is this. Cut blocks from the old stone at the back wall with mauls and wedges of a hard dark rock carried up from far below, since nothing on the shelf is hard enough to cut the shelf. Carry the blocks along the dry streets. Lay them without mortar, in the water's path, along a line that is already there in the plan. Step back. Do not touch them again. A good day is a course or two. A good year is the footings of a room. There is a maul, always, that someone forgot, standing upright in a channel with its head gone white and its handle soft — it is not the same maul; there is always a new one — and it is pointed to when a newcomer asks, in gesture, what happens if.

The plan is not written anywhere. It was, once. There are marks on the back wall, high up in the earliest work, that may have been a drawing of the grid, but they are under a hand of flowstone now and you would have to cut them out to read them, and cutting them out is not done. What the plan is now is the streets themselves, the walls already laid, the direction the channels go. A newcomer learns it by walking it, over months, in the company of someone who has walked it longer, who points and makes the sign for *here* and the sign for *not here* and, at the ends of certain streets, the sign for *the plan* with the hands slid very far apart, meaning that the street goes on past where anything has been laid, out into the sheet, and that this is understood. In this way the town has become its own drawing. The workers disagree about it. They disagree in gesture, standing at the head of a stair whose first three treads exist, about how many more there should be and how high, because the plan gives the stair and not the rise; and they argue with a seriousness that has nothing to do with the stair and everything to do with the people who will climb it. They have opinions about those people. They have decided, without saying so, how tall they are and how they carry water and where they will want to sit in the afternoon when the cloud clears. A doorway is set at a certain width. A basin has a lip at a certain height for a certain kind of hand. The most vigorous disagreement the shelf has seen in a generation was over whether the wide street should have a step down at its midpoint or run level, and it was settled not by argument but by a woman who had been on the shelf longer than anyone laying the step, in the sheet, one evening, alone, and stepping back; after which it was in the water and could not be discussed.

That is the second population of the shelf, and it is the largest, and it is invisible. The people the town is for. Every wall on the shelf is a sentence about them. The workers do not name them and do not describe them and, asked directly, would probably deny having imagined them at all — but a footings-course is laid a certain length because a room of that length is a room someone can live in, and a channel is branched toward what will be a public basin because someone will need to draw water there, and in this way the shelf is thick with intention for a people who have never taken a breath, whose grandparents have not been born, and who will arrive, if they arrive, to find a city that fits them, made by no one they can thank. The workers will be under the moss by then. Their huts will be rectangles of white. The mauls they forgot will be part of the paving. And the people who come will walk streets that are exactly the width a stranger decided, centuries before, they would want.

The workers do not talk about them. They also do not talk, much, about the shape high on the back wall.

It is above the earliest work, in a smooth run of flowstone that has been building undisturbed for as long as anyone has been here, and it is the outline of a person lying on one side with the knees drawn slightly up. The stone has taken it the way it takes everything — without bedding, without seam, faint growth lines running over the shoulder and down the hip like the inside of a tree — and it is a hand deep in white now, and every year it is a little less a shape and a little more the wall. There is no way to know what it was. It may have been a worker who lay down in the wrong place on the shelf's first cold night. It may have been a fold of stone the springs made on their own, before there was a town, that happens to look like a man asleep. It may be a story the workers tell each other about a worker, made visible by the wanting. They do not go up to it. They do not point at it if they can help it, and when a newcomer points at it they make the sign for *leave it*, which in that context means several things at once. But it is, whatever it is, the only resident of the town who has moved in. Everyone else is either building it or waiting to be born. The shape on the back wall has been there the whole time, has kept the rule, has not touched anything since, and is by now more the mountain's than anyone's. When the people come, it will be their oldest neighbor, and they will not know it is there.

And then the last inhabitant, who is also the landlord, and who was here before the stained films along the springline, and whom you have been standing inside since the switchbacks.

The water. Not the roar — the roar is the water's voice, and the shelf's, and the town's, but the water itself is quieter than that and much larger. It comes from too deep to know weather. It has been in the mountain for longer than there have been mountains here in this shape, moving down through the dark rock, taking the rock into itself, carrying it up, and at the springline meeting the air and letting the rock go. That is all it does. It does not distinguish. It has no preference for walls over baskets or baskets over sleeping men. It lays down a skin a day on whatever is in its path, and the plan of the town is, from the water's side, simply a change in what is in its path, which it accommodates without noticing. The workers understand this exactly. They have a sign for the water and it is the same as the sign for the mountain and it is the same as the sign, made low and flat and wide, for *everything here*. They do not think the water knows about them. They do not think it wants the town. They think, insofar as they think about it at all in words, that the water is the only thing on the shelf that is doing precisely what it has always done and will do precisely that when the town is finished and full and when the town is a smooth white hump on the side of a mountain with no one alive who remembers there were rooms in it; and that to build here is to borrow that steadiness, briefly, for the length of a course of stone, and then hand it back.

So: films of color living on their own dead; midges in the steam; dark birds that may have stopped singing; a beetle going slowly white; something in the high moss that is never seen whole; a handful of cold, pale, chalk-handed people who have given up the first person and get their bread from strangers below; a population of the unborn, described in every doorway; one sleeper in the wall who arrived early and kept the rule; and under and through all of it, the water, coming out of the dark and setting the dark down in white, considering nothing, taking everything, at exactly the pace it has always taken it.

That is what lives on the shelf. If you stay a week, the roar will go quiet and you will find yourself, without having decided to, using your hands to say *leave it* when you mean *goodnight*. If you stay a season, you will lay a course and step back and feel the particular lightness of having given something to a thing that does not know it has been given anything. And if you leave a boot by the sheet overnight, you will find in the morning that it has begun, without malice, to be furnished with a room.
