Start with the hands, because they are the first thing you would notice and the last thing you would understand.

Everyone's hands are cut. The floor is glass and everything they touch is glass or something built on glass, and so the hands of anyone past childhood are crosshatched with old openings, and the old openings have not healed the way a hand heals over the rim. They have closed grey. The water gets into a wound before the flesh can, and lays down its film, and the scar that forms is part skin and part the beginning of stone — pale, faintly ridged, hard to the touch, a little cool even in the bath-warm dusk. A knapper's palms, after thirty years, are almost entirely this. He can hold a fresh-struck blade by the edge. He can no longer quite feel it.

That is where I would begin, and then I would have to keep correcting, because the hands are not a wound. They are the first draft of something the whole body is going to do.

—

The people are not tall. The bells do not permit it and the weed does not require it. They are broad through the chest and long through the ribs, and they hold themselves in the water with a stillness that looks like laziness until you have tried to keep station in a current you cannot see. They are dark-eyed, and the pupil in the dusk is nearly the whole eye, and there is a sheen behind it that catches what little light comes down and gives it back, so that when two of them face each other in the thicket between the columns there are four small pale coins hanging in the smoke of the weed. The skin is grey-brown and thick. It is thickest where the water lingers — the backs of the shoulders, the outer forearms, the shins — and thinnest in the creases, where movement keeps the film from settling, so that an old woman's elbow is soft and her upper arm rings faintly under a fingernail. They are not ashamed of this. They oil the creases with weed-fat to keep them working, the way you would oil a hinge, and they let the rest go.

Their hair they crop close or shave, because loose hair silicifies at the tips into something like a bristle brush and catches in the cord. The children are the exception. Children are allowed hair, long and weed-dark, because children move too much for anything to settle on them, and because there is a season of life for being uncoated and everyone agrees it should be enjoyed.

—

Children are born in the bells. There is no other way; the water would fill them. So every birth happens in stale air that tastes of eggs and iron, under a dome that has been ringing with the same family's voices for two hundred years, and the child's first breath is the mountain's breath, sulphurous and warm. For the first year or so the child cannot be in the water at all except carried, and so you see mothers moving between bells with a small inverted basket held up over the infant's head — a private lantern of air, a bell the size of a hat, the child's face turned up into it and its feet trailing in the milk. The basket is new weed and does not last. That is deliberate. The one thing they do not want to turn to stone is the thing a baby breathes in.

Childhood is learning to stay down. That is nearly the whole of it. A child of four surfaces every minute, rising up through the pearl-coloured water into the fierce thin sunlight to gulp and come back, and the city looks after these children the way a village looks after children who wander, because a child who surfaces alone in the bright water is a child out of reach. By ten they surface every few minutes. By fifteen, rarely, and they have stopped rising to the surface at all and instead go to the nearest bell and put their face up into the air and take a breath and come down again, and they do this with a kind of embarrassment, as though it were a childish thing they had not yet quite outgrown.

It is not, I think, that they learn to hold their breath. I said that before and it was the wrong word. It is that something is changing in the lining of them — in the throat, in the thin skin inside the lips and along the lower ribs where the blue cast begins — and the water, which coats everything, has found a way to coat those places that does not seal them but works them, the way it works a frond into a column. The very old do not go to the bells. The very old do not, as far as anyone can tell, breathe. They have a colour like the inside of a mussel shell and they move slowly and they are not dead.

—

The language is made with the mouth shut. It is hummed through the sternum and the long bones and comes out of the whole front of the body, and in water it carries as a warmth you feel before you hear it, so that a conversation in the thicket is something you walk into like a current. It has very little in it for distance. It has a great deal for proximity — for above, below, behind-you, at-your-left-hand, coming — because nobody can see more than an arm's length through the weed and everything that matters is announced by touch and hum. They touch constantly. A hand on the shoulder in passing, a palm laid flat against the ribs to say *I am here and I am speaking*. To be out of reach is to be nearly out of the world.

There is no word for *far*. There is a word for *over the rim*, which is also the word for *lost*, and there is a word for *the western floor*, which is where the new tongues come up, and which means something like *not yet*.

Names are hummed and they are low and they are given late, at the age when a child stops surfacing. Before that a child is called by the sound of the bell it was born in — every bell rings differently, and the family knows its own — and so for the first fifteen years of life you are named after a room.

—

The city is their genealogy and they can read it by touch.

Every column was a frame and every frame was set by someone, and the ridges where the lashings were are as particular as handwriting. A woman moving through the old quarter in the dark can lay her hand on a pillar and say who set it, and in which decade, by the spacing of the cord and the knot they favoured. The dead are in the columns too. When a person is finished — when the hum stops and the body has gone the colour of the shell all the way through — the family lashes it upright into a frame of fresh weed, in a place they have chosen, and stakes it, and leaves it, and in five years it is a pillar you can build on. Grandmothers hold up roofs. Children are told which ones. A house-wall in the old quarter is, if you go down through the layers, a row of great-great-grandparents standing shoulder to shoulder with weed packed between them, and everyone inside knows it and would find it morbid to live any other way — to put the dead somewhere you could not lean on them.

Quarrels are in the walls too, and this is the harder thing. If two households fall out badly, someone sets a frame between them. It is not a wall yet. For a year it is a lattice of weed you could swim through, and the setting of it is a kind of speech: *this is where I stop*. If the quarrel mends before the frame goes rigid, they cut it down, and there is a word for that, and it is a warm word. If it does not, it greys and stiffens and in a generation it is stone, and the descendants of the quarrel live on either side of it without knowing why, and know only that this is where the city does not go. The old quarter is a maze of these. Some of them have doors knapped through, later, by people who wanted to be neighbours again. You cannot take a wall down here. Forgiveness is a door.

—

Now the animals, which I dismissed too quickly.

The soft armoured things — the graze-crawlers — I said were not eaten, and that is true, but I had not understood what they are for. They live on the new lava and they graze the film off it, and the people keep them. Every household has a few, kept in a lashed pen by the sleeping-bell, and every morning — morning being a matter of the hum of the city rising rather than any change in the light — a child takes one out and sets it on her grandmother.

That is what they are for. They are set on the very old. They crawl, slow and blind and patient, over the hands and the throat and the ribs, and they eat the film as it forms, and they keep the old ones' fingers able to close and their lips able to part. A crawler on a wrist is a courtesy. A crawler on a face is love. The old sit very still in the weed near the family columns with two or three of these pale things moving over them like slow thoughts, and it is the children's work to move the crawlers on when they have finished a place, and this is how the children learn where the old ones are going, because there is always more film than crawler, and the places the crawlers have not reached are turning, gently, to the colour of the columns.

When a family stops setting crawlers on someone, everyone knows what has been decided. It is not always the family that decides. Sometimes the old one lifts a hand — slowly — and brushes them off.

—

The pale fish live in the weed and are eaten and are not much thought about, except that they are the only thing in the sea that moves quickly, and so the children chase them, and so the children learn the currents.

The eels nobody has seen. They live in the deep channels where the floor has split and not yet healed, where the water is hotter than a body can stand, and they are known as a motion in the dusk at the edge of the city, a long darkness that goes by. The people say they are the mountain's fingers. They say it lightly. Nobody goes to the channels.

The weed I have been treating as vegetation and it is closer to livestock, or to kin. It is planted. Every band of it along every warm crack was set there, and is thinned, and is harvested at the right height, and its lineage is known: this stand came from that stand, which came from the fissure on the eastern shelf that was the first place anyone lived. It is dark red where the light reaches and black below and it is the only thing in the caldera that grows fast, that can be cut and does not scar grey, that dies and is not kept. They love it for that, I think, without saying so. It is the one thing they own that behaves like the world over the rim.

—

And the mountain.

I do not know whether to count it as an inhabitant, but they do. The whine you can hear at night with your ear to a column — the floor being thought about — is to them a voice, low and continuous, in the same register as their own speech, and they are not being poetic when they say so. They say the mountain talks the way they talk: through the body, into whatever is touching it. They say the reason the language is hummed is that the mountain taught it. They say that when a new tongue comes up glowing on the western floor and the water goes cloudy and every column in the city grows a thicker skin that year, that is the mountain adding to them, and the word for it is the word for what a parent does.

They do not fear it. That is the thing I keep coming back to. A people living on a floor that is being made under them, in a sea that is slowly turning them to stone from the outside in, and what they feel about it is not dread but something like being *attended to*. The outside world takes things from you — that is what they know about it, from the snowmelt that comes down the rim carrying dust that was once a peak. Here nothing is taken. Here you are added to until you are finished, and then you hold up a roof.

—

The old woman with the crawlers on her wrists lifts one hand, slowly, and a child comes. The child is all hair and quick eyes and surfaces every few minutes still, rising up into the pearl and coming back. The old woman lays the grey palm flat on the child's sternum and hums something — a place, an above, a not-yet — and the child goes and finds four poles of hollow weed and some cord, and stakes a frame into a crack in the new black floor at the edge of the thicket, where the columns thin out and the sea begins to be empty.

It is a small frame. It is not clear what it is for. The child does not know and does not need to. In a year it will be grey and in five it will be rigid and in twenty someone will build on it, and the child will be a woman by then with cut hands and a name, and she will lay her palm on it in the dark and feel the knots and know they were hers.
