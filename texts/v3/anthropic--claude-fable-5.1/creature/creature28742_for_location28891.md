Count first, because the place invites counting. It is small enough that you can hold the whole census in your head.

One animal. Some plants, in islands. Water, everywhere. Frost, in one place. A light, from below. And a shelter with no one in it.

That is the population. But *lives here* is a heavier word than *is here*, and most of what is on the shelf is only on it. The distinction takes a while to see, and then it is the only thing you see.

---

Start with the ferns, because they are the loudest.

They are not residents. They are arrivals. Everything about them says so—the speed of them, the rawness of the green, the way each stand grows up exactly where its spore came down and has not yet sent a runner toward its neighbor. A resident knows the room. These do not. Each clump of horsetail is convinced it is the only horsetail on the mountain. Each tree fern lifts its crown over its own patch of hot rock and looks at nothing. They live here the way people live in a new country the first year: with their whole bodies, and without belonging.

The broad-leaved thing in the pools is the closest to a native. It stands in water that would blister you, with its roots in the white mineral scum, and it does not look like a colonizer—it looks like it was waiting for exactly this and has finally been given it. In a hundred years, if the shelf is still here, this is the plant the shelf will be known for. Now it is a few stems in a few pools, patient, rubbery, unbothered. It is the first thing that has chosen the place rather than landed on it.

But none of them go anywhere. That is the mark against them. They are in their spots. They do not visit.

---

The water is more numerous than anything, and it is the one thing on the shelf that has learned something.

Everywhere else on the mountain, water does what water does: it finds the low place and goes there. Here it does that too—runs off the ferns, gathers in the fractures, steams, condenses, runs again—right up until it reaches the seam. And then it stops. It stands up. It holds. Not because it is blocked but because, here, that is what water does; a rule has been set, and the water is the only living thing on the shelf that has been given a rule and follows it.

You come to think of the standing lip along the seam as a kind of resident. A long, thin, clear one, three hundred paces from wall to drop, trembling slightly, going nowhere, always full and always draining and always the same. It is the most patient thing you have ever watched. It does not want to cross. It has been told. It is the shelf's only citizen in that sense: it has agreed to something.

The moss stripe along its edge is the water's shadow—the plants that found it, and drink from it, and have arranged themselves along it in a line straighter than any plant would choose. Even they are obeying, at one remove.

---

The frost is the cold block's creature, and it is the only thing born on the shelf that dies on the shelf, and it does both continuously.

It grows in the wet air—white, fine, furred, thickening on the block's dark faces until it can no longer hold its own weight—and then a sheet of it lets go, slides, breaks into the moat, and the bare stone beneath is already beading with new frost before the shards have finished turning in the water. Nothing tends it. Nothing eats it. It has no season. It simply is the shape that the shelf's warmth takes when it meets the block's cold, the negotiation made visible, and it lives at the block the way barnacles live on a hull—not on the thing but *of* the meeting of the thing and its surroundings.

It is a resident by the narrowest definition. It has an address. It never leaves. It knows nothing.

---

Something lives under the shelf.

You cannot say more than that honestly, and the shelf does not ask you to. The light in the hole is orange and steady and it has a source, and warm air rises from it evenly the way breath rises from someone asleep. It does not flicker. It does not change when the cloud comes up or when the sun goes. Whatever it is, it is not restless. It has the same quality as the standing water and the frost—a condition, kept, without effort.

And the first rule of the shelf—*the heat comes from below*—begins to sound less like geology and more like a description of a tenancy. The shelf is warm because of what is under it. The ferns grow because the shelf is warm. The whole sweating, dripping, colonizing room is somebody's roof, and the orange light is the lamp they left on, and the hole is where the roof was told not to be.

You do not go down. But you understand, sitting near it, that the question of who lives here has at least one answer you will never be able to check, and that the shelf is arranged as if that answer were the important one.

---

Then the shelter, and the person who is not in it.

You can read them from what they left the way you can read a hand from its glove. They were small, or willing to be small—the space is a lying-down space and nothing more. They needed no fire; the rock was their fire, and so there is no ring of ash, no scorch, nothing of the mess that people usually leave where they have been warm. They gathered. The cup of mineral bloom is not a meal; it is a sample. The horsetail bundle is not firewood; it is—what? A tally. A broom. Something to scour with. Something to keep.

And the flat stone with the line and the circle.

This is the note, and it is the simplest note it is possible to make, and it is incomplete. The line is the seam; there is nothing else here that is a line. The circle could be the hole, or the moat of ice around the block, or the block itself seen from above, or the shelf entire with its one wall missing. They saw the rules and tried to write them down and got as far as two marks. Either they had not yet decided what the third mark should be, or they had decided the third thing did not need one—that the cold, or the hole, was not a rule but a fact, and facts do not need recording, only rules do.

They stayed long enough to build a roof and not long enough for it to fall. They lived on the shelf's heat and drank the shelf's water and watched the shelf's three impossibilities with what looks, from the evidence, like calm. Then they left. The roof is putting down roots into the floor. In a few seasons the shelter will be a plant with a horsetail bundle inside it and no one will be able to say where the architecture ended.

They lived here. That is the correct tense. They are the only thing on the shelf for which it is.

---

Which leaves the lizard, and the lizard is where you end up, because it is the only thing on the shelf that does what you would do if you lived here.

It makes the rounds.

Watch it for a day and the circuit resolves. The frost first, in the morning, when the block is thickest with white and the moat is skinned with new ice: it walks out across the frost with its belly down, slowly, leaving faint prints that fill in behind it, and it stops at the far edge and stays there, on the coldest surface within a day's climb of anywhere, and its throat pulses, and it does not look cold. Then the seam, at midday: it goes to the wet side and lowers its head to the standing lip and drinks from the edge that will not fall, taking water directly from the rule. Then the ferns in the afternoon, the warm side, up a black fibrous trunk into the lime-green shade, where it lies along a frond stem with its head toward the drop and watches the cloud come up.

Cold. Wet. Warm. It is not regulating anything. A lizard that wanted heat would stay on the rock. A lizard that wanted water has a thousand pools. This one goes to each of the shelf's conditions in turn and stays in each for a while and moves on, and the nearest word for what it is doing is *visiting*. Or *checking*. Or—the word that comes and will not leave—*keeping*.

Its throat gives it away. When it pulses, the color that moves through the skin is not a color of the shelf's living things—not fern-green, not rust, not the white-blue of the mineral scum. It is a bruised iridescence with the seam's dull gray in it, the color of the floor of the line, the one color here that is not the color of anything you can name. It carries a little of the made things in it. It drinks from the seam and walks on the frost and does not go down the hole, and the not-going is as deliberate as the rest: the hole is a door, and the lizard does not use doors, because the lizard does not leave.

You never see two at once. You come to think there is one. Not one *species*—one lizard. Whatever made the seam and the block and the hole and then walked off and left the mountain to fill in the rest did not quite leave the shelf untended. It left this. A small dark thing with a broad head that goes around, and touches each rule, and finds each rule still holding, and goes around again. It is unafraid of you because nothing here has ever been a threat to it. It is uninterested in you because you are, for the length of your stay, simply one more condition on the shelf—a warm thing that moves—and it will visit you in your turn, once, briefly, and move on.

Or it is only the first animal, the way the ferns are only the first plants. Born of the mountain, come up with the rock, tasting everything because everything is new and nothing has taught it fear or appetite yet. Its throat is that color because that is the color throats are here, in a place where the ground is still ticking. You cannot rule that out. The shelf does not let you rule anything out.

But watch what it does. Everything else on the shelf is in one place. The ferns are in their islands, the frost is on its block, the water is at its line, the light is in its hole, the person is gone. Only the lizard knows the whole room. Only the lizard treats the shelf as a shelf—as one place with several conditions, to be walked—rather than as a spot it happens to occupy. Living somewhere is not being there. It is going around. It is knowing where the cold is and where the wet begins and which trunk faces the drop, and going to each, and coming back.

By that measure there is one resident.

---

So: who lives here.

Water that has agreed to a rule, and stands at it. Frost that is born and dies in the same breath, at the same address. A light with a source, under the floor, that keeps the whole place warm and asks nothing. The absence of a careful person who made two marks on a stone and did not make a third. A crowd of green arrivals who do not yet know one another's names. And a lizard the length of your forearm, dark, with a throat that carries the color of the seam, going around.

And the mountain, slowly, wetly, with ferns—moving in.
