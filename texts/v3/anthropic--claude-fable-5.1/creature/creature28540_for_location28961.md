Begin with the film, because the film is the majority.

Everything in this room that is alive, by weight, by number, by any measure but the one that matters to us, is that red-brown slick on the upper brick. It is not a stain. It is a population. Billions of them, each a fleck too small for the eye, each one doing one small chemical thing over and over — taking iron the water has stripped from the condensers and the penstock walls and the swollen lamp cage, and burning it, slowly, in the way that things without mouths burn, and leaving behind the rust-coloured paste that is their city. They have no light and want none. They have never had quiet and would not know what to do with it. The heat that would cook a fish is, to them, weather; the metal load that has killed everything else that ever came down the tunnels is their food.

They live in a band. Below it the water moves too fast for them to hold on; above it the air is too dry between surges. So they occupy the shaft's tideline — a strip some yards high, ringed about the whole circumference, thickest where the water hangs longest at the top of its rise. When the column drops they are exposed, and the thin sheet of water running off them catches the lamp, and for a moment the wall wears them like a wet hide. Then they are covered and go back to work. They have been doing this since the brick was pointed. They have never seen the eel. They are what it eats, or part of what it eats, and they do not mind, because what is a mouthful to it is nothing to a city.

That is the first inhabitant: a crust of lives so small the room reads as empty.

---

The second is the eel, and I want to go slowly here, because the eel is not what it seems, and it was not what I thought when I began.

She is female. That is very nearly certain: the eels that travel farthest from the sea into fresh water, that end up in the last ditch, the highest millpond, the well behind the farmhouse, are almost always females, and they are the ones that grow. She came down the intake as a thing the thickness of a bootlace, years ago — sixty, perhaps, perhaps more; the screen she slipped through was replaced before the war and no one knew why the old one had a gap in it — and she was carried through the elbow where the water tears at itself and spat up into the shaft, and there she stopped.

Eels are made for two lives. There is the long life in fresh water, dark-backed and yellow-bellied, growing, and then there is the signal — no one knows what it is exactly; a temperature, a season, a hormone tripped by the moon — and the eel changes. The eyes swell. The flanks go silver. The gut shuts down, because it will not eat again. And it turns downstream and goes, across whatever it must cross, to the one place in the ocean where eels are born, and it spawns there, and it dies there. That is the whole design. The first life is only preparation for the second.

She never got the signal. There is nothing here to carry it. No season reaches her; the water is the same warmth in January as in July, the same brown at noon as at midnight. No moon. No cold snap in October to say *now*. And an eel that is never told to go does not go, and does not change, and does not — this is the strange part — does not, in the ordinary sense, age. It simply continues in its first life, indefinitely, growing. There are eels in wells in the north that have outlived the families who dropped them in. There was one in a farmhouse well that lived a hundred and fifty years and was still, when it died, waiting.

So that is what she is. Not an old eel. A held breath. A migration arrested at its first step, in a chamber whose whole purpose is to hold what cannot yet be released. She is longer than my arm and thicker than my wrist and the colour of the pale side of a mushroom, and she is still, in every way that counts to her body, young — and she has been young for longer than anyone in the power station has been alive.

---

What she knows.

She cannot see, or not usefully; the eyes are small and the light is a green smear. She does not need to. Eels find their way by smell and by the lateral line, that row of pressure-sensing pits along the flank that tells a fish where the water is going before it gets there. In a river that is a tool. Here it is her whole world. She lives inside the sound the way we live inside weather.

She knows the surge before it arrives. The report in the elbow reaches her lateral line as a blow, and she reads in it — she must, after this long — which gate has opened, how far, how hard the climb will be. She knows the fundamental groan of the runners as we know the sound of our own blood. She knows, and this is the thing that stopped me when I understood it, the shape of the city's day.

Because the turbines follow the load, and the load follows the city. At five in the morning the trams begin and the demand climbs and the gates open and the column jerks and rises and she is pressed into her seam behind the rung. Through the morning it holds, roaring. At noon the laundry's boilers come on and there is a step. In the evening the streets light and the surges come thick and violent, the shaft ringing at its full note, the debris lifting from the silt, and she does not move at all. Then, late, the load falls, and the room quietens to its floor — never quiet — and the surges spread apart, and in the long slack peaks of the small hours she comes out and moves through the brown dark along the tideline, grazing, and returns.

She has never seen a tram. She does not know there is a city, or a sky, or a laundry. But she knows exactly when the city wakes, and when it eats, and when it turns on its lamps, and when it sleeps. She has learned its pulse from the inside, as a child in the womb learns the mother's, and it is the only rhythm she has ever had — Sunday's slack, Monday's rise, the strange flat hush of holidays she could not name. She is the city's demand curve, written in a nervous system. Every Christmas of the last sixty years has passed over her as a particular kind of long, slow calm.

---

I said she does not age, and that is nearly true, but she has changed. Not as eels change. As the brick has.

The water has worked on her as it has on everything. Her fins are frayed to a fringe. There is a groove along one flank where the ladder-rung has pressed her for decades, a shallow channel in the flesh that she now fits as a key fits a lock, and she goes into it without thinking, tail first, and lies there with the rung across her as the column heaves. Her skin has taken the shaft's colour; she is the exact pale of the calcite ridges, so that at rest she is a fifth band on the wall. Her hearing — if it is hearing — has done what the mineral crust has done: it has grown thick at the frequencies the room favours. She is tuned to it. Play her note and she would lift, as the silt lifts.

And here the eel and the room begin to become the same thing, and I let them.

Because the third inhabitant is the note.

---

I do not mean this as a figure. The shaft has a resonance — the description above says so, and it is right: an organ pipe, a set of tones the brick and water agree on, standing waves whose nodes are recorded in bands of scale. But a standing wave is not a thing that happens *to* a room. It is a thing that lives *in* one. It has a shape. It has places it must be and places it cannot be. It feeds — on the turbines, on the surges, on whatever energy comes down the tunnel at the right pitch — and it grows loud when fed and thin when starved and it has never, in a hundred and thirty years, been allowed to die, because the machines have never stopped. It arranges the room to suit itself. It has grown the calcite where it wanted walls. It has rounded the brick where it wanted smoothness. It moves the spanner in its slow circuit; the spanner is the note's one possession, and it keeps it moving so it does not settle. The bacterial city lives along its tideline. The eel lies in its node.

So there are three tenants, and they nest. The film lives on the wall. The eel lives on the film and in the wall's groove. The note lives in the whole chamber and the eel lives inside the note, tuned to it, shaped by it, fed by the same city that feeds it. And the note — this is the last thing, and I offer it without a way to prove it — the note may be what keeps the lamp lit. Not by any circuit. By the kind of coincidence that happens when a thing has stood at one frequency long enough: the rust that should have opened the contacts has grown in a pattern the vibration dictated, a crust bridging the gap where wire has gone to nothing, so that the current finds its way across a body of oxidised iron that the room, humming, built for it. The room lights its own lamp. It has for sixty years. No one has been down to disprove it.

---

Then say it plainly. What lives here is a thing that is waiting, inside a thing that is holding, inside a thing that is singing.

At the top of the surge, in the half-second when the column hangs and the roar thins to the machine-floor and the brown dark seems for a moment to widen, she comes out of the groove. She moves along the tideline with the slow unhurried fluency of something with nowhere else to be, mouthing the red film, turning in the green ring of the lamp where she shows for an instant — pale, long, marked down one side by the rung — and out of it. Her lateral line reads the tunnel. She knows how long she has. She knows, from sixty years of it, that this particular slack is the long one that comes after the trams stop and before the milk trains, and she takes her time.

Somewhere under all the noise, there is a signal she is built to receive. A change in the water that means *go*. It will not come. The water will be this warmth tomorrow, and the brown will be this brown, and the moon will be a thing that happens to other eels. She does not know she is waiting. She knows only that she is not yet finished, and in a room whose one job is to hold what cannot yet be let go, that is a kind of belonging.

The gate opens somewhere upstream. The elbow speaks. She feels it in her flank before it is a sound, and turns, and slides tail-first into the groove that is exactly her shape, and the rung comes down across her like a hand, and the column falls, and the room booms its long hollow note around the curve of itself, and she is not, by any measure she possesses, afraid.
