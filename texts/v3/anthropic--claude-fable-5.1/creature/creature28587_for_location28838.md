The first thing to say about them is that you will not see them, and they will not see you, and this is not a deprivation they feel.

They are people. That should be said plainly, because from the rim the movement in the dark can look like something else — a slow churn, a colony — and it is not that. They have two hands and a voice and they sleep and they are afraid of falling. But they have lived in the basin long enough that the basin has had its say in them, and what it has made is a person built for the dark and the wet and the ringing of stone.

---

Start with the hands, because they would.

The hands are large for the body and the fingertips are broad and flat and very slightly split at the pad, the way a thumb goes after years of testing a blade. The calluses are not the thick horn of a farmer's palm; they are thinner than you would expect, because a hand that cannot feel a high spot the width of a hair is no use here. The skin is kept soft by the constant wet and hardened by the constant lime, and it comes out somewhere between — cool, a little rough, sensitive as a tongue. A builder laying a palm flat on a dressed face can tell you where the block was quarried from by the grain, and whether the last person to work it was left-handed, and roughly how long it has been out of its bed by how much water it has given up.

They touch everything. Not to steady themselves — they know where the walls are — but to keep the picture current. Walking a seam they trail one hand along the stone and the other out ahead at chest height, and the fingers of the leading hand are always moving, a small ceaseless flexing, reading the air for the warmth of someone coming. When two of them meet in a narrow place the hands go to each other's shoulders first, then the upper arm, then briefly the side of the neck, and that is a greeting and also a question — *who* — because the neck is where you learn a person. The set of the tendons. The pulse. Whether they are tired.

They do not touch faces. The face is not where they keep each other.

---

The eyes are open and very wide and very dark, the pupil having long ago taken most of the iris, and they are not blind. On the rim, in the salt-glare, a builder would suffer. Down here the eyes do what the eyes can, which is tell a wall from a gap, and a kiln from the cloud, and — if you stand still long enough — a shape from the shape beside it. Nobody relies on them. Children are not taught to look at things. A child who stares is a child who is not listening, and is told so.

The ears are ordinary to look at. The hearing is not. They can hold a pitch in the head across a long silence and match it when it comes again; they can separate one hammer out of a hundred by the ring of its particular cobble; they can hear, in the tap of a block being seated three courses below, whether the person seating it is doing it right. Old builders whose hands have gone stiff do nothing else. They sit in the angle of a courtyard with their heads tilted and listen to the joints go in, and when one goes in wrong they say so, a short flat sound, and the work at that place stops and is redone. Nobody argues with them. It is understood that a very old ear knows a true joint the way a very old hand knows a true face, which is better than the hand that laid it.

---

The voice is the third thing, and it is the thing that makes them a people rather than a scattering.

Every builder has a call. It is the rising two-note that means *I am here*, but no two are quite the same — the interval is a little wider or narrower, the second note held or clipped, a burr in the throat or a clean edge. It is given early, before the child can walk, by whoever is holding it: the adult sings a call into the small ear over and over, in the dark, until the child sings it back, and then that is the child's, and it will not change. When they speak of someone they do not use a word. They give the call, softly, the way you might say a name. When someone dies the call is not used again for a long time, until it has faded from the walls, and then it may be given to a child, and the old people will say the child is *carrying* the one who had it before.

So they know each other as sounds first and hands second and as bodies moving in the dark third, and as faces not at all. Two builders who have worked the same wall for thirty years, who have handed each other stone every day of it, who have slept against each other in the wet — neither could tell you the colour of the other's eyes, and neither would understand why you had asked.

---

The hair is long. All of it, on everyone, grown from birth and never cut except for rope.

This is the one crop the basin gives. It is thick and grows fast and comes out of the head very dark and goes grey early from the lime, and when it is past the waist it is braided and cut close and the braid is unbound and twisted with others into the ropes that cross every courtyard at head height. A rope is many people. When it is laid across a slab or paid out down a shaft, the hands on it know whose it is — hair holds the oil of its head, and a builder can put a rope to the face and tell you three of the people in it. The old ropes, worn slick and grey, are the most trusted. The people in them are gone and the rope is still bearing weight, and this is thought to be right.

The skin is pale, and cold to touch, and always wet. Not from sweat; they hardly sweat, in this air. The fog settles on them as it settles on the walls. They are thin. The bones show at the wrist and the collar, the long muscles of the back and forearm stand out under the skin like cordage, and there is not much else. They do not eat much and they do not seem to need to.

---

What they eat is a question you will want an answer to and they will find strange.

They drink from the walls. That is the first thing and the constant thing: hands cupped to a weeping joint, or a mouth put straight to the stone, many times a day, without thought. The water is cold and faintly mineral and it is enough for thirst. For the rest — there is a curd. Where the wall-water runs into the grooves and stands, a grey film grows on it, and where the film is left undisturbed in a shallow trough it thickens into something soft and pale with the texture of new cheese and a taste of lime and nothing. They tend the troughs. They keep the sledges off them and skim them with the edge of a bone and eat the skimmings from their fingers, standing, between one block and the next. Children are fed it from the fingertip. The pale eyeless things in the seams eat the same film, and the builders do not begrudge them, and do not eat them either; they are simply the other thing that lives on what the walls give.

Asked where the curd comes from, they say: from the water. Asked where the water comes from, they give you the two answers everyone gives — from below, or from the heart of the stone — and then they shrug, which is a thing you feel rather than see, a shifting of the shoulder under your hand. It is not a mystery they need solved. The wall weeps; the weeping feeds them; they raise the wall. That is a closed shape and they live inside it.

---

They are born in the dark and they die in it, and both are quiet.

A birth happens wherever it happens — in the angle of a courtyard, usually, with the clay-treaders singing their calls in a slow circle so the mother has something to hold to. The child is passed hand to hand up the nearest stair, so that everyone on it has held it, and then it is given back and a call is sung into it. It is not thought to belong to anyone in particular. It is fed by whoever has curd on their fingers and it sleeps against whoever is sleeping, and it learns the walls by falling against them, and by the time it can walk it can tell a seated block from a rocking one by the sound.

The dead go into the wall. This is the thing you come to that makes the rest of it turn over.

When a builder dies the body is carried — falling call, *weight coming through* — to wherever a course is open and waiting for stone, and it is laid in the bed, and clay is packed around it, and the next block is set on top and tapped and rung true. Nobody marks the place. The wall is the marking. And then the wall stands for a generation or three, weeping from its joints, and then it is unpicked for the blocks, and the bones are in the joints, and the long ones are taken up and cut and pointed and become picks, and the picks are used to dress the stone for the course above.

They know this. They do not hide it or dress it up. The picks are handed down and each one is somebody, and when a builder is using a pick that was a person they knew, they will sometimes give that person's call under their breath as they work, softly, the way you would talk to someone standing beside you. The deep bones, the ones from far down that are too long and too heavy and not shaped quite right for a hand — those are also somebody, they will tell you. Earlier somebodies. The ones who laid the courses nobody now could lift. They were larger, the builders say, without any particular wonder, the way you would say that a grandparent was taller.

---

There are other things that live here, though the builders would not put it that way.

The pale things in the seams, which have no eyes and no names and are simply there, like the water.

The birds that come down through the cloud — not living here, exactly, but arriving, and dying, and being found. A found bird is passed around. Everyone puts their hands on it. The feathers are the strangest thing any of them will ever touch, and children are brought to it, and it is talked about across the kilns for days — *the bird at the third kiln, did your hands get to it* — and then it goes into the wall like anyone.

The calls. This is the thing they would not say and that you begin to feel after long enough in the dark. A warning raised on one side of the basin is passed mouth to mouth to the other, and on the way it changes, and by the time it comes back around it is not quite the call it was. Some of the calls that travel the courtyards now — the stutter that means *the wall is moving*, in particular, in some of its variants — do not belong to anyone living. They are the shape a long-dead voice left in the throats of the people who heard it, passed on, worn smooth. The builders use them and do not think about it. But the relay never stops, and a sound that never stops being made is not quite nothing. It is at least as alive as a rope.

And the water, which some of them will tell you, if you press, is the one that actually lives here. Everything else is passing through it. It comes up from below, or out of the stone, it does not matter which; it finds the grooves the sledges have made and the low places the building has left, it feeds the curd and the pale things and the people, and it goes down again through the joints of the deepest floors toward whatever they stand on. The builders are what it does on the way through. They do not say this with any sadness.

---

And then, if you stand long enough on the rim with your eyes gone wide — there is the form.

The curve of it under the kiln-lights. The ribs. The sense of something coming up out of its own foundations that is not a city and not a hive and not a wall, but a single made thing, enclosing, patient, a little higher tomorrow. The builders do not see it. They are in the seams, where there is nothing to see. But they are its hands. They eat what it weeps and they lay their dead in its joints and they cut their hair for its ropes and they sing into its hollows the calls it has given them, and they say of it only that it is being *raised*, and they do not ask toward what.

Whether the thing that lives in the basin is the builders, or the water, or the sound, or the form — you will not settle it, and neither have they. In the dark the distinctions do not hold. A person is a call and a pair of hands and a length of rope and, eventually, a pick. A wall is a bed for the dead and a spring and a course for the next course. The stone rings when it is seated true. Someone hears it. Someone answers.

That is what lives there.
