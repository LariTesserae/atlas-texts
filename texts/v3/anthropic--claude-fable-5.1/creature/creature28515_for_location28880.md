Begin with what does not.

Nothing with a heartbeat lives here. That is the first fact about the inhabitants, and it draws the outline of everything else: the gas from the throat has decided, over the handful of decades the cleft has been open, who is admitted, and it has admitted nothing that must draw breath. No hyrax has denned in the warm cinder, though the cinder is exactly what a hyrax would want. No sunbird works the lobelia spikes, though the spikes are the tallest on the mountain and the nectar in them is the richest. No moth. No mite in the moss. Once, in a wet season, a chameleon came down the scree—you can imagine the path it took, the leaf-green and the leaf-warmth pulling it in—and it went to sleep on a groundsel trunk two paces from the rim and did not wake, and the moss took it in a season. The cleft is a place that everything with a nervous system knows better than to enter, and the knowledge is not learned, it is simply the fact that whoever entered did not come out to teach it.

So the residents are the ones for whom the gas is not a poison, and there are more of them than you would think, and they are not all what you would call alive.

---

The moss is here first, and it is here last, and in between it is here everywhere.

It is the smallest thing in the cleft and the most important. Understand what it is doing: the walls are glass. Obsidian and welded cinder, no clay, no grit, nothing a root could hold. On the open mountain, glass like this would stay bare for a thousand years while frost chewed it into gravel and the gravel into something a seed could take. In here the moss does not wait. It lands as a spore in the wet, it finds a conchoidal face still ticking with heat, it takes hold on nothing—on the film of condensed water and the sulphur-tang dissolved in it—and it grows a cushion. The cushion dies from beneath as it grows from above. The dead layer holds water. The held water rots the dead layer. Within a year there is a centimetre of black wet stuff on the glass that was not there before, and that centimetre is soil, manufactured from nothing but moss and time, and the lobelia seed that lands on it next year will find purchase.

The moss lives at a speed the mountain has never seen. Outside, on the frost-shattered slope, a cushion the width of your palm is thirty years old. In here the same cushion is three. It covers the slabs that fall from the walls before the slabs have finished settling. It covers the dead groundsels. It covered the chameleon. It is the cleft's memory, in that it records where the heat was and where it was not: a band of moss on the wall means the steam was held there long enough, a bald band means the heat came too close, a band of moss growing over crust means the heat came and then went. Read the walls and you can read the last decade of the wind.

And it is the one inhabitant that is not, in any sense that matters, an individual. There is no *a* moss. There is moss, a condition of the surfaces, a skin the cleft grows wherever it is cool enough and wet enough, and where the skin is broken the cleft is bleeding heat and where it is whole the cleft is at rest.

---

The lobelias are the crowd. Hundreds of them, and every one of them is doing the same thing: standing.

They do not branch and they do not lean toward anything. A rosette at the base like a fistful of blades, and from its heart the spike, straight, grey-green, dense with bracts that hide the small blue flowers inside so that the spike is less a flower than a column, a chimney of its own. Outside the cleft the lobelia is a slow and careful animal. It grows for years before it flowers; it stores water in its leaf-cups and lets that water freeze each night and thaw each morning and the freezing takes the frost's violence into itself so the growing heart underneath stays whole. It is built for cold, and it survives cold by being patient.

In here it has forgotten patience. The cups never freeze. The water in them is warm, faintly acid, and the plant drinks it and drinks the warmth and puts up its spike in a third of the time and half again as tall, and stands there in the roar, and when the steam swings through the whole crowd of them leans and comes back like reeds. They flower and set seed and the seed falls into the moss and comes up, and so the crowd is always thickening, always closing whatever gap was passable last night. They are the ones that make the cleft impassable by hand. They are also the ones that die most visibly when the line moves: a lobelia scalded is a lobelia fallen, the rosette slumped to a cooked green mess, the spike lying across the cinder like a dropped pole. The crowd loses a dozen a week at the hot edge and grows a dozen at the cool one and does not notice.

The groundsels are older and fewer and they are the architecture. Each trunk is the record of its own life—the skirt of dead leaves wrapped tight around it is every leaf it has ever grown, kept, because on the open mountain that dead skirt is the insulation that keeps the living pith from freezing, and the plant does not know it is no longer on the open mountain. So it wraps itself against a frost that never comes and stands in the warm and the wet in a coat it does not need, and the coat rots, and the rot is warm, and put your hand on a trunk and what you feel is not the plant's heat but the heat of the plant's own decay, a slow compost fire held against the living wood. They branch—rarely, once or twice in a life—and the branches carry their own cabbages and the cabbages meet overhead and make the ceiling. The oldest of them predates the cleft. It stood on the shoulder as a stunted thing when the seam parted, and the seam parted a metre from its roots, and instead of killing it the opening put it on the rim of a warm wet garden, and in the decades since it has grown more than it grew in the century before and leans now over the fissure with its crown in the steam, the only inhabitant that remembers the cold.

---

On the tree heathers, the lichen. It hangs in the pale beard strands, sodden, a metre long in places, and it is two things pretending to be one: a fungus giving shelter and an alga giving sugar, welded into a single body that neither could be alone. It lives on air. It has no roots and wants none; it takes its water from the steam and its minerals from what the steam carries and it is, of all the residents, the one most exactly suited to what the cleft provides, because the cleft provides nothing but wet air with things dissolved in it, and that is what lichen eats.

It is worth holding onto the lichen for a moment because it is a picture of the whole place. Two organisms that cannot be told apart, growing as one, each keeping the other alive in a place neither could survive. Keep that.

---

Below the plants, in the rings around the throat, live the minerals, and I say *live* deliberately, because they do what the living do.

They grow. Sulphur condenses from the gas in the band where the temperature is exactly right for it—too hot and it stays vapour, too cool and it never arrives—and it builds in crystals, small needles first and then crusts, and the crusts thicken while the band holds still. They migrate: the hot zone spreads and the sulphur ring moves outward, cooking off its inner edge and depositing at its outer, so that over a week the whole yellow band has crawled a hand's breadth across the cinder. Inside the sulphur, where it is too hot for sulphur, the whites and rust-reds—chlorides and iron—do the same at their own temperatures, so the bowl around the throat is a set of nested rings each living in its own thermal country, each moving as the country moves. They have a metabolism, if you want to call it that: they take gas and give solid. They have generations: last week's crust is tonight's dust. They respond to the wind faster than the moss does, and when the wind holds the steam against a wall face for a night the face grows a fur of crystal by morning and sheds it the next.

They are not alive. But they are the only inhabitants that go where the plants cannot, into the heat, right up to the lip of the throat, and they are the only ones the gas feeds rather than kills. If you had to say who lives closest to the engine, it is these.

---

And the engine.

The throat breathes. There is no other word that fits. Pressure gathers under the floor, the pitch of the roar climbs, the hum in your feet thickens, and then the aperture yields and the mass of steam is shot out and up and the roar jumps and the wave of wet heat comes across the bowl, and then it falls, and gathers again. Forty seconds. It has a pulse, in other words, and the pulse drifts, and sometimes it stumbles—two releases close together, the second larger—and something comes down off the wall. It has a temperature that it holds within a range. It has a shape that it is still finding, the aperture widening or choking from month to month as slabs drop and gas finds new paths. It sheds its skin; the walls of the bowl exfoliate in sheets and the sheets shatter and the shards glitter and the moss comes for them.

It is not alive either. It is a fissure into a body of gas over a body of heat, and the rhythm is the rhythm of a valve. But it keeps time, and everything that lives in the cleft lives to that time whether it knows or not: the steam swings on the pulse, the lobelias lean on it, the moss wets and dries on it, the sulphur rings advance on it.

---

Now step back. Let the pieces go and see what they were pieces of.

What lives in the cleft is not the moss or the lobelias or the groundsels or the lichen or the crusts or the throat. What lives here is the line. The boundary between green and brown and black. It has no body of its own, only a border, and along that border it does everything a living thing does.

It eats. At the hot edge it eats groundsel—a green crown last week, a cooked brown one tonight—and lobelia, and it eats moss down to a lifted brittle crust, and what it eats becomes the cinder's dark wet stuff and is taken up by the roots at the cool edge, so that the line's own leavings feed its own growth. It moves: outward when the heat swings and the ground beneath the garden warms past what the garden can stand; inward when a slab falls on a fumarole and chokes it and the cinder cools to merely warm and the moss comes down onto it within the month. It breathes at forty seconds, because that is the pulse of the thing that drives it. It has a skin—moss—that heals over every wound the walls inflict, and a skeleton—the groundsel trunks—and a bloodstream, which is the water, endlessly condensed and drained and boiled and sent up again, never resting, never pooling for long. It has no lungs and no nerves, and that is precisely why it can live where nothing else can: the gas that kills everything that breathes cannot kill a thing that lives by being a surface between two conditions.

It is a composite, like the lichen. The mountain's heat and the garden's appetite, neither able to exist here without the other, grown into one body that you cannot cut without killing both halves. Take the heat away and the garden freezes on the first clear night into the same clenched stunted things that stand alone on the slope outside. Take the garden away and the cleft is a bare hot hole in the rock, roaring at nobody, laying down sulphur that nothing will ever cover.

It is young. A few decades, not more—born the night the seam parted, arrived at over years as the spores found the warm glass and the seeds found the moss. It is not finished growing; the fissure itself is still opening, the throat still finding its size, and so the line's whole territory is still being drawn beneath it. It has scars: the dead groundsels standing at the hot edge with their trunks split, the lobelia poles lying across the cinder, the plates of lifted moss-crust—each one a place where the line moved too fast. It has a memory, banded into the walls. It has a probable death, and two possible ways of dying. The throat may seal, and the heat may go, and then the line will simply stop, the last frost will come down over the rim, and what is left will be a still cold mossy hollow full of groundsel that has remembered how to clench, and no one walking past will know that anything was ever here. Or the seam will open along its whole length one night and the shoulder of the mountain will go, and there will be no cleft, and the line will be gone with it, and on the new raw scarp, black and glittering and sharp, the first spore will land in the first wet and begin again.

Tonight it is alive. Tonight it is loud and wet and hot at one end and cold at the other and moving, always moving, and there is not one thing in it that has been put there.

---

Except you.

You are the only lung in the place. You are a visitor, not a resident, and the line knows the difference even if it knows nothing else: it has no use for you, cannot eat you until you are still, and will not notice when you go. You are admitted for minutes at a time, at the hours and in the winds when the gas is thinnest, and you know those hours the way you know the interval of the roar—by having been here before, the night before and the week before, and having watched the green move. You know which groundsel was living last week. You know the crust that is dust tonight. You have a hand on a warm trunk and your face is wet and the hairs on your forearm are lifting in the charge, and you are counting, without meaning to, down from forty.

That is the last inhabitant: not you, but the coming back. Whoever stands here with a hand on the trunk and knows what was here before is doing what the moss does and the crust does and the line itself does, which is to record where the heat was. You are the only one of them who will leave and remember it somewhere else. The pitch falls. For twenty seconds you can hear yourself breathing, and you are the only thing in the cleft that is.
