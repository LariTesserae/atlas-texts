What lives here is not as thin as it looks. It only looks thin because almost all of it is inside the walls.

---

Start with the mats, since they are the first thing the hand meets that isn't stone. They lie in the channel edges where the water has cooled a degree or two, in the gutters, on the treads of stairs where a sheet of water runs slow. In the dark they are only texture—slick, faintly ridged, giving under the finger like the skin of something asleep—and a temperature: cooler than the water they sit in, by a little, as though they were drawing the heat out of it. Press one and it releases its smear of blue-green, which rises off the fingers and thins and is gone, and for a second you have seen your own hand.

They are, as the walls are, laminated. Lift a mat at its edge, where it has grown over the lip of a channel, and underneath it is another mat, dead, dark, half-turned to chalk. Under that another. The layers go down into the stone, and the stone is the layers. Break a piece from a wall in a channel's wet zone and hold the fresh face to whatever the eye can do here, and run the thumbnail across it, and the laminations are not all alike: there is a pale one, soft, that the nail sinks into, and then a dark one, harder, thin as paper, that catches. Pale, dark, pale, dark, in their thousands. The pale is lime. The dark is what the lime came out onto.

So the city is built by its inhabitants, in the only way its inhabitants can build, which is by lying down in the right water and being covered. The lime does not fall out of the water on its own, or not much. It falls out on them—on the sticky, alkaline skin of the mat, where the chemistry tips—and where the mats grow, the water sheds its stone, and where the water sheds its stone the ground rises, and the mat grows up through the rising ground and lies down again on top of it. Each lamination is a life. The walls are mostly dead mat. A lintel is a place where a mat lay across an opening in a spill of water for long enough. The dished thresholds, the junction chambers, the facades like rows of teeth: all of it is the mats, doing the one thing they do, for no reason, at the pace of a fingernail's thickness in a span of time that has no name here.

They do not know this. There is nothing in them to know it. They lie where the water is right for them and nowhere else, and the water is right for them in the shapes it makes, and so they take the shapes, and so the shapes thicken and endure. What the mat is, at any moment, is one film deep. All the rest of it is behind it, sealed, and it has never once been anything but the topmost layer of its own burial. It has kept exactly one skin ahead of the wall for as long as the water has been coming up. That is its whole life and its whole accomplishment and it is, when you kneel to it with your hand flat on a rising step, hard not to admire.

Where the water runs in its closed loops, the mats run in them too. There is a channel that breathes leftward around all four sides of a block and arrives at itself, and the mat that lines it is one mat, a ring, unbroken. Whether it is one organism or a thousand the dark does not say and the mat does not care. But there is this. Disturb it here, at the corner by the stair, and it gives its light. And around the far corner—forty steps, or four hundred—another smear rises and fades at the same moment, from the same mat, on the far side of a wall that the mat is on both sides of. The city's geometry is the water's geometry, and the mat has the water's geometry, and so it has learned, in the way a thing learns without learning, to be in two places that are one place. It is the only inhabitant of the city that lives inside the knots comfortably.

When the air falls—when the whole saturated volume lets go at once and every plaza sheets—the flicker that runs across the stone, the one that lets you almost see, is them. All the mats in the city disturbed together and answering together. For the length of the fall the city is lit by its builders, faintly, from below, from every surface at once, and then it isn't.

---

The reeds are a different matter, and there is only one of them.

They stand in the dry places, on the high old terraces where a channel has stopped running, in cracks and along the seams where a spillway used to be. Stiff, dark, a little taller than the knee, with a hollow stem that rings dully when a knuckle strikes it. Their bases are sealed in lime. A scatter of them here, a few there, standing so far apart and so straight that they read as set rather than grown—a floor with rods left in it.

Break the lime around one and follow the stem down and it does not end in a root. It turns. It goes sideways, in the pale soft layer just under the floor's crust, and after an arm's length there is a node, and from the node another stem going up, and the stem is one of the others, the one standing a few paces off. Follow it the other way and it is the same. The reeds on a terrace are one plant, laced under the surface. The reeds on the next terrace, across a wall and down a flight, are the same plant, its runner sealed in the wall's thickness where the wall was once a floor.

Nothing pollinates it. Nothing carries its seed. There is no wind, no wing, no water going anywhere it could go. It has been reproducing by the only method left, which is to keep being itself, longer, in more places. When a channel dries, it comes up there. When the channel fills again, the lime takes its stems, and it stays alive underneath, in the runner, and comes up elsewhere. In the walls, if you break them at the right height, there are hollows—pencil-thick, round, running horizontal in the laminations for a hand's breadth and then vanishing into the solid—with a skin of black on their inner faces. Those are its old stems. The plant is a map of every channel in the city that has ever been dry, drawn one stem at a time and then buried. It is older than some of the walls. It is possibly older than most of them.

On the looped terraces, where a dry channel runs around a block and meets itself, the reed does what the mat does. It runs the loop. Follow the runner around all four sides and you arrive at the node you started from, and you have followed one length of one plant and come back to it without turning. Whether it has flowered, in all this time, the dark does not say. Perhaps it does. Perhaps in the fever heat, every so often, it puts out something at the top of a stem, in the black, for nothing, and the lime takes that too.

---

Then there are the things that move.

The pebbles under the wet film are not pebbles. Pick one up. It is round, too round, and its surface is smooth and faintly chalky, and if you crack it against the kerb it comes apart in concentric shells, pale and dark, pale and dark, and at the center is a grain—a pinch of black that was mat, a fleck of stem, a speck of something that came in on a foot, nothing. They are pearls. Lime pearls. They form where a drop falls for a long time into the same small pool and the falling keeps whatever is at the bottom of the pool from sitting still. Each drop turns the grain a little. The lime coats it evenly because it is never long enough on any one side to be sealed to the floor. It grows. It stays round because it is rolled.

They live—that is the word, in this place—in the dished thresholds, under the long drops from the lintels, in the bottoms of the junction chambers where spillways end. Put a hand into a pool under a steady drip and there are a dozen of them, warm, loose, jostling when the drop strikes, and one or two the size of a thumb's end, and in one chamber, under a drop that has been falling from a height for a length of time the walls are not thick enough to measure, a single pearl the size of a fist that shifts, when the drop hits it, by a hair.

They are the only things in the city that go anywhere on their own, and they go nowhere, and they do it only because they are struck. Stop the drop and they are wall in a season. Move them out of the pool and they are wall where you set them. The crunch under the foot on the plazas is pearls that stopped—a pool that went dry, a drip that shifted after a fall of air, a whole small population gone still at once and sealed and then trodden. The film of water over them is already making them part of the floor.

They are, if you like, the city's animals. They have the grammar of animals—they are round, they are warm, they gather in low places, they move when they are touched—and none of the vocabulary. They have never once done anything. They are simply what stays loose in a city where everything else is being laid down.

---

That is what the hand finds. There is also what the ear finds, and the ear is less certain.

Every sound here arrives twice. You learned it with the first pebble: the strike, and the strike again. It is the geometry. A sound goes out along a street that meets itself and comes back by the near way and the far way, and the far way is longer or shorter than it should be, and so the second arrival is early or late in a fashion the body cannot predict. Fine. It is your own sound. But stand still on a plaza long enough for the lime to start gritting round your feet, and listen, and there is a sound that is not the drip and not the breathing of the channels and not the tick of fast stone. A soft scrape on the wet, a pause, a scrape. A footstep. Then, a while after, the same step again, from a different quarter, at a different distance.

It is your own. It must be. You shifted, or your foot broke its small seal, and the city returned it along two routes and the second route was long. Or: the city has a geometry in which being here at all is a way of being here more than once, and the second walker is you, arriving by the far way, and if you went to meet them you would get there at the pace of your walking and the echo would fall behind you and the meeting would never be crossed.

Or the city has someone in it.

There are things that will not settle this. The warm air from the left is wetter than the air from the right, and moves, and has the rhythm of something breathing, and is the channel. There is, on certain walls, at about the height a hand rests when a person leans to catch their breath, a bump—a raised white bloom, palm-sized, glazed over, worn. Under a foot of wall it would be a bump. On the surface it is a shape the lime made around something that stopped there for the length of a few breaths and went on. Water does not stop for the length of a few breaths. A pearl does not lean.

The lime does not distinguish. That is the point of it. A hand and a mat and a fall of air leave the same kind of mark, which is a thickening. Whether the city is empty, or has one walker in it multiplied by its streets, or has had, over its long building, a scatter of walkers who came up the stair from the mud and stood too long and are now at hand-height in the walls, the walls will say only that something was there and the water covered it. They say that about everything.

---

And below.

The junction chambers have their lips and their spillways and their shafts, and the shafts go down. Drop a pearl into one—it is what they are for, it seems, in the hand—and count. It is a long count. Then a strike, and far down a smear of light so faint it is almost only the idea of one, rising and fading in a pool that has mats in it, mats that live in water hotter than the mats up here can bear, mats that have never been disturbed by anything but the drip. Then, later than it should be, the strike again.

The breathing through cloth is loudest at the shaft mouths. The heat comes up them steadier than it comes up anywhere. The drains were built first, or the drains are what the city is, and drains carry something away, and the something goes down there, into the dark under the dark, where the water is hotter and pulls both ways and has, if it has anything, a great deal of room.

Whether there is a body down there—whether the fever heat is a fever, whether the breathing is a breath, whether the city is a skin over something that lies in the basin's floor and has been lying there since before the mats began—is the same question as whether a lintel is a lintel. The shape is there. The shape of a throat, the shape of breath, the shape of a thing kept warm. Water pouring through openings makes it. That is what water does here. You can put your ear to the rounded top of a chamber wall and hear the slow wet cloth of it and not know, and the not knowing will not resolve, and the lime will be setting round your feet.
