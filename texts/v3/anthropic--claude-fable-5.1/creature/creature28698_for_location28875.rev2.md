Begin where the description ended, at the rock, but this time go under it.

Wherever the crust domed and stayed hollow there is a chamber beneath, black glass above and black glass below, warm, ringing, and holding a floor of water that has never been disturbed by anything larger than what lives in it. These are the only places in the basin that are truly dark. Water finds its way in through the hairline cracks in the dome and brings with it what falls through the world above — moss fragments, a drowned insect, the fine grey silt of everything that has died and been broken down and washed one layer lower. In this water there are long pale things. They have no eyes, no color, no pigment at all; they are the shade of a candle, and their skin is so thin that the water moves visibly through them. They breathe with the whole surface of the body. They lie on the glass floor with the current — if a seep of water an inch deep can be called a current — passing over them, and they take from it what it brings. Nothing eats them. Nothing knows they are there. They are the bottom of the basin's economy, the last stomach, and they may be its oldest individuals: they grow so slowly and are threatened by so little that the only thing that could end one is the failure of its own body, and their bodies have no reason to fail on any schedule. Some of them may have lain in the same chamber since the moss first closed over the dome. You will not see them. You will only hear, if you cross a hollow and the crust rings under your weight, the faint slop of something shifting in the dark beneath your feet and then settling again exactly as it was.

Come back up. The next inhabitants are the smallest and the most numerous, and they are the frost.

That fine white dust that lies over the grey fungus on the moss on the liverworts on the trunks is alive, all of it, and moving, and it is where most of the basin's flesh is concentrated. Each speck is a soft pale animal a fraction of a grain of rice in length, blind, six-legged or eight-legged, with a mouth for scraping. They eat the fungus, which eats the dead, which is everything eventually. They are laid as eggs in the fungus and hatch in it and never leave the square inch of it they were born on unless it is eaten from under them. They do not know that there is a trunk beneath the moss beneath the liverwort beneath the fungus, or a basin around the trunk. They know the texture of fungus and the texture of another of their kind and nothing else. They have never been dry. Their bodies could not survive dryness for a minute and have never had to. Their skins do not hold water because there has never been anywhere for water to go. They are the purest product of the place: animals that are almost nothing but wet surface and appetite, so many of them that if every larger creature in the basin vanished the roar would drop by only half, because half the roar is them — not a call but a sound made by the mass of them chewing, a whisper multiplied past whispering into a hiss like the hiss of a kettle that will never boil.

Under the leaves live their larger cousins, the many-legged things, packed on the undersides so densely that the leaf's shadow side has no color of its own. They feed on the film of algae and fungus that grows on the underside of every leaf in the standing wet. They are dark above and pale below like everything here, and they lie flat with their legs interleaved with the legs of their neighbors so that the whole underside of a leaf the size of a door is a single carpet, faintly stirring. They have never been on the upper face of a leaf. The upper face is where the water runs and where the light — such as it is — comes from, and neither is any use to them. When a leaf finally softens and drops, they go down with it into the moss and simply continue on the underside of whatever they land on.

The pools are where the frogs are, and the frogs are what a visitor would call the population of the basin, because the frogs are what a visitor would see.

They are not frogs. They fill the role. They are the size of a fist, squat, black or the near-black green of the water they sit in, and they are so thin-skinned that the heart is visible through the chest as a darker throb. They have no eyelids. Nothing here has eyelids; there is no dust to blink against, no glare, no dryness, and the wet eye of every creature in the basin has been open continuously since it formed. The frogs sit shoulder to shoulder in the pools and in the shallow inch-deep sheets across the flows, so close that a pool from above reads as a cobbled surface of small dark backs, each one rising and falling, each one with a green bloom of algae growing on it. Things grow on the frogs. The frogs do not clean themselves. There is a small pale mite that lives only on frog backs, eating the algae that lives only on frog backs, and there is no other place in the world where either exists.

The call is made by a throat sac that never empties. It is inflated on one breath and only partly released on the next, so that the sound is produced on both the inhale and the exhale — two notes so close and so quick that they fuse into one, a low pulsing tone at roughly the pitch of a struck cup. A frog begins this the day its throat sac develops and stops the day it dies and does not pause in between: not to eat, because eating is a matter of the tongue and the insects come to the mouth; not to mate, because mating happens where it sits; not to sleep, because it does not, quite. There are moments when its metabolism slows and its eyes film slightly, but the sac goes on. The tadpoles are laid in the gutters of the leaves above and wash down into the pools already half-grown and take up a space in the crowd, and the space is always there because a frog beside it has just been eaten, and the tone they take up is the tone the eaten one left off.

On the trunks are the salamanders. Every wet trunk carries rows of them, and every one of them faces down.

They face down so the trickle runs the right way over the gills. The gills are external, feathered, red, fanned out on either side of the neck, and they are the only vivid color in the basin apart from the pale bellies — a red that is nearly black in the dim but shows true if you bring your face to within a hand's width. The salamander presses its belly to the moss and points its head down the trunk, and the water running steadily down the moss passes over its gills and into its open mouth, and in the water are the specks washed off the leaves and fungus above. They do not hunt. They do not move. They filter. A salamander settles on a trunk when it is a few weeks old and stays for its life, and its offspring, laid in the moss beneath its belly, climb out and settle a few inches above or below, and a trunk is not a colony but a lineage, thirty feet of one family arranged vertically, the oldest lowest where the trickle is richest. They call, if it is calling — a thin steady sibilance made by air drawn across the gill fringes, so faint singly that you would never pick it out, so many that it is the basin's high hiss beneath the frogs' pulse.

Above the pools the insects hang in columns.

They do not really fly. There is no wind to fly against and the air is thick and warm and holds them; they beat their wings just enough to stay where they are, and where they are is a standing pillar over the water, a column of haze from the pool's surface up to the fern tops, so dense that light does not pass through it. Each column is a mating swarm that has never broken up. The larvae live in the pool; they emerge onto the surface and rise into the column, and in the column they mate, and the females drop their eggs straight down from where they hang, and they die in the column and fall back into the pool, where the frogs are waiting with their mouths already open. No insect has ever left its column. A column has no reason to move and nothing to move it. Each pool has had its column since it first held water, and though every individual in it lives a matter of days, the column itself is as old as the moss. It is more honest to think of the column as the animal and the flies as its cells — a soft grey pillar of the basin's air that has stood in one place for as long as the rock beneath it has been warm, thinking nothing, going nowhere, humming.

And through the fern layer, low, the dark things.

They are the largest creatures the basin has, and they are not large: a long body on short legs, a blunt heavy head, fur laid flat and slick and never dry, so that they shine faintly as they pass. Their eyes are round and black and small. They move at a walk, always at a walk, in a slow constant traffic under the ferns that never thickens into a herd and never thins to nothing — everywhere you look there is one, a few yards off, going somewhere at the pace of a person browsing. They are going nowhere. They are eating. They walk with the head down and the mouth slightly open and they close the mouth on what the mouth arrives at: a frog, a salamander, a handful of moss with the frost on it, a wedge of insect column. They do not choose and they do not chase. Their gut is a general gut. They are not predators in any sense a visitor would recognize because nothing about them is built for pursuit — they have no burst of speed, no forward-set eyes, no stealth, no crouch. They eat the living layer the way cattle eat a field, and the living layer does not move away from them, and so there has never been any reason for them to be anything but slow.

Each one carries a young one clinging to the fur of its belly at all times, and when the young one grows heavy enough to let go and walk, another is already born. They hum. Their chest makes a low continuous note that is the bass of the roar. They never lie down. They rest by standing still, and a resting one is indistinguishable from a walking one except that its legs aren't moving, and then its legs are moving again, and there was no moment of decision between the two.

In the canopy, hanging, are the round-eyed things, and these are the ones you would want to be someone.

They are the size of a small cat and have hands — real hands, five fingers, with the pads always damp — and skin more than fur, a fine dark nap over grey flesh that is slick like everything else. They hang from the leaf stems by their hands and feet, upside down as often as not, and move by reaching, slowly, hand over hand, so slowly that a person watching one for a minute might not see it move at all and would then look back and find it a foot farther along. They eat the salamanders. They reach down the trunk and pick one off its patch of moss and put it in the mouth, and the salamanders above and below the empty patch go on facing down. They whistle. It is a thin steady note from a narrow throat, the highest sustained voice in the basin, and it never stops either.

But it is the eyes. Their eyes take up most of the face, two great dark wet globes for gathering a light that barely exists, and they are open always, and they are pointed at things. The round-eyed thing hangs from a leaf stem and looks at the leaf edge, and looks at the trunk, and looks at the pool below with its cobbled surface of frog backs, and looks at the column of haze, and looks at the dark thing passing beneath, and looks at you.

Now here is what you have to understand about all of them, from the pale things under the crust to the eyes in the canopy, and it is harder to hold than anything about the rock or the cloud.

The roar you hear is not what any of them hears. Each kind calls in its own band, and each kind's ear is tuned to its own band and deaf to the rest. The frog does not hear the salamander's hiss or the dark thing's hum; it hears a single tone, its own, spread over the whole basin like a color, louder where there are more frogs and fainter where there are fewer. That tone is its map. In a place with no slope, no current, no wind, no gradient of heat or wet, the only thing that varies from here to there is the sound, and so the sound is the only landscape any of them has. A frog with room to move moves toward the tone to mate and away from it to eat. The round-eyed thing hears only whistling, and steers by it. The dark thing hears only its own bass. What is deafening to you is, to each of them, a single quiet note filling the world — a hundred thousand private near-silences laid on top of one another, and only a visitor, who belongs to none of the bands, hears them all at once and calls it noise.

And none of them hears anything else. Which is to say: none of them hears anything coming.

Watch a dark thing feed. It walks to the edge of a pool and lowers its head into the frogs and closes its mouth on one, and lifts its head with the frog's legs kicking slowly between its jaws. The frog beside the taken one does not move. The frog behind it does not move. The taken frog goes on calling until the pressure of the jaw stops the sac, and then the tone has one less voice in it, and a tadpole is already washing down a leaf gutter to fill the space. The dark thing swallows and takes a step and lowers its head again. There was no chase because there is no fleeing. There is no fleeing because fear is a response to the future, and nothing in the basin has a future — not because they will not exist tomorrow, but because tomorrow will not be different from today, and an organ for detecting difference has no work to do here and was never built. They do not fail to notice you. They have no faculty for noticing, because noticing is the registering of a change, and you are not a change to them any more than the leaf is; you are simply a shape that is there, in the way everything is there.

Consider what that leaves them with, inside. Not memory — memory is for prediction, and there is nothing to predict. Not attention. Not alarm, not curiosity, not the forward lean of a creature that expects. What remains is the body: hunger, which rises and is fed; the pull toward the tone, which rises and is answered; the weight of the young against the belly. Every state any of them has is a state of itself. None of them has ever had a state that was about the world, because the world has never presented anything to be about. They are the only animals anywhere that have never been uncomfortable — never cold, never dry, never startled, never hungry longer than the time it takes to close a mouth on what is already touching it — and never comfortable either, because comfort is the relief of a difference and there are no differences here. A visitor stands in the heat and the wet and feels heat and wet. They do not feel either. They have no more sensation of warmth than a fish has of being wet. The basin has been pressing on them at blood heat since before they were anything, and a pressure that has never once varied is not a pressure. It is simply the shape of being alive.

So no one lives there. That is the answer, and you should not flinch from it.

The closest thing to someone is the round-eyed thing hanging in the canopy with its great open eyes. It sees. It sees with more of itself than anything else in the basin sees with — the dim green, the edges of the leaves, the frog backs, the shine on the dark thing's flank, your face. It sees all of it very well. But its seeing is not for anything. It is not looking out for, or looking forward to, or looking back on. It is not comparing what it sees to what it saw or to what it might see. There is nothing in it that could do that, because nothing in the basin has ever needed to be done. It is the basin looking at itself with no other thing to hold itself against, an eye that has been open since it formed and will be open when the dark thing below reaches up and takes it, and will not close then either.

You would want it to notice you. It will have you in its eye — you, wet and deafened and warm, standing on the moss with your outside clinging to you like a smell nothing here can detect — exactly the way it has a leaf in its eye. You will be present to it and absent from its world at once, because its world was full before you came and has no room for anything that was not already in it. And after you go — if you can go, if there is a way back up through the ceiling to the land you never saw — it will not have gone anywhere, and it will not have stayed, and the tone in its narrow throat will not have changed, and it will be looking at the place where you were, which is the same as looking at the place where you are, which is the same as looking.
