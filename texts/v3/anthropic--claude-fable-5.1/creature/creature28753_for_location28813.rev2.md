You will not see them at first. You will be looking for movement, and the basin is full of movement that is not theirs: the char turning all at once like a page, the newts that are not moving and then are, the shadows of the ceiling-plates sliding across the floor as the wind shifts the surface a hand's breadth above them. You will look for something upright and pale, because that is what you are, and there is nothing upright and pale here. It will take a while to understand that the shape you took for a fallen column, lying across the open floor between two clusters, has a hand at one end of it and the hand is doing something.

They lie down to work. That is the first thing.

They are smaller than you expect, or shorter; there is a lot of them but it is arranged low and wide. Standing on the root-mat, which they do, a grown one would come to your chest. But standing is not what they mostly do. They lie along the floor with their bellies to the mat and their feet braced, and they move by a kind of pushing crawl that raises no silt because there is no silt, and when they want to be somewhere else they rise a little and swim in the slow, bent-legged way of something that is denser than the water and knows it. Each one carries a stone. A granite cobble from the shore, palm-sized, worn smooth. It rides in the crook of an arm or is set down beside the work, and when they swim from one place to another it goes along, held loosely in a hand that trails, so that they cross the open floor a little head-down, weighted, like something being carefully lowered.

The skin is the colour of the root-mat. Not by design, or not only. The same fine growth that films the columns films them, a soft green-grey nap that they do not scrub away, and under it the skin proper is thick and slightly loose and cool, and under that is fat, a good deal of it, carried over the chest and back and along the outside of the thighs and hardly at all on the face and hands. The hands are the exception to everything. They are long and nearly bare and the fingers are joined by a web to the first knuckle and no further, so that the hand closes flat like a paddle and opens into something that can tie a knot in wet cord in the dark. The nails are thick and worn to the shape of the work. If you saw only a hand you would think it belonged to a person. If you saw only the rest you would not think that.

The face is broad and low, the eyes set far apart and very large and very dark, all pupil in the green light. The nostrils are two slits that close. The ears are almost nothing, a fold; they hear with the bones of the skull, and they hear very well, which is the whole difficulty of this place for them and the reason the town is built the way it is. The mouth is small. The teeth are heavy and blunt in back for snails and the pith of the plant and fine in front for char.

They breathe the way the char drum: from deep in the body, slowly, and then not at all for a long time. A grown one going down from a chamber will take five or six breaths that you can hear from across the dome, each one longer than the last, and the last one will go on until you think something is wrong, and then the mouth closes and the nostrils seal and they slip out through the low door and are gone for the better part of an hour. The heart slows to almost nothing. The fat holds the cold off the core. When they come up again into air the first breath is not a gasp but a long controlled sigh, and the second is the gasp, and then they sit for a while with their chin on their knees, quite still, and the colour comes back into the hands.

Outside, they do not speak. There is no point. The basin is a drum and the plant is always playing it, and a voice in open water here is a small thing under a large thing. What they do instead is touch the column nearest them and tap.

Every column stands in the same root-mat, and the root-mat is one organism, and a knuckle struck against a stem near the eastern cluster arrives, thinned but clear, at a hand laid against a stem in the west. The whole town is wired. Their tapped speech is slow and mostly about position and intention: I am here, I am coming, I have found something, the rasping on this stem is worse, do not come. It has a small vocabulary and long pauses and is conducted, often, by two individuals who cannot see each other and have not been in the same chamber for a season. In the mornings, when the fish drum and the plant is quiet, the columns carry a low irregular conversation all across the basin, taps overlapping, a town talking to itself through its own floor. By noon it stops. The pores open, the frying begins, and the plant is too loud to be spoken through. That is when they go in.

Inside is the only place they use their voices, and their voices, in that dead close air, are low and unhurried and pitched near the bottom of what you can hear. The speech in the chambers is another language from the tapping, or the same one with all its parts restored. It has words for the qualities of stems. It has, so far as anyone has been able to tell, a single word for both patience and lashing, and another that means both the filling of a dome with air and the raising of a child, and it does not distinguish between a thing that will happen and a thing that has been begun. Sitting in a chamber through an afternoon with two of them, you would hear perhaps forty sentences. Between the sentences they twist cord. The fibre comes from the stems, teased out of the softening cores of dead columns, and it is worked wet between the palms and the shin and it is the one thing they make constantly, all their lives, so that every chamber has coils of it hung along the sweating wall and every adult has a hank of it about the waist.

The work outside is the work of persuading. A young column comes up where an old one folded, a green spear no thicker than a wrist, and it is watched for a year or two, and then one morning someone lies down beside it with a cobble and a cord and bends it a little toward its neighbour and lashes it there. The bend is slight. A hand's breadth, no more; the stem would split if it were asked for more than that. In a year the lashing is cut and the stem, having grown a little into the bend, is bent a little further and lashed again. Someone will do this every spring for the remainder of their life, and their child or their neighbour's child will do it after them, and after some decades the two stems will meet and grow into each other, and after some decades more the seam will be lost under bark, and there will be an arch, and a thing that was begun will have happened. No one plans a chamber. Someone begins an arch. Someone else, seeing where it is going, begins another that will meet it. The scoring is the same: a rib or a flake drawn along the inside of a curve so the wall thickens there and thins opposite, a little each year, and the stem curls as it grows, and the person who scores it in the last year of their life is scoring what someone scored in the first year of theirs. The tool that does the scoring is often a rib. There are ribs in the lake from the char, which are too small, and from the divers, which come through the ceiling and do not always go back, and from the builders themselves, which are the right length and the right curve and are the ones most often used.

The dead go into the columns. A column that has died and is softening at the core is opened low, and the body is folded and put in, and the opening is lashed shut. The eels do the rest, over a few seasons, and the ribs are taken out after and dried in a chamber and become tools, and the tools wear down over a generation or two and are lost in the mat. Nothing else is done. There is no marker and there is no ceremony that you would recognise, though on the day a column is opened the tapping across the basin stops for a while, and the two or three individuals who did the folding lie down on the floor beside the stem and stay there through the afternoon crackle, outside, in the noise, which they do at no other time.

There are perhaps forty of them. There have been more; the paths say so. It is not clear that anyone remembers a time when there were more, or that the memory would be counted as important. The number seems to be held the way the plant holds its spacing, not as a rule but as a thing that has been found. A child is born when a chamber has come free. This is not a decision, or not one that anyone is ever seen to make; it is simply that the pairs who go into the chambers together in winter come out with a child in the years when there is somewhere for the child to be, and not in the other years. A child is a very rare event. It is born in air, in the largest of the old chambers, and it spends its first months in air, on the sweating floor, and it is carried down for the first time when it is old enough to close its own nose, and the first thing it learns, before it learns to tap, before it learns cord, is how long it can go without breathing and how to know when that is. Children are raised by whoever is nearest. For a few years a child moves between the clusters and belongs to none of them, and then one spring it is found to have chosen a chamber, or the chamber has chosen it, and the spacing closes around it and holds.

Winter undoes the spacing. When the ice comes in and the snow lies on it and the light fails, the plant stops leaking and the small chambers go stale within a month. So they leave them. Everyone in the basin goes to the four or five oldest domes, the ones with the most air in them, and lies down together in the dark on the root-mat floor, and the fat and the closeness and the slowed hearts carry them through. They do not sleep exactly. They lie in a torpor that lifts a little in the middle of each day and drops again, and in the lifted hours they speak, very low, and twist cord by touch, and above them through the plant's ceiling the ice groans and splits and the sound comes down and fills the domes with something like the ring of a struck bell. They do not go out in winter unless they must. They eat almost nothing. In the spring, when the light comes back and the plant begins to fry again in the afternoons, they come up out of the old domes lean and slow, and go to their own chambers, and wait for the air to fill, and the distances reopen across the basin one by one.

They know there is an above. They watch the diver come through the ceiling-plates with its eyes open and take a char and go, and they have a word for it, tapped and spoken, that is also the word for the surface and for anything that arrives from it. Now and then one of them goes up. They rise through a gap in the plates into the thin cold air of the rim and lie on the granite in the wind, breathing, the great dark eyes screwed almost shut, for as long as they can bear it, which is not long. Most come back down. The ones who do not come down are counted among the dead and their columns are not opened, because there is nothing to put in them, and their names, if the tap-patterns are names, go on being used for a while and then are not.

Whether they are people is a question the lake does not help with. They build and they bury. They have a language with a word in it that means both waiting and binding. They lie on the floor of the water beside the stems that hold their dead. They have made, out of one plant and a few kinds of bone and an enormous amount of time, a place to be, and they keep it at exactly the size it can be kept, and they have not, in all the generations the paths record, tried to make it larger. If that is a kind of failure, it is not one they show any sign of feeling. Watch one of them in the long morning light, lying against a young column with a cord in the flat webbed hand and the cobble set down in the mat beside them, easing the green stem a hand's breadth toward its neighbour and no further, and it is hard to see it as anything but the thing the whole basin is doing, brought down to the size of a body: holding a distance, in cold clear water, with the light coming down slow.
