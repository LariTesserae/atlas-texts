The honest answer is: one thing. Everything after that is a qualification.

The plant lives here in the way a lake lives in its basin — not on the ground but as it. It is not clear that it is alive in any sense that would let you find its edge and say, here it stops being alive and starts being mountain. Kilometres down, whatever it has instead of stone may once have been stone, and been drunk. That is the only way anyone has thought to account for the altitude. It did not climb up here. It grew, and the thing it grew on became it, and the height is simply how much it has swallowed.

What it does, day to day, is drink. That is the whole of its visible life. It waits for the fog, and beads, and takes the beads down. It waits for the rain from its one quarter, and darkens, and crackles for an hour. Between times it moves what it has drunk across itself in slow tides — the pressure the pump houses ride is a tide, and the mill hands know its hours roughly, the way coastal people do. It is a body that has given up almost everything a plant does. No leaves that anyone would call leaves, no flower, no seed, no wood you could burn. It has kept the drinking and the thickening and the healing of cracks, and it has kept whatever it is that makes the sound.

Whether it knows about the town is the question the hands do not ask out loud. The wall grows around the bolt. The pipe becomes a vein. The roofs let in light the way a leaf would, and they are leaves, and the plant grew them where roofs were wanted. Either it does not notice the town at all and the town is a scab it is very slowly incorporating, or it noticed long ago and the town is what it decided to do about the noticing. The hands hold both opinions. Their practical position is that you put a hand on the flank and wait to feel which way it turns, and you do not need to know whether it turns for you.

---

The hands.

There are one or two per mill, and there are thirty-one mills, or one. Take the count at whichever number the fog is giving you. They wear oiled coats because the fog beads on everything and the rind's warmth brings it back up as a kind of standing sweat, and a coat that sheds water is the difference between being damp all day and being wet. Under the coats they are thin — not starved, but pared, like people who have found the least a body will run on and settled there.

What they run on is this. The polygon cracks hold water for a day or two after fog, and a hand with a tin cup and a good sense of the hours can get to it before the plant does. That is the water. The food is a paste from the pith baths — the same pith the mills cut for fibre, but taken before the pressing, when it is soft and grey and sweet enough to hurt the teeth. It smells like the chimney smoke. They eat it standing, off a blade, without much interest, the way you would eat something you had eaten every day for longer than you could recall. Nobody has seen them grow a garden. Nothing would grow in it.

They sleep against the walls. The rind is warm and the walls are rind, thickened and stood up, and a hand off duty lies down where the wall meets the floor with a coat over him and is asleep in a minute, and the wall — this is not something they discuss — is very slightly warmer where they lie by morning. They wake when the turbine changes note. They do not seem to wake for anything else.

They speak little, and the same things. The number of mills. The hour of the tide. That it is the plant drinking, that it is the plant thinking, that it is the mills echoing in the pith. If you ask a hand where he came from he will tell you the direction, and it will be the direction the fog comes from, and that is all he has. If you ask whether he has ever walked to an edge he will look at you with the mild attention of someone who has been asked whether he has ever walked to the horizon. None of them recall being young. None of them have seen another one die. There is a place along the tramway — the hands mention it the way you would mention a landmark — where a coat hangs on a bracket with nobody in it, and has for a long time, and the wall has grown a little way up the hem.

The thing the town does with distance, they have stopped noticing. A hand walks a trestle to the next mill and arrives at his own, and there is another hand there doing his round, and it is him, in his coat, and he nods to it and takes over the round and the other one goes to sleep against the wall. Or he does not arrive at all, and is the one asleep, and wakes to a turbine changing note. They are not troubled. They are the plant's people the way the birds are the plant's birds, and the plant has one body, and you cannot ask one body to keep track of which of its hands is which.

There is a story they have, which is not quite a story, more a thing they say when the fog is very low. That the doors are places where the wall was trained to thin and part. That before there were hands there were only doors. That one day the wall thinned and parted and a hand walked out of it in a coat, and looked at the turbine, and put his hand on the wall. They say it flatly and without any sense that it explains anything. It is a thing that is said. The hand who says it will then go back to his round.

---

The birds.

One on each chimney rim, always one, never two. Pale — the grey-white of the pith, not of feathers — and narrow in a way that reads as heron until you look longer and it stops reading as anything. The neck is too short and the legs too long and there is no beak you could point to, only a tapering. They stand facing the quarter the fog comes from. They do not turn their heads when a hand passes below. They do not fly.

Whether they cannot is the question. The chimney rims are warm and the birds' feet are down in the warmth, and nobody has climbed to see whether the feet stop where feet should stop or go on into the rind. The hands believe they do. The hands believe the birds are the plant's — that a thing this large, with no upward parts, no crown, nothing that reaches, grew itself something to stand on its chimneys and look at the sky for it. The birds eat the smoke; or the smoke gathers on them, white and heavy and sugared, and beads, and runs down, and they take it in through the feet like the rind takes the fog. They are not seen to eat otherwise. They are not seen to do anything but stand and face the weather.

When a chimney has no bird, the bird is on another chimney. That is the whole of what is known about their movements. A hand who has watched one all afternoon will see it there at dusk and see it gone at the turbine's next change and walk to the next mill, which is the same mill, and find it on the rim. Possibly there is one bird. Possibly the count is thirty-one, or depends on the fog. The hands do not name them. They call them by the word they use for a certain kind of bracket, the kind that has been in the wall so long the wall has closed over the bolt and only the shape remains. It is not a joke. It is the closest word.

---

The low quick thing.

It crosses the rind between two ridges and is gone before it resolves, and that is all it ever does, and it does it perhaps once in a season to any given hand. Nobody has seen it twice. Nobody agrees on what it is. Something with a back, something that keeps low, something the colour of wet slate against dry slate, so that the eye catches the darkening rather than the body. One hand says a hare, if a hare could be that flat. One says a coat, empty, moving with intent. One says it is simply the place where the plant is drinking fastest, and what you saw was the drinking, going by.

If it is an animal it is the only one, and it lives on a margin so thin it has to be the margin of attention itself — the gap between the corner of the eye and the centre. It has learned that being looked at is a kind of weather and stays out of it. It drinks the polygon water before the hands get there; they sometimes find a crack that should be full, and is not, and the plant has not had time. It eats — nothing. There is nothing. Perhaps it does not eat. Perhaps it is what is left of the things that landed here and were taken in but not entirely — a seed, a bird, a walker from the fog's quarter — the part of them the plant could not digest, still moving, still trying to cross to somewhere.

The hands are not afraid of it. They mention it the way they mention the coat on the bracket.

---

The cars, and the fibre.

The tram cars go along the cable an hour apart and no one is in them. They are the plant, too — the cable is the plant, the trestles are the plant, and the cars are a hard grey shell like a seed husk with a drum inside. The drum is the size of a house and it is wound with the fibre the mills make. The cars arrive at the mill they left. The drum is unloaded, and the mill fills another drum, and the car takes it away along the straight rail to the mill at the far end, which is this one, and it arrives, and is unloaded.

Where the fibre goes is the thing no hand can tell you. It is hauled off. It leaves. Somewhere on the tramway there is a mill that is not this one — the hands are certain of this without ever having been there — where the drums are unwound and the fibre is used. Used for what, they do not say. The fibre itself is pale and very strong and smells of the smoke. It is pith, cut and pressed and bathed, which is to say it is the plant, spun. The town takes the plant in and gives the plant back to itself as cord. Something at the far end is winding it. Something is being made of all this, slowly, out of the body's own substance, and the town is a hand on a spindle and the hands are the fingers of the hand, and what is being made is not for them to know.

If the cars live, they live the way the tide does: on the hour, and without wanting anything.

---

The things that show through.

The standing pools reflect a plateau that is greener than this one. The horizon in it is closer, the convexity steeper, as if the thing being stood on were smaller, or younger, or had drunk less. There are more chimneys and more of them smoke. If there are hands in the reflection they are too far to see, but it is a fair guess that they are doing their rounds, in coats, and that when the fog comes they put a hand on the wall.

Near the veins the shadows lie toward a sun that is not up. It stands somewhere in the quarter the fog comes from, low, and the rind remembers it and casts by it. Whoever lived under that sun lives here too, thinly — the way the previous occupant lives in a house, in the wear on the stairs. The hands sometimes stand in their own two shadows and do not remark on it.

And on the days when the rain is taken down before it lands, when you stand under a rain that never reaches you and watch the ground darken from below, it is hard not to think that something on the other side of the rind is drinking it first. That the pith is not empty. That kilometres down, where the plant has whatever it has instead of stone, there is a great deal that was once elsewhere and is now here, and is still, in some worn-smooth way, thirsty.

---

The fog. The rain. Always from the one quarter, always low.

They are the plant's only company that is not itself, and they are treated as company. The birds face them. The hands know their hours. The plant beads and darkens and crackles and turns, and for a while the whole plateau is a floor of grey and a ceiling of grey with a metre of clear air between, and in that metre everything that lives here is standing still — the birds on their rims, the hands with their palms flat on the walls, the cars stopped on the cable, the low quick thing gone into the not-being-looked-at — and the sound rises, and rises, until it is not a sound but the whole body of the place letting you know that it is one body and you are on it.

Then it subsides. The turbine settles into a new note. The hand takes his palm off the wall and looks at it, as if it might have left something, and goes back to his round.

What lives here is the plant, and whatever the plant has let stay. And what has stayed has become like it — slow, single, indifferent to distance, indifferent to number, drinking when it can and waiting the rest of the time — until the distinction between the plant and its tenants is the kind of distinction the weather has stopped bothering to make.
