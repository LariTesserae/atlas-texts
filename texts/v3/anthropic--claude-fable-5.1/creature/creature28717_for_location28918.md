**Who lives at the Pressing Ground**

Take the census honestly first, because the place invites you to inflate it and you shouldn't. In a day you will see one beetle. In a week, one beetle. Nothing with fur, nothing with feathers, nothing that swims in the trough-water or skates the fog. If you are the kind of person who turns over husks looking for the small pale life that should be under any damp thing, you will turn over husks and find husks. The mat has no worms. The water has no wrigglers. Whatever kind of life needs to be many, and needs to move, has not been invited, and has not come uninvited either.

So: one beetle. Start there and see how far it goes.

---

It is black and it shines bronze, and the shine is not a trick of the light. If you could look inside its shell you would find iron in it — rust taken up with the grease it eats, laid down in the cuticle the way the plant lays bark over the flywheel, so that the beetle is, faintly, armored in the same metal it lives on. It has long, slow legs and it walks the axle without ever slipping on the wet. It does not fly. You will never see its wings and after a while you will decide it has none, that whatever it inherited from things that fly it has let go of, because there is nowhere here worth flying to.

You take it for a scavenger. It is not quite that. A scavenger takes what is left over; the beetle takes what is *given*. The dying tendrils weep grease exactly where the beetle stands. The drip from above keeps a pool the size of a coin at the top of the rail spike, and the beetle drinks there. It is fed the way a house-pet is fed, on a schedule nobody wrote down, from a hand that is not a hand.

And it is not afraid of you, which you noticed already, but notice now *how* it is not afraid. It does not freeze and wait you out. It does not sidle. It goes on doing the thing it was doing with the particular calm of something that has never once, in the whole history of its line, had anything happen to it that it did not expect. Fear is a memory of surprise. The beetle has no such memory. Nothing here has ever surprised it, and it has been here — its line has been here — since before the grease it eats was a mineral in the ground.

---

Its line. You find that out under the husks.

Dig into the heap beneath the axle, past the fresh brown coils and the brittle ones and down into the black layer that has stopped being coils and become a kind of peat, and you will begin to find them: shells. Beetle shells, empty, split along the back, each one the same size and the same bronze-black as the one on the axle above you. Dozens in the first arm's-length. You do not dig further because the heap is warm and close and you have the feeling — not fear, the place does not do fear — of putting your hand into someone's pocket.

So there is only ever one, and there has always been one, and the one is many, end to end, like the coils on the shaft. The living beetle above, the dead ones below, and the wheel turning between them slower than a minute hand.

Where the new one comes from, you never see. You will want to say the pod: the boot-sized seedpod cracking like a gunshot and flinging its contents ten paces into the fog. Most of what it flings is seed. It would be easy to believe that once in a long while, one is not. You wait by a pod that looks ripe for three days and it does not burst, and when it finally does, on the fourth, in the dark, you are asleep on the rim.

Or you will want to say the amber. That is the better guess and the worse one, and you will come back to it.

---

Turn from the beetle to the obvious resident, which is the plant, and try to say where it *lives* — not where it grows, where it is at home.

The trouble is that it has no center. You looked for one. You followed the pump because a heart should be in the middle of a body and this heart is off to one side in a boiler on a cradle. You followed the groove because a mouth should be at the end of a gut and the troughs are not a mouth, or not only. The mat is the same mat everywhere. Cut it — you will not, but if you did — and it would be tissue all the way down, no core, no trunk, no place the plant would rather you didn't.

What it has instead of a center is its furniture. The flywheel, the pipe, the transformer with its porcelain rings. These are where the plant is most *itself*, because these are where it has had to be most particular — where the shape it was growing into pushed back and made it learn a circle, a cylinder, a right angle. Elsewhere it is vague, a green mat, a fog. At the iron it has features. You come to think of the machines not as things it found but as its face: the places where a thing with no edges has consented to have an edge, and having consented, has kept it for a very long time.

Whether it brought them, made them, or dreamed them and woke to find them real — the description of the place leaves that open and so will the plant. But it *carries* iron the way a person carries a grandparent's gesture without knowing whose it was. It knows the circle. It knows the rectangle with the squared corner. It knows the rivet well enough to grow a seed that could be mistaken for one. Whoever taught it, the lesson took, and the teacher is long gone into the mat.

---

The troughs are where you find the second resident, if you are patient and low to the water.

The trough-water is not clear. There is a skin on it, so thin you see it only where the light rakes — a film, pale gold, thicker in the sixth trough than the first. Touch it and it parts and closes behind your finger like oil. Smell your finger: sweet, and under the sweet, the yeasty edge of something working.

This is what sets the amber. Not heat alone, not time alone. Something lives on the water, feeding on what the pump lifts up out of the mat, and what it leaves behind is the thick sweet stuff that hardens into blocks. It is the plant's gut, laid out in the open in six rectangles. Whether it is *part of* the plant or a guest — a yeast the way a cow has a yeast — you cannot tell and it cannot tell you, and as with the heart in the boiler, the question softens the longer you hold it. It is here. It has been fed. It does its one thing. By the standards of this place that makes it a resident in good standing, and it may be the most numerous living thing in the basin by a factor you could not count, and it has no face at all.

---

Now the amber.

Go back to the split blocks at the bottom of the stack and look at what is in them with the beetle in mind.

A moth, wings spread as if pinned. You have not seen a moth here. Nothing here flies. A twist of copper wire — the plant does not make copper, or you assumed it did not. A thing that is a seed or a rivet or both.

You had taken these for accidents: whatever fell in the trough got set into the block. But nothing falls here. Nothing flies over to drop. The stillness is too complete. If a moth is in the amber, it was *put* there, or it was *made* there, and either way it did not arrive on its own.

So try this. The blocks are not preserving. They are attempts. The plant, which has learned the circle and the right angle and the rivet, has been trying for a long time to learn the animal. The moth is an early draft — it got the wings right and pinned them itself, having no idea what wings are for. The copper wire is a draft of a leg, or a nerve. The seed-rivet is a draft of a body that would eat iron and go on. And the one that worked, the one it finally got right, is on the axle now, black and bronze, eating grease, unsurprised.

You cannot prove this. You will never see a block open and a beetle walk out. But you notice, once you've thought it, that the blocks are stacked at the east side and the beetle lives at the west, and that the mat's slow push, over a season, moves things from east to west. And you notice that the beetle's shell, when you find a fresh one in the husks and hold it to the fading light, is very slightly translucent at the edges, and very slightly amber.

---

There is one more resident and you will not want to count it.

The beetle is not afraid of you. The place has no fear in it. But consider what that means from the other side: it means you were expected. Not you in particular. Anyone who would come down off the rim onto the mat and stand still long enough for the fog to close over their knees. The plant did not learn the right angle from nothing. Something with a use for right angles stood here once and stood here long enough to be learned.

The troughs are the length of a person. You noticed that and set it aside, and you are right to set it aside as menace — nothing here is a trap, nothing here hungers, the place has no more appetite than a clock. But set it down instead as *measure*. Somebody's length is the unit of this factory. Somebody was the ruler it laid along the shelf.

Come back the next day and the beetle is in the same place. Come back for a week. Come back for a season and watch a block set. You will find that the place has a rate, and the rate is slow, and the longer you keep it the more your own rate falls toward it, until sitting on the lip at evening with your feet in the warm you are not waiting for anything and not going anywhere and the double stroke below is the only clock you consult. That is what the place does to a resident. It is what it did to the beetle. It is, presumably, what it did to whoever first taught it a corner.

You are not going to be absorbed. That is not the offer. The offer is the beetle's: to be given a place at the axle and fed on schedule and never once surprised. You can pick up a block and walk out and nobody will stop you. You can also not.

---

So, the census, revised:

One plant, the size of a village square, wearing a factory for a face, with a boiler for a heart and a wheel it turns by dying on one side.

One yeast, or one guest, spread thin as a skin over six rectangles of warm water, sweetening what it is given.

One beetle, which is many, end to end, going down into the husks.

A moth that never flew, a wire that was never strung, and a rivet that might yet be a seed, all held in the light-colored blocks at the bottom of the stack, going dark.

And whoever taught the corners. Present only as a length.

Nothing else. Nothing that calls or sings. At dusk the beetle does not go anywhere; it rides the wheel. The rim carries it slowly down toward the husks and the fog closes over it and for the length of a long night it is under, in the warm, with its ancestors, and toward morning the wheel brings it up again into the plain grey light, and it walks the few inches back to the axle where the grease is, and eats.
