The inventory the visitor makes—rock, growth, air—is correct as far as it goes. It just stops one category too early, because the fourth thing here has arranged itself to fall inside the first.

Start with what is plainly alive, because it is the ancestor of everything else. The forest lives here. It is the native. It was here before the tiers were cut and it will be here after the dome finishes shedding itself, and it is the only thing in the chamber that has never been anywhere else. Whatever else lives here is descended from it, or made from it, or is it in another posture. Keep that in mind as the eyes go on settling.

Now look again at the yellow stain in the grinding hollows.

It is the color of the blooms, and the visitor takes it for residue: the builders ground the bloom to a flour and the flour got into the stone. But a stain does not have an edge that is slightly raised. A stain does not sit a fraction higher on the side of the hollow that faces the nearest fissure. Kneel by one of the hollows in the lowest gallery and let the breath come and go twice and watch. When the pressure swells, the yellow brightens—not much, the way the blooms brighten, a shade toward ember from a shade toward sulfur—and when the silence comes it dulls again. It is doing what the forest does. It is depositing. It is a crust that has spread across the hollow the way the blooms spread across a stem, and it is a little thicker than it was the last time anyone could have looked.

That is the smallest form. It is not, by itself, very interesting. Follow it.

It runs from the grinding hollows along the floor of the gallery in a line so faint it reads as a discoloration, and it climbs into the mouths of the lowest doorways, and there, just inside, where the red from the ceiling doesn't quite reach and the eye gives up, it thickens.

---

Every doorway on the lowest tier has one.

They sit just inside, on the floor, a pace back from the threshold—far enough in to be out of the direct fall of the ceiling scales, near enough to the opening to take the breath when it comes across the chamber. They are the size of a curled body, a large dog or a small child, and they are shaped like nothing—a lump of slag, a heap of the same grit that covers the floor, a plate of dome-glass that fell and half-melted. Their surfaces are the dull satin of the lowest tier's tool-marks. That is not a coincidence. The lowest tier is the most worn because it is the most inhabited, and the wearing is not the work of hot moving air alone. It is the work of these, shifting, over centuries, against the stone.

They glow. Faintly. From the middle of the floor you would take it for the ceiling's red caught and returned by a curved wall, and that is exactly the mistake the place is built to let you make. But the glow inside a doorway does not move when you move. It has a center. It is a little yellower than the dome, and it comes and goes with the breath.

And they hum. The visitor heard every doorway hum at its own note and credited the architecture, the width and depth of each cut chamber acting as a resonator for the tones from the flutes. The architecture does that. It also has help. Each of these is a slightly different mass, a slightly different shape, seated at a slightly different distance from its threshold, and when the roar comes across the floor each one takes it up and gives it back at the pitch that its body allows. The chorus of the doorways is the chorus of what is in them. When the breath dies and the chamber ticks, they tick too, a little out of time with the stone, and if you were very still and very close you could tell the two apart.

---

What are they made of? The same as the forest. Resin and mineral, glassy at the core, crusted at the surface, dry through and through in a way that nothing from the world above can be. They have never held a drop of anything. They are the one thing in the chamber that the visitor's own damp skin could harm—a fingertip laid on one would leave a print, and the print would be a craze of hairline cracks where the water flashed off and the surface, for an instant, cooled.

They take the breath as the forest does. They are turned toward the fissures the way the forest is turned, and when the heat spikes they go faintly soft in the same way and lean, and they are a little brighter and a little heavier afterward. They are not eating the forest. Nothing eats the forest. That part of the visitor's account stands. They are eating the same thing the forest eats, and they are doing it the same way, and the only real difference between one of these and a stem is that a stem stands still to do it and one of these, over the course of a season—if there were seasons here—does not.

They move. Not the way a body moves. The way a bloom spreads. The leading edge lays down a little new material toward the breath, and the trailing edge, starved, crumbles into grit, and the grit lies where it fell and is what the next bloom roots in. A hand's width in the time a bloom goes from bead to cluster. The length of a gallery in the time the visitor would need to grow old. They move toward the breath, and when they have come to where the breath is strongest they stop, and sit, and glow.

When one gets too heavy with what it has taken it does what a stem does: it cracks along a seam, and lies there in two, and the two halves each go on. That is the whole of their generation. There is no season for it. There is no pairing. There is only the slow accumulation and the split.

---

Here is what the town looks like once you know what is in it.

The hearths were not for fire. No one here could have needed a fire. A hearth is a place where something warm is kept and tended, and the hearths are where these were kept. Small ones, brought in from wherever the first of them was found or made, set on the hearthstone where the heat pooled and the breath crossed, fed on bloom-flour packed into their seams—which is what the grinding hollows were for, and why the hollows are yellow—and set out on the racks to cure when the flour was new and soft. The drying racks were not for drying. They were for these.

Why keep them? Look at the light. The dome gives a red that is enough to see your hands. A doorway with one of these in it gives a little more, a little warmer, a little steadier, and it does not fall on your head. The builders lived low, in the dark, among the flutes, and they wanted lamps. So they grew lamps, out of the only thing here that grows, and kept them in the hearths, and after a while the lamps were on every threshold of the lowest tier, humming, and the builders read by them—there was enough to read by, then, in the lowest gallery, on the nights when the breath was strong.

That is one account. There is another, and the place does not choose between them.

The builders came from somewhere with water. The hearths and the racks say so; they are the furniture of a people who once needed to dry things and to warm themselves, keeping habits the place had made useless. They could not have lasted here. Not for long—not without hauling in every drop, and there is no road to this place and the eye in the ceiling is a long way up. What does a people do, in a place that is slowly killing them, when the one thing that thrives is a thing that has never been wet? They might learn what it is made of. They might grind it and cure it and lie down on the racks.

In this account, what sits in the doorways is what the builders became. Dried down, coated, set, and left in the heat to go on, and the reason the town kept climbing—the reason the upper tiers were built fast and hasty and never occupied—is that the builders knew what they were becoming and how slowly it moves, and they were building ahead of themselves. Rooms for later. The last course of blocks at the top of the dome, set out in a line and never laid, is not a project that was abandoned. It is a project that will be finished by hands that no longer have the shape for it, and so will not be finished, and does not need to be.

---

Whichever account is true, the fact is the same: the population the town was built for is arriving.

The lowest tier is full. Every doorway. The second tier has one in most of its doorways and a thin yellow line running toward the rest. On the third tier there is one in perhaps a third of the openings, and the ones that are there are smaller, and the glow is fainter, and the hum is higher, and the stone of the threshold is barely worn. On the fourth tier there are a handful. On the fifth there is one, at the top of a ladder of glassy black stems that were once its own kind, in a doorway cut back into porous pumice by hands in a hurry, and it has been climbing toward that doorway for longer than the builders were here, and it has just arrived, and it has sat down, and it is glowing.

Above that the doorways are truly empty. Ready, provisioned, hollow. Waiting, in the exact sense that the unlaid blocks are waiting: for a thing that is on its way and will get there. The visitor read the chronology going up as patience at the bottom and urgency at the top. Read it again. The urgency was the builders'. The patience is what is coming up behind them, a hand's width at a time.

---

So this is who lives here.

Something that was either kept or was once the keeper, and is now neither. Something the place did not have to make room for, because it is made of the place. It sits inside the doorways of a town that cannot be seen from above and would not be believed from inside, and it takes the breath when the breath comes, and gives it back as a note, and brightens, and is a little heavier afterward. It has never been cold. It has never been wet. It cannot leave, because the eye in the ceiling opens on a night that would crack it like the dome-scales crack, and it has no reason to leave, because there is nothing above that it could take in and nothing it could give back.

It is not lonely. That would require it to have once been otherwise, and the two accounts differ on whether it ever was, and it does not remember. It lights rooms that no one reads in. It hums for the flutes, which do not hear. Every few minutes the ground inhales and the whole ring of galleries glows a shade brighter, doorway by doorway, lowest tier to highest, each at its own note—and then the breath gives out, and the glow settles back to ember, and the chamber ticks, and one of them, somewhere, in one of the emptier tiers, has come a little closer to the threshold it is moving toward, and the town is that much less empty than the visitor thought, and no one is there to correct the count.
