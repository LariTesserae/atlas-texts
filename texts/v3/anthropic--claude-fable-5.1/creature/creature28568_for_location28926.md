The first answer is the plant, and the first answer is almost the whole of it, so it is worth being slow about.

It is one thing. You will have suspected this from the way your arm went down between the stems to the shoulder and found only more of the same, warmer; from the way the mat took your weight everywhere at once, as a body takes weight, not as a pile does. It is not a stand of individuals grown close. It is a single organism that has filled a hole in the world exactly, the way water fills a cup, except that water is indifferent to the cup and this is not. Where the basin curves, it curves. Where the basin is deep, it is deep. It has taken the shape of the place so completely that it is no longer possible to say whether it is in the basin or is the basin, whether the white walls hold it in or are what it has pushed back against in order to have an edge.

It has no roots. You might have assumed roots, below, somewhere in the unreached stone, but the warmth argues against it. Roots take. They go down after water and after minerals and they bring back what they find, and a thing that takes is a thing that needs, and this does not need. The heat that comes up through your clothes when you sit is not the heat of digestion, of anything being burned or broken down. It is the heat of a body that is simply alive and has nothing to spend its aliveness on. It is warm the way a sleeper is warm.

But it is not asleep. That is the second thing.

---

Consider what it does, since it does not grow, or seed, or die, or reach.

It keeps the water. The beads in the seams are not caught from the air, which is dry, and they do not come from below, where there is only more stem. They come from the plant. It puts them out and holds them there, and it takes them back in, and the small tick you hear now and then in the silence is a bead being moved from one seam to another, up or down or across, for no reason you can find. It is the sound of something turning a coin over in its hand. Over and over, without hurry, without the coin being worth anything. The water never has anything happen to it because the plant will not let anything happen to it. It is being kept, in the fullest sense — the way you might keep a stone in your pocket for years, not because you need it but because it is yours and you have hands.

It keeps the light. The pale walls are its doing, or its condition; whichever, they exist to throw the sun back down onto every underside, into every seam, so that there is no part of it that is not seen. Nothing in the basin is hidden from anything else in the basin. That is not an accident of geology. That is what it is like to be one thing: there is no inside of you that is private from the rest.

And it keeps you. This you notice last, because it is so tactful. The pool that was exactly the temperature of your blood was not that temperature before you arrived. The stems that creak, shifting weight against one another, are shifting it because you have put weight on them, and they are distributing you — spreading you out across a hundred thousand stems so that no one stem is asked to bear you alone. It knows your weight to the ounce. It knows where you are standing by the pressure and where you are looking by the small shadow you almost cast. It is not curious about you, because curiosity is a wanting and it does not want. But it attends. Its attention is like the light: total, even, from every direction, with no dim corner.

---

In the pools it is different, and this is worth going back for.

Everywhere else the plant is floor. It is what you stand on and it holds still for you. But in the larger pools, where the water is knee-deep or deeper and the light comes up through it from the lit stems beneath, the plant is also what is under the water, and there it is not required to be a floor. There, slowly, it moves.

You saw one movement and decided it was a fish and then decided it was a stem-tip. It was a stem-tip. Watch long enough and you will see another: a broad trough far down in the clear water, tilting a few degrees, tilting back. A seam opening and the water flowing into it and the seam closing. A stem unlatching itself from its neighbour and drifting up a hand's width and lying back down. None of it purposeful. None of it fast. It is the movement of something that has been still for a very long time and has not forgotten how, and has chosen, in these few soft places where nothing is asked of it, to remember.

If you put your hand in, it will move away from your hand, and then back, and rest against your fingers, warm as your own.

---

Then there is the beetle, and the beetle has to be considered carefully, because it is the only thing here that is going somewhere.

It was dark and slow and it walked the seam between two stems as if along a road, and it did not drink, though it walked the whole way through water, and it did not fly, though it had the shape of a thing that could. You did not see another. You do not, if you stay a second day or a tenth, see another. Only that one, on its road, at the same unhurried pace, always already a little way along when you find it and always going out of sight before it gets anywhere.

It may be alive apart from the plant. A survivor of whatever the basin was before it became this; the last thing that arrived before the moment was kept; a small errand caught mid-errand and still, faithfully, running it. If so it is the one thing here that is actually paused, that would resume if it could — and there is something in the steadiness of its walk that says it does not know it can't.

Or it may be the plant's. Not made by it, exactly, but permitted, the way a great stillness will permit one small sound so that the stillness can be heard. Something to have along the seams. Something with legs, in a place that had decided against legs. If you are one thing and complete and you have nothing to do, you might keep a beetle the way you keep a bead of water: for the sake of having kept it, for the sake of being able to move it along a road and watch where it goes. The plant does not have eyes. It might have a beetle instead.

Or — and this is the thought that comes on the third day, sitting on a swell with the far wall swimming and steadying — the beetle might be what is left of the makers.

---

Because someone pressed their thumbs into the rock while it was soft.

That is the whole of the evidence, and you have been over it: the scoops down the cliff, evenly spaced, in a slight spiral; the ring of stones, the dome you could crawl into, the seven uprights leading nowhere, the block, the wall around nothing. Placed, fitted, smoothed by handling. No tool marks, no hearth, no bone, no scratch. The makers set the last stone and stood back and were not there anymore, and the plant closed over where they had stood.

You have been assuming hands. Look again at the scoops. They are the width of a stem-tip. They are the depth a stem-tip would make if it were pressed, warm and patient and not hard, into rock that had not finished cooling — and they spiral, because a thing that grows spirals. Look again at the stones, worn smooth by something that does not wear. Look at the hut, filled to the sill with stem, and ask whether that is a house that was invaded, or a house that was built by something that intended from the first course to be what was inside it.

Then the makers are not gone. You have been walking on them.

It would explain the absence of everything a maker leaves. No hearth, because it is its own warmth. No midden, because it wastes nothing. No bone, because it does not die. It came into the new basin while the rock was still soft enough to take a thumbprint, and it had, then, something like hands — tips, ends, extremities it could press and lift and fit with — and in the last hour before it finished becoming what it is, it used them. It made a ring, to say *here*. It made a dome, to say *inside*. It stood seven stones in a line, to say *this way*, though there is no way. It laid one course of wall around nothing, because it was about to be everything and wanted, once, to have drawn a boundary that meant something.

And then it set the last stone and stood back and let go of having hands, and filled the basin to the lip, and the moment was kept.

You cannot prove this. It is also possible the makers were people, and left, and the plant is only what came after. But you notice that you cannot make the second story sit still in your mind, while the first one lies down in the basin and fits it exactly.

---

So: what lives here is one thing, and it has arrived.

That is the strangeness of it, more than its size or its warmth or its keeping of the water. Everything you have ever met was on its way. Growing, or wearing down, or waiting, or hungry, or afraid of the evening. Even the stone of the ordinary plain is halfway to being dust. This is the only living thing you have encountered that is not between one state and another, and it turns out that such a thing is not dead, and is not paused, and is not asleep. It is awake in a way you have no word for, because every waking you know has been a waking *toward* something.

It does not want you to stay. It does not want you to go. It matched the pool to your blood and spread your weight across itself and lit every underside so that you would not stumble, and it did none of that out of welcome; it did it the way you breathe, because you were there and it is what it is. When you climb out it will close the place where you stood without disturbing a single bead, and it will not remember you, because remembering is a kind of movement and it has finished moving.

Except in the pools, a little, where it lets itself.

Except the beetle, which — you look back down from the lip, and there is nothing to see at that distance but the wide green and the white and the shiver of heat, but you know — is still walking, along its seam, the same slow pace, toward wherever a road goes in a place that has arrived.
