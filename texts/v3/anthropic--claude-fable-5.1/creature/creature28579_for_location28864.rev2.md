**Who lives in the Pith**

Start with the eyes, because that is what you notice first about the people, and because it is the light that made them.

They are narrow. Not the narrowness of a squint, which is muscular and tires, but a settled narrowness, the lids grown thick and heavy and low so that the eye looks out through a slit no wider than a fiber. Children are born with their eyes fully open and screaming, and for the first many frosts of their lives they are hooded — a band of tight-woven cord with a cut in it, wrapped and rewrapped as the head grows — until the lids learn. Adults do not wear the band except when going up, toward the skin, where the light gets worse. The lashes are very long and very pale and they freeze; a person sitting still for the length of a meal will have small white needles at the edge of each eye, and brush them off with the back of a wrist without noticing, the way you or I would blink.

What the eyes see is not what yours see. They have given up detail for tone. A face at arm's length is a soft thing to them, a shape more than a set of features; they know each other by gait and voice and by the sound of a particular person's frost cracking off their shoulders. But they can tell apart a dozen whites that would be one white to you. The white of tissue an hour old and the white of tissue a day old. The white that has water behind it. The faint grey that means a wall is thinner than it looks and might open. The particular dull, matte, almost yellow white of a place where the growth has stopped — which frightens them more than a mouth opening does, because it means something under there has gone wrong, and they walk around it.

They read the fog for distance. You already know that. What you may not have understood is how completely: they have no other word for far. A thing is *one fog* or *four fogs* away, and if the air is clear — which happens, in the still hours — they say nothing is far, because nothing is, because there is no way to know.

---

Then the hands.

Everything here is new and everything new is sharp, and the hands of the people are a history of that, if a short one. Cuts across the pads of the fingers, across the heel of the palm, along the outside edge where a hand braces on a torn wall. The cuts do not scar the way yours would — there is no dirt to get into them, the sap is very clean, the cold keeps them slow — so they close pale and smooth and are cut again. An old hand (and *old*, here, is a word for hands and for the deep breaths and for almost nothing else) is a hand gone glossy, the skin worked into something like the cast sap they make their pegs from.

They do not wear gloves. They could; the cord they weave is fine enough. But the hand is how they listen to the wall. Flat palm, fingers spread, held a while: and under it, the push. A wall pushing evenly is a wall you can sleep against. A wall pushing harder on one side than the other is a wall about to belly. A wall that pushes and then stops and then pushes again, a stutter, is a wall that will tear within the hour, and they mark it with a spiral and move off and then, sometimes, come back, because a fresh tear is food.

---

I had not meant to come to food so soon, but it is where the hands lead.

There is very little of it. There is the frost-eater, the clear slug — they call it the *walker*, since it is the only thing besides themselves that crosses the walls on its own — and it is eaten raw, whole, cold, and is said to taste of almost nothing, a faint sweetness, a faint salt. Its amber organs are the only color most of them will see in their lives that is not white or grey or the pink-grey of a person, and children will hold one up to the light before eating it and turn it and turn it. There are the tear-beetles, hard and dark and bitter, gathered by the handful out of fresh sap and cracked between the teeth. There is the sap itself, which is drunk more than eaten, thin and cold and very faintly sweet.

And there is the plant. Where a wall has just opened — in the first hours, before the frost takes the new surface — the tissue is soft. It has not yet hardened into fiber. It can be pulled away in long pale strips with the fingers, and it is wet and cold and dense and it is most of what they eat. They are, in the plainest sense, eating the inside of the thing they live in, and only where it is newest, and it has never once seemed to notice. A band will follow the sound of mouths opening for days, camping at each fresh tear in turn, stripping the soft edge, moving on before the edge grows hard and cuts them.

This is why they cannot be still. Not only because the floor will grow around them. Because food is wherever the plant has just torn, and the plant tears everywhere, and never twice in the same place.

---

They move in bands of five, six, eight. Never more than nine; I asked, and was told that ten people need a camp large enough to stay too long. Never fewer than four, because someone must always be awake with a hand on the floor.

That is the shape of a night. Three or four sleeping in a ring, the tent — a low pale thing of woven cord, pitched on hardened-sap stakes that will be pulled and carried — over them, and one sitting at the edge of the ring with a palm flat down. Feeling the floor rise. When it has risen enough that the sleepers are in a hollow, the watcher wakes the next watcher and they judge together whether to move the ring or wait for the frost. Usually they wait; the frost cracks and falls and that is the end of a *sleep*, which is their word for the interval, and it is not a fixed length and it does not have to be. Two, three sleeps, and the stakes come up. The floor where they lay keeps their shapes for a while, six shallow ovals pressed into the white, and then lifts, and forgets them.

They are, all of them, always slightly tired. It is the ordinary condition. You can see it in the way they stand — not stooped, but *poised*, weight forward, as if already leaving. Even their sitting has a leaving in it.

---

The language is what you would expect and then, in a few places, it is not.

There is the one word — it is a short word, a sound like a breath let out — that is the plant and the place and *here* and *it* and, in some constructions, *weather*. You do not need to distinguish. You are never anywhere else.

There is no word for shadow. When one of them was shown, once, by someone from elsewhere, a hand held over a lamp, the word they reached for was the word for a lens — the darkening in a wall where water is buried — and that is what they call it still, a *water-mark*, a place where the light has water behind it.

There are a great many words for white, and a great many for the sounds a wall makes.

And the verbs of growth have gone everywhere. A friendship thickens. A quarrel tears. A child, learning, *opens*. A person who has stopped — sat down and not risen, which happens, which is what their word for despair is — is said to have *frosted*. The dead are *sealed*. And when two bands meet, which is rare and by accident, the greeting is the word for a mouth opening, said quietly, a small wind between them, and then they trade thorns and news and go the two ways they were going.

---

The dead are sealed. Take it literally.

A person who dies is carried to the nearest fresh tear and laid in the soft tissue at its edge, and the band waits — this is the longest anyone waits for anything — until the edge has swelled over them. A sleep, or two. Then they go. The body goes into the white the way the tear-beetles do, a dark shape at first, then a darker place, then a lens, then nothing. Someone will find it, cutting a wall for another reason a year from now, and drink the water that has gathered there and not know. They know this. It is the point. To be sealed is to become water in the wall, which is the only permanent thing they know how to become, and it is not very permanent.

They do not mourn long. Not from hardness. From the same reason they do not carve pictures: the thing you would sit with is not there to sit with. You can grieve a person; you cannot grieve a place, because there is no place. The tear has healed and the healed wall has moved and the buttress is a different shape.

Children are born on the move, more or less. A band slows for a birth but does not stop, and the child is walking — carried, but *walking*, in the sense that they are on a route — before its lids have learned the light. They are named for the sound the wall made, or for the white it was.

---

What they believe about the plant is not what you would call belief. I keep looking for the reverence and it is not there.

They believe it breathes; that is a claim about mechanism, and they have evidence. They believe the breaths are its breathing and the mouths its mouths and that the wind between two breaths is the same wind that moves in their own chests, and this is not a poem to them. It is anatomy.

They do not think it knows they are there. They do not think it *doesn't*. The question does not arise; you might as well ask whether the frost thinks the wall knows. What they will say, in the flat tone — always the flat tone — is that they are something the plant *has*. Not a part of it, not separate from it. The way it has frost. The way it has water in pockets and walkers on the walls and beetles in the tears and, far down, the other thing. The plant has all of these and they are all brief and it will have others.

Some of the younger ones, who have been closer to the skin, say the surface is where the plant keeps its outside, and that if you stood on it you would be *on* rather than *in*, and they say this the way you would describe a wound. The older ones don't talk about the surface. When pressed they say it has no inside, and stop.

---

I said little lives here and what does is pale. Let me be more careful.

The walker, the frost-eater: forearm-long, clear, slow. It crosses the walls in a track you cannot see except as absence, a stripe of bare wet white through the fur of frost. It is not quite a slug; it has a stiffening along its back, a clear rib, and where two of them meet they lie alongside each other with the ribs touching for the length of a sleep, and then part, and neither seems changed. The people say they are the plant's tongue, tasting the frost, and this is a joke, but it is the kind of joke that is said often enough that it has stopped being one.

The tear-beetles. Dark, hard, thumbnail-sized, quick. They are the only quick thing. They come out of the sap of a fresh tear — out of, not into; no one has seen one arrive — and swarm in it and eat it and are eaten, and when the tear seals they are sealed in it, and for a while you can see them, a peppering of dark specks in the white, and then not. The people use them to read the age of a wall. Specks near the surface: new. Specks so deep they are a grey haze: a month, which is as old as anything gets that they can point to. Below that, the walls are clean white all the way through, and that is not because there were never beetles. It is because there was never *anyone*, and the beetles have gone too deep to see.

The flyer. I have nothing to add except that they do not agree it is an animal. A grey slip across the high brightness, silent, gone. Some say one thing, some say many, and a few — the ones who go up — say it is not a thing at all but a place where the light tears, the way the wall does, and heals over. They say it the way they say everything. You cannot tell if they mean it.

---

And the large thing.

Here I want to slow down, because it has become more itself the longer I have stood here and I do not want to leave it as a shape.

It comes up from below, from the deep breaths where the walls are thick and the light is a shade less unbearable and it is, slightly, warm. It comes slowly. It grazes, taking a bite the size of a house out of a wall, and the wall heals over in a day, and it goes down. That is what I had. That is what you had.

But consider the warmth. It comes up from the warm deep and it is a very large animal and it has been moving, and where it has been, the air is warmer than the air around it — warm enough that the frost on the walls has *melted*, not cracked. That is how the people know it has passed, before they see the bite: a stretch of wall gone wet and bare and soft-looking, the frost-needles fallen not in shards but in drops, the sap running instead of beading. It is the only warmth in the Pith that does not come from a body they can see. They walk into it without knowing and stop, all at once, and look at each other, and one of them goes forward and finds the bite and cuts the three marks.

Rude. That was the word I was given. It is rude to linger where it fed. I understood this as a matter of manners and it is, but I had the manners wrong. I thought it was the manners you extend to something dangerous, or something that eats. It is the manners you extend to something *old*.

Because the grazer is the only other thing here that leaves marks. And the only thing whose marks are older than a week — its bites heal, but its warmth stays in the wall a long time, weeks, and the people can feel it with their palms, a place where the push is different, softer, like new tissue that never quite hardened. And it comes from below, from the breaths that are a month old and then, beneath those, breaths no one has been to, and it must have come from somewhere, and everything here is new, and it is not. It may be as old as the plant. It may be *what the plant is for* — the thing that walks its insides and eats its walls and keeps the breaths from closing, the way a tongue keeps a mouth. The people do not say this. They say it is rude. They cut the three marks and they move off and, I noticed, they are quieter for a while after, the way you are quiet after passing someone very old who did not look at you.

---

So who lives here.

Frost lives here. I want to say that first, not as figure. The frost is the most populous thing in the Pith, and it is born and it grows and it is shed, and it is the model for everything else. The walkers eat it. The people count by it. The dead are named after the sound of it falling. Every living thing here — the slug, the beetle, the grey slip overhead, the pale poised people in their bands of six — is doing what the frost does: forming on a surface that will not hold still, brightly, briefly, and then somewhere else.

Except the grazer, maybe. And the light.

The people are leaving as I finish this. Not fleeing; leaving, the way they always are, the stakes already up, the tent rolled, one of them with a palm flat on the wall beside me, reading it, deciding. She takes the hand away and there is a print in the frost, and beneath the print the wall is already a fraction closer than it was, and she says the short word — the one that is the plant and the place and *here* — in the tone you would use for the temperature, and they go into the fog, and the fog takes them, and the wall groans somewhere far off and the wind comes and it is still again.
