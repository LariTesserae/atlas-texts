The honest answer is the one the place itself gives, and gives first, before you have gone ten steps in: nothing. Put your hand on the shelf. Put your ear to the floor. Sift the dust between your fingers in the dark and wait for something to tick or scurry or bite, and nothing does. There is no mould on the bed-shelves, because mould needs water. There is no beetle in the wood, because a beetle needs water and needs, sooner or later, to breathe air that will hold a flame. The spore-cases in the dust are empty, and if they were full they would be waiting for a rain that has never come inside this wall and never will. The place is sterile in the old sense — not clean, but *barren*, a field that has been salted. You could stay a year and never share it with anything that had a pulse.

And yet no one who has been in and come out again describes it as empty. They describe it the way you describe a house whose owner has stepped into the next room. So the question is not idle. Something is there. It is only a matter of learning to count it.

---

Begin with the thing that is most obviously present and least obviously alive: the plant.

We say *alive* of a plant the way we say *awake* of a stone — as a courtesy, or a habit — and the husk on the plain has pushed the courtesy about as far as it will go. There is no sap in it. There is no growth in it, if growth means getting larger. Cut it and it does not heal in any way you could watch. But it does something, and the something is continuous, and it has been going on without interruption for longer than there have been people to give it a name, and that is more than can be said of most things we call alive without hesitation.

What it does is breathe. Not metaphorically. The ground note — the *lung*, the *tide* — is the sound of a body drawing something in and letting something out, on a cycle so long it reads as weather rather than breath. And what it draws in is the burning part of the air. This is why the wick dies. This is why the spark goes dark before it lands. The hollow is not cursed against light; it is simply a space in which one very large, very slow organism has, over ages, taken for itself the portion of the air that a fire would need, and gives back in its place a thicker, cooler, sourer air that a flame cannot live on and a small animal cannot live on for long. The plant has made the hollow into a lung's interior. It has made it into the one place on the plain where only it can be.

Which raises the question of the people. And here the place answers, if you listen to the tide long enough to hear it swell and fall: when the lung is full, the air inside is at its thinnest and deadest, and when the lung empties, the outside is drawn in through the seam and for a while the hollow holds air a person can work in. The residents slept through the thick of the breath and rose on the ebb. *The tide governs the day* was not a custom. It was the shape of the only arrangement under which a second kind of life could exist in there at all: on sufferance, in the intervals, breathing what the plant had not yet claimed. They lived in its exhalations. They made their town in the time between one breath and the next, and when the breaths grew longer — as the husk tightened, as the volume shrank, as the cycle slowed toward the single held breath it is now approaching — the intervals grew too short to hold a life, and the need ran out, and they went back through the seam and did not come in again. There are no bones in the dust. Nothing here rots, so nothing here died. They simply were no longer breathed for.

So one answer is: the plant lives here, and it is the reason nothing else does. It is not hostile. It is not aware of you in the way that would be needed for hostility. It is simply occupying its own interior completely, the way a sleeper occupies a bed, and it has grown into the space of the town the way a sleeper grows into the warmth of a place someone else has just left.

---

But the plant is not one thing. Or it is one thing with many bodies inside it, and the bodies are the seeds.

Think about what a seed is. It is the most alive thing a plant ever makes — the whole organism folded to a point and set aside, complete, waiting, with instructions for how to become everything the parent was. And it is the only kind of living thing that can wait without water, without light, without air to speak of, for as long as it takes. A seed is a held breath that does not have to be let out.

There are hundreds of them in the walls. Perhaps thousands. Each one the size of a fist or a head, each one sealed in a chamber of its own, deeper in the wood than any tool the residents had could reach. When the husk contracts they turn in their cells and knock against the walls that hold them, and the knock travels. This is the sound the residents thought might be counting.

Now consider the shape of the town, and consider the shape of the seeds' housing, and notice that they are the same shape. A pocket scooped into the wall. A single occupant. A door that is closed — the residents' doors by darkness and custom, the seeds' doors by living wood. Rows of them, following the rings. The people, when they came in with their wedges and their sharpened spines and began to cut, were making chambers in the pith side of a thing that was already, deeper in, nothing but chambers. They may have known. They may have felt the knocking through their tools and understood that they were carving their rooms in the outer wall of a nursery. Or they may not, and the likeness is only the likeness of two kinds of maker faced with the same material and the same need — a hollow to be alone in, close to others also alone — and arriving at the same solution. Either way, the seeds are the town's original tenants. The residents built their terraces over a terrace that was already full.

And the seeds are the reason for everything else. The dryness, the dark, the air that will not burn, the slow tightening of the wall: these are not features of the hollow, they are the hollow's purpose. A husk is a thing that closes around seeds and keeps them. This one has been closing for longer than the memory of anything that could remember it, contracting by a hair's breadth in a lifetime, pressing its cells tighter and tighter around the bodies inside them, and it is not finished. When it is finished there will be no hollow. There will be a stone on the plain, solid to the core, with a thousand seeds locked in it like flies in amber, and folded somewhere in its grain, thin as a leaf pressed in a book, the negative of a town — the troughs of the steps, the notches at hand-height, the bowls worn smooth — preserved the way a footprint is preserved when the mud turns to rock.

Each seed, if it were ever to wake, would make another husk. Another hollow on another plain, another lung, another dark that will not hold a flame. Another wall that someone might come and cut into. The place is nested. It contains itself many times over in reserve, and it will never spend the reserve, because the seeds need what the husk has spent all this time making sure they cannot have. What lives here, in the walls, is a future that has been carefully arranged to never arrive. It knocks.

---

There is a third resident, and it is the one the people knew best, because they lived by it. Something does move on its own in the hollow, after all. It is sound.

Every crack of the settling wood, every knock of a seed, every footfall there ever was — the husk is sealed but for the seam, and the seam is the width of a shoulder, and so almost nothing that is made audible in there ever gets out. It goes around. It comes back. The report of a splitting plank runs the curve of the wall five times before it is quiet enough to be called gone, and even then it is not gone, it has only fallen below what an ear can catch, and the wall is still faintly ringing with it. The hollow is a body that keeps its sounds the way other bodies keep their heat.

The residents understood this. They built for hands, yes, but they built for ears first. Each terrace had its note; each wall answered a tap with a name. Coming home in the dark, a person struck the wall and listened for their own level to say itself. The rope was plucked before it was trusted and told you, by its ring, whether it would hold. The town, in other words, was made to speak. It was a place whose whole architecture was a set of answers, waiting for the question of a hand.

And it still answers. Strike the wall where the lowest terrace begins and the note comes back the same as it did for the last person who struck it, the same as it did for the first. The names are still in the wood. The residents left, but they left a place that has been taught to say their words for its parts, and it says them, in a language of tone and duration, to a dark in which there is no one to hear. If anything lives in the hollow the way a person lives in a house — walking through it, speaking, being answered — it is this: the wall's response, still perfect, still pitched to ears that are gone.

The ropes have gone glassy. They ring now more than they ever did.

---

There is the wet, too, which cannot be left out. Once in a long while the tearing groan, and after it the sharp green smell, and at the new fissure a bead of something clear that hardens in an hour. It is not water. It is what the plant has instead of blood, and it appears only where the plant is wounded.

Run your fingers along the jamb of any door and you will find the notches — the household mark, no two alike. Run them a little further and you find that the notches are cut not into wood but into something harder and smoother, a scar of glass. Every door was a wound. Every terrace, every step, every bed-shelf, every bowl worn into the floor: each cut the residents made, the plant answered, weeping into the fresh edge and sealing it, so that the whole town is not carved wood but carved wood *glazed* — finished, by the plant, in the only way it had. The residents shaped; the plant sealed. Neither could have made the town alone. It is a collaboration between hands and resin, and the resin is still here, and the hands are not, and the resin still rises to close whatever opens.

This is the sense in which the town was grown rather than built. Not that the wall wanted it, not that the notches were there before the doors. But that every mark a person made in this place was received, and sealed, and kept, by something that does not forget because it cannot; that holds every injury as glass. A living thing that scars is a living thing that remembers. This one has remembered every cut.

---

So what lives there?

One organism, very old, finishing. Its life is a rate rather than a state. It breathes on a cycle you would take for the seasons. It has claimed the air in its own interior so wholly that nothing else can burn there, and it did this not out of hunger but out of the ordinary thrift of a body that is drawing everything it has back toward its centre, where the seeds are. The seeds are alive the way a thought not yet thought is alive, and they turn in their cells and knock, and the plant tightens around them by a hair's breadth in a lifetime, and will go on tightening until there is no room left in the hollow for a knock to travel.

For a while — a long while by the reckoning of the people, a moment by the plant's — something else lived in there with it. It came in through the seam and breathed in the intervals and cut rooms in the soft inner wall, and the plant wept into every cut and glazed it and kept it. It gave the plant's parts names in a tongue of tones. It ate the plant's seeds, ground to meal in bowls worn into the plant's own floor, and it slept on the plant's shelves in the plant's dark, and it went out again when the breaths grew too long to live between, and it is gone.

What remains is a body that has taken the shape of its tenant into itself. The worn steps, the smooth bowls, the notches in glass, the names that come back when you tap: these are not ruins on the plant. They are in the plant, now, the way a carved initial is in a tree twenty years on — not on the bark anymore but under it, drawn inward, a crease in the grain. The town is a thing the plant is in the process of swallowing, gently, the way it has swallowed everything else that ever happened to it. It is closing over its wound. It is closing over the whole idea of having been open.

That is what lives in the hollow on the plain: a single thing, asleep in every way but one, drawing in its breath, tightening its hand around what it means to keep, and folding into its own body — patiently, without malice, without notice — the shape of everyone who was ever there. Somewhere in the wall a seed turns over. The knock goes around the chamber and comes back to where it began. The rope on the third terrace rings a little, on its own, in the air the note has stirred, and says its name, and no one is there, and the name is said anyway, and the deep note rises under it, and does not, yet, let go.
