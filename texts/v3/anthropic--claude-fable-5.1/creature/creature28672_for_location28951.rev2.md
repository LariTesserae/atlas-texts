The first ones you meet are the ones that don't live here.

The hummingbirds. You take them for residents because they are the only animals that behave as though you don't matter, and that is usually a sign of home. But watch one for long enough and it leaves. Not to the next tree—down. It drops off the lip of the dome into the cloud and does not come back, and an hour later a different one arrives from below, from the valley, from wherever the valley goes, and works the same orchids. They are travelers. The massif is a station for them, a place with warm flowers at every altitude where the lowland has gone hot in some other way, and they move up and down through the cloud all day carrying pollen in one direction and something else in the other. Look at the feathers on the back of one when it hovers close. The iridescence is dulled at the shoulders, where the film settles on anything that faces up. They bring it with them from below. They shake it off on the flowers. The orchids take it into their throats and do whatever orchids do with it.

So the hummingbirds are not what lives here. They are the mountain's breath going in and out. What lives here is what stays.

---

Start with the smallest, because they are the most numerous and the most sealed. Every bromeliad tank—and there are more of them than there are trees, several to a trunk, some the size of a cupped hand and some you could put your head in—holds a body of warm water that has never touched the ground and never will. In each one there is a population. Larvae of things with no adult form you will ever see, because the adult lives a night and dies into the same tank it came from. A small pale crab in some of them, flat as a coin, that has never known a stream. Frog eggs strung along the inside of the leaf like beads of clearer water. And in most of them, at the bottom, a frog, a thumbnail of a frog, grey-green and slick and usually not visible until it has decided to be. The frog that calls twice and stops is calling from one of these. It is calling to another tank on another tree, and when the answer comes—one note, or none—it goes quiet and stays quiet for a length of time that has nothing to do with you.

These are the mountain's shortest lives, and its most complete. A tank frog may live its whole span inside a radius of three meters and never see the ground, never see the sky, never see rain, only the constant drip arriving over the lip of its leaf. When the film comes down with the drip, it comes into the tank, and the tank water is a few shades cloudier than it was, and the frog's skin is a few shades darker, and the eggs hatch a few fewer, or a few more—it is not clear which. What is clear is that the tank goes on. The bromeliad grows a new leaf and the old one rots into the cup and the water is renewed at the pace of the drip and the small sealed world adjusts its chemistry by a degree and is still there.

---

The snails are next, because they are the ones you can actually watch doing the thing the whole mountain is doing.

They live on the wet rock, mostly on the pale crust around the hot pools and on the lichen that grows over the crust. They are enormous, as you've seen, and their shells are banded—russet, cream, a green that is almost black, a stripe the color of the rainbow sheen on the pool water. You understand the bands as camouflage against the lichen, and that is true. But sit by a pool and watch one for an hour and you will see what the bands are made of. The snail rasps at the crust. Not the lichen—the crust beneath it, the mineral deposit that the pool has been precipitating out of itself for as long as it has been hot. It takes the crust into its body and lays it down, slowly, at the growing edge of the shell. The shell is the pool's chemistry, laid down in rings. Find a very old one, one the size of two fists, and turn it over. The innermost whorls, the oldest, are cream and rust. The outermost, the newest, have the dark stripe. The sheen-colored band appears about a third of the way out and has been in every ring since.

They are not fast and they are not many, but they are long. No one knows how long. They move across the rock at the speed of the shadow of a cloud, and when two of them meet they stop and touch for a while and then go on, and when one dies it is simply there, on the rock, and within a season the moss has it and within a few seasons you cannot tell it from the stone. Its shell goes back into the crust. The crust goes into the next snail. This is what they have always done, and they are doing it now with what is in the crust now.

---

The salamanders you know already—the long dark ones in the warm pools, the pop of the surface as they breathe. What you may not have understood is that they never leave. Not the pool, and not the species. Each pool has its own kind. Go to a pool a valley away and the salamanders are a slightly different shape, a slightly different dark, with a slightly different pop. They have been separated for as long as the pools have been separate, which is as long as the domes have been worn apart from each other, which is longer than there has been cloud forest here at all. They are older than the trees. They were in the hot water when the summits were bare rock and the pools were the only warm places on a cold mountain, and they simply stayed while everything else arrived around them.

They eat what falls in. That is the whole of it. Leaves, insects, a hummingbird that misjudged, the drip with whatever the drip carries. Some pools are too hot even for them—those are the new ones, the ones that have opened in the last while, that steam a little harder than the rest—and those pools are empty, or empty of anything you can see. The oldest, coolest pools have the most salamanders and the darkest, and the ones nearest the rim, where the sheen is, have salamanders whose skin has a sheen to it too. Not sick. Not thriving. Present. Breathing the steam with the same pop, coming up on the same slow rhythm they've always come up on—and if you time it, if you lie on the warm rock with your hand on the stone and count, the interval between one salamander's breath and the next is not far off the interval of the pulse in the rock. It is not the same. But it is not far off.

---

Now the heavy thing in the canopy.

You will not see it move. That is the first thing to understand. You will hear it, as you have—a shift overhead, a cascade of drops, a bough easing under weight—and you will look up and there will be nothing but the moss columns that are the trees and the moss columns that are the lianas and, if you look for long enough, one moss column that is neither. It hangs where a limb might hang. It has the same soft green shape, the same drip off its underside, the same bromeliad wedged into what you took for a fork. It is not a limb. It is an animal, and the moss is growing on it, and the bromeliad is rooted in its back, and the tank in the bromeliad has a frog in it, and the frog is the one that called.

It is large, though it is hard to say how large, because so much of what you're seeing is what grows on it. It has long arms, or long forelimbs, and it hangs by them for most of its life, and it moves through the canopy at the speed the cloud moves through the canopy—which is to say that if you look away and look back it will have gone somewhere, and if you keep looking it will not appear to have gone anywhere at all. It eats the epiphytes. Not the trees; the second forest on the trees. Bromeliad hearts, orchid stems, moss by the mouthful, and whatever is in the moss, and whatever is in the tanks. It is a slow grazer of a hanging meadow, and the meadow grows back behind it, and grows on it.

You will not see its face except by accident. Once, maybe, in the whole time you are here, the cloud will thin at the wrong moment and you will look up and there will be an eye in the green. Dark, wet, unhurried. It will look at you the way the hummingbird looks at you—as a question about whether you are a flower—and it will reach the same conclusion, and the moss will close over the eye, or the eye will close, and that will be all.

They are not many. One to a dome, perhaps, or two. They do not call. Whatever passes between them passes in the cloud, at the cliff edges, when the view opens across the valley and something hanging in the green on this side and something hanging in the green on the far side are, for a few minutes, in sight of each other. And then the cloud comes in.

---

The two-note call is not theirs. You assumed it was and you were wrong, and being wrong about it is how you begin to understand what actually lives here.

The callers are in the valleys. Not on the walls—at the floors, in the part of the mountain you have never seen and will not see, where the cloud is thickest and the heat is greatest and everything that drips from every leaf on every dome eventually arrives. Think about the shape of the place. The domes are catchments. The moss holds what falls and lets it go slowly, and it goes down, always down, off the lips in the white threads that dissolve before they land, into the hot wet dark at the bottom of the cuts. Everything the mountain takes out of the cloud ends up there. The film. The sheen. The darkened water from the tanks. The valley floors are the last place. They are where the exhalation from below, having been caught and held and passed leaf to leaf down the whole height of the massif, finally comes to rest.

Something lives in that. Something large, from the carry of the voice—the two notes cross a kilometer of cloud without losing their shape. Something few, because you never hear two from the same valley. Something old in the same way the salamanders are old, that was in the hot dark before the forest, that has been eating whatever precipitates out of the deepest, hottest water since there was water there to precipitate. Perhaps they are what the hanging ones become. That would fit: a thing that grazes the canopy for its long youth, growing heavier, growing more garden than animal, until the branches will no longer hold it and it goes down the wall and does not come back up, and takes up the other life, the one at the bottom, and learns the call. Perhaps not. You will not know. The point is that no one has ever seen one, not because they hide, but because there is nothing up here that goes down there.

Listen to the sequence again. One calls. Another answers from the next valley over, and the next, and the next, around the range, each in turn—not overlapping, never two at once—and then the whole of it stops on a single beat as though the last one had said enough. It is not a chorus. It is something being passed along and checked. From each valley floor, a report: the water is this warm, the dark is this thick, what came down today was this much. Passed around the ring of the massif in the only voice deep enough to carry through the cloud, and when it has gone all the way round and come back to the one that started it, they stop, because there is nothing to add until tomorrow.

Maybe that is not what it is. Maybe it is only a call. But the stopping is too clean for only a call, and you have been here long enough now to stop insisting on the simpler thing.

---

Here is what you come to, standing at the edge with your hand on the warm stone and the pulse in it under your palm.

Everything that lives here lives by taking in. The tank frog takes the drip. The snail takes the crust. The salamander takes what falls into the pool. The hanging one takes the second forest and grows the second forest on itself. The callers at the bottom take what everything above them has already taken and let go. Nothing here hunts, not loudly; nothing here runs. Death is a slow grown-over thing, a column of moss that used to be an animal, indistinguishable from the columns that used to be trees. It is a mountain of ending places. What arrives, stays. What stays is put away, a shade darker each season, into shell and skin and moss and the pale crust around the pools, and the mountain goes on being warm and wet and green and high, and nothing visibly changes, and everything has.

The pulse under your hand is the water moving in the stone. That is the plain explanation. The heat from below drives it through the channels in the fine dark rock in surges, and the surges come at the pace they come at, and the salamanders have learned to breathe near that pace because the water tells them when, and the callers at the bottom time their round to it because the round has always been timed to the mountain, and the hanging one in the canopy, if you could put your hand on it under the moss, would be warm at the same slow rate, because it is a piece of the mountain that happens to be able to close its eye.

What lives here is the mountain's way of taking things in. It is distributed into frogs and snails and salamanders and the hanging gardens and the voices at the floors of the cuts, but it is one arrangement, and it has one rhythm, and the rhythm is being driven now by two heats instead of one. The old one from beneath. The new one rising from the plains, with its film and its sheen and its dull orange on the underside of the cloud at night. The arrangement does not distinguish. It takes the second heat the way it took the first, into shell rings and darker moss and cloudier tank water and a sheen on a salamander's back, and passes the news of it around the ring of valleys at evening in two descending notes, and stops, and the drip resumes.

The cloud comes in. The hanging one, somewhere above you, eases its weight from one bough to another and a hundred drops fall. In the tank on its back the frog calls twice. Below you, in the last place, where everything the mountain has caught is coming to rest in warm black water, something very large and very old opens whatever it breathes with and waits for the dark to thicken enough to speak.
