Nobody lives here. That is the official position, printed on the sign at the cage: work site, occupancy by permit, no overnight. The crews ride down at the start of a shift and up at the end, and the district is supposed to empty behind them the way a stadium empties. But the pumps do not know about shifts, and the water arrives whenever the surface tells it to, six hours late, and something has to be standing there when it does. So the first things that live here are the ones the sign doesn't count.

---

The pump tenders. One to a station, sometimes two, on rotations long enough that *up* has become a word they use the way sailors use *ashore*. You find them by the wrong kind of light: every station has a cabin bolted to its flank, a shipping container with a window cut into it, and the window is always fogged from the inside because there is a kettle in there and a heater and a cot the permit forbids. The door sticks. Everything with a hinge sticks, here; the tenders carry graphite in their pockets and grease the world as they go.

They are grey. Not from age — most are not old — but from the place. Cement dust settles into the creases of the hands and the lime in the air dries them until the knuckles crack and the cracks fill with more grey, so that a tender's hands look like the floor: poured, jointed, sealed. They wear whatever keeps the wet off and they wear it until it fails. Their boots are the boots that left the print in the slab. They know it; the tender at H-5 can tell you whose.

What the place does to them first is the hearing. In a week you stop hearing the pumps. In a month you hear only the intervals — the four-second beat between two stations, the quarter-tone shift when a third comes up — and you can sleep through a roar but a station going quiet drops you off your cot like a hand on the shoulder. The tenders can stand in the dark between islands and tell you which of the eleven stations has a bearing going by the shape of the silence around it. They do not think this is a skill. They think it is what ears are for.

Second is the weather. They know it before the shafts do. The flumes rise a centimeter or two a good while before the roar arrives — the water knows first, the way a dog knows — and a tender walking past will glance at the mark on the channel wall and say *rain up there, noonish*, and go back to the cabin to move the cot off the floor. They know what kind of rain, too, by the taste. They dip a finger. Road salt in winter. Pollen in spring, a yellow skin on the lake at K-43 that the heron stands in up to its shins. One week in September, something like cider, which they still talk about. They will drink from the flumes, cold and quick and full of grit, but they will not drink from the water that comes through when the face breaks into old work. That water is warmer, and slower, and tastes of something none of them will name.

Third is the horns.

---

The horns were in a manual. Four signals: start, stop, hold, fault. The manual is on a shelf in the H-5 cabin under a coffee can and no one has opened it in a year, because the code has grown the way a language grows in a valley with one road in. There are about thirty signals now. Nobody wrote them down. They pass tender to tender, at the change of rotation, in the cabin with the kettle on — *this is convoy, this is face-through, this is rain-walking, you'll hear that one a lot* — and they have drifted the way spoken things drift, so that the signal the far stations use for *hold* is a beat longer than the near ones', and everybody knows it, and nobody corrects it.

*Face-through* is two short, a long, two short, and it means the drill has come out into something that was already there: a cavity, an old chamber, a duct with a different water in it. It is a courtesy signal. It tells you the flumes may change taste for a shift. *Convoy* is a rising pair, and if you are the tender at N-30 you sound it not for the trucks but for the heron, so it will be off the flume and up on the conveyor bracket before the headlights sweep the columns. It has flown into the lights once. It hit nothing, and came down at P-12, and stood for an hour without moving, and since then N-30 sounds *convoy* early.

There is a signal that means *heron in your flume*. There is one that means *cat at the plant*. There is one, a single long note held until the breath goes, that no one will tell you the meaning of. You hear it maybe once a rotation, always from the far end, from the station at the sump. The tenders go still when it sounds, and then they go on with what they were doing, and if you ask, they say it means *all clear*, and it does not.

---

The heron does not have a name. There was an argument about this early on and the side that won argued that to name it was to claim it, and it is not anyone's. It is the only inhabitant here that is entirely uninterested in the pumps.

It has a route. Morning — meaning the hours when grey light stands in the shafts — it works the flume at M-19, where the channel narrows and anything the surface has sent down has to pass in a lane a hand's-width across. It stands with one foot in the moving glass and watches the lane. What it takes is small: minnows the colour of the water they came in, the occasional worm uncoiling in the current, and once, the tender at M swears, a goldfish, orange as the lamps, from someone's pond up there in a storm. Afternoon it is at the lake at K-43 when the midges lift. Night it is at the sump.

At the sump it does not fish. It stands on the rail — its feet grip the cold steel the way they'd grip a branch, the tendons locking — and looks down at the black plaza of water trembling with the intake, and does not move for hours. The tender at the sump says it is waiting for something to come up. The tender at the sump says a lot of things.

It has a dry place under the conveyor bracket at N-30, where the spoil-river passes overhead and gives off a low heat from the friction of the belt, and below the bracket there is a whitewash of droppings on the floor, fan-shaped, the second stain in the district after the lime. The tender at N has put a plywood offcut over it so it doesn't get scraped by the sweeper. It is, she will tell you, the only thing in the place with an address.

---

The rest of the life you have to be shown.

The moss is a map of the lighting. Under every sodium lamp, on the sweating concrete, a green smear the size of a palm; between lamps, nothing. Walk the district with the lamps out — it happens, when a string trips — and you could find your way by the moss, if you could see it, which you can't. In the six-month quarters it has become a fur, and begun to climb, and the tenders there treat the lower meter of each pier as spoken for. They step around it.

The fern at tie-hole 4, pier J-22, has the orange ring. The tender who painted the ring has gone up and not come back — rotations end — but the ring is repainted when it fades, and no one has been told to do that. The fern has three fronds now and is growing sideways, toward the lamp, with the fixed intent of something that has decided where the sun is. It is not wrong. It is just not right yet.

The three pale stalks from the silt at K-43 were moved before the lake rose. Somebody dug them out with a putty knife and put them in a coffee can and the can is on the sill of the H-5 cabin, in the fogged window, in the light that comes off the station's own panel. They have put out a third leaf. Nobody knows what they are. There is a bet.

The midges are the weather's second clock. They breed in the warm skin of water on the pump housings — you can watch it, if you have the patience, a wriggle in a film no thicker than paint — and they lift off the housings in clouds and go to the lamps and turn in the light like slow smoke. The hour before a storm arrives in the shafts they all lift at once, every station, and the tenders read that too. The moss eats what the lamps kill. The heron eats what the moss doesn't get. The cats eat the heron's leavings and jump at the lamps.

The cats. Five, or six; they are the colour of cement dust and invisible until they move. They live under the batch plant near the face, where the mixer drums are hot and the ground is dry and the noise is so complete that nothing can sneak up on them. They came down on the trucks, or in the plywood, or with the rebar bundles, the way cats come anywhere. One had kittens in a form-tie box and the crew that found them re-routed a pour. The tenders leave the ends of things by the plant's east skid and pretend not to. When *convoy* sounds, the cats are already gone.

The eels are a story. But the tender at the sump drops in the end of every meal and watches the surface, and the surface, which trembles constantly from the intake, sometimes acquires a second rhythm under the first — a slower thing, a turning — and the tender writes the time in the log beside the pump hours. Eels live in anything wet and dark that connects to a river, and this connects to everything.

---

Now the things that live here without being alive, which is most of it.

The concrete, first. It is working. The tenders call the fresh piers *green* and touch them the way you'd touch the flank of a horse: palm flat, briefly, to see how it's doing. Green concrete is warm and smells sharper and is not to be leaned on; you can leave a print in the skin of a two-day pier with your thumb, and there are thumbprints, at shoulder height, on every pier in the newest hall, because no one can resist it. In a year they will be permanent. In a hundred, a mystery.

Second, the bloom. The lime that leaches out at every drip and crusts white on the grey — the tenders call it the frost, and the frost is the district's growth ring. A pier with frost to the knee is older than a pier with frost to the ankle. It moves at a rate anyone can measure and no one has bothered to, except at K-44, where the tender there has a caliper and a stalactite. The stalactite is under a permanent drip from a ceiling sweat-line. It was a grain of rice in the spring. It is measured on the first of every month and written in the log, and it is, at last check, three millimeters, and the tender treats it with the exact seriousness of a person who knows they are the first of a very long line of people who will measure this and that most of the line will not be people.

Third, the water. It is the oldest thing here, and it is only passing through, and it is always here. It was in the old work's channels before this slab was poured; the drills let it out sometimes, at the face, and it comes into the new flumes warmer and slower and full of a fine red silt that settles on the grit. It goes where it went before. It finds the same low places, because the low places were never the concrete's idea; the concrete just filled a shape the water had already agreed to. When the pumps stop — they will; everything does — the water will not notice. It will take a week, the tenders reckon, for the lake at K-43 to reach the lamp string. They have marked it.

---

And fourth. The ones who were here before.

They are present the way the boot print is present, lifted too late. Not ghosts. Nobody at the face talks about ghosts. They talk about aggregate. But the spoil that comes back along the ceiling on the black river is broken slab and rebar of a gauge nobody stocks, and in among it there are tie-hole cones with a different taper — a steeper cone, from a form bolt of a different pattern — and there was a brick, once, glazed the colour of a sodium lamp, with a thumbprint fired into the underside where someone had picked it up green. The tender at H-5 has it on the shelf under the coffee can, on top of the manual.

And at row W, before it was filled, the arch had frost. Thick frost — to the shoulder, to the spring of the curve — and stalactites that were not grains of rice but fingers, a whole hand of them in a row along the crown, and beneath them a shadow on the old floor that was not a stain but a mat: moss, dead, black, a smear the size of a palm, in a place that had no lamp and had not had one for a very long time. Which means that it had had one, once. Which means that something had lived at the edge of that light too, and kept to it, and waited for whatever their pumps were, and had a cabin somewhere with a kettle in it and a window that fogged.

The crews filled the arch and lined it and it is column row W. The map at H-5 shows it as row W. The tender who redraws the map paused over it, the once, and then didn't.

---

What lives here is whatever keeps to the edge of the light and waits for the pumps. The heron does. The moss does. The tenders do, in their fogged boxes, with their graphite and their calipers and their thirty words. The concrete does, curing in the dark, warm as a body, becoming the cold thing it will be. The water waits for nothing and is here anyway.

There is a new signal this rotation. Three short, off the far end, at the change of shift. Nobody will tell you what it means, and you are past the cage, and you are going to learn it the way the heron learned the trucks — by being here when it sounds, and seeing what moves.
