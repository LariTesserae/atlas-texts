Start with the census, because the census is what the room offers first and it is a kind of decoy.

Newts. Several hundred, perhaps a thousand — the count is impossible because counting requires a boundary between one and the next, and here a newt's throat is another newt's tail and a third newt's toes are spread across both. They are the small brown kind with the butter-yellow flanks, the kind that in an ordinary spring would be in a cattle trough or a cart-rut full of rain, lifting their heads to breathe every few minutes. Here they do not lift their heads. Their eyes are open and gold and they have been open for longer than a newt lives. That is the first thing to say about them: they have outlasted their species' allotment many times over, and they have done it without eating, without shedding, without the tail-fanning, throat-pulsing business of being a newt. What remains of newt in them is the shape and the eye. The rest has been quietly replaced by the arrangement. They are newts the way the walls are stone.

Isopods, in the gaps. Woodlice, pill bugs, whatever the field above calls them. They are not so much inhabitants as grout. If you took the newts out — you could not, but if — the isopods would remain as a gray lattice with newt-shaped holes in it, and the lattice would hold, and the room would still be full. They face every direction. They are not waiting. Waiting has a direction.

Snails, a few, sealed against the wet floor of the pools with their soft feet, their shells glowing faintly with the sourceless light so that each one looks like a small lamp with the wick turned down. The leech-thing, which is not quite a leech, which has too many segments and something at the front that might be a mouth or might be an eye, lying along the rim of the largest depression like a fallen bracelet.

And the fish.

Stay with the fish a moment, because the fish is different from the rest and the difference matters. Everything else in the room is still. The walls are still, the droplets are still, the newts' eyes are open but the eyes do not track. The fish's gills move. Not quickly — a slow lift and settle of the red membrane, then a long interval, then again. It is the one pulse in the place. It is the only thing that can be called an event. And because it is the only event, everything else is arranged around it: the newts, by whatever slow drift brought them to their positions, have ended with their eyes on the fish. Not most of them. All of them. A thousand gold slits with their horizontal pupils, aimed at a fingernail-colored thing in a cup of water the size of two hands, and the fish is blind and does not know, and the gills lift, and settle.

If the room has a heart, it is there. Put a stethoscope to the field above — you would need to know where — and under the ringing note you might find, just under it, so slow it could be mistaken for a fault in the instrument, a beat.

Now the hand.

Someone came. The handprint says so, and the eight stones say so, and the pot most of all says so, because a pot is a thing carried and set down, and setting down is a decision. Whoever they were, they came into the room when it was — what? Emptier? Darker? There must have been a way in, once; there must have been a time before the ceiling closed and the light began. They pressed a hand into the wall to test it, or to steady themselves, or for no reason, and the wall took the print and kept it. They laid a fire ring. They stood a pot in the corner.

The description says nothing of them remains but the shape of the hand and the choice of eight stones. That is true in the way a census is true. But consider the large newt lying belly-down across the stones. It is larger than the others — noticeably, the way one figure in a crowd is a head taller — and it lies not among the newts but on the ring, with its chin at the center where a fire would have been, and its eyes, alone of all the eyes in the room, are not on the fish. They are on the handprint.

I do not say the newt is the maker. The room does not permit that kind of statement. But the room has a rule, and the rule is that whatever enters is given a place and then closed over. A root hair enters and becomes a white thread. A pot enters and becomes a doorway for newts. A hand enters and becomes a hand-shaped hollow. It would be strange — it would be a violation of the only law we have — if a whole person had entered and the room had simply let them go.

So here is what lives there, offered the way everything about this room has to be offered, as a best guess made from outside with an ear to the grass.

What lives there is one thing.

Not a colony, not a population. The walls are its skin — that is why they are soft, why they have the give of cheese and not the give of nothing; rock does not give. The dimples are not failed crystals but the beginnings of scales, or pores, held at the beginning. The water is its blood, in perfect circulation, condensing and lifting in a closed loop that never has to move to move. The isopods are its connective tissue. The newts are its eyes — a thousand of them, and it has arranged them to look inward, at its own single pulse, which is the fish. The note is its voice, or its breathing, or the sound of its attention, which it has never once withdrawn from itself. The root hairs are what it eats: two or three white threads a century, taken from the field above and stopped and counted and made part of the total. It is a very slow animal. It is possibly the slowest animal there has ever been. It does not want, because wanting is a lean, and it cannot lean.

And it has one memory. Only one, because a second would have to be paid for with the first. The memory is a hand. Something warm and separate came down into it when it was young — younger — and touched it, and it took the shape of the touching and has held the shape ever since, and the largest of its eyes is turned to look at that shape and not at anything else. If it could be said to think, that is what it thinks about. If it could be said to feel, that is the whole of what it feels: the exact pressure of five fingers and a palm, sustained, never released, never returned.

The cattle stand on it. The rain does not reach it. Every century it eats a thread of grass. It looks at its own heart with nine hundred and ninety-nine eyes and at the place where it was touched with one, and it rings, and the ringing is the sound of a thing so completely balanced that it has become a single continuous instant, and the instant is lit, and inside the instant a blind fish lifts its gills, and settles, and everything watches, and nothing has ever needed to happen again.
