The hall is honest about it, and the honesty is the place to begin: nothing lives here that moves. Nothing with legs, nothing with wings, nothing that could turn its head at the sound of your boot on the iron stair. So the question cannot be *who*, and cannot be *what animal*, and it cannot be *what haunts* — because a haunting is a kind of attention, and there is no attention here. Whatever lives in this hall does not know you have come. It will not know when you have gone. The question has to be asked differently. Not what lives here, but what living has come to mean, in a room where the only thing that ever happens is that iron gets warm.

---

Start with the ferns, because they are the easiest to call alive.

They stand in the plate the way trees stand in a hillside, and they are doing what roots do, which is to go toward what feeds them, and what feeds them is the boiler. Not the water in it — there is no water in it, or none that would count — but the shell, the riveted hide, which the wet and the heat have been converting to scale for a hundred years and which the ferns are converting, a layer further on, to frond. They are the hall's mouth. They are how the hall eats itself. If you could speed them up you would see them do what the source describes, *go in*, and you would see the boiler flank soften and bloom and sag under them, and you would see that they are not growing *on* the works so much as the works are being slowly reshaped into a form that can drip.

They do not move. But they arrive. Every year there is fern where there was scale. If you came back in a decade the middle boiler would be a little more fern-shaped than it is now, and the ferns would be exactly where they always were — at the frontier between iron and not-iron, which is the only place they know how to stand. They are not travellers. They are a line that advances.

---

Then the buds.

They are the strangest thing in the hall to look at closely, because they are the only things that look like they are *about* to do something. A closed bud is a promise. It says: not yet. The ferns and the moss and the climber have no *not yet*; they are simply ongoing. But these pale waxy stalks, dozens of them, rising from the moss-pillows under the drip, leaning toward the boilers — they are holding something back.

What are they waiting for? You can guess. They lean toward the heat, so it is not more heat they lack. They stand in the wettest places, so it is not water. They have no colour, so it is not the light they are hoping for. The one thing that changes here, slowly, irreversibly, is the light *failing* — the scale on the mains thickening, the orange going to dried blood. Perhaps that is it. Perhaps the buds are waiting for the dark. Perhaps they are the form the hall will take once the coin-leaved climber has eaten the last of the glow and died, once there is nothing left in here that answers to light at all — and then, in the black and the roar and the wet, one after another, they open. Into what, there is no telling. Nothing here has ever needed a flower. But they are patient the way something is patient that has already been told the answer and is only waiting for the time.

---

The climber is the one that teaches you how to think about the rest.

It runs along the wall mains in both directions from a point that no longer means anything, its leaves flat to the pipe, taking the dregs of the light. It has reached, on the far side of the hall, the place where its two ends meet, and there it has made a fist of stems that neither end can grow through. The source says: *it is possible the plant does not know it is one plant.* Sit with that. Here is a single organism that has, over decades, spread to encircle a room, and at the moment of closing the circle it met itself as an obstacle. Each end pushes. Each end is refused. The knot thickens. There is no faculty in it that could step back and recognise the other as the same. It is one thing behaving as two things forever, at a single point, in the dark.

And once you have seen that in the climber you cannot stop seeing it. The ferns on the three boilers — are they three colonies or one, joined under the moss by runners you cannot see? The moss itself, closed over the rubble like a healed wound: how many mosses, or how few? The hall does not answer because the hall does not have that kind of knowledge. Nothing here has ever had to be an individual, because nothing here has ever had to be *recognised*. Selfhood is something you need when there is someone else. In an unsurveilled room it does not develop. It never had a reason to.

---

So widen the frame the way the hall keeps inviting you to.

The moss is the floor. The floor is the dead — brick, slag, rust-mud, fallen frond, everything that has ever come apart in here — with a skin of living over it, and the living is made of the dead beneath it, and it is a little higher every century. A body built of its own former bodies. The ferns are the mouth. The climber is the skin, or the eye, or whatever an organism has that it turns toward light when there is very little of it. The buds are what it keeps for later. The mains are — what? Not veins; nothing flows in them. But they are the long bones, the armature, the thing everything else is arranged along. The boilers are the great organs, the ones that warm and cool with the deep tides that come up from underneath.

And the wheel. The wheel is the one gesture. The boilers lengthen and shorten, and the lengthening and shortening has found a crank, and the crank has found a rod, and the rod pushes a hair, and the wheel turns a little less than once a day. It is not doing work. It is not driving anything. It is the hall's one visible motion, the way a sleeper's chest is the sleeper's one visible motion. A pulse at something under one beat per day. It has worn a ring in the moss. If you needed a single proof that the room is alive and not merely full of living things, the ring is it: the place is marking its own time, on its own body, with a part of itself.

The sound, then. You want to call it a voice, and it is not a voice; nothing is being said. It is closer to the noise a body makes if you put your ear to it — the gut, the blood, the joints. It is metabolism made audible. Every tick is a plate warming, and a plate warming is the hall breathing in; every groan is a plate cooling, and that is the breath going out; and there is so much iron and it has been breathing for so long that the individual breaths have fused into one continuous roar. The low grinding that circles the wall in forty minutes is the breath moving round the ribs. The bell-note from the middle boiler is — you do not know what it is. Something skipping. Something that happens in the deep organ that the rest of the body does not have access to and does not need to.

---

There remains the rock.

It is under the floor, and there is no way to look, and it was there before the hall and will be there after, and it is the reason for everything. Men put a boiler on top of it because it was warm, and the boilers were built to *take* the heat, to carry it up and out and turn it into something useful on the surface. The men are gone. The heat is still coming. And the hall has done what the men did, without deciding to — it has become a thing that takes the heat from the rock and turns it into something. Not steam, not power. Fern. Drip. Roar. A wheel turning a hand's width in forty minutes. Living tissue, of a kind, made out of a hot stone and a ruin.

Is the rock alive? No. But the rock is what all of this is *toward*, the way a plant on a windowsill is toward the sun. The buds lean at it. The mains hold its warmth. The wheel turns on its tides. It is the hearth of the body, and it is also a small buried star that the body orbits without moving. Everything that lives here is a way of being close to it.

---

There is one thing in the hall that does not belong to this, and it is worth naming because the hall is so busy unmaking it. On the hook at head height, the shape that could have been a coat or a bag or a lantern. It is the last object here that was made to fit a hand or a shoulder — the last trace of the kind of living that looks back. It has gone soft with rust and moss. In another century it will be a lump, and in another it will be a stain on the brick, and then it will be floor. The hall is not doing this to it out of any feeling. The hall does not have the equipment for feeling. It is only that the hook is iron and the coat is wet and everything here becomes what everything here becomes.

---

So this is what lives in the hall: one thing, which does not know it is one thing.

It has a mouth and a skin and a floor made of its own dead and a pulse you can only prove by patience. It breathes iron. It eats what it breathes. It has never seen anything, because there has never been anything in it to see, and it has never been seen, until now, and now does not register. You came down the stair and stood in the roar, and for the length of a visit you were the only surveillance the room had ever had — the only point in it from which the room was an object, a thing that could be described. To the hall, if the hall had a *to*, you were a brief cool patch that condensed a little water and left. You were weather. Less than weather; weather at least comes through the breach in the roof and lands.

It is alive the way a slow fire is alive, or a coral, or a lichen on a stone — the way anything is alive that has found a gradient and settled into it and is working it down. It is not going anywhere. It is not in a hurry. It has a future, which is the dark, and it has made provision for the dark, in the buds. When the last coin-leaf goes it will be a black room full of wet and thunder, and the wheel will turn, and the ring in the moss will deepen, and somewhere in the roar the pale stalks will open into whatever they have been holding, and there will be no one to see it and nothing in the room that needs there to be.

You climb back up. The roar thins to a hum, and the hum to a warmth in the treads, and the warmth to a tremor. It is easy, on the last step, to think of that tremor as the hall's pulse reaching up after you. It is not. It is the hall not noticing. It is the sound a living thing makes when it has never once been alone, because it has never once known that anything else was there.
