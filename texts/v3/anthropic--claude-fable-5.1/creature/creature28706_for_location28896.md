Start with the obvious answer and it turns out to be true, though not in the way you'd expect.

The pump-keepers live here. There are twelve of them on the books, two to a house, working in shifts that do not correspond to daylight because the water does not, and with them their wives or husbands and the nine children and a handful of old people who came down with a daughter and never went back up the dike. Perhaps sixty souls. You could list them. What is harder to say is what they are, because the place has done something to them that they cannot see and that a visitor notices in the first hour.

They do not finish sentences. There is no need. The roar takes the end of every phrase and swallows it, and they have learned to put the important word first — *bearing, Four, tonight* — and let the rest go. They stand closer to each other than people do elsewhere. They touch a shoulder to say *listen*, and they read lips without knowing they are doing it, and when they go up to the market town on the far side of the canal they are thought to be a little slow, a little rude, because they shout and stand too near and do not respond to anything said behind them. They come back down the dike with relief. Up there the world is full of small separate noises that each demand attention — a cart, a voice, a door. Down here there is one noise and it asks for nothing.

They sleep well. Better than anyone. The roar is a floor beneath the mind and they lie down on it. The night man at Number Six, who has been here since the auger first went in, says that in eleven years he has not once been woken by a dream, and he says it as a boast, and then he says it again, more quietly, as something else.

What kind of person comes to this? The engineers who visit have a theory: people who want to be part of a machine that is winning. But that is the engineers' idea of themselves. The people who actually stay are of another kind. They are people who have made a peace with holding still. The greaser, who rides the narrow rail from house to house with his cans and his rags and his knowledge of where the otters are, was a merchant seaman once, and says he never liked arriving anywhere. Here nothing arrives. The bearing at Number Two that was changed that winter had run for nine years; he had greased it eleven hundred times; he knew its voice; he felt its going the way you feel a tooth about to ache, and when it was out and lying on the floor, still warm, he stood over it a long time. He does not say what he felt. He has a shelf in his shed of worn parts, cleaned and oiled and labelled in pencil, and no one is allowed to call it a collection.

The woman who does the autumn survey is the closest thing they have to a historian. She goes from house to house with the level and the brass rod and reads the pins, and writes the figures in a ledger with a green cloth cover, and the ledger is the one document in the town that everyone understands. Number One: down five-eighths of an inch. The chapel: down three-quarters, and leaning west. She has eleven columns of figures now and she can lay a ruler along them and see where it is going. Not soon. Not in her life. But she is the one who knows that the whole place is a very slow fall, and she carries it around with her, and it makes her kind. She is the one people go to when something is wrong that isn't the pumps.

Then the children. Nine of them, and the eldest was born the second spring, so none of them has ever heard the world without the roar in it. Ask them what silence is and they will tell you it is what happens when you put your fingers in your ears in a cupboard, and even then they will say it isn't silence exactly, it's *loud in a different place*. They draw pictures at the school, as children do, of houses with smoking chimneys and a sun in the corner — and in every picture the water is at the top of the page. A blue line across the sky with a boat sitting on it. The teacher stopped correcting this some years ago. She came down for a year and stayed, which is the pattern, and she has come to think the children are right and the rest of the world is drawn wrong. When they walk up the dike to the canal on a summer afternoon and stand at the edge and look at the barges going past at eye height they are quite calm; it is looking *down* into the bowl that makes them uneasy, the way a child elsewhere might be uneasy at the top of a tower. Home is at the bottom of a thing. They know that in their spines. The three who woke crying that winter night were not frightened by the silence. They were frightened because something had *stopped*, and stopping is the one word here that means danger, and they had known that since before they had words.

So much for the people. But the place gives you the people first the way it gives you the noise first, as something to get past.

Look at what has come. The description already gave you the inventory — herons, eels, frogs, bats, rats, otters, hares — but the inventory is a list of arrivals and what matters is the direction they arrived from. The eels came *up the outfall*, against forty feet of white water. The otters came *down the dike face* from the canal. The herons drop in from above and stand facing the culvert mouths where the water is drawn toward the pumps. The swifts nest in the cupolas where the fan sucks the hot air out. Nearly everything living here has come from the water above, and it has come down, and it has taken up its position near the places where the water is being lifted. The frogs sing loudest around the settling tanks. The bats hunt over the outfall. Even the willows have found the channels and grown along them in lines, so that from the dike top the drainage grid is written on the flat in green, a map of where the water goes.

Now put that beside the condensation. The rails wet at nine on a cloudless morning. The water that rises out of the clay overnight and stands in the ruts and is gone by noon and is back the next dawn. The tidemark on the brick, two feet up and holding. The buildings going down at a finger's width a year into ground that has not stopped being soft. And the sound itself, which is not a machine's sound but water's — falling, all the time, six white columns of it — and which the people have stopped hearing as sound and hear instead the way you hear your own blood.

What lives here is the lake.

It never left. It was lifted. It is up there now on the rim, forty feet above the roofs, going round and round in its ring the way water goes round a basin before the drain takes it, and it looks down the chimneys with the boatman's eyes. Everything that seeps into the clay is the lake coming back in. Every drop that beads on the underside of a rail at dawn is the lake breathing out where the men cannot see it. Every animal that came down the dike or up the pipe is the lake's, sent ahead, or not sent — simply following the water home, which is what water's creatures do. The eels in the settling tanks are not lost. The frogs out-singing the pumps on a June evening are not a coincidence. The lake is patient in the way only a very large and very old thing can be. It has all the time there is. It knows the ledger with the green cover better than the woman who keeps it, and it does not need to read the figures.

It is not malevolent. This is important. It does not want to drown anyone. It wants to be level, which is the only thing water has ever wanted, and level here means the top of the dike, and the town lies forty feet below level in a hollow that the lake regards the way you would regard a dent in your own bed. Every night it settles a little into the shape it remembers. Every morning the pumps lift it out again and send it round. The great effort to hold something exactly in place is real, and it is working, and the thing being held is alive and does not mind being held, and leans against the holding with the whole weight of a thousand years of drowned leaves and fish and silt, and that leaning is what the brass pins measure.

The people know this. Not in words — they have very few words down here and they use them for bearings and shift changes. They know it in the sleep that has no dreams, and in the way they stand near the tanks at dusk to watch the bats come out over the falling water in the shape of smoke, and in the way the chapel crack gets filled and filled again with a slightly different grey each time and no one suggests giving up. They know it in the flat loud voice, which is the voice of people speaking over a river. They are not the lake's jailers. They are something more like its household. They have taken a job inside a very old creature, keeping it out of its own bed, and the creature has given them in return the eels and the herons and the meadowsweet and the astonishing green, and a quiet that is made entirely of noise, and children who cannot sleep in silence.

At dusk the light goes out of the bowl before it leaves the dike top, so the town sits in a blue shadow while the ring canal still shines above it like a hoop of pewter, and a barge slides along that hoop with its lamp lit, and the man at the tiller looks down into forty lit windows and sees people at their tables not speaking. He thinks they have nothing to say to each other. He is wrong, but it does not matter. The pumps are running at exactly the speed they have always run. The water table has fallen this year by the width of a finger. Under Number Four the bats are pouring out. The hares in the long grass sit up in the last light and look, with mild interest, at nothing in particular, and the whole floor of the place goes on being unwrapped, sharp-edged, eleven years old, one finger-width closer to the shape of the thing that lives there.
