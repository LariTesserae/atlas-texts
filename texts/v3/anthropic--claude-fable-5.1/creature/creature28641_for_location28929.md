The first answer is that nothing lives here, and it is wrong in a way that repays taking slowly.

Begin with the tree, because the tree will let you.

It has been where it is for longer than the ring beneath it took to cut, and the ring is a finger deep in stone that does not cut easily. In all that time nothing has landed on it. No weight has ever rested in its canopy except water. No wind has pushed it — the only air here rises, and rises gently, so the great flat leaves have never once been turned over to show their white undersides to the sky. Nothing has shaded it. Nothing has climbed it. Nothing has died in it and been carried off. Its bark is grey and unbroken and has the finish of something handled, but it has never been handled; that is only the water, which has run down it every hour of every day it has existed and taken away, each time, a quantity too small to name.

You would call it alone, but the word wants a comparison, and the tree has none. It is not alone the way a tree in a field is alone, with the memory of a wood around it. It is alone the way a single word is alone on a page that was never meant to hold a sentence. If it attends to anything — and its two grey cords, one landed and one still feeling downward through the air, look very much like attention — then what it attends to is the stone. It is reading the fissures. It is finding out, over centuries, where the seep runs beneath the surface and where the rock will let a root in, and it is going there. This is the only project on the shelf. Everything else here merely continues; the tree is *going somewhere*. When the second trunk has thickened to match the first and the third cord has come down and found its place, the tree will have moved perhaps ten paces toward the back wall, and it will do this again, and again, and there is nothing on the shelf that could ever stop it. Long after the ferns have leaned their last lean and the red thing has forked into every corner it can reach, the tree will be walking. The shelf will be the tree.

That is one inhabitant.

The cushion plant is another, and it is stranger, because it is warm.

You noticed this and could not account for it. Kneel by it again. The stone beside it is warm as a body; the plant is warmer, warm as a body that has been working. It is not one thing. It is thousands, each a rosette no larger than a grain of rice, packed so tightly that the dome cannot be pressed in by a finger, and the living ones are only the outer skin. Beneath them, held in the mass, are all the ones that came before — the plant's own dead, kept, not shed, packed down into a wet dark core the plant grows on and around. And in that core, very slowly, something is happening that happens nowhere else on the shelf. The dead are being taken apart. Not by anything that came from outside — nothing has ever come from outside — but by the plant itself, its own living tissue breaking down its own dead tissue for the little it can recover, and the breaking gives off heat, the way a heap gives off heat. The cushion is the only thing on the shelf that eats, and what it eats is itself, and this is why it is warm.

It is the size of a sleeping body and it lies at the lip where the mist is thickest and it is, in the only sense that applies here, digesting. When its grey nodules split and let go their dust, the dust falls an arm's length and lands on wet stone and, so far as you can tell, does nothing. The plant tries anyway. It has been trying for as long as it has been here. Nothing else on the shelf tries to leave.

The ferns you already understand: they lean toward the places they are going to become, and they are so heavy with water that even the leaning is slow. The moss is a single thing, one individual laid along the seam for the length of a man and the age of — there is no way to know; it may be the oldest living thing here, older than the tree, and it does not walk or eat or try to leave, it only holds the seep and grows a hair's width toward the wet. The red thing is harder. It does not reproduce, does not flower, does not shed. It forks. Every fork is a decision that never resolves into anything but two more decisions, and the whole spreading diagram of it across the corner of the shelf is the record of a mind — if it is a mind — that has only ever asked one question and answered it the same way.

Those are the residents you can walk around.

Now put your palms flat on the stone again, and consider what you are touching.

Body-heat. The phrase came to you when you first arrived and you let it go as a figure of speech. It is not one. The stone is the temperature of blood, and it is that temperature at noon and before dawn and in the season of thickest cloud and in the season of thinnest, and it has been that temperature since before the tree's ring was a scratch. A thing that holds a temperature against the world is spending something to do it. That is what warmth *is*, in a living body — the sign of a fire being fed. The plants on the surface spend almost nothing; that is the whole character of the place, that nothing here is used up. So the spending is happening below.

You do not need to picture a sleeper under the mountain. Perhaps it is rock that has not finished being made. Perhaps it is water going down a long way and coming back changed. But whatever it is, it is the one thing in this whole silent room that is doing the work that living things do, consuming to stay warm, and the shelf is where the mountain lets that show. If you want the true resident — the one whose house this is, the one the plants are only furniture for — it is beneath you, and it has no edges, and you are kneeling on the small part of it that comes to the surface. The warmth through your palms is the nearest you will get to it. It has been steady for so long that the tree took root in it, and the cushion sleeps against it, and the whole shelf has arranged itself the way things arrange themselves near a hearth.

Then there is the axe.

If it was made, someone came down from above, the way you did, and stood where you are standing, and was — as you are — the only animal for a very long way in any direction. They found the seam. Hands with a stone in them do what hands with a stone in them have always done, and the seam made it easy; it is the kind of stone that wants to be struck. So they struck it. Facet by facet, along an edge that was already nearly an edge. And then they had a tool, keen and heavy, in a place with nothing to cut. No animal to butcher. No wood dry enough to shape. No enemy. Only the great leaves, which they may have tested the edge on, and which would have healed as nothing else here heals — which is to say, not at all; a cut leaf would still be here, lying grey under the tree, and there is none.

So they set it down. Or dropped it. Or — this is the possibility that will follow you — they looked around the empty room of warm stone and understood that they had made the only object of intention in a place that had never contained one, and that it did not belong here, and put it back among the plates it came from as nearly as they could.

Or the seam made it, on its own, and there was never anyone. The seam has time for that. Either way, the axe is the one thing on the shelf that waits. Everything else here is complete in itself — the tree does not need a second tree, the moss does not need to be seen. The axe was made for a world, and the world is not here, and it lies on the wet stone with its edge toward the cloud and waits to be picked up.

Which brings you to the last inhabitant.

Nothing here is as loud as you. Nothing here is as fast. Nothing here has a heart, or joints that click when it stands, or a breath that clouds in the cool air and adds itself to the sweat of the stone. You are the shelf's animal — its only one — and so was whoever made the axe, and so is everyone who has ever come down the mountain to this place and will. You come at intervals the shelf does not measure: a lifetime apart, ten lifetimes, a hundred. You stay an hour or a day. You are warm-blooded and you look at everything and you touch the cushion and the trunk and you kneel and put your palms down, and then you find the axe, and pick it up, and stand there holding it.

Between visits, nothing. The stone does not remember a footprint; the wet slides it off. A hair that falls from you goes over the lip in a thread of water and is the first hair the cloud has ever received. If you eat here — think before you do — a crumb dropped on the stone will lie where it fell for a century, grey, then paler grey, softening into felt, the only thing on the shelf that ever came from a mouth.

So: what lives here.

The plants live here by staying, and they stay so completely that they have given up the parts of life that involve going — flowers, seed, death that leads to anything. The warmth lives here by spending itself somewhere you cannot see. The axe lives here by waiting. And you live here by leaving, which is the one thing nothing else on the shelf can do, and which is why you are the only creature the place has ever had.

Most people put the axe back. They find the plate-shaped hollow it lay in, already filled with the shelf's sweat, and set it down and feel the water close around its edges, and stand up, and their knees click, and they go back up the way they came.

Some take it. Those axes are out in the world now, on shelves and in drawers and in the ground with the people who carried them, and they are the only pieces of this place that have ever gone anywhere. The seam does not miss them. The seam is already making more. And the shelf, which has never noticed anything, does not notice, and goes on doing the one thing it does, which is to hold its temperature against the cool air coming up out of the cloud, hour after hour, and wait for the next animal.
