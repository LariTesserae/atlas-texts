Begin with the birds, because the birds are what the eye is given, and see how long they stay birds.

One bird, then. Lift one out of the mass in your mind and set it on the lip beside you, where none of them ever stands. It is the length of your forearm from bill to tail and weighs less than that suggests, because most of it is wing. The wings are long and bent back at the wrist, a tern's wings, made for staying up cheaply over a long distance. The body is narrow. The plumage is grey-white but not clean grey-white; there is no clean anything here, and the feathers are dusted along their edges with gypsum and stained, under the wing and around the vent, a yellowish tea-colour that does not preen out. The cap is black and comes down low, over the eye, like a hood pulled forward against weather. The eye itself is dark with a thin pale ring around it and a membrane that sweeps across it often, sideways, against the flour in the air. The bill is the colour of old brass — that is exactly right, a dull yellow-brown with a sheen only where it has rubbed — and it is straight and slightly heavy for a bird this size, with a faint downward curve at the tip that is not a hook. The legs are short. The feet are webbed but the webs have split and thickened, and the toes carry a crust of white salt that never quite wears off except on the rim of the fly-wheel, and so the birds that have stood on the wheel are the birds with clean feet.

It smells of ammonia and fish and something mineral behind that, like a struck flint.

Put it back. Alone it is not itself. Alone it goes quiet, and a bird of this place that is quiet is a wrong thing, a machine part lying on a bench.

---

What the calling is, up close, is not what you expected. You expected a cry — gulls cry, terns cry, something rasping and complaining and directed. If you could isolate one of these voices you would find it is two sounds: a grating note that climbs slightly and then a dry click, like a pebble dropped on a plate, and then the grating note again, and the click, and so on, with no interval in which the bird is choosing to call. It does not call at something. It does not call to anything. It calls the way a hive hums, or a heart goes; the sound is the bird's condition rather than its speech. Forty thousand of those two-note pulses, none in step, make the roar, and the click inside each of them makes the grind, and the near-continuous overlap of forty thousand throats makes the hiss you took for steam.

They do not stop to sleep. A bird sleeps standing, for a few seconds, a few hundred times a day, and the calling drops out of it for those seconds and comes back before it wakes, the way a snore does. There is never a moment when enough of them sleep at once for the sound to thin. When the cloud passes and the colony quiets, they have not gone silent — they have dropped to the click alone, forty thousand dry ticks with the grating note held back, and that is what lets you hear the beetles.

---

They are paired, but the pairing is hard to see and may not matter much. One bird goes on the flight; one stays on the scrape. When the flight comes back the returning bird stands on the iron a while and then drops into the mass and finds its scrape — how, in that, you cannot tell; there are no landmarks and the neighbours are identical — and the sitting bird stands, and they change places with no ceremony, no greeting, not even the ducking-and-turning that seabirds do. The one that has been sitting steps off the eggs and the other steps on and the first one walks, on the backs and heads of the others, to the iron, and stands there, and then goes down into the mass somewhere else to wait for the next flight. That is the whole of the pair bond. It might not be the same two birds each time. It might not matter.

The eggs are one or two to a scrape, chalky, grey-buff, exactly the colour of the plain above, so that from the lip a bare scrape with eggs in it looks like a bare scrape. The shell is thick and rough with salt. Under the shell, if you held one up to the ordinary light, there would be a faint blue, the same blue that shows in the depth of the strata in the undercut wall, and you would wonder briefly whether that was the egg or the stone showing through, and then you would put it down, because nobody here holds anything up to the light.

The chicks hatch grey, with the down already the colour of the dust, and the eyes sealed. They stay under the parent for some days, and then between its feet, and then they stand beside it, in the space that does not exist, and are fed by regurgitation: fish, half-dissolved, the black threads of brine-fly, and a pale gelatinous something that the adults bring up last and that the chicks take most eagerly, and that you cannot identify and are not meant to. A chick that steps toward the wall goes to the lizards. A chick that does not step anywhere becomes a bird that has never stood on anything but this stone, and flies out over the lip one day for the first time with the flight, and comes back, and stands on the iron a while, and drops in. It has never seen the plain. It has crossed the plain a hundred times at a height where the plain is a pale nothing, and has never once landed on it. The plain is not part of the place.

---

They die. There is no room for a carcass; consider what that means.

A bird that dies on its scrape is not moved. The neighbours close over it a little, the way the flank shows and closes when a wing lifts. Within an hour there are beetles under it, and by the end of the day it has settled — not decomposed, settled, flattened the way snow settles — and the birds around it are standing partly on it. By the second day it is a plate of feathers with the shape of a bird pressed into it. By the third the feathers have been trodden into the surface and worked at from beneath and the plate is a slightly darker patch, and by the end of the week there is a scrape there again with a bird on it, on eggs, and the stone under the scrape is one layer thicker.

That is the millimetre. Each pale-and-less-pale line in the undercut wall is a season of the dead, pressed and cemented by the brine that comes up from below and dries in the cool. The colony stands on the colony. You put your hand flat on the wall and there are, under your palm, some hundreds of years of these birds, each year thinner than a fingernail, descending into the blue dark, and the birds above you are calling on top of it in the same two notes the birds in the wall used, and the only difference between the ones above and the ones below is which side of the surface they happen to be on this year.

They do not visibly age. There is no grizzled bird, no bird with a broken bill, no bird with the ragged flight-feathers of the very old. Either they die young, all of them, or they do not show it. The birds that stand longest on the iron before dropping in may be the oldest. It is only a guess.

---

Now the beetles, which you have been hearing all this time under everything.

They are the size of a thumbnail and the shape of a seed, hard-backed, with an iridescence so dull it is really only a way of being dark — a shift from black-green to black-bronze as one turns in the shade, like oil on old water. The elytra are fused; there are wings underneath but they have not been used in longer than the plain has been flat, and the beetles that go over the lip and out onto the gypsum, which they sometimes do by accident, dry and die within the hour, and the wind takes their husks, which are the only thing in this country the wind still has to take. Their legs are short and clawed for the crust. They move in currents, and the currents have a direction, and the direction changes with the day: outward from the centre in the morning, back in the afternoon, through the shade the colony casts on its own feet, and along the channel between the two rails where nothing else walks. What they carry is everything that falls. Guano, down, shell, the dead, the pale jelly the chicks miss, the lizards the birds kill and leave. They carry it down. They carry it into cracks and press it and pack it and their own castings go on top, and the brine comes up through the whole of it and hardens it. They are the masons. The stone is theirs as much as it is the birds'. Their larvae are in the top two or three layers, pale grubs the length of a rice grain, blind, chewing sideways through the season before last. When a lizard runs across a bare patch and the stone rings, it is ringing because it is hollowed, very slightly, everywhere, by them.

Under the beetles is the glitter — mites and springtails and things too small to have shapes at this distance, a shift in the grain of the surface that could be mistaken for the light moving if the light here ever moved. They live on the beetles and on the birds' feet and in the feathers and on the lizards and on each other. They are the reason the surface is never still even when nothing on it is walking.

---

The lizards are the only thing in the place that looks at you.

They are flat and quick and the colour of the salt, so exactly the colour that on the wall they are visible mostly as an interruption in the strata, a place where the fine pale lines are covered for a hand's length and then aren't. Their skin has the same laminated look as the stone, scales in fine ridges. They live in the cracks in the dish wall, in the undercut, in the cold breath that comes up from the bottom. They eat beetles, patiently, one at a time, and chicks that stray, and eggs that roll, and each other. They have a rule and they keep it: the first two metres of wall, the fringe where the birds stand a little apart, and no further. A lizard that goes in past that is under nine birds instantly and does not come out, and the beetles have it by evening. Whether the birds are killing an intruder or simply closing over a gap, the way they close over a dead neighbour, is not a distinction the lizards can afford to explore.

They do look at you. A lizard on the wall will turn its head and hold you with one eye and not move until you move, and then it is somewhere else. Nothing else in the basin registers you. The birds treat you as sky.

---

On the iron, above the feet, are the spiders, and the spiders are the one thing here that has made something you might call beautiful.

Their webs are strung between the salt blades on the evaporating pan and across the spokes of the fly-wheel high up, and between the winch drum and the upended skip, and each web is crusted. The brine mist that rises off the colony on cold mornings settles on the silk and dries and the silk goes white and stiff, so that the webs look like frost, or like the drawings of webs in books, every strand thickened and visible. They no longer stretch. When a fly hits one it does not stick so much as break through, and the spider comes down and takes it in the hole it made, and mends the hole, and the mend is soft and dark for a day and then goes white like the rest. The spiders are small and pale and slow and live entirely in the air above the colony, on what rises out of it, and have not touched the ground in generations, and would die if they did, as surely as the beetles die on the plain. Each layer of this place is a country with a border.

---

And the iron. What lives in the iron.

The birds first: the iron is the antechamber, the place a returning bird stands before it re-enters the mass, and there are always several hundred of them on the pan and the drum and the wheel, standing, not calling as loudly, looking at nothing. Then the spiders. Then the salt itself, which does something on the iron it does not do anywhere else — grows outward from the metal in blades, white and orange, a hand long, in the shape of feathers or of frost on a window or of the ferns that have never grown here. The salt on the stone is flat. The salt on the iron reaches. Something about metal, some slow exchange between rust and brine, gives it a direction to grow in, and it has been growing for as long as the pan has stood, and the birds nest among the blades as if they had been put there for the purpose, and perhaps they have; perhaps the basin has simply used the iron as it uses everything, as one more thing to make stone around.

Then the knock.

Stand at the lip long enough and you stop hearing the roar and start feeling the knock through your feet, one and one and one, twenty or thirty seconds between, and each knock is a hitch in the wheel. There is no belt. There is no steam, no heat, no smoke, no smell of anything burning or burnt. The pipe goes down into the floor into the cold, and something down there goes one, and the wheel comes round a spoke, and the birds on the rim step to the next spoke without looking, and something down there goes one.

You could say it is the brine. The salt-makers drove a bore into an artesian pressure that was there before them and is there after them, and the pressure comes and goes with something below — a tide in the dark, a slow breathing in the water table — and each pulse shoves the piston they left in the cylinder a little and the piston turns the crank a little and the crank turns the wheel a spoke. You could say that. It accounts for the knock. It accounts for the wheel. It does not account for why the wheel is worn smooth in exactly the pattern of birds' feet and nothing else, or for why the returning flight lands on it first, always, the whole colony coming home to a thing that ticks, or for the way the knock goes down into the cold and does not come back, as if it were being taken rather than made.

Better to say that what lives in the iron is time, in the only form this place has ever had it. The birds do not have seasons, in the ordinary sense; the light is the same, the cool is the same, nothing grows or dies back. What they have is the flight and the return, and the knock, and the wheel that keeps the knock, and they land on it and stand on it and step to the next spoke and do not look down, because a thing that keeps time is not a thing you look at. You stand on it.

---

The salt-makers are here somewhere. There is no hut, no grave, no bone on the surface, nothing the beetles would have left. If they are here they are in the wall, in a stratum a millimetre thick, some fingers below the present, indistinguishable from the birds of that year, and the brine has come up through them and hardened them and the beetles have chewed sideways through the season and the colony has stood on them and called. They came to make salt and found the salt was already made and the making of it was not theirs, and the place did to them what it does to everything, and now they are a pale line, and the pan they brought grows feathers.

---

Now go back to the top and see what has happened to the word bird.

It is still the right word. They have the wings and the bill and the eggs and the two-note voice, and they fly out and come back, and one lifted out of the mass and set on the lip would be a bird, for the few minutes before it went quiet. But the thing you are looking at from the lip is not forty thousand birds any more than a reef is a hundred million polyps. The birds are the part of it that moves. The stone is the part that remembers. The beetles are the part that turns one into the other. The brine coming up through the layers is the circulation, the flights going out and coming back are the breathing, and the knock in the iron, which the birds keep and stand on and do not look at, is the pulse — and the whole thing has been sitting in this dish, calling, since before the wind finished with the plain, growing downward one dead season at a time into a cold that has never been in the sun.

What lives here is that. A single grey animal eighty paces across and however deep the blue goes, with a surface made of birds. It has no face. It has forty thousand mouths and it is not saying anything with any of them. It eats fish it will never let you see caught, from water it will never let you see, and it gives back stone. It has one rule for every layer of itself and every layer keeps it. It has taken a machine into its body and made the machine keep its time. When the shadow of a hawk goes along the far wall, the whole animal flinches once, in a ring, a third up and a third down, and settles.
