From the rim they are the small figures in oilcloth, and for the first hour that is all they are.

The oilcloth is the same on everyone. A dark waxed stuff, hooded, hanging to the shin, greened at the shoulders where the drip lands hardest and blackened at the hem where it drags. It hides the shape of whoever is inside it, so that from a thousand feet up you cannot tell the men from the women, or the children from the old, or a mason from a gate-tender from someone carrying tea. You see movement, and the movement is all one kind: along a terrace face, across a footbridge, into an arch and gone. Nobody runs. Nobody stands still for long either, except the few who stand beside the gates, and they are standing still for a reason.

You take them for workers, the way you would take the figures on any industrial ground for workers, and wait to see where they go when the shift ends. But the shift does not end in any way you can see, and they do not go anywhere that looks like away. They cross to the next wheelhouse. They climb. They vanish into the dark behind a terrace and come out two levels down. After a while you stop looking for the part of the valley where they live, because you begin to suspect there is not one.

That is nearly right. It is not quite right, and the way in which it is not right is most of what there is to say about them.

---

Closer, they resolve.

They are pale, with the particular pallor of people who have never had a whole day of sun — not sickly, but fine-skinned, faintly damp, the look of something kept under glass. Their eyes are large and their pupils stay wide in the grey-green light, and on the few days a year when the cloud lifts entirely off the basin they behave as though they were mildly unwell: they work in the cistern mouths, they pull their hoods forward, they do not look up. The rim is not a place they like. When they must go to it they go quickly and come back squinting.

The valley has written itself most legibly on their hands. Fingers that are never dry become white and deeply creased, the nails soft, the pads swollen and then furrowed like the surface of the road. Everyone's hands look like this from about the age of six. Against it they use an oil pressed from the seed of the tea, one of the few things in the basin that is not wet, and the smell of it — green, slightly bitter — is the smell of any dry room where people have been sleeping. They rub it into their hands in the evening with the same barbering care they give the moss around a gauge-glass. A person with cracked hands is a person who has not been looking after themselves, and someone will mention it.

They speak low. This is the first thing you notice when you are near enough to hear them and it takes a while to understand that it is not shyness. Small sounds carry here; a spanner dropped three terraces down arrives at your feet as a clear bright note; a raised voice on one level is heard on nine, and every person on those nine levels turns to look, not alarmed but attending, because a raised voice means a fault. So they do not raise their voices. Speech is for arm's length. For distance they have the bells, one stroke each, and a two-note whistle, and a flat clap of the palms that means *look here*, and chalk. The effect, once you are among them, is of a people who are intimate in public and reticent in private, which is the opposite of the usual arrangement. Two people will stand with their hoods almost touching and murmur for a quarter of an hour, and anyone may pass within a yard of them, and nobody would think of listening.

Their hearing has gone the other way. They can tell, without pausing in what they are doing, which wheelhouse has just stopped — not the direction, since the ground-note comes from everywhere, but *which one*, the way a conductor knows without looking which instrument has dropped out. Children play a game of it. One closes their eyes, and when the note changes, names the wheel. They are usually right.

---

Where they live is the thing that takes longest to understand, because the answer is in plain sight from the first hour and looks like something else.

When the water leaves a quarter — when the gate above it closes and the drip on its walls thins to a film and then to nothing, and the penstock goes quiet, and the crew that was in its wheelhouse has already crossed to where the wheel is beginning to turn — the quarter does not empty. It fills. Within the day the bedrolls have arrived, and the cooking-pots, and the children, and the old people carried up in the cargo cars. A dry wall is a wall you can sleep against. The water moves on, and the households come in behind it, into what it has vacated.

So a home here is the place where nothing is happening. And it lasts as long as nothing happens, which is to say until the gates change again and a family wakes to find the wall over their heads beginning to bead. Then they roll the bedding and go. Nobody hurries. Nobody minds, or nobody appears to. A woman rolling her blankets while the drip starts above her has the expression of someone doing washing.

This gives the basin a geography that reads backwards from the rim. The lit quarters — the ones under the strung lamps, humming, wet, scaffolded — are the working quarters, and nobody lives in them. The dark quarters, where the lamps are off because the lamps are on the working circuits, are where the people are. The living quarters smell of oilcloth drying and tea and the small tallow lamps they use for themselves, which are kept low because tallow has to be carried over the saddle. The working quarters smell of wet iron and moss. You can walk from one to the other in a minute and it is like walking between two seasons.

They own what they can carry. A tool-roll, a pot, bedding, a lamp, the oil. A stick of chalk — everyone has chalk, from the youngest who can hold it, and the marks on the walls are the valley talking to itself: *this stair closed. This way. Water to this terrace by the fourth bell. Soft wall, do not lean.* Chalk washes off in a week, which is about as long as anything written here is meant to be true. There is no paper to speak of; paper does not survive the air. What has to last is cast in bronze, and the bronze-caster's shop, which is one of the few works that stays in one place, spends its whole existence making the basin's permanent text: gate numbers, gauge plates, the small oval tags on the penstocks. A few dozen words, most of them numerals. Everything else is chalk and memory.

---

The old are the memory.

There are no maps. A map would be wrong before the ink dried, and everyone knows it, and so nobody has drawn one for a very long time; the idea itself seems faintly comic to them, like a portrait of a cloud. What they have instead is the old, who carry the valley in layers. Ask where a certain conduit runs under a certain terrace and whether it is sound, and a crew will not go and look. They will go and ask someone who remembers when it was last opened — who remembers the water being there, forty years ago, and the chalk marks that were on the wall at the time, and who was working the gate. The old are moved with great care when the water comes, set down in the dry quarters nearest the gate-houses where they can be consulted, given the best of the tallow. They are not revered exactly. They are referred to. An old person's mind is the only record the basin has of its own former shapes, and it is dying and being renewed at roughly the rate everything else here is, which nobody finds alarming.

They are named, all of them, from a stock of perhaps thirty given names, so that any name is shared by a great many people at once. What tells them apart is the water. A child is fixed in memory by which gate was open when it was born — not the terrace, which will be gone, but the fall, the head of water, which recurs. So a person is *the one from when the long west penstock was running*, and an old person's full designation, if you ask for it, is a small hydrological history: the gate at their birth, the gate at their first work, the gate the year they were married. They give it without any sense of it being unusual, the way you might give a street address.

Children are few. This is one of the first things a stranger notices and one of the last things anyone here will explain, because there is nothing to explain: a place with room for nothing has room for few children, and the number is held roughly level by no one in particular, the way the number of turbines is held. The children who are here learn the stairs before they learn to speak — learn to walk on the worn center of the tread and never on the mossed edge, learn to read chalk before they can read anything else, learn the bells.
