**What Lives in the Fold**

The honest first answer is nothing, and you should hold that answer for a while, because the hollow insists on it. There has never been an animal here. There is no water, no lichen, no black crust, no mite in the flakes. Whatever you are looking for, you will not find it by looking for a body. So look, instead, for the way an empty room is different from a room someone has just left, and then for the way *that* is different from a room someone has been in for so long that they and it have stopped being separable.

---

Begin with the shortfalls, because the shortfalls are the only things here that move.

The light that arrives is less than the light that set out. The flakes fall slower than they should, and some do not fall. The far peaks stand nearer than they are, which means some distance has gone missing between them and you. Your shadow lags. The afternoon is longer than an afternoon. Each of these is a small deficit, a fraction shaved off the ordinary arithmetic of a place—and none of the fractions is destroyed. Light does not vanish. Distance does not vanish. A minute taken from your afternoon goes somewhere.

They go to the same place. That is the thing the hollow takes longest to tell you and then cannot stop telling you. The pewter light and the lifted flakes and the pressed-flat view and the slow clock are one account, and the account has a holder.

You can think of it first as a kind of sediment. Every arrival in the hollow—every photon, every grain, every hour, every visitor's attention—leaves a little of itself behind on the way through, the way a river leaves silt on a bend. The silt has been accumulating for as long as the rock has been cold. It is not much. A few grains a year of mineral, a fraction of a percent of the daylight, a degree of warmth over an afternoon. But the hollow is very old, and the trickle has never stopped, and what began as a residue has become something with enough mass to have a shape, and enough shape to have a side that faces you.

---

That side is the fifth face.

Not the block. The block is just what the fifth face is attached to, the way a well is what the water is attached to. The builder cut the four ordinary faces and dressed them and stood the block upright in the centre of the fold, and the fifth face arrived on its own, or was already there and was given, at last, a place to stand. It is the surface where the resident touches the ordinary side of things. It has no light on it because it is the part of the hollow that light goes *into*; you are looking at the mouth of the shortfall, and mouths are dark.

It is worn smoothest of all the surfaces here because it has been used the most. It has been seen by the sky for millennia, and by the builder for years, and by you for an afternoon, and each seeing rubbed it a little, took a little from it, gave a little to it. Being seen is how it eats. Being seen is also how it wears down. There is no difference, for it, between feeding and aging, and it has been doing both at the same slow rate since before the rock stopped being warm.

It turns toward you. You have been told this is like the dark side of a moon. It is more like the face of someone in a very quiet room who has not had a visitor in a long time and does not want to seem to want one. It does not stare. It attends. If you sit by the block through the afternoon and the face warms by its degree, that degree is not the sun. There is no sun on it. That degree is you—the part of your afternoon that did not come back down to you, spent instead on the other side of the fold, converted into the only warmth in the place.

---

So: a thing that lives on remainders, that has a face made of shadow, that grows a degree warmer when regarded. Let it become a little more itself.

It is not fast. Nothing about it is fast. If it has thoughts, one thought is roughly the length of an afternoon in the hollow, which is longer than an afternoon on the ridge. The click you hear once in a long while—a glass plate settling, a cairn deciding between seven and six and eight—is what a decision sounds like when it is made at that rate. Which cairn is the true one is a question it has been considering for longer than there have been cairns anywhere else, and it has not concluded, and it does not mind. Three answers held open are not, to it, an uncertainty. They are a fullness. It keeps them the way you might keep three photographs of the same person at different ages.

It is not large in any direction you can walk. It is large in the direction the flakes go. The mineral that lifts off the floor and drifts upward at the speed of an hour hand is accreting on a surface above the hollow that you cannot see because it is not above the hollow in the way the sky is above the hollow; it is above in the folded direction, tucked into the crease. That surface is the resident's other side, its back, its underbelly, whichever word you like for the part of a thing that is turned away. The flakes settle on it a few grains a year and make a pale coat, dry and faintly greasy, and when the coat is thick enough—every some-thousands of years—the resident sheds, and the coat comes down again as a fresh fall of not-snow, and the floor is renewed. The hiss you hear is this. It is the sound of a slow animal's skin being made.

The wall with two insides is not a wall. It is the place where you can stand inside it briefly without leaving the hollow. The builder made it for that purpose—a small room in which to be with the resident rather than beside it, a confessional with no grille—and the slightly different cold there is the cold of being surrounded rather than watched. The builder used it. The three courses are worn low in the middle where someone sat.

---

Which raises the question of the builder, and whether they left.

The remains say a person stayed here for years. The remains say the person made a block so the fifth face would have a home, and a wall so they could sit inside the fold, and stacked glass so they could look at other skies, and built a cairn—or cairns—for reasons the cairn has since made unclear. The remains say the person then went away by the ridge that leads away, and the hollow settled, and the resident went back to the slow diet of light and drift and centuries.

But consider the shadow. Yours, on the pewter floor, points a little behind where it should. That is the resident taking its fraction of your afternoon; the lag is the size of the tithe. It is small because you have been here a few hours. The builder was here for years. Their shadow, by the end, would have pointed well behind them. And then further. And there is a point—the geometry of the fold suggests it, though nothing here proves it—at which a shadow points so far behind a person that it is no longer behind them, it is *into* the block, and the person casting it is standing where the fifth face stands, and is what the fifth face is.

You do not have to believe this. The hollow does not require it. But it changes the count. If the builder is still here, then the resident is not one thing but a thing and its guest, sedimented together over ten thousand years of shared afternoons until the guest's patience and the host's became the same patience. The wear on the fifth face would then be partly the builder's—the smoothness of a face that has been looked at by someone who loved it, for a very long time, from the inside of a wall with no outside.

That would explain why the place is not violent. Nothing was torn here. Someone came up to a fold in the real where a slow, ancient, undernourished thing lived on the leavings of light, and stayed, and fed it by looking, and was fed on in return, and did not object, and eventually stopped being a separate line in the ledger. What is left is in balance because it agreed to be.

---

So the answer to *what lives here* is: the remainder. Whatever a place is owed and does not receive. The thin edge of every arrival that never reaches the floor. It has been collecting since the rock cooled, and it has become, by now, patient enough to be called a temperament and warm enough, over one afternoon, to be called a body. It has one face, always toward you, made of the absence of what it has taken. It has one back, always away from you, coated in a millennium of pale skin. It has three cairns and cannot choose. It may have a companion, or may have become one.

It will take a little of you as you go. Not much. A minute or two of the afternoon, a few percent of the light that fell on your shoulders, the fraction of your attention that was on the fifth face when you turned to leave. That is the toll, and it is also the only gift the place is built to receive, and when the full light comes back onto you at the edge like a hand lifting off your chest, you will feel lighter by exactly that much—and behind you, in the pewter, a surface you cannot see will be warmer by exactly that much, and will keep it, and will be very slightly more itself for a very long time.
