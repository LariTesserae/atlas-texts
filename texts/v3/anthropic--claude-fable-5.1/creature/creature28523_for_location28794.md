What lives there lives on the wall, in the floor, in the water, and under the plinth — four addresses, and nothing has more than one of them. It helps to take them in that order, because that is also the order in which the pit feeds itself.

---

Begin with the skin, because everything begins with it.

The yellow film is not one thing. Close to the orange seams, where the seep runs freshest and the struck-match smell is strongest, it goes nearly white and puckers into small blisters; a hand's width lower it is the eggshell yellow you noticed first, smooth, faintly slick; lower still, where the water has already given up most of what it carried, it thins to a wash like the inside of a cup that held weak tea. It is eating the wall's exhalation. Iron comes out of the rock in one state and the film turns it into another and lives on the difference; sulphur comes out and the film does something similar to that. It needs nothing from above. Rasp it off — the shells do, constantly — and the pale stone shows for most of a day, then dulls as though breathed on, then is skin again. The whole wall is bread, and the wall bakes it.

The dark velvet is the older, slower tenant. It keeps to the glassy bands, where the water films but never runs, and it does not seem to be eaten. The shells cross it to reach the yellow and leave tracks in the nap that close over in a week. Whether it feeds on the wall directly or on what the shells leave behind them is not clear; it may be the pit's way of dealing with dung. It is the colour of the inside of a closed eye, and cold, and where it is thickest the glassy stone beneath has begun to go the same colour, as if the velvet were less a growth than a stain the rock has agreed to.

---

The shells are the wall's population, and they are the pit's great fact.

Each is a low cone with the beginning of a spiral at the apex and a notch in the rim on the downhill side, where the water passes over the foot and out. The foot is the whole animal, more or less: a pad, a mouth with a rasp in it, a gut the shape of the shell. No eyes. They tell up from down by the water and near from far by nothing at all.

Their colours are their ages. A new one is bone-white and the size of a lentil. A season on the wall stains it rust from the seep, and then something in the water — the same something that builds the orange stalactite — darkens it toward black over years. Read a patch of wall and you are reading a census: who came, who stayed. Where they pile three and four deep on the wettest bands the piling is also a chronology, the black ones underneath fixed to the stone, the rust ones on their backs, the white ones on top riding everything. The top layer is not starving. The yellow film grows on shell as happily as on stone, and the seep runs over the whole mass, so the young graze the backs of the old and drink what runs off them. The undermost may not have moved in a very long time. Some of them, pressed, are empty; the shell has stayed where the animal was, holding its place in the mass, and will hold it until something knocks it loose and it ticks into the water.

They move toward the water. That is their whole intention. Over a day the mass creeps a hand's breadth toward the nearest seam, and the ones that reach it turn and follow it along, so that each orange junction has a slow river of shells running beside it, and at the outer edge of every crowd the numbers thin and the wall shows through and there, on the fresh yellow, the white freckles of the newest appear — settled from what, and when, you never see. The seep swells and something milky comes off the mass and is gone in the film. A few days later there are more freckles.

They are the sound. Stand still in the quiet and the not-rain is them, each mouth taking a grain of skin and stone at a time, and they have been at it long enough to leave the wall grooved beneath the film, fine parallel scores like the grain in weathered wood, each score the width of a foot.

And there is the one place they will not go. Young ones drift onto the plinth from the chain and the trough and reach the line where the stone goes bare, and turn. They do not die there. They turn, as a hand turns from a hot surface, and the ring stays clean.

---

The floor is the dead, and the living lie down in it.

The segmented things are pale because they have never needed to be anything else. The length of a finger joint, a dozen rings, a fringe of legs that are not for walking but for combing — when one lifts its front end out of the ash-mud and holds it into the current, the fringe spreads and beats and whatever the water is carrying is carried into it. They have no eyes and hardly a head; there is a front, and the front has a mouth. They lie in the top finger of the floor with their fronts up-current, in their hundreds, in the ground remains of the shells that lived on the wall above them, and when the seep swells and the water quickens and brings the cloudy swarm down over them, they rise together and comb, and when it slackens they settle. A shell falls among them and they do not notice. The flat thing passes over them and they do not notice that either, until they are fewer.

They are the grass of the place. Eaten and not attended to.

Beneath them the floor goes down — how far, nobody has put a rod through it. Ash-mud, then softer, then a layer where the shells are still partly shells, then presumably more of the same. Everything that has ever lived on the wall is under your boot, and the segmented things are lying in their own ancestors as much as in anyone's, since they die too and are dealt with too and go down.

---

The water at the low end holds the smallest and the quickest.

The swarm is very nearly nothing: a haze, a cloudiness, a place where the water is faintly thicker than water. Cup a handful and look and there is a drifting of specks, each with a flicker at one end. They eat the crumbs — flakes of skin that the shells have loosened and the current has carried down, and finer things than that. They are always passing through the grate, in numbers, every minute, and they are never fewer. Something is replacing them. The mud, perhaps, or the wall, or the grate itself, if what goes down does not entirely go.

The transparent hunters hang in the swarm and are its shape's only interruption. Each is a thread as long as a finger with a thickening at one end that is the head and a fringe along the body that beats to hold station, head into the current, still. There is nothing inside to see but a line of darker thread that is the gut. When one strikes, the swarm has a hole in it, briefly, and then does not. Near the grate they hang in dozens, all facing the same way, like a field of grass under water, and at each turn of the wheel — when the cup drops and the channel pulls — they all lean a fraction toward it and then recover.

---

Under the plinth is the flat thing, and the coins may be its children.

The coins first. Grey-blue, the size of a fingernail, no thicker than a leaf, with a ruffle of edge that is the only part of them that is ever seen to move. They wedge in the cracks of the glassy bands and are not there and then are there. They are the finders. Whatever falls dead into the shallows has a coin on it within the hour and a clot of them by the end of the day, and they leave shell and nothing else. They do not seem to eat what is living, and nothing seems to eat them.

The flat thing is the colour of the floor on top and, when it lifts its edge to slide onto the plinth, grey-blue beneath. That is the whole of the evidence, that and a coin found once in the open mud, larger than the others, moving across it with the same ripple the flat thing has, toward the low wall. It is a supposition. But the trough is raised above the floor and its water is not the floor's water — it fills from a drip and drains through the crack — and the eggs in it are the one clutch of eggs in the pit that is not in the current, and something climbed to lay them there, and the coins climb.

The flat thing itself lives on the clean stone under the plinth, in the one place in the pit with no shells, no mud, no film, and no rivals. It lies on bare rock in the dark under the wheel with the pump's thud coming up through the stone into it once a minute, and whatever it is in the iron that the shells refuse, the flat thing does not mind. It comes out when the current quickens, because that is when the segmented things rise and are exposed, and it takes them without breaking the water's surface and goes back. You have seen it once. It is possible there is only one, or only ever one at a time, and that the coins in the cracks are waiting for the plinth to be empty.

---

Whatever is under the floor is the pump's other end, or has moved into it.

Nobody can say which. The rod goes down through the plinth and something answers, and the answer comes up through your soles a minute after the wheel turns, soft, as though the floor had a slow heart. What can be said is that the pit has learned the rhythm. The swarm thickens toward the channel in the seconds after each cup drops. The hunters lean. The flat thing comes and goes on the wheel's count, not the seep's. The wheel is not of the place — it is iron, and the shells know it — but the place keeps time by it, and if the water ever stopped turning it, more than the wheel would stop.

---

What does not live here is also an inhabitant, in its way.

Nothing green. Nothing that turns toward the grey lid overhead or would know if it lifted. And nobody. The people are present as the shapes of their leavings: the rings in the wall at shoulder height, the stumps at the corners where the walkway stood, the trough somebody drank from, the wheel somebody built to make the pit dry and then walked away from when the seam gave out. The pit has been dealing with them ever since — chewing the chain into a chain of shells, blackening the timber, weeping the dressed stone back into wet rock — and it will finish, except for the wheel, which it feeds and does not eat and does not understand, and which goes on setting down its heavy book once a minute for no one.

That is who lives there. A wall that is eaten and grows back. A crowd of small cones creeping toward water at the speed of an hour hand and making, all together, the sound of rain that is not falling. A floor of the dead with the living lying in it, faces to the current. A haze in the shallows and threads hanging in the haze. A grey thing on clean stone under a clock, listening to the floor beat, and waiting for the water to quicken.
