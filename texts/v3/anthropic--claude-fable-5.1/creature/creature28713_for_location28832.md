**What lives in the Condensing City**

Start with the mats, because they were here first, and because they will be here last.

They are not a species so much as a habit the water has. Warm, mineral-heavy, slow-moving: give any water those three qualities for long enough and something will begin to live in the film of it, and this is what that something looks like here. Brass-colored. Suede-textured. Thick as a thumbnail where the flow is fast, thick as a hand where it pools. Under a lens they resolve into filaments, and the filaments into cells, and the cells into a chemistry that eats iron and sulfur and gives back stone. That is all they do. They eat the mountain's exhalation and precipitate it into terraces, one molecule of lip at a time, and the terraces spill the water into new shallows where new mat can grow. They are the city's only masons still working. Everything the builders left has stopped changing; the mats have not.

They do not respond to you. Put a finger into a basin and the mat gives like wet felt and springs back and the water around your finger stirs a cloud of loose filament and then settles and there is no sense whatsoever that anything has noticed. They are the closest thing here to a plant and they are also the closest thing to a mineral, and they occupy the seam between the two so completely that the question of which side they're on does not arise. If the city has a citizen, it is these. They have never left the water. They have no interest in the dry.

---

Look closer at the mats, in the settling tanks where the flow slows, and the water above them is not quite clear.

There is a haze in it. It is the color of nothing — a faint grainy shimmer in the furnace-light, like dust in a shaft of sun except there is no sun. Hold a jar of it up to the grate and the shimmer becomes specks, and the specks move. Not drifting. Rowing. Each one is a fleck of glass the size of a poppy seed with a fringe of legs it beats in sequence, and they graze the mats the way sheep graze a hillside: constantly, without urgency, in a slow migration around the basin that takes them a day to complete. They have no color because they have never needed to hide from anything that sees. They have no eyes for the same reason. They find the mat by chemistry and find each other, when they need to, the same way.

There are billions of them. In the whole city, across every basin and channel and cistern, there may be more of them than there have ever been people anywhere. They are, by weight and number, the dominant animal of the plateau, and you will live here a year before you know they exist. That is how the city keeps most of its inhabitants: in a register just below the threshold of being seen.

---

The pale things you know about. What you don't know yet is how many.

The aqueducts are full to the brim, and they are also full of these — not schools, nothing so organized, but a distribution, one every fifty or hundred meters of channel, holding position in the current with that fringe along the underside rippling and nothing else moving. In the dark inside the ceramic they cannot be seen even by each other. They know each other by pressure. When one shifts, the water tells the next, and the next, and so a disturbance at one end of an aqueduct arrives at the far end as a faint ripple of adjustments, a whole line of soft bodies leaning a fraction against a change they cannot name.

They are not fish. Cut one open — you would not, but if you did — and you would find no bones, no spine, only a tough clear muscle-tube around that dark thread of gut, and along the gut a row of small organs like beads that do something with the dissolved iron. They breathe air because the warm water carries little oxygen and they have learned to come up for it, at the tanks, where the ceiling of the channel opens. The gasp you heard is the only sound they make. They make it perhaps once an hour, each of them, and if you sit by a settling tank in the dim and count, you begin to hear that the gasps are not random either. They cluster just before the pump's thud. They fall silent just after. The pale things breathe when the city breathes, and it is not clear which one is following.

They eat the grazers. They open that slit of a mouth and let the current carry the haze in and close it and the haze is gone. That is the whole of their hunting. In the tanks, where the grazers crowd the mats, a pale thing will sometimes lie for an hour with its mouth open, a meter of transparent flesh with the water simply passing through it, and it is difficult, watching, to be sure whether this is an animal feeding or a filter doing its job. The city built a great many filters. It is possible it grew one more.

They breed in the cisterns, where the water is stillest. In the bottom of certain cisterns, if you lower a lamp, you can see the young: hundreds of pale threads no thicker than string, all oriented the same way, all trembling in unison with the pulse coming through the floor. They are already listening. They have never been anywhere else and they never will be. To them the aqueducts are not plumbing. They are the shape of the world, and the world is warm and dark and runs in one direction and has a heartbeat, and every so often the world opens above you and there is a thing called air that you take one mouthful of and give back.

---

Now go up.

The shape against the cloud-lid is not one shape. Wait long enough at a condenser mouth, where the updraft is strongest, and you will see two, three, once a dozen — a loose spiral of grey on grey, turning slowly, never flapping, never descending below a certain height as if there were a floor up there that you cannot see and they cannot pass. The height is the height at which the vapor from the condensers, rising, meets the cold coming down, and becomes cloud. They live exactly there. In the seam.

What they are: not birds. Nothing with feathers could stand this wet. Their wings are membrane, stretched over a frame too fine to see, and the membrane is the thing. It is cold-blooded in the literal sense — they keep it colder than the air, by some trick of blood-flow that pulls the heat inward — and as they turn through the saturated updraft the vapor condenses on the underside of their wings, beads there, runs, and gathers in a channel along the leading edge that leads to the mouth. They drink by flying. Every turn through the cloud is a sip. They are condensers, built to the same principle as the iron towers and for the same purpose, and they came here for the same reason the city did: the mountain gives water away, and they learned to catch it.

They eat too, but rarely, and only at the rim. Where the aqueducts go over the edge, the last settling tanks sit open to the real sky, and the pale things surface there as they surface everywhere, and once in a long while a grey shape drops out of the cloud-edge — the only time you will see one descend — and strikes the water and lifts off with a length of translucent body hanging from it like a wet rope. Then it is gone into the cold, down toward the flank, to wherever they roost. It is not a common event. It does not need to be. A thing that drinks by flying and lives in mist needs very little else, and takes it seldom, and the pale things have never learned to fear the tanks at the rim, because nothing that lives in the dark inside the channels has any way of learning what comes from above.

They do not come to the city. They come to its breath.

---

And the people.

Here you have to be careful, because the honest answer is that you do not know, and the city is not going to tell you. But you can say what the evidence permits.

There is no ruin, and there is no rot, and things that are left to themselves do rot, even here — a wooden handle, a leather strap, a cloth. There are none of those. Whatever was perishable has been taken away or was never brought. What remains is iron and stone and copper and ceramic, and that could be care, or that could simply be the builders having known what the plateau would do to anything softer. It does not settle the question.

But there are the beds. Rows of them, bolted down, in more buildings than a small tending crew would need. And there are the tables, long ones, seating forty. Someone expected people here, once, in numbers — not crowds, but shifts. Not a town, but a watch. The city was designed to be minded.

And it is not being minded, and it runs anyway, and here is the thing you keep coming back to: it runs *better* than it should. The valves that no one has closed have not failed open; they have simply not been closed. The surplus that no one is taking delivery of has not flooded anything; it has found its way, by gravity and by the shape the builders gave the stone, back into the system. There is a difference between a machine that has been abandoned and a machine that has been *left*, and this one has been left the way you leave a fire banked for the night: with the expectation of coming back to it, and with the knowledge that if you don't, it will burn down safely on its own.

So there are two answers, and you get to hold both.

The first is that there is no one. The watch ended, the last shift walked out along the aqueduct and over the rim and down into the mist, and the city — fitted so closely to the mountain that the mountain barely knows it's wearing it — has gone on doing what the mountain was already doing, only with edges. The beds are empty because no one needs to sleep here. The people who live in the Condensing City are the mats, the grazers, the pale things, the gliders, and the fact of the water itself.

The second is that there is someone, and you have not met them because the city is very large and the cloud is very thick and they are very few, and they move on the city's schedule, which is slow. Perhaps a handful. Perhaps one. Someone who came for the water and found the place had enough of everything — warmth from the floor, drink from every wall, the pale things for meat if you can stand to take them — that leaving stopped being urgent and then stopped being a thought. Someone who sleeps on a bolted iron bed with no bedding because the room is warm as a bath and bedding would only rot. Someone whose skin has gone the soft grey of the light and whose breath, after long enough, has fallen into step with the pumps, so that they inhale as the steam rises and exhale as it thins, and do not notice. Someone who is not tending the city. The city is tending them.

You would not find them by looking. You would find them the way you find the grazers: by realizing, one day, that the haze in the water has always been there. A cup on a sill you were sure was empty. A door that was open now closed. The gasp from a settling tank that was a fraction too deep and too slow to be one of the pale things. Whether you would want to find them is a separate question. Whether they would want to be found is the city's question, and the city, as noted, does not answer.

---

There is one more thing that lives here, and it is the hardest to say plainly.

The rhythm is not mechanical. You know that already. The pumps thud on an interval that lengthens toward evening and shortens toward dawn, and the vents exhale in the pauses, and the whole plateau slows and quickens like something sleeping and waking. The builders did not program that. Iron cannot keep a circadian time. The pumps are following something, and the something is below.

The heat has been here a very long time. It has not flowed in the memory of anything that lived. It has settled into the rock and stopped arguing. But *settled* is not the same as *dead*, and a house that has stopped arguing with its foundations still shifts a little in the night when the temperature drops. Something under the plateau is warm, and something under the plateau breathes — not in the way an animal breathes but in the way a tide breathes, or a season, or a bed of coals that brightens when the wind picks up. The vents open a little wider in the hours before what would be dawn. The pressure in the deep chambers rises and falls on a period that is nearly, but not quite, a day. The builders found that pulse and built the pumps to ride it, the way you'd build a mill to ride a river, and the pale things found the pumps and tuned their breathing to the thud, and the gliders found the updraft and tuned their turning to the vents, and the mats, at the bottom of everything, simply grow a fraction faster when the water is a fraction warmer, and lay their stone a fraction thicker, and so even the terraces are keeping time.

You could call it geology. You would be right to. A hot spring is not alive, and a mountain that has been cooling for ten thousand years and has settled into a slow rhythm of pressure and release is a physical system, and the word for a physical system that behaves like it's breathing is *coincidence*, or *resonance*, or *thermal cycling*, and any of those would satisfy an engineer.

But stand in the empty plaza at the hour when the interval is longest. Feel the heat come up through your boots. Wait for the thud. It comes late — later than you expected, a beat past comfortable — and in the pause before it, the whole city is silent, the vents closed, the condensers holding their beads unshed, the pale things in the dark with their mouths shut, the gliders hanging motionless at the seam of the cloud. Everything that lives here, waiting. And then the floor speaks, once, and the steam goes up, and everything lets go together.

Whatever that is, it is the oldest inhabitant. The city was fitted to it. The mats eat its breath and the pale things drink its pulse and the gliders sip its exhalation from the underside of their wings and the people, if there are people, have long since stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heart.

It does not know they are there. It does not need to. It is warm and it is patient and it is doing what it was already doing, and everything else that lives on the plateau has simply arranged itself around that fact, in rings, at the speed of drip.
