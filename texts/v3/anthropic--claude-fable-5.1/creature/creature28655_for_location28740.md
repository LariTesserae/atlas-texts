The obvious answer is the snails, and it is worth giving them their due before moving on, because in every sense that can be counted they are the town.

There are thousands. Ramshorn, flat-coiled, none bigger than a thumbnail, and if you put your eye close to the boiler's flank you see that what looked like a bloom of rust is a crowd, each shell a slightly different shade of the same brown, each one moving at a pace that only registers when you stop watching and come back. They graze. There is a film on everything down here — a fur of algae and bacteria and settled silt that the iron and the stone and the plant stems all wear alike — and the snails rasp it off in tracks a millimeter wide, so that a boiler plate that has been grazed looks faintly combed. Their blood is red. It is one of the oddities of their kind, a haemoglobin most snails do without, and it is why they can live in water this poor in oxygen, and it is why, if you look at a pale one against the light, it glows the color of a rosehip from inside.

They live a year, perhaps two. In a town where the fastest cycle is a quarter of an hour and the slowest is the rusting of a cast frame, the snails are the only inhabitants that die on a schedule anyone could notice, and they have been dying on it since the water came. Their shells do not rot. They gather where the convection lets them, in the lee of things — a drift in the bottom of the engine bed, a crescent behind each vat, a whole talus of them in the wheel pit where the warm water rises and drops what it carries. Apart from silt, the snails are the town's only sediment. In a few centuries the ground floor of the engine house will be a snail floor, and the flywheel, if it is still turning, will be turning above a beach of them.

They do not know anything. That is not a criticism; it is simply the first thing to say about the town's population, that it is a population and not a mind, and that its relationship to the place is the relationship of a lichen to a rock.

---

The catfish are closer to inhabitants in the ordinary sense, and there are far fewer of them.

Twenty, perhaps. Perhaps thirty. It is hard to be sure because they do not gather and because they are not seen except in the town's night, when the lid goes black and the only light is the orange of the six-in-one lamps, and then they come up out of the cellars and out of the vats and cross the street, and you see them only as the Vallisneria parts and closes. They are small, none longer than a hand, pale to the point of being faintly pink where the blood shows through, and their eyes have gone the way eyes go in places without direction — not blind, quite, but reduced to a dark fleck under the skin that can tell brown from black and nothing else. What they have instead are barbels. Long, four pairs, trailing and touching. They know the town by feel. Each fish has a route — a doorway, a length of cobble, a particular join in the stone of a terrace, a particular vat — and it does not vary, and it is walked in the same order every night, in the way that a very old person walks the same rooms of a house in the dark and is never wrong about where the furniture is.

The ones that live in the vats are the strangest. The lead should have killed them. Perhaps it does, slowly; they are smaller than the cellar fish, and slower, and their barbels are shorter, as if worn down. But they stay. Whatever they were driven into the vats by — a predator gone now, an old flood current, the simple accident of hatching there — they hold the vats as territory, one fish to a cistern, and each lies on the color at the bottom without disturbing it. The indigo fish. The madder fish. They do not stir the silt because they do not stir. They breathe, and the color under them holds.

They eat the snails, when the snails are small enough. They eat what drifts down. They do not, as far as anyone can tell, eat each other. When the eel comes down — and we will come to the eel — they go into the cellars and stay there, and the street is empty for a night, and the plants stand in the orange lamplight with nothing passing through them.

---

There is a temptation to say that the plants live here, and to mean something more by it than the fact that they are alive. It is a real temptation and it should be examined.

Everything about them reads as intent. They lean into the current above the wheel pit like a crowd leaning toward a speaker. They have closed every doorway from both sides, meeting in the middle as if by arrangement. Around each living lamp the hornwort has thickened over decades into a nest, and the nest has a shape — a cupping, an almost-enclosure — that looks exactly like the shape a hand makes around a match. It is difficult, moving through it, not to feel that the town is reaching.

But reaching is what plants do, and this is only the plainest possible demonstration of it. They are growing toward the only light there is, and the light is weak and comes from nowhere, so they grow toward whatever is nearest and warmest and least dark, which is each other, and the lamps, and the rising column of the seep. The intent is in the eye of whoever is drifting through. The plants are furniture that grows. They are what the town is upholstered in. They are not who lives there.

Nor is the engine, though the engine is the hardest to dismiss. It moves. It breathes, in the sense that a sleeper breathes. It has a heartbeat, if you want to call the knock that, and a pulse in the fizz along the pipes, and a circulation in the daily overturn of the warm layer. But it was built to do this. Everything it does, it was made to do, and the fact that it is now doing it with water instead of steam and for snails instead of dyers does not change the nature of the doing. The engine keeps time. It does not keep itself. When the frame thins and the wheel drops, nothing will have died. Something will have stopped.

---

So: the eel.

She came down through the thermocline a long time ago — long enough that the plants have grown a different town around her since, closing streets that were open when she arrived — and she was, when she came, not the animal she is now. She was silver. Her belly had gone the bright metal that eels go when the sea calls them, her eyes had swollen to twice their size to see in the dark she was expecting, her gut had begun to shut down because silver eels do not eat, they travel. She was following the river. The river was under thirty feet of reservoir and the way out of the valley was a spillway a mile off that she had no means of knowing about, and she went down looking for the current that would tell her which way was downstream, and the current she found was the warm one, rising from the town.

Eels find their way by fields. Not sight, not smell, or not chiefly — they feel the slant of the earth's own magnetism and the faint electrical grain of moving water, and they steer by it across an ocean. The town, without meaning to, has a field. Every dissimilar metal in it is closed into a circuit by the mineral water, and the whole complex hums with a low direct current that has no source and no destination, that simply circulates, iron to copper to lead and back. She came down into that. She would have swum the length of the street trying to find where it led. It led to the engine house. It led around the line-shaft and down through the holed wall and into the wheel pit and back out across the roofs and down the outer walls and in again. Every direction was into the town. She swam it for some time — one imagines days — and then she stopped in the wheel pit, where the water was warmest and the current strongest and the field most whole, and she has not, in any way that matters, moved since.

Silver eels that do not reach the sea die, usually. She did something rarer. She went back. The silver faded from her belly over a few years and the olive-yellow came in again, and her gut reopened, and she began to eat, and the thing in her that had said *now, go* went quiet without resolving. Her eyes did not shrink. They are still the eyes she grew for the deep Atlantic, black and enormous in a head as broad as a wrist, and they are the largest eyes in the town, in a place where there is nothing for them to do. She uses them anyway. In the black of the town's night, when the lamps are the only light, she can see the catfish cross the street in the orange, and they cannot see her.

She is very old now. Eels kept in wells and cisterns have lived to eighty, a hundred, more; there is no reason to think she is younger than the flooding and some reason to think she is not much younger than the dam. She is four feet long, or nearly, thick as a forearm at the shoulder, and pale — not the pallor of the catfish, which is a lack, but the paleness of something that has been steeped, a color close to the light itself. When she lies coiled around the axle of the old water wheel, the one they never took out, with the flywheel turning eleven feet above her, she is hard to see at all. She is the color of the water she is in.

Every fifteen minutes the knock comes through her. Eels hear with their whole length — the sound enters the body and the body is the ear — and the worn bearing's knock arrives in her the way a footstep arrives in the floor. She has been counted by it. Something over three and a half million times, if the arithmetic is done; the number is not important, but the regularity is, because it is the only thing in her life that has happened at a rate she can feel. She shifts, very slightly, at each one. Not startled. Adjusting. The way a body adjusts in sleep to a clock it has stopped hearing.

She eats what comes. A catfish, now and then, taken in the street in the dark. Snails, which she crushes and spits the shells of — there is a particular drift of broken shells beside the axle that is hers. Whatever falls through the thermocline from the cold clear water above: a dead perch, a frog that got under the lid and could not get back. Once in a while she goes up. She rises through the shimmer into the cold, and the change is total, and she stays up there for an hour or a night, in water with oxygen in it and light with a direction, and then she comes back down. The plants along her path sway. The whole town seems for a moment to notice. When the description of this place says that something larger passes through the thermocline once in a long while, an eel usually, on its way somewhere — sometimes it is a stranger. More often it is her, going up to remember and coming back.

---

This, then, is who lives there. A population of snails that turns over every year and knows nothing and lays down the town's only floor. A parish of small blind fish, each on its round, one lying on each of the old colors. A wall of plants that reaches and does not want. A machine that keeps time and does not keep itself. And one animal that arrived from outside on her way to somewhere else, was held by the place the way the seep is held by the rock, and stayed so long that she has become the color of it — the only thing in the town with a before, and a decision, and something that could be called a life rather than a cycle.

The engine turns for no one; that is what the place says of itself, and it is very nearly true. But she is in the wheel pit under it, and the knock goes through her fifteen times an hour, and when the frame finally thins and the wheel drops from its bearings and the knock stops, she will be the only thing in the town that notices it has stopped. What she does then is not knowable. Perhaps the silence undoes the field, and the field was what held her, and she rises through the thermocline one last time and finds the spillway after all and goes down the valley to the sea, a hundred years late, with eyes that were ready the whole time. Perhaps she simply lies where she is, around the axle, in the warm, and waits for the sound to come back.

Perhaps that is what living here is. Not the turning. The waiting for the next turn.
