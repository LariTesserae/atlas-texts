Start with the smallest thing, because it is the most honest about what lives here.

The slime in the old pools is not one thing but a mat of very small things, and what it eats is the beat. It lies in a film a hair thick over the finest ash, in the pools that have stood longest — the ones at the antinodes, where the shaking is strongest and has ground the fill beneath to a flour, so that the water lies on a bed as smooth as glass and the standing waves on its surface are fine as engraving. Nothing else will live there. The reeds cannot hold. The lichen shivers off. But the film does nothing between strokes, and so the shaking cannot wear it; and when the concussion comes up through the fill and the whole pool flexes like a drumskin, something in the film — a membrane, a charge, a great many very small lives doing one thing at once — gives up its green and holds it for as long as the shaking holds and then lets it go. It is the only thing on the plain that the sound feeds rather than removes. It is the one native. Everything else here is being slowly taken away; the slime is being slowly given.

The reeds are its opposite and its complement. They live in the lines of quiet, the film in the fields of noise, and between them they map the plain's whole interference pattern in green on black: the reed-lines drawn along the channels, the slime-fields spattered across the flats between. You could stand at the rim on a strong night, if you could find the rim, and see the shape of the sound.

The reeds are useful. Their stems are short-jointed and stiff, more like wire than grass, and they can be split and twisted into a cord that does not rot in the wet. The stunted willows on the warm flanks of the engine-houses give a few withies a year. The moss holds heat. The lichen is good for nothing, and is the only thing here that nobody has found a use for, and grows on anyway.

You take them for people at first, and the mistake is not a bad one. Let it stand for a while.

You see them as a change in the lamp. At the base of an engine-house, the amber smear of an inspection light goes dark for a moment and comes back, and you understand that something has walked across it. Then again. Then the fog thins for a breath and there are two figures on the ground below the housing, low, moving slowly along a line you cannot see, and one of them bends and lifts something flat and dark from the ground and turns it over in its hands and puts it in a basket, and the fog closes.

Closer, they are small. They walk bent forward, not with age but the way a person walks on a deck, knees soft, weight on the balls of the feet, always ready for the ground to move because the ground is always moving. Their chests are deep — not broad but deep, the sternum standing well forward, as though the hollow where you feel the sound has been enlarged in them to hold more of it. Their skin is grey with wet ash and never dry. Their eyes are large in the dim. Their hands are the strangest part: the palms thick and very smooth, the fingertips blunt and shining, the skin over the knuckles worn thin. You have seen those hands before, in the brass. The bearings are worn to the shape of hands; the hands are worn to the shape of the bearings. Neither of them was made that way. They have been rubbing against each other for a very long time.

They have ears. They do not use them for anything. A child born into this pressure never learns to pull one sound out of another, because there is only the one sound, and it is everywhere and has no edges; what the child learns instead is to hear with the whole body. The soles of the feet. The shins. The hollow of the chest. A tender standing on the plain half a mile from the nearest engine can tell you — if you knew how to ask — which engines are running, which is labouring, which has a bearing going, by the way the beats arrive through the ground and interfere in the bones. They stand still to listen the way you might close your eyes to see.

Their language is tapping. Fingers on iron, on the flat of a forearm, on the back of the hand, on the ground. A sentence is a pattern of small beats laid over the great beat, and the great beat is the grammar: what a tap means depends on where it falls in the stroke. A touch on the rising side is a question; the same touch on the fall is an answer. Rapid taps in the pause at the top of the beam are urgency. They cannot speak far from an engine, because there is nothing to speak against; the words have no floor. Two of them out on the plain will say nothing to each other until they are near enough to a housing to feel the beam, and then they will stand with a palm on the brick and talk for an hour, and you will see nothing but four fingers moving on a wrist.

---

The engines are fed with the plain. That is their work, and the plain's.

The older fill is full of fuel. The works that dumped this ground for centuries were careless with their coal, and the fill is threaded with unburnt lumps, with coke, with a black tarry slag that catches slowly and burns hot and dirty. The tenders go out along the reed-lines — the nodes, where the ground is quiet enough to walk on without feeling sick — with a rake and a willow basket, and they lift the plates of made ground and sort them by hand, feeling for weight and grain, and take what will burn and leave what will not. Then they bring it back and it goes in the firebox and comes out of the chimney as ash and falls on the plain and is dug up again. The engines burn the plain's past to keep the plain's present dry, and put the past back on top when they have finished with it. The fill is the fuel is the fog is the fill. The tenders are the part of the cycle that walks.

They also cast. Under the floor of every engine-house is a heap of old fittings, prised out of the buried works over generations — valve bodies, pipe-flanges, bearing shells — and when a bearing goes they melt down what they have in a crucible set in the firebox and pour it into a mould of packed ash and fit it, rough, still warm. It wears fast. It takes the shape of its wear. In a year it is as smooth as the one it replaced and in ten it is gone and the pile under the floor is a little smaller. Nothing new comes in. There is only what was dumped here once, and the slow business of using it up.

The first thing a child is taught is the pools. It is carried out along the reed-line and stood at the edge of the nearest slime-field and its hand is taken and pressed to the water, and then taps are made on the back of the hand: this pattern is *ours*; this finer one under it, coming from the left, is the *next*; this one is the *one beyond*. By the time it can walk unaided it can stand at the door and read the whole basin off a single pool — which engine is strong, which has quickened, which is out of time and by how much. They knew the far engine was labouring last night before its own tenders did, because the hexagons on the water outside their door had gone to spirals. They have no picture of the plain. They have never seen it; nobody has. What they have is the water, and the water is the picture.

---

They live inside, on the warm side of the cylinder.

The engine-houses are a single room around a single machine, and the room is the machine's heat. Against the brick where the cylinder stands there is a bed of moss, and on it the tenders sleep, lying flat with their backs to the floor so the beat comes up through the length of them. They cannot sleep otherwise. The few times an engine has failed while its people slept, they have woken all at once in the sudden soft place, sick and grasping, palms flat on the ground, feeling for it.

The floor is lit by the slime. They keep it in shallow trays — old fire-doors, mostly, laid flat and filled with ash-water — set where the beam's concussion comes cleanest through the flags, and it glows with each stroke, so that the whole interior of a house pulses: green, dark, green, dark, two or three times a minute, forever. The gauges glow yellow and no one reads them. The tenders read the beam. They read the brass. They read the pool outside the door. The gauges are lit because they have always been lit and because the oil is there, and this is as much of a reason as anything on the plain has.

There are few children. One is born in a house every few decades, maybe, and is carried against the warm iron until it can hold itself, and learns to walk on a beating floor, and never in its life stands on a still one. When the old ones can no longer keep their knees under the beat they are carried out along the reed-line and laid down in the channel among the reeds, where the ground is quiet enough to hold something, and the wet takes them slowly and the fill closes over. Another layer. The reed-lines are where things can stay, and so that is where the dead are, and if you knew to look you would see that the reeds grow thickest in certain stretches, and are a little greener.

---

Here is where the picture changes, if you stay long enough for it to.

You took them for keepers — for a maintenance crew, the last shift of some enterprise that forgot to send relief. Then, when you saw the moss beds and the children, for a village: a few dozen souls scattered across a day's walk, holding a line because their grandparents held it. Both of these are true and neither is what they are.

They grow where the reeds grow, and for the same reason. They were not sent here and did not come here.
