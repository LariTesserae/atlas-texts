Start with what you can put a name to, because there is so little of it.

The skin. In the shallow upper pools, where the film of water is a finger's depth and the grey light comes closest to being light, that brass-colored membrane is not one thing but a nation of things too small to have a border between them. Cells, in the way that lichen is cells, in the way that a rug is threads. They take what the water brings — iron, sulfur, the faint dilute sweetness of dissolved rock — and they take the dregs of the light, and out of these they make more of themselves and, without meaning to, more of the stone. Because the skin is not merely sitting on the mineral. It is the reason the mineral has frills. Every ridge on every basin lip is a place where the skin grew a little, and the water, passing over the raised edge, set down its grain of stone on it, and the skin grew over that, and so on, one layer of the living and one of the dead, until the lips of the pools are ledgers of the two taking turns. If you broke a rim and looked at the break you would see it: pale bands and darker bands, a tree ring that is not a tree, going back further than the eye can follow. The skin is the oldest thing in the hollow that is alive, and it has been building its own house grain by grain the whole time, and the house is the hollow.

It has no age you could give it. Any single cell lives a day, a week. The skin has lived since the seam first opened. It does not remember and does not need to; it is memory, in the only sense that matters here, which is that it is the shape of everything that has happened.

Then the fly. Look for it along the wet ridges in the middle pools, where the skin is thickest, and you will find it doing what it does: three paces, stop, two paces, stop, a quick rotation of the head, on again. It is the color of the walls, slate going to brown, and about the size of a grain of barley. Its wings are two small stubs that fold flat along the back and have not lifted anything in a great many generations. There is nowhere in this hollow to fly to. The mist would bead on a wing and drag it down within a yard, and above the mist there is only the grey coin, and beyond the grey coin is a world with no brass skin in it and nothing this fly can eat. So the wings went. The legs got longer and the feet got the fine, splayed pads that let it run on a surface that is almost liquid. It grazes the skin. It walks over the living membrane and rasps up what it can and leaves a track, a fainter brass behind it, that heals over by the next time you look.

Its young are in the skin, small pale commas that eat their way through the mat from the inside and pupate in the ridges and emerge already at home. The whole species — if it is a species, if there is enough of it to be called that — lives in this one basin of the world and has never met another fly. Once, a long time ago, a winged ancestor fell in from above the way the leaves fall in, and did not die, and here is the consequence. You saw one this morning and one an hour later and thought they might be the same fly. They might have been. There are perhaps a few hundred of them at any time, and they are all, in the way that counts, the same fly, rearranged.

Then the thing in the deep pool. It is harder to see because it does not want to be seen, or does not want anything, and because it is the color of the water. Let the pool go still. There, on the ochre floor, that pale jointed shape moving the way a slow thought moves: forward, adjusting, forward. It has more legs than you would guess and no eyes at all, only the smooth places where eyes used to be. It came in with the water. Somewhere under the hollow, in the dark where the seam begins, there is a network of cracks and pockets full of the same warm water, and things live in that network, and this one's ancestors were among them, and when the crack found daylight some of them were carried up into the basin and found it good and stayed. It is a cousin of the small animals you would find under a wet stone in a garden, and it is nothing like them. It eats what settles: the fly that dies and sinks, the leaf that fell before the mineral had finished sealing it, the flakes of the skin that break off and drift down. It eats the hollow's leavings. It is the floor's mouth.

It does not know there is a sky. It has never needed to. The temperature has never changed in the lifetime of any of its line, and it has a body built for exactly this temperature and no other, so that if you lifted it out into the ordinary air it would begin to die at once, not from injury but from the fact of difference. It has arranged itself so that nothing can happen to it here. Nothing has.

Three lives, then. A skin that is a country, a fly that is a village, and a pale animal that is a single sentence repeated. That would seem to be the census.

But you felt the place was occupied, and these three do not account for it. They are too small, too diffuse, too indifferent. What you felt was more like a presence in a room — the sense you have when you enter a house and know before any evidence that someone is at home.

Consider the shelf again. Three fitted stones, dry, above the drip. Consider the bowl ground into the basin lip, and the groove beside it where a vessel was set down and lifted and set down. Consider the cord that is stone now.

Those were not made by people passing through. A passing person does not fit stones. The notches in the wall are spaced for one stride, not several; the bowl is worn smooth on one side, the side a right hand would favor. Someone came here over and over, for years, alone. Someone knew the way down in the dark and knew where to sit and knew which pool was warm enough and which was too mineral to drink and which was for the vessel. Someone tied that cord around that knob for a reason that was clear to them and is not clear to you and left it, and came back, and found it a little harder each time, a little more the color of the wall, and did not untie it.

And then they stopped coming — or they did not stop. That is the thing you cannot know from inside the hollow. The bowl is waiting. The shelf is dry. The steps have not filled in the way your footprints are filling in, which means feet have been on them more recently than the stone would like, or means nothing, because the stone has no opinion. Perhaps they come once a year, in a season the mist will not let you name, down the notched wall with a vessel and something for the shelf, and sit in the warmth for as long as you sat, and go up again. Perhaps they died before your grandmother was born and the place is simply slower at forgetting than you expected. The hollow does not distinguish. To the hollow the person is another leaf that fell in from above and stayed a while and left an impression the width of a body, and the mineral is closing over the impression at the same unhurried pace it closes over everything, and whether the leaf falls in again next autumn is not the hollow's concern.

So the visitor is not the presence either, or not all of it.

Go back to the skin.

You touched it and it shrank from your finger. Think about what that means. It is aware, at the level of chemistry, that something has arrived that is not water. It responds. It responded to the leaf and the cord and the person's hand at the bowl, and to your foot in the shallows, and in every case the response was the same: withdraw, wait, return, grow over. It is doing that to your footprints now. It is doing that, very slowly, to the shelf and the steps and the stone cord. The skin is not merely living in the hollow; the hollow is what the skin has made, and is still making, and every surface in it that is not moving water is the skin's shell or the skin itself. The walls that weep are the skin's outside. The basins that nest and overlap are its many mouths, each one taking the water in at the lip and letting it out at the apron. The seam where the water enters is where it drinks. The crack under the lowest pool is where it lets go. The one low continuous note is not the sound of water leaving rock. It is a pulse.

And the fly, then, is not a resident. The fly is what lives on the animal's hide the way small things live on the hides of large ones, grazing, tolerated, never quite noticed. The pale creature in the deep pool is what lives in its gut, eating what it does not need. The person with the vessel was something that came to drink from it and was permitted to, the way an ox is permitted to drink from a river, and left a mark, and the mark is being smoothed the way a river smooths a mark. You were permitted too. You are being smoothed too.

It is one thing. It has been one thing since the seam opened. It has no eyes and no wish for them and has never been anywhere else and cannot go; it is the size of a chapel with the roof gone and the age of the water, and it holds one temperature and one note and one grey level of light regardless of what the sun outside is doing, because it has arranged the mist to keep them so. It is not growing and it is not dying. It has stopped needing a direction. Everything that falls into it becomes it. Every visitor who comes down the notched wall and sits in the warmth and finds it hard to leave is feeling what it is like to be, briefly, inside something else's patience.

What lives there is the place. There is nothing in the hollow that is not it, and the hollow is full because it is one body pressed against its own walls, and it was occupied before you arrived because the occupant is the thing you stepped down into. It closed over your skin like a hand resting on a stone in the sun. It was a hand. You were the stone.
