**The Tenants**

Start with one. Not the ranks — one animal, chosen at random from the ten thousand on the north wall at about eight meters, the depth where the light is still good.

Its tube is white and about the length of your hand, and it is not smooth. It carries fine ridges, dozens of them, tight near the base and wider toward the mouth, the way a tree's rings space out in wet years. Each ridge is a growth increment. The animal adds to the tube only at the lip, a rim of new mineral every so often, and so the tube is a record of its whole life stacked bottom to top, and the base — fused to the basalt, then to its neighbors, then to the coppery plating that has crept over the fusion — is the oldest thing about it. If the tarn has a founding generation, they are down there, at the base of the honeycomb, walled in by their own descendants.

The body that reaches out of the tube is dark, nearly black, and it has no face. What it has is a bellows. The front third is a hood of muscle that flares open on the intake and clamps on the exhalation; behind that, inside the tube where you cannot see, is a basket of fine tissue through which the water passes, and it is in this basket that the animal's real work happens. It is not eating what it pumps. There is nothing in the water to eat — no plankton, no drifting weed, nothing green has ever grown here. It is farming. The basket is packed with bacteria that take the dissolved sulfide and iron and copper the seeps release and burn them, slowly, at three degrees above freezing, and pass a fraction of the energy back to their host. The pump provides the flow. The tenants provide the meal. The knock — when the exhaled body slams home against the rim — is the sound of a stroke completing and a fresh charge of water being drawn across the farm.

Cut one open and it bleeds turquoise. The blood carries copper the way ours carries iron, and it is nearly the same color as the water, so the cloud disappears at once into the tarn, as if the animal were only a slightly thickened region of the lake that had decided, for a while, to have edges.

They do not leave the tubes. They cannot; the base is sealed. Whatever they need must come to them on the current they collectively create, and whatever they produce — waste, spawn, warmth, the fine calcareous silt of the growing tubes — goes out on it. They are stationary and the lake moves. It is possible to look at the whole knocking wall and see it as a single organism that has simply outsourced its circulation to the water it lives in.

---

The grazers, one level down, are built for the opposite problem. Nothing comes to them. They must go to it.

Each is a low dome of overlapping plates, dull ochre-brown, streaked where the plating has begun to take hold on the shell as it does on the rock. Lift one — it takes real force, the foot grips like a suction cup on glass — and the underside is a broad pale muscle with a slot in the front, and in the slot, a belt. The belt is the rasp. It runs on rollers of cartilage, a loop of tiny hooked teeth advancing over the rock a few millimeters at a stroke, and the teeth are black and glossy and very hard, because the animal has done what limpets do on iron-rich shores and armored them with mineral pulled straight out of the water: iron oxide, laid down in the tooth cap grain by grain, harder than the glass it scrapes. The tarn feeds them the tool and then the tool feeds them the tarn.

They have no eyes and no need of any. They find the film by taste, through the sole of the foot, and a grazer set down on a patch of wall that has already been scraped will sit motionless for a long time and then begin, very slowly, to turn, until the foot finds a fresh edge of ochre and locks on. They do not cross one another's tracks if they can help it. From above, in good light, the crater wall below the pump-ranks is a map of bright swaths — polished glass, mirror-clean — separated by narrow ridges of untouched dull film, and the map is being redrawn constantly at a speed of perhaps a body-length a day.

What passes through them is a paste of glass and film. The film is stripped in the gut; the glass is not; it comes out the back as a fine grey flour and falls. Every grazer is also a mill, and the powder on the floor of the tarn — the drifts, the softened profiles of the lower shelves — is the collective output, the crater's first stratum, laid down by an animal that has never once seen it.

---

The ribbons are harder to hold in the eye, because there are so many of them and they are nearly nothing.

Take one from the shoal at fifteen meters. It is the length of your forearm, four fingers wide, and thin enough that you can read the pattern of the wall behind it. There is a dark thread down the center — the gut, and the little that is in it — and along both edges, running the full length, a row of pale rectangular cells stacked like coins. Those are the batteries. Each stack is a column of flattened muscle cells that have given up contracting and instead hold a voltage across themselves, and when the animal fires, all the stacks discharge together, and the current runs out through the water in a ring around the body, and you feel it as a tap on the skin and a taste of tin on the tongue.

Whether the discharge is for the ribbon or for the shoal is unclear, but the shoal behaves as though it were for the shoal. A ribbon that fires is answered, within a second or two, by the ribbons around it, and the answer spreads outward in a widening ring and dies at the edges, and then somewhere else another one starts. Watch long enough and you begin to see that the rings are not random: they run along the current, downstream, so that the whole population is continually passing word in the direction the water is turning. What word, or whether it is a word, there is no telling. Perhaps it is only *here* — perhaps in water this cold and this dark and this crowded the only thing worth saying is that you exist, and the only way to say it is to shock everyone within reach.

The cost is the plating. Every discharge that finds the wall leaves a fleck of copper behind, but every discharge that finds a neighbor leaves a fleck on the neighbor, and the ribbons themselves are being plated, thinly, over a lifetime. The young are clear as water. The old are visibly dulled, veined with brown, and heavier; they ride lower; they brush the wall more often and ground more often and plate faster, and in the lowest depths, where the vent fluid is richest and the shoal is densest, you find them at the end of the process — ribbons that have stopped swimming, that hang against the rock stiff and dark, that have become knobs of metal with a faint outline of animal inside. This is the lumpy plating near the floor. It is a shoreline of the dead. The ribbons that are still moving flick past it constantly, firing, adding to it.

---

The bells are the exception to everything.

They are the only inhabitants that face upward. Each is a fist of clear jelly with a bubble of gas inside it, and the bubble is the whole art of the animal — inflated or bled off by a thin muscular valve, so precisely that a bell will hold a depth to within a few centimeters through a whole day's slow rotation. They are clustered just below the surface, thousands deep, a ceiling of soft lenses, and from underneath they cut the sun into a moving lace.

Where the gas comes from is worth asking. It is not the seep gas; that is sulfide and it would kill them. Look close at a bell for long enough and you will see it: a tiny bead rising from below, silvered, no bigger than a grain of sand, and the bell tilts toward it and takes it in. The ribbons make it. Every discharge that runs through water splits a little of that water into its gases, and the bubbles too small to see rise through the shoal, through the pump-ranks, up the column, and the bells harvest them. The electricity of the middle depths is the buoyancy of the surface. A population of animals that has never oriented to light is holding up, by accident, the only population that has.

The bells feed on what rises with the bubbles — fine particulate off the mill, flecks of spent film, the dust of dead tubes. They have a faint green tinge that the other animals lack, and it may mean nothing, or it may mean that something in the jelly has begun, in this one place in the whole tarn, to notice the sun and make a little use of it. If the lake ever grows anything that could be called a plant, it will begin here, in the bells, as a stain.

---

There are the small ones.

In the powder: segmented, pale, blind, each a finger long, visible only as ridges moving under the surface. They eat what settles — dead ribbons, before the plating claims them; the fine calcareous ash that the tube-lips shed; whatever the grazers have not taken from the glass. Their castings are darker than the powder around them, and in the lowest drifts the whole floor is spotted with them, so that the sediment is being reworked and turned almost as fast as it is laid. In an ordinary lake this would be the job of a hundred species. Here it is one.

In the honeycomb: the crawlers, no bigger than a fingernail, many-legged, quick by the standards of this place. They move across the outside of the tube-ranks in loose troops, cleaning. The tubes grow a fuzz — a bacterial felt that thickens in the still water between the pumping jets — and the crawlers graze it off and keep the tube surfaces bare. That is the ordinary business. But where a tube stands plugged, they gather, and that is not ordinary.

The plugged tubes are scattered through the ranks — one in several hundred — and the plug is a dark waxy mass, glossy, filling the mouth completely. It is not the animal's hood. Watch a plug over a series of dives and it changes: it darkens, it sinks a little into the tube, the crawlers around it thicken from a dozen to a hundred and then cluster so densely the plug disappears under them. Then, one day, they are gone, and the tube is empty and clean to the bottom, white as bone, and nothing is left of whatever was in it.

The kind reading is that the pump died and the crawlers took it. The other reading is what the accounting suggests. Nothing here is lost. The tubes are never abandoned in decay; they are always emptied *cleanly*, and the crawlers that leave a finished tube are more numerous than the crawlers that came to it, and a swarm of them, followed, will drift out into the current and — but they are too small to follow far. Perhaps the pump, at the end, goes back into the lake as a hundred small things that will grow up to be something else in it. Perhaps the bells, or the burrowers, or the ribbons are what a crawler becomes. Perhaps the whole tarn is not several species at all but a single household with its members in different rooms doing different work, and the plug is simply a door closing between one job and the next.

Or perhaps not. The evidence is a clean tube and a taste of tin.

---

Then there is what lives under the floor.

The seeps are small: a scatter of orange chimneys no taller than a hand, each with a wisp of warmer water shimmering above it. But every so often the shimmer thickens. A milky thread comes up out of a chimney — a cloud, pale, dense, turning in on itself — and drifts a meter or two before the current takes it and pulls it apart across the floor. Look in the cloud with a light and it is alive: specks, threads, a haze of something too small to name, all moving. It is sent up. It arrives.

The eruption did not make the animals. It made a room for them. Below the plateau, in the cracked hot rock the meltwater drained into and the aquifers the mountain broke open on its way up, the chemistry that feeds the tarn has been running for longer than the tarn has existed, and something has been living on it in total dark — not a community of hand-length things but their ancestors, or their seeds, a smear of life in the pores of the rock that has never had walls to build on or open water to pump. The tarn is its first window. The population above is what it does when it is given one. The pumps and the mills and the shoal and the bells are all, from below, an outgrowth — a bud that has pushed up into the light and taken on shapes the dark never needed.

The thing in the rock does not know about the light either. It only knows that a channel opened, and that what it sends up does not come back.

---

So: what lives here.

A wall that breathes and knocks. A skin of plated animals that eat the mountain and shed it as flour. A cloud of living voltage passing word downstream. A ceiling of lenses holding themselves up on the cloud's exhaust. Worms in the flour, cleaners in the honeycomb, closed doors in the ranks, and under the floor a slow pale exhalation from a resident that has never seen the room it is furnishing.

None of them is warm. None of them is fast. None of them will still be alive, as individuals, when the plating reaches the rim or the powder fills the lower shelves or the first green stain spreads through the bells. But they are the only thing on the mountain doing anything at all, and the noise of it — the knocking and the grinding and the crackle — is the sound of a place being made habitable by its inhabitants, from the inside, at the pace of stone.
