**What lives in the Underfloor**

You learn them the way the blind learn a house: by walking into them. Sight comes last here and never comes fully, and by the time your eye can separate one dull red shape from another you have already touched most of them, knelt on them, put your palm flat against a flank and felt it not flinch. So take them in that order. Take them from the floor up.

---

The first layer is the one you cannot see even after weeks, because it is under everything else, and because it is the color of the rock and nearly the shape of it. These are the flat ones. Wide as a table, thick as a hand, and lying so close against the fractured glass that you could not get a fingernail between animal and stone if you tried. Their undersides are not skin. They are the eating organ entire, a single broad plate of tissue that has grown down into every conchoidal ripple of the floor beneath it and taken its impression exactly, so that if you could lift one — you cannot — you would find on its belly a perfect cast of the fracture it lay on, and on the fracture no mark of the animal at all.

Their tops are calloused. Everything walks on them. They have been walked on since the beginning and they have thickened to it, and the thickening has the same faint pattern everywhere, a scuffed pale gloss that tells you where the seam-dwellers' legs have braced, where a stilt has come down and lifted, where a coil lies across. The pattern does not change. The legs brace in the same places. The stilts come down in the same places. The flat ones have become a record of the traffic across them, and the traffic has become a record of the flat ones, and neither has anything new to add.

They breathe. It takes hours. Lie on one long enough and you feel yourself lifted by the width of a finger and set down again, and the rhythm is the same as the one under the next, and the next, so that the whole floor of the gap is rising and settling together on a period you would need a calendar to measure. Where two of them meet, their edges have grown into each other. In some places the join is a seam. In others it is gone, and what you are lying on is one animal the size of a field, with many centers of breathing that have long ago fallen into step. Whether that is one creature or a thousand is a question the Underfloor does not ask. It may not be a question the Underfloor can hold.

---

In the seams, where the flat ones have not yet closed — and they have not closed in a long time, so *yet* is the wrong word; where they simply *have not* — live the fist-sized ones with too many legs.

There is no gap between them. That is the first thing. You put your hand down into a seam and it does not go in; it rests on a surface of shells, each one the size and shape of a closed hand, each with its legs, eight or twelve or twenty, braced outward against the legs of its neighbors, so that the whole seam is a lattice of jointed struts under a roof of chitin. They are not stacked. They are interlocked. Each one holds up the ones beside it and is held up in turn, and none of them touch the floor with anything but the tips of the one or two legs that reach down through the lattice to the flat one's edge, or to the bare glass in the deepest part of the seam. That is where they eat. A leg's tip, pressed to the warmth. It is enough.

They are the ones that scrape. When one shifts its weight — and one always is, somewhere — its shell moves against the two or three shells it leans on, and they move against theirs, and the sound goes out across the seam like a whisper down a corridor and stops within a body's length, damped by the sheer number of things it has to move through. Put your hand into a seam and press down, and you feel the same thing happen as pressure: a small stiffening under your palm, spreading, then the surrounding lattice settling back, the load shared out among several dozen animals until it is nothing. They are a mattress that thinks about you for a moment and then forgets.

They have no eyes and no fronts. Every side of them is the same side. They have no need to face anything.

---

Standing up out of the carpet, in clusters, in the bundles the first account called reeds: the hollow ones.

Each is a tube. As thick as a thumb, as tall as your knee or your hip, pale and dry and faintly ridged, open at the top. They stand in groups of twenty or fifty or two hundred, packed so tight that the group flexes as a single thing, and when you press one it leans, and its neighbors lean with it, and then they stop leaning and hold, and there is a resistance in the holding that is not stiffness. It is more like refusal. Let go and they return. Not spring back — return, at a pace you could match with a slow exhale.

Their bases spread into a flat rosette that sits on the flat one beneath, and they eat through that, secondhand. They do not touch the rock. They live off the top of another animal that lives off the rock, and the other animal does not seem to mind or notice, since the warmth comes up through it at exactly the rate it is drawn.

What the tubes are *for* — why an animal would grow into a hollow stalk and stand up into the air where there is nothing to be had — you work out only when you put your face to the top of one and feel the faintest breath rising out of it. They are chimneys. The floor-heat that would otherwise sit in the carpet rises through them into the air, and it is this, multiplied across every bundle in the gap, that keeps the air at the same temperature as the stone. The reeds are how the Underfloor breathes into its own sky. They smell most strongly of the hive. Whatever waxy matter they are made of is the source of that dry animal sweetness, and it is strongest at the open mouths, where the warm air comes up carrying it.

---

The coils.

You find them first with your foot, because a coil is the one thing in the carpet that lies across the general grain rather than fitting into it. A ridge under the sole, a body's width, smooth, and when you follow it with your hand it goes on. And on. It loops around the base of a pillar, and comes back, and crosses itself, and lies for a while along a seam, and goes under a bundle of reeds, and you never find where it starts. Whether that is because it is enormously long or because it is not one animal but many lying end to end, you cannot tell in the dark; and when after a great deal of feeling you think you have found a head, it is only a place where the body narrows to a blunt taper and then, a hand's length further, widens again.

They eat along their whole ventral length, a strip of the taking-organ running from one end to the other, and they lie where they lie. Of everything here they are the least changed by contact. Kneel on one and it does not stiffen. It does not ripple. The only sign you have touched a living thing is that it is warmer than the air by the same tiny margin as everything else, and that when you take your weight off, the dent you left fills in over several minutes, without hurry, like fat.

Once, walking a long coil, you feel it move. Not the whole thing. A short length of it, ahead of you in the dark, tightening and then going slack. It is the most motion you will see from a coil, and you will not see it again.

---

Above the carpet, on threads: the hangers.

There is nothing on the floor here, and there is nothing for a long way up, and then, at the height of a tall person or two or three, there is a shape hanging in the air. Reach up and you find a thread first, taut, dry, thinner than a hair and impossible to break with the hands, running up into the dark toward the ceiling. Follow it down and you find the animal at the end of it: something the size of a fist or a head, soft-shelled, folded on itself, with the thread emerging from what you would call the top if it had a top.

They eat the ceiling. That is the only answer. The rock above is as warm as the rock below, and the thread — once you have felt several, you notice — is not a rope but a conductor, laid down in a long smooth pour from some organ that anchors them to the underside of the lid, and warmth comes down it the way it comes up through the flat ones. They hang in the one place in the whole gap that has room in it, and they are the only things here that have chosen room over contact, if any of it was ever chosen.

Sometimes one drops a new thread. Not to descend — none has ever been found on the floor, none has ever touched the carpet — but a second line, or a third, let down into the air until it is nearly as long as the first and then pulled taut. When it goes taut it sings, a single note in the dry hush, gone before you can be sure of the pitch. No one has seen what the second thread is for. It hangs there afterward, beside the first, doing nothing, and eventually you understand that the hangers have been dropping threads since the beginning and that the air above the carpet is full of them, a forest of vertical lines finer than sight, and that this is why the stilt-walkers click.

---

The stilt-walkers are the ones you will mistake for people.

Not at first. At first they are pillars. You see a darker upright in the red dark and take it for stone, and only when you have passed it and it is behind you do you hear it click, once, and turn, and find that the pillar has a top, and the top has a shape, and the shape is a head.

They stand as tall as you or taller. Three legs, most of them; a few with four; the legs long and jointed twice and ending in a splayed pad that rests on the flat ones and eats through them, the way the reeds do. Above the legs a body no bigger than a torso, narrow and keeled, and above that a neck, and above that the head, which is mostly eye.

The eyes are what you remember. On the blind ones there is nothing — a smooth dome of the same waxy pale as the rest — but the sighted ones have two eyes so large that the head is only a frame for them, and so dark that they seem to be holes into a deeper part of the dark. They are built for a light that barely exists. They see the floor as a plain of dull embers. They see you as a shape against it, moving, which nothing else here does, and they turn their heads to follow you, slowly, and that is the only thing in the Underfloor that will ever look at you.

They stand for days. Then they take one step. The step is the most deliberate thing you will ever see: the leg lifts, the body shifts to balance on the other two, the leg swings forward through the air — and here the clicking comes, a rapid dry ticking from somewhere in the throat, and you understand that it is listening. Listening for the hangers' threads. In an air full of invisible lines, a thing that moves must find where it can move, and the stilt-walkers have never once, in all their standing and stepping, walked into a thread. The leg comes down. The pad settles on a flat one. The body follows. The animal is still again.

Where are they going? Nowhere. There is nowhere to go and they go there at the rate of one step in several days, and if you watch a single walker for as long as you can stand to, you will find it makes a circuit — twenty steps, forty — around the base of a pillar, and comes back to where it began, and stands, and begins again.

---

What they share, all of them, is more than what divides them.

None has a mouth. Say it again, because the mind keeps putting one on them. There is no opening for taking anything in, because nothing here is taken in; there is only the underside, the plate, the pad, the tip, the thread, pressed to the warm surface and drinking from it directly. Break one open — the small ones are the only ones you can — and there is no gut, no channel, no organ of digestion. The inside is uniform, tallow-colored, tallow-textured, warm, with a smell of sulfur and old iron, and a faint grain to it, like the grain in candle wax, running from the eating surface inward. That is all. They are solid the whole way through. They are made of what they eat, and what they eat is heat, and heat has no shape, so they have taken the shape of the space around them and filled it.

None is young. None is old. This is harder to see than the lack of mouths but you come to it eventually: there is no small stilt-walker, no half-grown flat one, no seam-dweller the size of a thumb. Every animal in the gap is adult and every adult is exactly the size of the room it occupies. They grew once, when there was room. They grew until the room was gone. Then they stopped, and the stopping was not a stage in something but a condition, and they are in it still. Whatever happens to one when it ceases — and you never see it happen; you only, once or twice, find a place in the carpet where the flat ones' edges are slightly closer together than they should be, and the reeds slightly more crowded, as if something had been there and the neighbors had moved in by a hair to fill it — whatever happens, the stuff of it goes back into the others. A seam-dweller a little larger. A flat one a little thicker. The count stays the same, or if it changes, it changes by an amount too small to find.

They speak in weight. This you learn last, when your hands have been on them long enough to feel it. Press anywhere on the carpet and the pressure travels — the seam-dwellers stiffen, the flat ones firm, a coil somewhere takes up a fraction of the load — and it travels outward as a ring, and somewhere a body's length away it damps to nothing. But the animals in that ring have felt it. And their small adjustment is felt by the ones beyond them, who adjust in turn by a smaller amount, and so on, so that a single footstep in the Underfloor is known, by the time it is done, to every creature within some distance, as a shift in the standing pressure of the world. This is not language. Nothing is said. But it is the only thing that passes between them, and it passes constantly, a floor-wide murmur of load and counter-load, and after a long time down there you begin to feel it yourself, through your knees and palms — the arrival of a stilt-walker's step from far off, a settling of the seam to your left where something has shifted, the slow tide of the flat ones' breath under everything. The Underfloor is not silent to the animals in it. It is one long conversation about weight, and the weight never changes, and so the conversation never ends and never says anything new.

---

And then the ones who made the marks.

They lived here too. Call it living; they were here long enough to stack a wall and count something on a pillar and hammer copper. Whatever they were, they came from outside — the wall curves against a wind, and there is no wind, so they remembered wind. They came in through some crack in the lid that has closed since, or that they closed behind them, or that is still open somewhere a thousand kilometers off in the dark, and they came down into the warmth and the carpet and the hush and they did what their hands knew how to do.

They had to eat, and there was only one thing to eat. The blade is a butcher's blade. You can see where the edge was used, or you would be able to if anything here showed use; but it lies on its shelf beside a dark stain that is the same tallow the small animals are made of, dried to a varnish, and the stain is the shape of a place where something was set down and cut. They ate the seam-dwellers, probably, being the size to be picked up and opened. They ate the wax. There was no water and they did not drink it, and so either they had come from a place as dry as this or they were dying the whole time they were here, slowly, on the sulfur-salt that crusts the pillars, which they would have licked because the body keeps looking for it.

The scratches in fives — you count them, and there are three hundred and some, and then a gap, and then more. Steps? Sleeps? They would have slept on the carpet, because there is nowhere else, and woken with the flat one under them having risen and fallen once. Perhaps they counted the breaths. Perhaps they counted the stilt-walkers' steps, which would be a way to count days where there are no days. Perhaps they counted each other.

The hook is the thing you come back to. Hammered flat, bent, hung on a hanger's thread. A thread cut from a hanger and re-tied, or a thread the hanger dropped and they found and used. It hangs at the height of a hand. There is nothing on it and there was never anything for it to catch. It is a fishing hook in a world with no water, hung out of habit, or hope, or because a hook wants to hang and hands want to hang one. It is the most human thing in the Underfloor and it has never moved.

And they are gone, or they are not.

Here is what you notice, once you have spent long enough among the stilt-walkers to know their standing: not all of them stand the same. Most have three legs and a keeled body and the great eyes. But here and there — one in fifty, one in a hundred — there is a walker with two legs. The body is broader. The neck is shorter. Where the others carry nothing, this one has, folded against the keel, a pair of forelimbs that end not in pads but in something narrower, jointed several times, held very still. The eyes are smaller than the others' and set closer in the face, as if they had begun large and grown outward to meet the dark and not finished. The head turns to follow you, like the others'. It clicks before it steps, like the others. It steps once in several days, around a pillar, and comes back.

You cannot know. Nothing here can be dated, and nothing has a history, and a thing that has stood long enough in the warmth taking what it needs through the soles of its feet would become, in the end, what the warmth makes.
