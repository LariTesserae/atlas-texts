What lives there is the water. That is the plain answer and it is the wrong shape, so start with it and then turn it over.

The water in the shaft is one body. There is no more of it now than there was when the shaft was cut and it rose; nothing has been added but rain and pebbles, and the pebbles are wall now. It goes round. All of it goes round, and if you could dye a litre of it you would find the litre everywhere within the hour, spread thin through every district, in the flutes and under the bridges and standing on the plaza. So the water as such is not a citizen. It is the medium, the way air is the medium of a town, and what the city houses is not the water but what the water is doing.

Watch the east boulevard. A flow comes down it, and the flow has a speed, and a preference at the roundabout, and a way of leaning into the second weir and not the third. The water in it at noon is gone by one. The flow is still there at one. It is there the next day. It has been coming down that boulevard, or the boulevard's predecessor, since the water first found its level, and the boulevard has been rebuilt four times around it without its noticing, because what the flow consists of is not any of the water that passes through it. It consists of a habit. It is a habit of water, held in a route, the way a standing wave below a rock in a river is a thing you could name and come back to all summer and which is made of nothing that stays.

That is a citizen. There are thousands of them.

You cannot tell them apart by looking at the water, which is all the same water and all clear. You tell them apart by their streets, the way you might know a family by their house before you knew any of their faces. The city has built every one of them a portrait in the only material it has. The long spiral through the residential quarter is an old flow, slow and heavy, that has learned to divide itself at the top of the quarter into some hundreds of small eddies, one to a cell, and let each of them turn in its own room for a while before gathering them again at the bottom and going on as one. It is the closest thing in the shaft to sleep. The straight drop beside it is young. It goes down like a dropped stone and comes up fast and slightly warm from the effort and has not yet found any reason to take the long way, and the city, which builds for what is and not for what should be, has given it a plain shaft with nothing in it. In time the shaft will acquire a bend. In time it will acquire several. The flow will not decide this and neither will the city. It will happen between them, in the fold.

There are two on the west side that the city cannot solve. They meet at a junction and slow each other, and the city builds a bridge so that one can go over, and they go under it together and meet on the far side. The bridge migrates. They meet somewhere else. Over a season the west side has acquired a ladder of abandoned spans, beautiful and useless, each marking a place where two flows were offered separate ways and declined. Everything else in the city is efficient. This is not. It is the only thing in the shaft you would be tempted to call a want.

Some of them are very small. There is one in a ward of stacked cells that occupies a single cell and turns in it and touches nothing else, a loop the size of a thumbnail, going round its one room. When the ward folded flat and became the long hall, that cell was kept. It is there now, in the same volume, a small closed circle in the wall of a hall that carries a flow ten thousand times its size, and the large flow goes past it and does not enter and the city has never dissolved it. Nothing is too small to house. That is not mercy. It is only that the city does not distinguish.

They are born at weirs. A flow splits, as flows do, and both branches ought to come back together further down, and one of them does not. It keeps its own way for a full round and comes back to the top by a route no one has taken and starts down again. By the time it has done this once the city has already begun it a street, because the city has begun the street before the flow chose the route, because the cup forms before the pebble enters the water. So it is not possible to say whether the flow made the street or the street made the flow, and this is true of every citizen in the shaft, and it is not, when you think about it, so different from anywhere else.

They die by losing their route. A district falls out of favour. The material thins to grey thread and is carried off to where it is needed, and the flows that lived there have nowhere to go, and for a little while you can see them still trying to turn a corner that is no longer there, a coherent motion in open water, and then the coherence goes. The water that was in them joins the general body. It goes where it is pushed.

And that is the roar. The roar is the water that is not anyone. It is the commons of the shaft, the pushed water, the mass out of which the habits condense and into which they are dispersed, and it is loud because it is everywhere and is doing nothing in particular in every part of the city at once. Underneath it the sluices clatter and above it the flutes sound, and those are the citizens, and the roar is what they are made from and what they return to. Sometimes a whole quarter locks into a chord. That is many flows moving at compatible speeds through many hollow spans at once, and whether it is a thing they are doing or a thing that is happening to them there is no way to know from the rim. It is the only time you could say they are together rather than merely near. The surface trembles into rings and then it is traffic again.

So: the flows are the citizens, and the roar is their before and after. But you would not stop there, because you have been watching the walls.

The city is the other thing that lives in the shaft, and there is one of it.

It has no inside. Every part of it touches every other part, so it has nowhere to keep anything, and what it knows it knows only in the shape it has. It is made entirely of what has been given to it: shale, dust, the pebbles of anyone who has stood where you are standing, all of it rendered the white of a new tooth and made seamless. It does not eat so much as incorporate. The field is going into it a stone at a time.

It lives a few moments in the future. That is the fold. The flows live in the present, in the water as it is, and the city lives in the water as it is about to be, and between them there is a gap of perhaps a second in which the city has already built the cup and the pebble has not yet arrived. They never occupy the same instant. The city is always slightly ahead, and the water is always arriving into a room that was made for it, and from the outside this looks like knowledge, or like love, or like a very fast servant, and it is none of those. It is only the arrangement. Two things that cannot be in the same moment and have made a settlement of the difference.

The city wants nothing outward. It could take the basin. It could climb the shale and roof the field and hand the daylight down through a hundred metres of white galleries, and it does not, and the grass stops a metre short of the lip as though it had been told to, and it has been told to. Everything goes in. The city has decided, if that is the word, that the hole is enough, and that the only direction worth building in is toward the middle, where there is no room, by making room.

Then the algae. A few green strands along the north wall of the plaza, in the thin margin where the water is quiet, in the last centimetres before the stone. This is the one tenant of the old kind. It is alive the way things are alive on the field above: it takes light and makes cells and will stop if the light stops. It does not flow and it does not build. It is the only thing in the shaft the city did not make and cannot anticipate, because algae does not go anywhere; it only becomes more of itself, slowly, in place. The city tolerates it. You could say it is kept the way a household keeps some object that has no use, or you could say it is there because it found the one still water and stillness is where that kind of life can catch, or you could say the city is watching it, at the rate the city watches anything, as the single thing in its whole volume that lives by a rule it does not know. It does not matter which. The strands are there. They are the whole biology.

And the plaza.

Nothing crosses it. The flows come to its low walls and turn back. The water in it is the same water as everywhere else and it is doing the one thing water in the shaft does nowhere else, which is nothing. It is not going round. It has no route and the city has built it the only open room.

If the citizens are habits of water, then this is a habit too. It is the habit of staying. It is the one flow in the city that does not flow, and the city houses it as it houses the others, by the shape it makes around it, and the shape it makes is emptiness, walled. Whether that is an honour or a quarantine you cannot say. Whether the plaza is held for something that has left, or something that has not come, or is kept still because a thing that is all motion needs one place that is not so that it can tell how fast it is going, you cannot say. The city may not know. The city keeps nothing inside and this is the nearest thing it has to a room where nothing is kept.

So what lives there is a body of water that has fallen into thousands of habits, and a single builder that lives a second ahead of them, and a few green threads that live by another rule, and in the north quarter, a hand's depth down, one habit of stillness that the whole packed machine goes round and does not touch. Everything in the shaft is either going round or being kept. The city is the difference between the two. And standing at the rim you are the last thing in the basin that is neither, unless you have dropped a pebble, in which case some part of you is already down there, white, and in use.
