Start with the plant, because you are standing on it, and because it is the one inhabitant that never leaves.

It doesn't know it is one thing. There's no reason it should. Each cushion grows on its own account — outward from its own center, a hand's width in a lifetime — and where it meets its neighbor it simply continues, and the neighbor continues into it, and neither has any way of registering the join. Unity is a fact about it, not an experience it has. What it has instead is a kind of appetite with a direction. The living skin grows fastest where the ground beneath is warm and rich, and the ground is warmest and richest along the tunnels, so the plant is always, slowly, leaning toward the birds — thickening its roof over the burrows, drawing its growth into the ridges between them, so that over centuries the whole surface has arranged itself around the colony the way scar tissue arranges itself around a splinter it has decided to keep. If you could see it from above, speeded up, you would see it flowing toward the noise.

It has no seasons of its own. Nothing here freezes and nothing here truly dries. Its year is the birds' year: the months of the roar, when the tunnels are warm and the droppings come down like a slow rain of salt and nitrogen and the skin puts on a bronze flush of new growth; and the months of quiet, when the colony is at sea and the plant lives on what it stored, and the surface goes a darker, waiting green. It marks time by occupation. The leaves are furred to drink cloud, and the cloud comes up the mountain because the lowlands are warm, and the lowlands are warm for reasons that have nothing to do with the plant, and so twice a day it is given water by a country it will never touch. Everything it needs arrives. It has never once had to go and get anything.

Underneath, in the dead mass, it is doing one other thing. The peat is acid, and the acid is slowly eating the granite. Where the fibrous layer sits on bare rock the stone is pitted and softening, a skin of grit forming between the mountain and the plant. It has taken this long to begin. The plant is, at last, making soil — for no one, since nothing else will ever grow here, and at a pace that makes the rock's own erosion look hasty. But it's doing it. The mountain was finished and the plant found one more thing to do to it.

---

The birds live in the dark.

This is what's hard to hold onto, watching the sky fill at dusk — that the flying is the smallest part. An adult comes in over the edge of the table with the light going and drops into a hundred thousand others all doing the same thing and finds, out of a seamless plain of identical cushions under a fog, the one tunnel that is hers. She does it by smell, probably. Each cushion has its own slight chemistry — this one has more droppings from this year's chicks, that one has a dead bird in it three seasons old, this one grew over a hearth — and the bird has the scent of her own tunnel-mouth the way you have the smell of your own house, and she finds it in the dark in a gale of wings. Then she is down, and she is in, and she spends the next many hours in a fibrous burrow the width of her own body, in a warmth that is the plant's and a noise that is the whole colony's, and this, not the sky, is where she lives. The ocean is where she works.

The chick knows nothing else. It hatches in the dark and stays in it for the whole of its first life: a tunnel floor of dry compacted stems, a smell of its own down and its parents' fish, a temperature that never changes, and the roar. The roar is its first world. It lives inside that sound the way something at the bottom of the sea lives inside pressure. Out of that undifferentiated wall of voices it must learn one — the call of the adult coming back to this tunnel and no other — and it learns it in days, and after that the roar is background and one thread in it is meaning. Every chick on the table is doing this. A million small deafnesses, each with a single door in it.

The parents are gone for days at a time. The chick sits in the dark, growing fat, listening for its door. When the adult comes she drops into the tunnel mouth and calls and the chick answers, and that exchange — two voices out of a million finding each other in a fog — is the whole colony's sound taken apart into its pieces. The roar is not a roar. It is that, happening everywhere at once.

They leave when they are ready without ever having seen the place. A fledgling comes up out of its tunnel one night into open air for the first time, into wings and stars and the drop of the escarpment, and goes over the edge toward a sea it has only smelled on its parents, and does not come back for years. When it does, it comes to this cushion. It is that specific. Somewhere in it is the shape of one tunnel in a plain of tunnels, held across an ocean.

---

Between the birds there are the small things, and they live on rhythm.

In the water-cups, when the cloud has filled them, something hatches. If you kneel and look — you will have to look; it is the size of a grain of rice and very nearly transparent — you'll see them, a faint glassy flicker in the dark water, hundreds to a pool, swimming in the brief lake the plant has made. They have an hour, or a day. They feed on what the cloud brings down and what the birds have dropped in, and they mate, and they lay in the peat at the bottom of the cup, and then the plant drinks the pool and the cup goes dry and the adults die where they lie, so small that they leave no trace, and the eggs wait. They can wait a long time. A hollow that doesn't fill for a year has eggs in it that will hatch when it does. There are cups the plant has grown over and closed, and inside them, in the dry dark, are eggs that were laid when the walls were new, and if the plant ever tears there and the cloud gets in, they will hatch. They live at the plant's pace and the cloud's pace, both at once, and neither pace is anything like a life.

The beetles keep the tunnel mouths. They are black, and slow, and they walk with the deliberate air of things that have never been hurried in the history of their kind. They eat what the birds drop at the threshold — fish scraps, scale, a spilled crop, the crumbs of a moult — and when a chick dies in a tunnel they go down after it in a long patient procession and come back up over the course of weeks. The flies are faster and take the same work in the air. The spiders string their webs across the water-cups, low, at the height a fly comes in to drink, and sit in the rim of the cushion with their legs drawn up, pale as the ash in the old hearths. When the cloud comes the webs sag with beads and the spiders wait that out too. Everything here waits. It is the local virtue.

The gulls don't wait. That is what makes them foreign. They come up from below each morning in ones and twos, big and dark and unhurried in the way of the competent, and they land at the colony margins and walk. They know the table. A gull that has been coming here for twenty years knows which cushions have thin roofs and which tunnels have chicks near the mouth and which of the standing pools the fledglings blunder into on their first night out, and it works them like a man working a shoreline, and when it has eaten it lifts and goes back down into the cloud country and sleeps somewhere none of this can hear it. They take perhaps one chick in a thousand. The colony doesn't register the loss. The plant registers the damage — the torn roofs, the bare peat — and closes it, at its pace, and the gull is back the next day with its long professional stride, and there is no malice anywhere in it. It is just the one inhabitant that commutes.

---

And the dead layer keeps things.

It is dry down there, and acid, and cold enough, and nothing rots that isn't rotted by the beetles first. So the plant is an archive, and everything that has ever gone down a tunnel or fallen into a cushion's crack and been grown over is still there. There are birds in the peat a thousand years old, whole, feathered, flattened to the thickness of a leaf, the wing-bones still articulated. There are eggs that never hatched, hard as stone. There are the small dark bodies of beetles in layers, like grains in wood, so that if you cut the peat you could count the seasons in them. There is the plant's own history — the leaves it grew and shed, stem on stem, packed so tight that a hand's depth of it is a century. And there are the things the people dropped.

That is how you know the people are old here. A hook goes down a burrow after a chick and the sinew breaks and the hook is gone, and the plant closes over the tunnel in a generation, and the hook is in the peat with the birds. When the people dig a new tunnel — or widen an old one, or cut a step into a cushion's flank to sit on — they find these things. A scoop of wing-bone with an edge worn by a hand that has been dead for longer than there have been walls. A wooden pin. A child's tooth. They find them and they know what they are, because they are the same things they carry now, and they put them back. They have a rule about it. What the plant has taken, the plant keeps.

---

So: the people.

They come up when the sky fills. That's the only calendar it needs. Down in the cloud country, where the fog is made, in some valley you can't see from here, there is a morning when the high air goes grey with wings for the first time in a year, and the people who have been watching for it see, and gather what they carry, and start up the escarpment. It's a day's climb. They come in small numbers — a few dozen, no more; the table can't feed more, and they know to the tunnel how many it can feed — and they come the same way each year, and their feet have made no path, because the plant takes footprints back in a week.

They speak on the way up. They fall quiet at the lip. Not from awe — they've done this since they were carried up as infants — but because from the lip on, speech is useless, and they've stopped bothering. Their language on the table is a language of hands. It is not the language they use below; it grew up here, in the roar, over more generations than anyone has counted, and it's a full language, with a grammar and a way of being rude and words for things that exist nowhere but on the plant: the flush of new gold on a cushion that means the tunnels under it are full; the smell of a burrow with a chick in it as against one with an egg; the particular sag in the surface that means the peat is thin and the roof will give. Children learn it up here, from their third or fourth season. Below, they use it only to speak of the table, and only among themselves, and only when they want the other people in the valley not to understand.

The old ones are deaf. It comes to everyone who does this long enough; the roar takes the ears a season at a time, and by the age when their hands are the most fluent they hear almost nothing anywhere. This is not counted a loss. The deaf are the ones who go first onto the table, because the noise can't turn them around, and they are the ones who sit at the polished stone. They sit with their palms flat on the plant. They say — their hands say — that they feel the pulse in it, the long swell and drop that runs through the colony's sound, and that it comes through the peat as a shudder, and that it is the plant breathing, and that the birds are only its voice. No one argues with them. It might be so.

What they take, they take with the wing-bone scoop, reaching down a tunnel to the elbow and feeling for the heat of a chick and bringing it up fat and blind and blinking, and killing it there with a turn of the wrist. Never two tunnels adjacent. Never a cushion that was worked in living memory — and living memory, for this, is kept in the walls. The walls were never shelters. They are tallies. Each low arc of stone encloses a stretch of cushions that was taken in a given generation, and stands as a mark that says *not here, not yet*, and the plant is climbing the walls at the exact pace it takes to heal the ground inside them, so that when a wall has gone under, the cushions it enclosed are ready again. They built the walls to be swallowed. That was the point of them. A wall you can still see means *wait*. A wall you can only see as a line in the green means *now*.

They eat under the roar, at the hearths, on the last stone there is, and they throw the bones down and the plant will have those too. They sleep the third night — never the first, never the second; the first two nights on the table are a kind of ordeal nobody is spared, lying in the dark inside a sound that comes through the ground into your teeth, and the children cry and cannot hear themselves crying. By the third night they sleep, and by the seventh they sleep well, and by the time the colony's season is turning and the fledglings are coming up blind out of the tunnels at night and going over the edge in their thousands, the people have been inside the roar so long that they've stopped hearing it as anything at all. It is just the shape the air has.

Then they go down.

They go down slowly, in the last of the birds, and the roar falls away behind them as the escarpment cuts it, and somewhere on the descent there is a point — everyone knows where; it is a particular shoulder of rock — where you can hear your own feet on the stone for the first time in a season. The children stop there. Every year the children stop there and turn around and look up at the lip of the table with their faces working, because the quiet has come down on them like a hand over the mouth and they don't know what it is. The old ones don't stop. They can't hear the difference. They walk on down into the cloud country with the scoops in their belts and the meat on their backs and the plant's warmth still in their knees, and they will be back when the sky fills, and their hands will have things to say that no one in the valley will follow.

That is who lives there. A plant that has never gone anywhere. A bird that lives in the dark and works at sea. A gull that commutes. The small ones that wait. And a people who visit, and have visited so long that the visiting has become a kind of residence — who are kept in the peat with the birds and the hooks, and who have made the only thing here that stands up out of the ground, and made it so that the ground would take it. Nobody on the table is trying to become anything else. The plant found the end of its shape, and everything that came after fitted itself to the plant, and there isn't anywhere further for any of it to go.
