Start with the moss, because the moss is most of what lives here and because everything else is a variation on its answer.

It is one thing. Not one species: one organism, a single continuous body of green laid over the whole tableland, a hand deep, a forearm deep, a man's height deep in the hollows, and there is no point on the plateau where you can stand on it and say you are standing on a different moss from the one a horizon away. It has no edge except the edge of the world. It has no center. Cut it — and the people cut it constantly, for roofing, for fuel-tinder, for the grey gruel they boil from it — and the cut closes in a season, and it closes in exactly the way every other cut has ever closed, because to the moss there is only one cut, which it has been healing for longer than the willows have been alive. It does not know it is one. It has nothing to compare itself to. It has not been plural for so long that plurality, if you could put the idea to it, would be as foreign as flight.

The people do not think of it as alive, quite, or as dead. Their word for it is closer to *continued*. When they lay it over a slab roof they do not think of themselves as taking a piece of it. They have laid the moss on a stone. The moss is still all of it. A dwelling under moss is a dwelling inside the one green thing, and this is felt to be a protection, and it is: the moss is the only inhabitant of the plateau that the rule can do nothing further to, because there is no longer anything it could become the same as.

It is also, in a small way, warm. Not from the sun — there is not enough sun — but from the slow inward chemistry of a body that size. Put your hand a forearm into it in the hollows and the cold lets up, a degree, two. People sleep in it when they are caught out. You go down into the sward and it takes your shape and holds it, and in the morning the shape is gone.

---

The willows are the other answer, and the opposite one. They refused.

Each stem is itself. Each has held itself apart for three thousand years, four, by the one strategy the rule cannot fault: it has grown into a shape that nothing else has ever grown into. Look at one closely and you can read its history as a history of leaning away. Here it kinked to the left because a stem to the left was, a millennium ago, beginning to resemble it. Here it doubled back on itself. Here it went flat along the ground for a body's length and then reared. Some of them lean away from stems that are still there, silver and crooked, leaning away in turn. Some lean away from nothing: from a gap in the thicket where a stem once stood that either folded into another or died, and the survivor is still, a fingernail a century, bending away from where it was.

The people know them. Not all of them — no one could — but the willow-thickets nearest a dwelling are known stem by stem, and the oldest have marks cut into them, low, near the root-crown, in the same ledger as the stones. When one is cut for fuel or for a tool-haft the rings are counted, aloud, in a slow voice, all the way in to the center, and the number is written and the writing is kept. This is the only place on the plateau where a number is spoken with reverence. It is not a count of like things. It is a count of years, and each year was different, or the willow would not have lived through it.

There is a husbandry of crookedness. People go out with copper blades and bend the willows further from each other. They stake a stem sideways. They shear one flank of a thicket so that the thicket takes on a lopsidedness no other thicket has. They graft, occasionally, a stem onto a stranger's root so that it grows in two directions at once and is like nothing. This is not gardening as the lowlands understand it. It is the opposite of tending toward a form. It is tending away from all forms, and the willows that receive the most of this attention are the least beautiful things on the plateau and the safest.

---

Then there are the shoulders that were animals.

They lie a day or two out from most dwellings, never near — not from superstition but because they are alike to each other, and if a dwelling were built too close to one it would have, over decades, to be watched. They are low. They are faintly the shape of something with its legs folded under it, or of many somethings that have settled into one heap, and the moss over them is a different green, a shade the people have a word for that is used for nothing else. It is a little darker. It is a little bluer. In the evening light when the cloud tears, it is possible to believe it moves — a long slow rise and fall, as of a flank — and no one who has watched for an hour has been able to say whether it did.

Children are taken to one. It is the only lesson that is given the same way to every child, and so it is given only once, and never in the same words. The parent walks the child out over the moss and stops at the edge of the darker green and says something. What is said differs. What is meant is: *this is what happens to the ones who are too many*. The children, afterward, cannot describe an animal. No one on the plateau can. The words for the particular beasts folded, long ago, into a single word, the way the beasts did, and that word means something like *the crowd*, or *the same thing walking*, and it is used for the shoulders and for nothing living.

---

The people.

Ask them how many of them there are and they will not answer, and not from secrecy. A number is a way of saying that its units are alike. They have never counted themselves and they would find the request obscene in a small tired way, the way you might find a joke about someone's death. What they have instead is the list of marks. Every living person has a mark, cut into the stone beside the door of their dwelling and cut nowhere else on the whole plateau, and the ledger-keepers hold the list of all of them, and the list is very long, and it is not a number. If pressed, a ledger-keeper will say that it takes about a season to read the living aloud.

They are short, deep in the chest, with the barrel ribs of people whose air is thin. Their hands are large-knuckled and lined so finely and so completely that the skin of a palm, held close, looks like the surface of the gneiss. They are fog-pale over most of their bodies and burned a dark leathery red across the face and the backs of the hands, because when the cloud lifts the white light at this altitude is total and they have all spent their lives working in it a few hours at a time. Their eyes are habitually narrowed. Their teeth are worn to the gum at the back from willow-bark. They are thin. Thinness is not a fashion here; there is not very much to eat.

What there is: the root-crown of the willow, which under its bark holds a dense white pith that can be pounded and boiled and is bitter and sustaining. The woody centers of the cushion-plants, which go sweet and faintly resinous with age and are dug out with a copper hook. The moss, cut in slabs and boiled long into a grey gruel that tastes of nothing at all, like the cistern water, and fills. That is the whole of the diet. Nobody keeps plants in rows. A row is a likeness, and a row of the same plant merges by the second season into one plant that is stranger and less edible than the row was. The people gather. They gather across distances, and they know, each one, the individual willows and cushions of a territory a day across, and they take a little from each.

They are always a little hungry, and they are always a little cold, and they do not much mention either. Both are alike to every other day's hunger and cold, and to mention them would be to name the one day.

---

The children are the hardest thing.

Twins do not happen. Or they happen, and a woman who has carried two feels them, some months in, become one, and bears a child heavier than it should be, with a look. The look is not describable. The people say such a child is *arrived from further off*. It is not unlucky. It is what the plateau does.

A single child is alike to its parent in the way children are, and so a child cannot stay. It stays for a while — long enough to be fed, to be taught the marks, to walk on the moss without falling into it — and the whole of that while the parent is watching for the first thing the child does that no one has done before. A wrong word. A stone stacked on a stone in a way that is not a wall. A song. When it comes, and it always comes, because children are engines of the unprecedented, the parent takes the child to the ledger-keepers and the child is given a mark and a dwelling, and the dwelling is placed at a distance from the parent's that is calculated by how alike they have been found to be. Some children can stay within sight. Some are sent to the far shoulders, several days out, and the parent walks out to see them once a season and the visit is different every time on purpose.

It is understood that this is love. It is not understood as anything else. A parent who kept a child close would, within a few years, find a single person in the dwelling who was neither of them and both, and who would look at the world with an expression that was hard to place. The people have seen this. They do not let it happen.

---

Their speech is slow and there is very little of it.

The language has been eroded like everything else. There are no synonyms; every pair of words that meant the same thing folded, at some point in the deep past, into one word, and so there is exactly one way to say anything and it is said exactly. There are no two words that sound alike. There is no rhyme, because rhyme is a likeness of sound, and the pairs that once rhymed have run together. Most strikingly, to a visitor: there is no repetition. You cannot say a thing twice for emphasis. If you do, the second saying folds into the first, and the person you are speaking to has heard it once, and cannot remember that you said it again, and looks at you with the particular patience of someone who has watched a stranger try to push a mark into stone that already bears it.

So they say things once. They wait before speaking to be sure of what they are going to say. They listen with a quality of attention that people from below find almost aggressive, because the thing being said will not come around again. Silences are long and are not uncomfortable. A silence is not alike to anything.

There are songs. Every song is sung once. It is sung by one person, alone, usually out on a shoulder in the hour after the cloud tears, and it is made up as it is sung, and when it is finished the singer walks back to the dwelling and does not sing it again, because to sing it again would be to make the second singing the first and lose the evening. The great songs of the plateau are the ones nobody remembers. They were sung, and they were entire, and they were given to the one hour they were for. If you ask an old person what the best song was that they ever heard, they will say they heard it, and that is all.

---

They remember very little, and they are not troubled by this.

A life on the plateau is long — the cold preserves, and there is nothing to catch — but in retrospect it is short. The days of fog are alike; they fold. A woman of seventy has lived some twenty-five thousand days and remembers perhaps four hundred of them, and the four hundred are the ones that were different: the day she was given her mark, the day a wall was finished, the day the cloud stayed down for a month and the channels froze solid and she walked out on the ice, the days someone came. The rest are one day. She has lived it, she will tell you, a great many times and once. She knows what the one day is like. She could describe it to you in the same slow precise words she would use for any of the others, and it would take about as long, and it would be true.

Nights fold worst of all. Sleep is alike to sleep. Most people, asked how many nights they have slept, would say they have slept a night, and they would mean it. A recurring dream is the most dangerous thing a mind here can do, and people who begin to have one are taught, by whoever is nearest, to spoil it — to wake and go out into the fog and do something so strange that the dream, when it comes back for the sleeper, finds the night unrecognizable and lets go.

This is also why they write. Not much — the marks are not a script, and the ledgers record marks and volumes and ring-counts rather than events — but each person, at the end of a day that has been different, cuts a small notch in the door-slab. Not for what happened. Just that a day happened and was its own. Old door-slabs are ragged along one edge with a few hundred of these, and the people run their thumbs over them without looking, the way you might touch a coin in a pocket.

---

To love here is to spend your life learning to differ from the one you love.

Two people alike enough to want each other are alike enough to be a hazard to each other. A couple begins at a distance — a wall's length, a shoulder's width, more — and the whole of their life together is the work of becoming so unlike that they can approach. One takes up copper, the other stone. One walks the far rota and the other keeps a cistern. They cultivate different failings. They learn, deliberately, to see the same fog and think different things about it, and they test this, when they meet, by describing the morning to each other and listening for the point where the descriptions diverge, and being glad. It is slow. The ledger-keepers measure them, some seasons, the way they measure cisterns, and tell them how much closer they may build.

There are couples who have spent forty years at this and are now near enough that each can see the other's smoke. There are couples who have made it — who have grown so wholly, specifically, exhaustively themselves that they share a hearth and sleep on the same shelf and are the only two people on the plateau who do, and everyone within a horizon knows who they are and is a little in awe. The children, when there are children, are as distant as any. It is the parents, at the end, who are allowed to come in.

---

And there are the ones who did not.

*Becoming general* is not sudden. It is a thinning at the edges that the person notices last. Neighbors notice first: it becomes hard, when speaking to them, to be sure which of two people one is addressing, though the two live a day apart and have never met more than seasonally. Then the person notices their hands. Their hands look like someone's hands. Then their dwelling, on the morning rota, is found to have shifted — not moved, but the walk to it from the nearest cistern is a little shorter than it was, and the shortening is in the direction of another dwelling, a horizon off, where someone else is looking at their hands.

The remedies are known and they are the whole ethic of the place. Go somewhere. Make something. Cut a corner into a wall. Most people who begin to thin catch it and stop it. Some cannot, or do not, and their neighbors watch it happen at the length of a wall and cannot do it for them.

Then, one morning, there is one dwelling where there were two, and the mile between them is gone from the moss and from everyone's memory of the moss, and inside the one dwelling is one person.

They wake with two stocks of memory that are almost the same. This is what the folding is from the inside: not a doubling but a near-perfect overlay, in which the great mass of remembered days lies exactly on top of itself and vanishes into one, and what stands out — what is sharp, what the person can hold — are the places the two lives differed. A different childhood shoulder. A different hearth-stone. A day one of them spent walking and the other spent sick. These few divergences are all that is left of two whole people, and the folded one carries them like a handful of pebbles picked from a floor of sand.

They are given a new mark. The two old marks are struck from the list and never cut again. They are not pitied, exactly, and not feared. They are treated as arrived from further off, like the heavy children, and it is noticed that they are the best of the ledger-keepers — that a person who has felt from the inside what a repeated mark costs will spend the rest of a long life making very sure it does not happen to a stone. Their old dwelling, the second one, stands empty on its far shoulder with its tools on their pegs and is not entered again, and the moss comes over it in a few centuries, and it becomes a shoulder among the shoulders and in time becomes the others.

---

Nobody knows where the dead go.

There is no bone on the plateau and this is why. A dead body is alike to every other dead body in the one way that matters, and a person who dies in a dwelling is not there by the next lifting of the cloud. Nobody carries them. The shelf is empty and the blankets hold a shape for a while and then do not. The people say, without much emphasis, that the dead have gone to be with the dead, and they mean it as geography: somewhere on the tableland there is one shoulder that is the grave of everyone who has ever lived here, folded into a single low rise under the one moss, and it is not marked, and it cannot be found, because it is a little more like every other shoulder every year and is, they suspect, somewhere near the middle, moving very slowly, toward wherever the middle is. Some say it is under every shoulder. The two ideas are not felt to be in conflict.

It is not a sad thought here. It is the only place on the plateau where being alike is permitted, and the people think of it, when they think of it, with something close to relief.

---

There is one more inhabitant, and the people would name it first if you asked them, and would look at you strangely for needing to ask.

The fog is one fog.

It has come down onto this surface so many times, in so exactly the same way, that every coming has folded into every other, and what lies on the plateau for days at a time and then lifts and tears and comes down again is a single thing that has never left. It has no other of its kind. It is older than the moss. It is the rule itself in the only form the rule takes that can be seen, walked into, breathed, and the people regard it — not with worship, which is repetition, and not with fear, which is useless — as the other person on the plateau. The one that is there when the wall-walker comes over a shoulder and finds the grey room a hundred paces across and no one in it. They do not speak to it. They would have to say the same thing twice.

---

In the hour after it tears, someone is out on a shoulder with an adze.

She is old. The rota she walks is the far one, and she has walked it for fifty years and can remember, of those fifty years, about the fifty days of it that were unlike the others. The stone under her hand is new — cut this season from the living gneiss, pale and heavy and glowing a little in the low gold light — and she has ground it into a shape no other stone in the wall has, and now she is cutting the mark, and the mark is one the ledger-keepers gave her this morning, and it has never been cut before and will never be cut again. She works slowly. Chips of stone fall into the moss and the moss closes over them.

A horizon off, someone she has known for forty years is measuring a cistern, and if the volume is what they hope it is, they will be allowed, this season, to build a hundred paces closer. She does not look up. She would not be able to see him. But she knows where his smoke is, and it is not where hers is, and that is the whole of what she has been working toward, and the adze rings once on the stone, and the sound does not carry, and the cold comes down.
