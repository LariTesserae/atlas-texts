Begin with the ones you can count, because counting is the last thing you'll be able to do here.

---

The tubeworm has no mouth. It had one, once. It arrived here as something you would not have seen — a fleck with a gut and a mouth and cilia to row itself — and it drifted in through the lifted edge and settled on a seam in the rock where the water tasted right, and for a few days it fed the way animals feed. Then it stopped. The bacteria it had eaten did not pass through it. They stayed, and multiplied, and the worm grew tissue around them the way a pearl grows around a grain, and the gut closed, and the mouth closed, and the animal became a house. What you see as a worm is a sleeve of living wall around a colony it can no longer distinguish from itself. The red plume is blood — hemoglobin, the same molecule as yours, packed so dense the plume is the color of a cut — and it takes up oxygen from the water and, separately, sulfide, which would kill you and would kill the worm too if the blood did not carry it bound and harmless down the length of the body to the bacteria waiting there. At the other end, hidden in the rock, the worm has a root. It is not a fanciful word. The posterior of the tube grows down into the carbonate and the fractures beneath it, thin and branching, and through that root the animal drinks directly from the seep. So the tubeworm lives with one end in the poison and one end in the air, and its whole body is the distance between them, and its entire life is the work of holding those two things apart while passing one to the other. It cannot leave. It cannot turn. It will live like this for two hundred years, perhaps three — the cold does that, slows everything until a century is an adolescence — and when it dies the tube will stand, and the rock will fill it, and it will be a rib.

The mussel kept its mouth. This is the mussel's whole character: it kept everything. It has a gut it barely uses and gills swollen to three times the size they ought to be, and in the gills the bacteria live, and they eat methane, and the mussel eats them, or is fed by them — the distinction has gone soft. But when the seep falters the mussel can still filter, still take the thin rain of particles drifting down from the lit water two thousand meters up, still be an ordinary animal for a while. It hedges. It is the cautious one in a city of gamblers. And the rock is punishing it for its caution the way the rock punishes everything that stays: the lower valve is already stone, cemented so thoroughly the mussel could not open it if it wanted to, and it does not want to; it needs only the hinge-end, a crack the width of a fingernail, and through that crack it breathes and feeds and vents and will do so until the crack, too, is filled. Every mussel on the ceiling is at a different stage of this. The young ones can still move — a little, a thread at a time, letting go and re-anchoring. The old ones are where they are. The oldest are indistinguishable from the wall except that, once in a long while, the wall opens a hairline and closes again.

---

On the tubes, limpets. Tiny cones, each one clamped to a tube-wall and scraping it, grazing the film of bacteria that grows on the outside of the worm's house as thickly as on any other surface. The limpet does not know the tube is a body. To the limpet it is ground. Inside the tube, sharing it, a scale worm lives alongside the owner in the narrow gap between the worm's soft body and the wall it built — uninvited, unremoved, taking what falls, and perhaps the tubeworm cannot feel it there and perhaps it can and the scale worm is simply one more condition of the place, like the cold. On the limpet's shell, something smaller. On that, smaller still. There is no body in this room that is not also a place, and there is no place that is not also a body, and if you keep going down you arrive at the ones for whom this is not a metaphor.

But not yet.

---

The amphipods are the weather. Hundreds of them, rice-grain pale, and none of them attached to anything, which makes them the room's only free population — and freedom, here, looks like this: all of them oriented the same way, holding in the water at the exact depth where the seep's warmth balances the room's cold, drifting toward the fractures when the fluid surges and drifting back when it fades. They do not decide this. They are reading a gradient with their whole bodies and their bodies move to where the reading is best. If you wanted to know the state of the seep without instruments you would look at the amphipods, at how tightly they are bunched and where. They are the city's needle. They live a year or two. They mate in the water, unseen. They are eaten steadily from one corner of the room and do not appear to notice, because noticing would require an amphipod to be somewhere other than where the gradient puts it, and there is no such place.

The snails are the other kind of movement, the kind that leaves no trace but the result. They cross the mussel-shingles grazing the same bacterial film the limpets take from the tubes, and each snail is heading somewhere, and none of them ever arrives while you are looking. Their pace is measured in shells per day. What they have instead of speed is certainty: the trail of mucus a snail lays down is a record of a route, and other snails follow it, and over years the routes wear into preferences, so that the snails of this room move along paths no one laid out and everyone uses. If the room has streets, they are these.

The squat lobsters are the only ones who keep distance. One to a niche, spaced out along the tube-avenues like sentries or like tenants, each with its claws folded to its chest and its long antennae laid back along its body. They are the room's nearest thing to a predator of size, and they are not much of one — they pick bacterial film from the rock, they take the occasional amphipod that drifts within reach, they clean the dead out of tubes when a worm dies and the tube is briefly open. When a lobster molts it does so in its niche and eats the shed. When two lobsters meet on the same tube they raise their claws at each other and one backs off, and they never seem to remember which. Their spacing is the residue of ten thousand of these encounters. They are alone together in the most literal way available: each one has neighbors, and each one has never touched them.

---

Now the ones this is actually about.

The white lawns on the floor are the visible fraction of a population that is everywhere else invisible and that is, by number and by mass and by every measure but size, the city. They are bacteria and they are archaea and they are not one kind but a consortium — cells that eat sulfide and cells that eat methane and cells that eat what those cells excrete, arranged in layers a few cells thick and organized by chemistry into something like a tissue. They are in the lawns. They are on every shell, every tube, every grain of the floor. They are inside the tubeworm, so completely that the worm's body is mostly them. They are in the mussel's gills. They are in the mud beneath the room, in numbers that make the lawns look like a scattering, doing something down there in the dark that nothing in the room above can see.

What they are doing is building the room.

The seep-fluid comes up carrying methane, and in the mud a partnership of cells takes the methane apart without oxygen — no light, no air, a chemistry older than either — and one of the things left over is bicarbonate. Bicarbonate meets calcium in the seawater and falls out as carbonate, and the carbonate is the slab. It is the lid. It is the ribs the tubes become and the cement that takes the mussels and the rind forming across the crack where the light comes in. The rock is not doing this to the city. The city's smallest citizens are doing it, grain by grain, as a consequence of eating. The ceiling is their exhalation. The floor rising to meet it is their exhalation. The walls of the city are a metabolic waste product of the thing that feeds it, and every animal in the room is living inside the digestion of its own food supply, and will be buried in it, and this is not a failure of the arrangement. It is the arrangement.

So when you ask who lives here, this is one answer: something that has no body of its own, that is spread across a million bodies and a slab of stone and a cubic meter of mud, that eats the last breath of things that died in another age and were buried and cooked and are now seeping upward as gas, and that turns that breath into worm and shell and rock. It has been doing this for as long as there has been methane and seawater and cold. The tubeworms and mussels are its recent experiments in having limbs. The city is what it looks like when it comes up for air.

---

The fish is not part of this.

That is the fish's whole distinction. It is the only animal under the slab with no bacteria in its tissues and no root in the rock and no shell for the carbonate to take. It arrived from outside — as a juvenile, thin enough to slip through the lifted edge — and it stayed because the amphipods were here, and it has been here long enough that its pale body has the shape of the corner it lives in. It is an eelpout, or near enough. Its eyes are grains, but they are eyes, and they can register the blue bar on the floor, and it is the only thing in the room that can. It is the only thing in the room that has ever been anywhere else.

It could leave. Probably. The gap is narrower than when it entered, and the fish is larger, and there will come a year when those two facts cross and it does not know that year and will not recognize it when it passes. It does not appear to consider the question. It holds in its corner and the amphipod cloud thins toward it and once in a while, between one look and the next, its mouth is closed on something. Its heart beats perhaps once in a slow breath of yours. It may live thirty years like this. It is the city's witness, and it is not watching, and when the lid closes it will be the one thing inside that is not becoming stone — a soft body in a hardening room, the last free volume, until the water goes foul or the amphipods run out or it simply, one day, stops, and the sediment covers it, and the rock takes the shape of it without ever taking it.

---

The dead are the majority. Say it plainly. For every tubeworm with a plume there are three or four ribs — columns of solid carbonate with a worm-shaped hollow long since filled, standing in the same ranks along the same seams, mouths sealed. For every mussel that can still open there are a dozen that cannot and a dozen more beneath those, valves cemented shut, forming the ridge the living stand on. The dead have not been removed. The dead have not decayed, not in any way you would recognize; the cold and the chemistry saw to that. They have been incorporated. They are load-bearing. New larvae settle preferentially on them, because they are the highest points and the most stable, so the living grow outward from the dead like a crown on a stump. The city's dead are its foundation and its scaffolding and its streets, and they are still facing the fractures, still leaning in the direction the warmth last pushed them, holding a posture that was, when they held it, alive.

It is possible to say that the whole room is a single organism with a long memory and no mind, in which the dead are the skeleton and the living are the skin, and the skin is thinning as the skeleton thickens, and the whole thing is slowly becoming its own fossil while still breathing. It is possible to say that. The mussels would not agree, if mussels agreed with things. Each one is only opening its hinge.

---

And the last inhabitant is the one that has not arrived yet.

Somewhere above, in the grey-blue, a larva is drifting. It has a mouth and a gut and a fringe of cilia, and it is no one — a fleck, one of a cloud of millions released from a mussel or a worm at some other seep along this slope, riding the slow deep currents in the hope, if hope is a word for something that cannot be disappointed, of a taste in the water. Most of the cloud will drift until they starve. A few will pass over the slab and feel, in the chemistry, a change. A few of those will sink. One of those will find the lifted edge while the edge is still lifted, and drift in along the blue bar, and touch the ceiling, and hold.

It will not know that the room is closing. It will not know that it is choosing the oldest rib to settle on because the rib is high and hard, or that the rib was once what it is about to become. It will settle, and feed for a few days with a mouth it is about to lose, and then it will begin — as everything under the slab began — to turn itself into a place.

That is who lives here. The ones who came in through a crack and stayed until they were the wall. The ones who eat the breath of the buried and exhale stone. The one with eyes, who could leave, in a corner. The dead, holding still. And the one on its way, who does not know any of this, and does not need to, and is already almost here.
