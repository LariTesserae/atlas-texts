**What lives at Shell Height**

Start with the builders, because everything else in the bowl arranges itself around them the way iron filings arrange themselves around a magnet, and because they are the ones who will have to leave.

---

A builder begins as a bead of the same paste that fills the storage cells: pollen and resin, ochre, dry. It is pressed into a nursery chamber at the bottom of the living tiers, where the wax is thickest and the rock behind the wax is warmest, and the chamber is closed around it. What happens inside is not seen. But the wall of a nursery cell is warm to the touch in a way the walls above it are not — warmer than the rock that backs it — and if you lay your palm flat against it for long enough you feel a very slow, very faint pulse, not a heartbeat but something more like a tide, and you understand that the thing inside is being fed. The older builders come to the nursery wall in ones and twos and press their undersides to it and stay. What they pass through the wax is not food exactly. It is heat, and it is chemistry, and it is whatever the builders have instead of a name for themselves.

When the cell opens — from the inside, always from the inside — what emerges is pale. Almost colorless. A translucent thing the size of a fist with its six legs folded under, and it does not move for a long time. Then it does the first thing every builder does, which is to put its head down against the ramp and hold it there. It is reading the wall. It is learning that this direction is warm and that direction is warmer and the world is a slope.

That is the whole of their perception, and you have to sit with it for a while before it stops seeming like a lack. They have no eyes because the bowl, for them, is not lit; it is *heated*, and the heat is everywhere at once and comes from below, so there is nothing to look at and no direction to look. Their pads read the stone. The flat underside of the head reads the air. A builder standing on the floor of the caldera knows the center as a blaze, the rim as a dusk, and the outer slope of the mountain — which no builder has felt and lived — as a darkness so total that the word for it, if they had words, would be the same as their word for the bottom of the necropolis. The clouds below the mountain are a black sea. Water is a kind of cold that moves.

The young one climbs. It climbs because up is where the wax is soft and the soft wax is where the work is, and the work is what a body of its shape does when it is warm and fed: it presses its underside to the working face, and something comes out of it, and the something is the same material as its own shell, and the something cures. A builder is not distinct from its city in the way a bird is distinct from its nest. Its cuticle and its mortar are one substance, secreted by the same glands, aging at the same rate. When you see a young builder on a high ledge, pale on pale, you are seeing a piece of the wall that has not yet decided to stop moving.

They darken as they age. So does the wall. The amber of a new cell and the amber of a new shell deepen together through honey and tea and tobacco to a brown that is nearly black, and a builder's age is legible from across the bowl in the same way the wall's is: by tone. There are no other marks. The shells do not scar, because nothing in the bowl strikes them. They only thicken.

---

What passes when two of them stop on a ramp and touch heads is a map.

Each builder carries in its body the felt shape of the warmth — where it was strongest when the builder hatched, how far it has drifted since, how much cooler the nursery wall is now than the nursery wall it was fed through. This is not remembered the way you remember. It is held in the body the way the body holds its own temperature, as a baseline that everything else is measured against. When two heads touch, two baselines are compared. The one who hatched further in and longer ago carries a warmer memory; the one who hatched recently carries a cooler one; and the difference between them is a rate. This is how they know the stone is dying. Not by measuring it. By being, each of them, a slightly different age of the same slow fact, and by standing still together long enough to feel the difference.

Decisions are not made this way. Nothing here decides. But the direction of the work drifts, one comparison at a time, because a builder who has just been told (by touch, by chemistry, by the faint tremor through the ramp) that the warmth is further up than it thought will, when it reaches the working face, press a little higher. Ten thousand of these and the city climbs. The wall is not designed; it is the accumulated shape of a very large number of small corrections, each of them made by an animal with no idea of the whole. Which is to say it is like a coral reef, or a language.

There are no castes. This surprises you, given the districts. But the districts are ages, not roles. The pale young are at the top because the top is where the soft wax is, and the young are the ones whose glands still run freely. The middle-aged tend the storage tiers because their secretion has slowed to a rate suited to sealing and unsealing rather than laying down. The old — the dark ones, nearly black, moving so slowly that you can lose track of whether they are moving at all — go down. Against the traffic. Past the storage, past the nurseries, into the tiers where nothing else lives. They find a chamber that is already empty and cooling, and they back into it, and they press their heads to the floor one last time, and then they seal the opening from the inside with the last of what they have.

That is what the base of the wall is. It is not abandoned. It is full. Every dark cell at the bottom of the city has a dark shell in it, and the shell has fused to the wax, and the wax has fused to the rock, and the three are no longer separable. The builders live on top of their dead in the most literal way possible: the dead are the foundation. When the new ones press their undersides to the working face and lay down the amber, what they are building on is a column of ancestors, growing darker and denser toward the bottom, going all the way down to the first layer, the one laid on stone that was still too hot to touch by a builder that is now indistinguishable from it.

So the city is a memory. Not metaphorically. The base of the wall remembers the warmth of the first season the way a shell remembers the shape of the animal that made it, and the top of the wall remembers this season, and between them, layer by layer, is a complete record of the cooling — a thermometer, read from the bottom up, made out of bodies.

---

The plants are the second thing the builders make, and it is not clear which came first: the making or the plants.

The paste in the storage cells contains seed. Not much — a grain of hard resin the size of a lentil for every handful of ochre — but enough. When the frontier moves, when the working face climbs a finger's width and the new moss begins to crisp across glass that was bare last season, you can see the middle-aged builders carrying paste in their mouthparts down from the storage tiers and out across the floor to the edge, and pressing it into cracks in the rock, and moving on. The rosettes that come up afterward come up in the places where paste was pressed, which is why they are spaced so regularly: they are spaced at the interval of a builder's stride.

Whether the builders are planting the rosettes, or the rosettes are using the builders to get where the warmth is going, is a question that dissolves the longer you watch. The plants feed the builders resin. The builders feed the plants position. The trees lean toward the wall because the wall is where the warmth is longest, and also because the builders score the wall-facing branches and the scored branches grow toward the wound, and also because a tree that leans toward the wall gets scored more, and fed on more, and has more of its seed carried. Everything here is a cause of everything else.

The trees are the oldest living things in the bowl and they are the ones that make the place feel like a place rather than a colony. A trunk of layered horn as thick as a man is not built in a generation of builders or in ten. They stand in the center where it was warmest longest, six of them, leaning, and their crowns of blades turn edge-on at noon so that the light falls through them in stripes. If you put your ear against one you hear nothing, but if you put your palm against one you feel that it is warmer than the air and warmer than the stone beside it, and that it is, in some sense you have no instrument for, *working* — pulling something up from the rock and holding it and giving it out as resin through the healed scars along its branches. Whether it is alive in the way a lower tree is alive, or is something closer to a very slow machine for turning heat into a substance that other things can eat, is a distinction that may not mean anything here.

They will die. They are the ones that will not follow. The rosettes can be reseeded at the frontier and the shrubs can be reseeded and even the horn-trees, in principle, can be reseeded — there are trees at the middle distance that are younger and smaller and already leaning — but these six, in the center, are on stone that will be cold before they can move, and they know it in whatever way a tree knows anything. They are leaning very hard. They are giving out more resin than they need to. The builders that feed at them come away darker.

---

Now the others.

The flat, many-legged things that live in the galleries and clean the walls are moths. Or they will be. This is the one metamorphosis in the bowl, the one creature that changes shape rather than only thickening, and it takes a long time. The larvae are welcome because they eat what accumulates: mineral dust, the fine ash that sifts in from the floor, the tiny crawling things that would otherwise pit the wax. They are welcome in the way that a well-behaved guest is welcome — the builders step over them, and touch them sometimes with the underside of the head, briefly, and move on. When a larva is ready it climbs to the highest gallery and fixes itself to the ceiling and hardens, and the builders wall it in with a thin pale layer, a courtesy, and after a season the layer is broken from the inside and something with wings like flakes of mica comes out and dries and waits for dark.

The moths are the only things that leave. At night, when the stone's warmth pools in the bowl and begins very slowly to rise, they glide on it — up, in long silent spirals, ledge to ledge to rim, and some of them over the rim and out. What they find out there no one in the bowl knows, because the moths do not communicate with the builders in any way you can detect. But some come back. They come back down the warm updraft in the hour before dawn and land on the highest, palest wax and lay eggs in it, and the eggs become the flat things that clean the galleries, and the circuit closes. And it is possible — you find yourself believing this without evidence, which is what the bowl does to you — that the ones that do not come back have gone somewhere warm, and have laid their eggs on stone rather than wax, and that when the builders finally arrive at the next mountain there will already be a generation of mica-winged things waiting on it, having gotten there the fast way.

The stick-things in the shrub-blades are the closest thing here to a threat. They stand so still that you find them by their shadows, and what they are waiting for is a moth — one that has landed on a blade to rest, folded its wings, become invisible. The stick-thing does not lunge. It leans, over a period of minutes, until it is touching. Then it is feeding. The moth does not struggle, because whatever the stick-thing does first is something that makes struggling irrelevant, and the whole event has the quality of a leaf slowly closing over another leaf. Nothing is heard. Nothing is seen unless you are already looking. You come away with the sense that this is what predation looks like when it has been stripped of urgency, and that it is worse.

The round things that roll between rosettes and sit in the spiral centers are doing something to the seed. They are, as far as you can tell, warming it — they are slightly warmer than the air and slightly warmer than the stone, and a rosette that has had one sitting in its center for a season is a rosette whose center has set fruit. They have no front or back because they need none; every direction is a direction a rolling thing might need to go. Whether they are animals or something the rosettes make and release, you cannot tell, and the more you watch the less the question seems to belong here.

And the burrows in the ash, with their collars of resin, hold the things you never see. They are the oldest population. This is a guess, but it is a guess the bowl supports: the collars are on every ash-patch in the caldera, including the cold north curve where nothing else lives, and if you climb the rim and look down onto the outer slopes you can see, in the ash there too, in the cold, in the dark that would kill a builder in an hour, the same collars. They were on this mountain before the vent opened. They are on every mountain. They live in the ash because the ash is everywhere, and they come up at night to take what falls — the snapped leaf, the dropped wing, the builder that lost its footing on a high ramp and fell and lies on the floor with its legs folded — and by morning it is gone and there is only a slight disturbance in the flour and a collar that is a hair's breadth taller than it was. They are the reason the floor is clean. They are the reason the bowl has no bones.

---

There is one more thing that lives here, and it took you a long time to see it, because it looks like a builder.

It is the same size. The same dome, the same segmented back, the same amber-to-brown. It stands on the ramps the way the builders stand, and when a builder approaches it, it does what a builder does: it stops, and lowers its head, and waits for the touch.

The touch lasts longer than the others. That is the only tell. Where two builders confer for a minute, sometimes two, this goes on — five minutes, ten, the builder standing motionless with its head against the other's, and then, when the other finally backs into a side-opening to let it pass, the builder goes on up the ramp a shade paler than it was. Not much. But you have learned to read tone here, and it has lost something. It has been told a map that was not a map. It has had something taken out of it through the same channel by which everything else here is given.

You never see the mimic feed in any other way. You never see it lay wax. It has a shell but the shell does not darken; you see the same one — you think it is the same one — season after season, at the same tone, moving among builders who are darkening around it. It is not welcome in the way the cleaners are welcome. It is simply not detected. The builders have one sense that matters and it has learned to speak in that sense, and there is no second sense to check it against. Whether it is a parasite from outside that found its way over the rim, or something the colony produces from itself the way a body produces a tumor, is not a distinction you can make.

You do not know what it does when the city moves. You suspect it moves with it.

---

The last thing, and then you should leave, because you have been here long enough that the cold at night has started to feel like a personal remark.

Every season, one builder goes over the rim.

It is not the oldest and not the youngest. It is one of the dark middle-aged ones, the tenders of storage, and there is nothing to mark it out beforehand. It finishes whatever it was doing — sealing a cell, carrying paste — and then instead of returning to the wall it walks outward, across the frontier, across the bare glass beyond the frontier where the moss has not yet reached, and up the inner face of the rim where there is no wax and no green and the stone is only warm the way a stone in the sun is warm. It climbs slowly. It stops often and puts its head down. At the top it stands for a long time on the lip, and you can see, if you are close enough, that its legs are trembling, because the air coming over the rim from outside is the coldest thing it has ever felt and it is feeling it with its whole body.

Then it goes over.

Most do not come back. The outer slope is dark, in their sense — dark all the way down to the black sea of the clouds — and a builder in the dark cannot find its way and cannot stay warm and has perhaps a day before it stops. But the mountain is large and has other faces, and on some of those faces there are cracks, and in some of those cracks there is warmth, and a scout that finds one puts its pads on it and knows — knows in the only way it knows anything — that the world has a slope here too, that there is an inward.

And it comes back. It comes back over the rim on trembling legs with the darkness still in its shell, and it goes down the inner face and across the glass and up the first ramp to the first builder it meets, and it puts its head down. And what passes is a map. Not a map of here — a map of *there*: a direction, a distance, a warmth. And the builder who received it goes on up the ramp and meets another, and puts its head down, and the direction spreads through the city the way everything spreads through it, one contact at a time, until it is held in ten thousand bodies as a baseline they measure everything against.

They are not waiting to leave. They have never done anything but leave. The city on the wall is the shape of a departure that has been going on since the first layer was laid, and the scout is not a scout so much as the front edge of an animal three hundred paces across, feeling with one small dark pad for the next warm place, so that when the center finally goes cold and the last horn-tree stops giving out resin and the wall stands complete — dark at the base, pale at the rim, every chamber sealed — the direction will already be in them, and they will go over the lip together, in silence, in a long dark line, down into the black to find it.
