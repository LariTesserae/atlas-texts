Start with what you already saw, because you saw it wrong.

The lizards are not lizards, quite, though it does no harm to call them that. They have the shape — the flattened body, the long splayed toes, the tail held straight behind like a rudder — but the skin is not scaled so much as tiled, each small plate raised and separate, so that the animal looks less like a reptile than like something roofed. The plates are dark on top and thin at the edges, and along the flanks they lift very slightly from the body, a fraction of a millimetre, so that air can move beneath them. This is how the animal regulates. Lying on rock hot enough to cook an egg, it draws heat up through the belly, and the belly plates are thick and dense and hold it, and then it bleeds the surplus out along the flanks where the plates stand open, and that is the orange flush you saw — not colour but the animal's own heat, showing through the thin edges the way a lamp shows through a shade. When they are cold the flanks close and go black. When they are exactly right, which is most of the time, they glow faintly, and the whole plateau, seen from a distance, is dark with a warmth under it.

They came up, as you supposed, from the desert. There is a related animal down there still — smaller, paler, quick, living the ordinary life of desert lizards, which is a life spent mostly in the dark, wedged into a crack, waiting for the sun to make the world possible again. At some point a population of them found the flanks of the massif and followed the warmth uphill the way roots follow water, and somewhere on the long climb the ones that reached the top stopped needing to be quick. There is nothing here to be quick for. The food comes down from the air on its own. The night never comes. The cold of the high desert, which kills an unhoused lizard in an hour, stops a hundred metres below the rim and cannot climb any further. So they grew bigger and slower and darker and thicker in the belly, and they lost the habit of running, and they lost, as far as anyone can tell, the habit of being alone. A single one of them, lifted off the rock and set down on a cool slab, does not flee. It flattens, and waits, and after a while it begins to move — not away from you, but toward the nearest warmth, which is the crowd.

The crowd is the animal, really. Watch one ledge for an hour and you see that the layers turn. The ones at the bottom, pressed to the stone, are hottest and driest; the ones on top are cooler and take the drizzle; and slowly, without any visible decision, the top ones work their way down and the bottom ones rise, a stirring so gradual you have to mark an individual — a chipped plate, a stub tail — to be sure it is happening at all. Nobody drives it. There are no fights. A hot animal simply becomes, at some point, more willing to be climbed on, and a cool one more willing to climb, and the ledge turns over like a slow pot. The insects are taken by whoever happens to be on top when they drift close. The heat is taken by whoever is on the bottom. It evens out. It has been evening out for a very long time.

They breed in the crust. That is what the terraces around the pools are, or partly what they are — mineral, yes, laid down by the water, but shot through with eggshell and shed skin and the small light bones of the ones that did not rise again, so that the ochre and the chalk-white and the strange soft yellow are the colours of lizard as much as of stone. The eggs go into the warm deposit just above the waterline, where the ground itself does the incubating, and the hatchlings come out already dark, already slow, and lie down. Something has to eat them, and things do — the birds take the small ones as readily as the large; the insects, some of them, lay in the eggs. But the numbers hold. You could take the whole crust of the plateau apart layer by layer and read the animal's history in it, every generation compressed a little further into the rock, until at the bottom the bones are only a stain and the stain is only a colour and the colour is what you thought was mineral.

The insects are simpler and stranger. They are flies of a sort, hard-cased, dark, with wings that fold flat against the body and the same raised-tile texture on the back as the lizards, which is either convergence or coincidence and is in any case the reason the surface of the plateau looks continuous — the same dark grain at every scale. They breed in the pools. Their larvae live in water that would blister you, grazing the pale mineral floor, which is not mineral either but a mat of something microbial, grey and orange and green, that lives on the sulphur and the heat and needs neither light nor anything from above. When the larvae pupate they float, and when they hatch they rise with the steam, and the steam carries them a body-length into the air and drops them, and they rise again. Most of a fly's life is spent in that column, rising and falling, feeding on nothing, the whole fountain of them a kind of slow-motion boil that exists only to be within reach of a lizard's tongue.

The birds are the only things here that keep the desert's manners. The pale ones nest in the columnar cliffs, in the gaps between the basalt pipes, where the drizzle runs down the rock and the heat comes through it and the eggs need nothing but to be left. They fly the way things fly at this altitude — long wings, few strokes, a lot of falling disguised as gliding — and they take the flies off the tops of the steam plumes at the moment the flies stall. They never land on the plateau. Their feet are not made for it; the rock would hurt them. The dark one, the large one that turns overhead — there are five, or six, or a breeding pair and its grown young, hard to say — nests further off, on the cold outer flank, and comes over the rim in the mornings and works the ledges all day and goes home at dusk with the last of the light on its back. It is the only thing in the whole system that leaves and returns. It takes one lizard a minute, give or take, across the visible plateau, and it has taken one a minute for as long as the lizards have been here, and the crowd closes behind it each time as if it had never come. It is not clear that the lizards know what it is. Nothing hunts them on the ground. They have no word, in whatever language their bodies speak, for above.

And the mat — the grey-green not-moss in the cracks — is older than all of it. It is the same organism that floors the pools, or a cousin, come up out of the water and learned to live on drip and steam. It grows a millimetre in a decade. There are patches of it on the north side of the plateau, in the shelter of the cliffs, that are the size of a room and have been growing since before the desert below was desert. The lizards do not eat it. The flies do not breed in it. It sits between them and under them and around the base of every rusted trestle, and it takes the mountain's breath and turns it, imperceptibly, into itself, and it will be doing that when the pipeline is a red stain and the lizards are a colour in the crust.

That is what lives here, and you could stop there, and it would be true.

But there is the machine.

You noticed, without noticing, that the one turbine that runs is the one at the end of the row — the one nearest the door, the one whose bearings are easiest to reach. You noticed the red valve wheel, faintly bright, and you told yourself the drizzle had kept it so, and the drizzle does keep things wet, but wet iron rusts, and this wheel has not rusted, and paint does not stay red for forty years by being rained on. You noticed that the scale on the exhaust channel is a stalactite everywhere except at one point, where it has been chipped back to bare concrete in a clean curve, the size and shape of a bucket's rim. You noticed the bootprint fossilised in the crust, and you thought: old. You did not think to look for a newer one.

There is somebody in the turbine house.

They came up with the works, or with the last crew that came to shut the works down and did not finish, or after everyone else had gone, on the cable-way, before the cable-way stopped. It does not much matter which. What matters is that they stayed, and that they have stayed long enough now that the massif has taken them in the way it takes in everything — not by killing them, which would be the desert's way, but by warming them until they stopped wanting to leave.

They are old, or look it. The heat and the drizzle and the sulphur have done to their skin what they do to iron: darkened it, textured it, laid down a fine mineral rime in the creases that gives them, in certain light, the same dull glitter as the rock. They are thin, because there is not much to eat here that a person can eat — flies, if you are willing, and the birds' dropped kills, and eggs dug from the crust, and whatever the cable-way brought up before it stopped, rationed and then gone and then, apparently, not missed. They dress in what the works left: boiler suits, several, worn one over the other and rotted through at the shoulders. They do not wear boots. Their feet have gone hard and grey and lizard-plated on the soles, and they walk the hot rock without appearing to feel it, which is a thing that takes a body a long time to learn, and which the body does not forget.

They keep the turbine. That is the whole of what they do, and they do it the way the mat grows — slowly, without hurry, with the assurance of something that expects to be doing it for a very long time. In the mornings they walk the pipeline with a hammer and tap it, listening for the change in the note where the scale has grown thick enough to choke the flow, and where they find it they open a hatch and reach in and break the scale away with a length of bar and the lizards on the pipe lift their heads and settle. In the afternoons, when the storms come, they sit inside with the door open and the bearing warm under one hand and let the rain hiss on the rock and do nothing. They oil what needs oiling from a drum that must be nearly empty and that they will, presumably, not be able to replace. They chip the exhaust channel back with a trowel so the condensate runs, and they take the hot water from it in a bucket and wash, and once the bucket has cooled they drink from it, and that is the only clean water on the mountain.

And they go out to the wellheads. This is the thing you did not understand. When the pressure in the deep rock shifts — and they know when it is going to shift; they have known for so long that they could not tell you how — they take the red wheel from its hook by the door and walk out across the plateau to the fourth wellhead, or the seventh, and fit the wheel to the valve stem and turn it a quarter, a half, to bring the turbine's feed back to where the bearing likes it. It is a long walk. The lizards see them coming. They see the red wheel, which is the only red thing on the plateau and the only thing that ever moves across it at walking pace, and a ripple goes over the ledges ahead of the walker and turns to follow, and by the time the valve is turned and the note in the turbine house shifts, the whole crowd has already faced east and is waiting for it.

So it was never the sound they were hearing. It was the keeper going out. And the keeper goes out because the rock is about to change, and the rock changes because the mountain breathes, and the mountain does not know about any of it. But the lizards have learned the shape of the walk, and the keeper has learned the shape of the rock, and the machine turns between them at a speed that suits them both, and that is the agreement you felt yourself outside of. It is not a large one. It has no terms. It is only that everything here has been near everything else for long enough to move in step.

At night the keeper sleeps on the concrete by the bearing, where the floor is hottest, and the lizards come in through the peeled roof and the broken door and lie down on them — across the chest, along the legs, in the crook of the arm — because a person is a warm rock like any other and this one is warmer than most. The keeper does not brush them off. Under a coat of small hot bodies, in the low steady turning of the one machine, they are perhaps the most comfortable person alive, and they sleep like the mountain sleeps, which is to say not at all in any way you would recognise, and with everything still going on.

Whether the line down the mountain still lights anything, they do not know and have stopped wondering. Perhaps there is a town on the desert floor with one street lamp that has burned for forty years and nobody has thought to ask why. Perhaps the wires end in sand. It has not been the point for a long time. The point is that the mountain gives heat and the machine takes it and turns, and that as long as it turns the bearing must be kept, and the keeper is who keeps it, and the lizards know the keeper's walk. That is what lives here. Not the lizards, not the person, not the turbine — those are only the parts. What lives here is the arrangement, the slow warm agreement between the rock and the things on it, and it does not need you, and it will not notice when you leave.
