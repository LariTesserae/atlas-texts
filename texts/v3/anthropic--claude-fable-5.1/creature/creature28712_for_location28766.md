The first thing you will notice about the people is that they do not put things down.

A woman at a market stall weighs out dried horsetail with the scales held in the crook of her arm. A man eating his lunch on a girder keeps the bowl in one hand the whole time, and when he is finished he does not set it beside him but clips it to his belt by a loop worked into the rim. Nobody leans a tool against a wall. Nobody leaves a coat on a chair. Everything has a hook, and everything is on its hook, and a stranger who sets a bag on the ground while she looks at something will find that three people have glanced at it and one has moved it, without a word, onto a peg.

It is not fussiness. It is that the ground is not a place. A thing put down on it may be a hand deep in moss by morning or a meter in the air, tilted, or gone under a blister. Floors are winched while people sleep on them. The horizontal is a courtesy the city extends to itself and can withdraw. So they hang their things, and they hang themselves — they sleep in slung beds, they sit in slung chairs, they carry their children in slings, and a person from elsewhere who watches a family at dinner will have the odd impression of a room full of pendulums, all very slightly out of phase.

The second thing is the hands.

Speech goes two meters and dies. The lattice does not. A knock on a girder on the east side can be felt in the west, faintly, in the palm, and so the people here have learned to listen with their hands. A winch hand will stop on the way home and lay a palm flat on a cable — not to check anything in particular, just to know how the building is hanging, the way you might touch a horse's neck. Children put their hands on the girders to feel the trams coming. Old couples sit with one hand each on the same tension strut and do not need to talk. There is a knocking that goes around the basin at night, district to district, a slow irregular pattern that is not quite a code and not quite a habit; nobody instituted it and nobody can say exactly what it means, except that if it stops somewhere, someone goes to look.

Their voices, when they use them, are low and a little hoarse and pitched for close range, and they stand close — closer than you will be comfortable with at first — because there is no other way to be heard. Strangers find them intimate to the point of rudeness. They touch elbows to get attention. They lean in. They breathe on you. It is not affection, or not only. They smell of wet wool and cold moss and, faintly, of scorch.

---

The winch crews were the first people hired and are already, in a settlement not thirty years old, something like a caste. Every building has one; the big buildings have several, working shifts, and the families have intermarried and trained their children and developed the small vanities of a trade — a particular knot, a way of coiling, the leather aprons worn to a gloss at the hip where the cable runs. They do not say a building *stands*. Nothing here stands. They say it *hangs*, and a building that has settled well after a night's adjustment is said to be *hung true*, and this is the highest praise they have for anything, and they will use it of a person.

They are patient in a way that looks like slowness and isn't. A winch crew moving a tenement does not hurry, because hurrying a hanging building is how you learn what a cable sounds like when it goes — a sound that even the moss cannot fully take, a deep soft cough, and everyone in the basin who has heard it once turns their head at anything like it for the rest of their lives. So they lift a handspan and wait, and read the gauge, and lift a handspan. The good ones can tell you which of a building's cables is carrying too much from the floor of the tram, by the way the beard of moss on it hangs. They watch the moss the way sailors watch the water.

The moss-cutters work above them, in the girders, and are a different sort. Younger, mostly, because the work is hard on the shoulders, and quieter even than the rest, because you cannot talk with a heated knife in your hand and moss dripping into your eyes. They come down at the end of a shift smelling of burnt vegetation with their forearms mapped in small pale burns, and they are the only people in the basin who will tell you, unprompted, what they think the moss is. They think it is one thing. Not many plants — one, the whole basin's worth of it, cables and girders and blisters and the coat-lining of the foundry, one green animal with its mouth in the new ground, and when they cut a beard off a cable they think of it as trimming a nail. They are careful to cut it and not kill it. There is a part of the lattice on the north side where a crew, years ago, scorched a whole span bare with lamp oil to be rid of it, and for two months the sound of the trams came through that span like a scream, and people rerouted to avoid walking under it, and the moss came back, and nobody has tried again.

The stone-cutters keep different time from everyone else. Their clock is the clouding. A blister that has just come up is a soft grey lens, and for something under a week it can be cut and it is beautiful — you can see a hand's depth into it, and it holds light — and then it clouds and it is nothing, it is rock, it is what the city is built over rather than what the city sells. So the cutters live in the days between. They can look at a fresh ridge and tell you it is *two days from clouding* the way you might say a pear is two days from ripe, and they work fast and with the concentration of people racing something that does not know it is a race. They do not like the foundry, which takes the softest and the best of the new stone by prior arrangement, and the foundry does not like them, and this is one of the few things in the basin that anyone will raise their voice about, and when they do the moss takes it and it comes out sounding like a complaint about the weather.

---

The assay office is three clerks and a director, and the director has changed the labels eleven times in four years, and each time he has written the new classification on a slip of card in a very good hand and tacked it above the sample drawers, and he has kept the old slips. There are eleven in a folder. He does not look at them. He has, in a private notebook that the clerks have all read, a twelfth classification, and it consists of a single word, and the word is *unfinished*, and he has not tacked it up because it is not a category, it is a surrender.

One of the clerks — the youngest, who keeps the cold drawers — has a sample she has not reported. It came up under the market four years ago and she cut it herself and put it in the lowest drawer, in the damp, and it has not clouded. It is a grey lens the size of a thumb and you can still see a hand's depth into it, and it is still faintly warm, and once a month she takes it out and holds it and puts it back. She does not know what it means that a piece cut from the ground and kept cold and dark and moist has not finished becoming stone. She suspects it means the clouding is not something the stone does. She suspects it is something the stone does *when it is left alone*, in the light, on the ground, with nobody watching, and that she has interrupted it, and she has no intention of telling the director this because the director would want to interrupt more of it, and she is not sure that is a thing anyone should do.

The foundry crews are the best paid and the quietest and they do not like to be watched, and everyone in the basin knows this and almost nobody knows why. This is why. The work is done by hand. The stone comes in soft and the furnace keeps it soft and it is pressed and turned and cast in moulds and, for the fine work, the bearings and the little bells, shaped between padded palms like something being taught. The crews say it takes a shape better if the room is silent, and this is nonsense, and they believe it, and they hold to it, and a foundry hand at the end of a shift has hands that stay warm for an hour after, so that their people know them in the dark by the warmth. That is the part they do not mind you knowing. The part they mind is this: they do not believe the ground rises faster around heat. They believe it rises faster around *shaping*. They think it comes up under the foundry to see what is being made of it. Not one of them has said this aloud outside the foundry doors and not one of them would, and if you put it to them they will look at you with the flat patient face of a person who has been working soft warm stone in silence for eleven hours and is not going to start talking now.

---

The sumps have keepers. It is not much of a job — a gauge on a post, a line of stakes, a notebook — but the water is the city, and a pool that fed a district can be lifted and tilted and drained in a week, and so someone walks the sumps every morning in the grey light and reads the stakes and writes the numbers. The keepers are solitary by temperament or become so. They are the people who see the pale shapes most, because they are the people who stand longest at the edge of still water in a place where nothing else is still, and they do not talk about it to anyone but each other, and when they do they do not say *shapes* or *fish* or *things*. They say *the tenants*. As in: *the tenants are lying low in the west sump this week*, or *the tenants have gone up with the north pool, I do not know where they are now*. They say it flatly, as a matter of record, and they put it in the notebooks, and the assay office has never asked to see the notebooks, and the keepers have never offered.

The people of the swallowed street, on the southern side, are the calmest in the basin. It is a particular calm and everyone recognizes it and nobody entirely trusts it. They stopped winching. They did not decide to; the ground simply got ahead of them and one morning the winches had no more chain to give and the crews stood on the tram-rail and watched the stone close over the ground-floor lintels and cloud, and after that there was nothing to be done, and so nothing was done. They call the swallowed floors *the cellar*, as though it had always been so, as though every tenement came with two stories of solid rock under it as a matter of design. They come and go by the rails. They say the buildings are warmer now. Some of them say the rooms are still down there, furnished — chairs set in stone, a kettle on a hob, a bed — and one old man on the third floor will tell you his mother's bed is under him, in the clouded dark, made up, and that he sleeps better for it than anyone in the basin, and that the rest of the city is welcome to its winches and its gauges and its handspan every night. The children of the street dare each other to lick the ground-floor windows, where the stone came up against the glass on the inside and clouded there, and they say it tastes of iron and of something they do not have a word for, and they are probably right.

---

The children are the ones who show you what the place does to a person, because they have had nothing else.

They are born into the hush and they do not know it is a hush. They speak late and low and they are astonishingly good at reading a face. They put their hands on everything to feel what it is doing. They play on the blisters — there is a game, forbidden and universal, of standing on a rising dome and being the last to step off before it splits, and they are very good at it, and they know the ground under their feet the way children elsewhere know a bicycle. They are kept from the sumps and go to the sumps. They keep insects in their pockets, the slow cold soft-bodied ones, which do not object. They collect the moss-voles, or try to; the voles are varnished with moisture and impossible to hold and the children have a whole vocabulary of what a vole feels like going through your fingers.

They have a word for everywhere else. It means, roughly, *the stopped place*. When they go up the switchbacks for the first time and stand on the rim road, on honest geology, they stamp. Every one of them. They stamp on the ground to check that it is really not going to do anything, and it does not do anything, and they look at their parents with an expression of deep unease, because a ground that holds still seems to them like a held breath, like something waiting, like a person pretending to be asleep. They come back down the switchbacks and the first blister they see, they put a hand on it, and their shoulders come down.

The old are a different matter. There are two kinds. There are the ones who came down as adults and adjusted, and they are indistinguishable now from anyone else except for a habit of listening to the tram with their ears rather than their hands. And there are the ones who came down and did not adjust and stayed anyway, for the money or the stone or someone they followed, and they are recognizable at a distance by a certain stiffness, a way of standing too straight, a reluctance to lean. Some of them go up to the rim at night. There is a place on the north switchback, a shoulder of real rock with nothing hanging from it, where a person can stand and shout, and the sound goes out over the basin and is eaten by the moss before it has gone fifty meters, but at least for a moment it was loud. The ground-born find this embarrassing to the point of pain and pretend not to know about it, and everyone knows about it, and the place is kept clear.

---

You cannot dig here. This is the first thing a city needs to solve and they solved it the only way there was.

They lay the dead on new ground. Uncovered, wrapped in moss-cloth, on a blister that has come up within the last day or two, still soft and lensed and faintly warm. Someone sits with the body the first night. The moss has it in a fortnight and the stone in a month, and after that the ground takes it up, slowly, a few centimeters a week or a meter in a night, and the oldest graves in the basin are now well above head height — long welted ridges, clouded and hard, with names knocked into them by a chisel, and the moss deep over the names, and the winch crews cut the moss back from the names once a year and it is the only cutting the moss-cutters will not do, and nobody has asked them why.

*Where is she now* is a reasonable question about a dead woman here. People visit graves that have moved. A ridge splits and a family stands on either side of the split and does not know which half to talk to. A grave rises into the underside of a warehouse and the warehouse is winched a meter and the grave rises another meter and there is a conversation, low and close and taking most of an afternoon, about whether it is the warehouse or the grave that should move, and the grave wins, and the warehouse is trolleyed forty meters west, and the ridge comes up through the space it left and stands there in the grey light with its names on it, furred over, hung true.

---

The animals do not seem to find any of this remarkable.

The flat-billed birds nest in the beards of moss under the tenements and are winched up and down with the buildings and do not stop feeding their young to notice. They are small and dark and work the moss with a sideways motion of the head, and they are the only thing in the basin that makes a noise on purpose, a thin dry clicking, and even that goes about a meter. The moss-voles were here before the city; they came in off the rim slopes when the moss made a place for them and have never, as far as anyone knows, touched stone — they live in the layer, in tunnels through the wet green, and a vole dug out and set on bare new ground will stand rigid with its varnished coat streaming and will not take a step until it is put back. The insects are slow and cold and soft and silent and there are a great many of them and they like warm skin; you will find them on your wrists, on the back of your neck, not biting, just settling, the way a cat settles, and the people here brush them off without looking with a gesture so habitual it has become a kind of punctuation.

The grazers come down from the rim at dusk. Long-legged, mild, the colour of the overcast. They walk on the rising ground with their knees loose and their heads low and they browse the fresh moss on the newest blisters with an absorption that is almost devotional, and they do not startle, and they will let a child walk up and put a hand on them. Where they choose to graze on a given evening is read as weather. The ground is quiet there, people say; the grazers know. The assay office says the grazers are eating the mineral bloom and go where it is freshest and that this correlates with nothing. The assay office has also, this month, changed the labels again.
