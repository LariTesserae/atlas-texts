You see the hands first, because the hands are what is carrying the bundles, and the bundles are what is coming toward you along the causeway before anything else does.

They are dark, and they shine. The sap does that. It comes off the cut culm grey-green and dries — insofar as anything dries — to a lacquer, and the lacquer does not wash off because there is no water here clean enough to wash with and no air dry enough to let it go, so it stays soft and takes another coat, and another, and after twenty years a cutter's hands have the same ribbed and glossy look as the walls of the older houses. They are the same material, finished the same way. The fingers of the right hand are set in a curl the diameter of a culm at the base, thumb-thick, and they stay that way at rest, so that a cutter sitting on the dock with nothing to hold is nonetheless holding something. Children's hands are pale and startling. You look at them and it takes a moment to understand what is wrong, and what is wrong is that they are the colour of hands.

The feet are bare on the gangways. Shoes rot. The soles have gone thick and white, tendon-coloured, and the toes have spread, and they know rot through a plank before the eye does; a person walking a gangway in this town does not look down, and does not fall, and cannot tell you how. If you ask they say the plank *gives*, and they use the word the way you would use it for a look on someone's face.

Then the faces. They are not weathered, because there is not, in the usual sense, weather — no sun to line them, and only the one wind, always from the same quarter, so that if it has done anything it has done it evenly. They are smoothed. They are a little full, a little soft at the edges, the way a hand looks after a long time in a bath, and they stay that way for a very long time. People here do not look old until they are very old, and then they do not look old so much as kept. The resin in the air does that too, or the bargemen say it does. The bargemen say the people of this town do not age; they cure.

---

The second thing you notice, after the hands, is that nobody is speaking to you at the volume you expect.

Everyone who has done a decade on the floor of the works has lost the band the rollers occupy, and since that band is roughly the band of an ordinary speaking voice, the town has arranged itself around the loss. There are two ways of talking. On the floor there is hand-talk — forty or fifty signs, all of them about cane and iron: *feed, jam, wet, more, stop, done, you, me, mine, the tanks,* and *the engine,* which is a flat hand turned over slowly on the wrist, the flywheel. Off the floor there is chest-voice, low and long, pitched under the grind rather than over it, so that from the far end of a gangway a conversation sounds like the water moving under the mat. They lean in to each other. They stand closer than you are used to. They watch mouths. It is not intimacy, or it is, but it is also simply how the words get across.

Children hear everything, and are used as ears. A child will be sent to sit by the tanks and listen for the bubble, and will report it — *slow, slower, one just now* — in a piping voice that carries the length of the shed and that adults hear the way you hear a bird you cannot see. Children here are prized for something they will lose. Nobody says this. A child who has begun to go down into chest-voice on its own is understood to be ready for the floor.

At the shift change, when the knock stops, voices come out too loud and then correct.

---

They wear what they make. The fibre that comes off the racks in the tanks is graded, and the second grade does not go down the canal; it is spun in the houses and woven on frames of pegged culm and made up into shirts and trousers that are stiff for a season and then soften, and then take the body's shape and hold it. A shirt on a peg in an empty house still stands out a little at the shoulders where the arms were. The cloth is grey-green new and goes, with wear and water, to the colour of the water, and it lasts, where cloth off the barge is gone in a season, and it smells faintly of hay and turpentine. They do not smell it. They have never smelled anything else.

Hair is kept short, in both sexes, because it never dries and long hair goes green at the ends. The very old women let theirs grow, and it goes the colour of the root-mat, and they wind it and pin it with a culm-splint and do not mind.

Teeth are amber. That is the preserve — a dark syrup boiled from the first liquor off the tanks before it goes to resin, eaten on barge-flour bread and on the sweet pith of the rhizome, which tastes of the ground and is the nearest thing here to fruit. There is no meat that did not come salted up the canal. There are no eggs. Children here know birds the way you know something from a story, and the egret, when it comes in over the leaf-crown, is not a bird to them; it is *the egret*, singular, as though it has been the same one since anyone remembers, and it may have been.

Everyone carries a knife. It is a cane knife, hooked, the blade worn back over its life to a crescent, and it is kept from ferning by being wiped in resin every evening and hung on a peg where the engine's warmth reaches. The knives are older than the hands that hold them. A knife passes down, and a good one has been ground so many times that its hook is nearly a ring, and the person who inherits it learns a slightly different stroke to suit it, and so the way a family cuts is, over generations, the shape of its iron.

---

There are forty roofs and forty strips and forty names, and a house's name is its strip's name, and the strip is named for the first person who was laid into it.

That is where the dead go. There is nowhere else. You cannot dig here; you can only cut. So when a strip comes round in the rotation and is taken down to stubble, that is the year that house's dead go into it — laid on the cut mat and pegged down with split culm, and covered with the leaf-trash from the cutting, and left. The shoots come up through in the spring. By the third year there is nothing to see, and by the fortieth the culms that are being carried to the works are, in a sense that nobody in the town would bother to make precise, the person. The strips are named for the dead because the dead are in them, and every strip is cut by the descendants of what it grew through. Nobody finds this grim. It is the place you go. The alternative, which is the barge and the canal and burial somewhere firm, is spoken of the way one speaks of a relative who moved away and stopped writing.

Given names come off a short list, perhaps a dozen, and the house-name carries the meaning. There are five Anas in the town and nobody confuses them, because one is Tallis's Ana and one is Wend's, and Tallis and Wend have been dead a hundred years and are, respectively, the fourth and the nineteenth strips out along the north side, and everyone knows which is which by the colour of the leaf.

Marriage is within the town by house, or out of it by barge. The bargemen sometimes leave a hand behind, or take one. A person who came up the canal as a young man and has been here twenty years is still, in the town's eyes, from the barge, and you can tell it too: his hands are wrong. They are dark and glossed like everyone's, but the curl is not set, and he puts things down differently. It is not held against him. It is simply true.

---

There is the cat, which came up the canal as a kitten in a flour sack and has never left, and has never hunted, because there is nothing to hunt. It is fed on salt fish and press-cake crumbs and the fat from lamp-wicks, and it is the one creature in the town that is idle, and the town cherishes it for that in a way it would not admit to. Someone always knows where the cat is. When the engine is stopped for repair the cat gets up off the press-cake and walks the length of the shed and sits down in front of the cold flywheel and looks at it, and the stokers say it is checking, and they are half joking.

There is the egret, which has a name the children do not use in front of adults.

And there is what lives in the tanks.

The ret is a culture. It is bacterial, or fungal, or both; nobody has looked, and nobody would know how. When a tank is drawn off, a bucket of the old liquor is kept back and poured into the new fill, and it has been done that way for as long as the tanks have stood, which is longer than the engine, so that what is in them now is continuous with what was in them at the first ret and is, by some reckoning, the oldest living thing in the town. The tank-tenders — two women, generally; that is just how it has fallen — speak of it in the singular. *The tanks are slow this week. The tanks didn't like that last cutting.* They know it by smell and by the sound of the bubble and by the colour of the skin on the surface, which runs from cream to a grey that means trouble, and they feed it by watching. It has never been outside the town. It has never been anywhere. It is fed cane and it makes fibre and resin and a sourness in the air of the low wing that gets into the tenders' clothes so that you can tell them in a crowd with your eyes shut, and that is the whole of its life, and it is, in its way, the thing here that is most purely of the place.

---

They sleep through the works. The knock and the grind and the hiss are what the houses are built inside, and a child is born into them and does not, as far as anyone can tell, hear them at all after the first year. What wakes the town is silence. When the engine is stopped for a repair the whole hamlet lies awake in the dark, listening to nothing, and people get up and stand on the gangways in the cold that comes when the stack is not breathing, and speak too loudly to each other, and go back in, and do not sleep until the hiss begins again.

The stokers speak of the engine in the grammar that is used for the very old — it *wants*, it is *tired*, it is *in a mood* — and they do not think this is anything other than description. The maker's plate is a smoothness they polish. Nobody has read it in three generations. The engine burns what the plant has been, and breathes out what the plant drank, and the stack leans, and the people who feed it sleep in the drying wing on the warm press-cake beside the cat, and when one of them dies it is understood that he goes into his strip like anyone, but the others will say for a while afterward that the engine is off, and it may be.

---

They know they are missing something.

Not the birds — they have not missed the birds; you do not miss what was never there — but the bargemen bring news, and news is full of things that have no referent here: a dry road, a hard shadow, a horizon at the height of the eye. The town listens to this the way it listens to the egret. It is interesting. It is not the shape of the place. A young person now and then goes down the canal to see it, and some come back, and the ones who come back stand on the dock the first evening with their hands hanging and look at the leaf-crown and say nothing in any register, and the next morning they are on the floor, and their hands begin to darken again, and in a year the curl has come back into the fingers and the town has closed over the absence the way the mat closes over a cut.

The ones who do not come back are put down, when they die wherever they die, as a small gap in the naming. Their house's strip is one person short. It grows anyway.

---

At the shift change the knock stops, and for ten minutes the town stands still.

It is not a rite. Nobody has agreed to it. It is that the noise has been holding them up, all of them, the way the water holds up the mat, and when a part of it is withdrawn they feel the drop and stop where they are — on the gangways, in the doorways, at the tanks with the bucket half tipped — and in that interval they hear what you hear, the hundred thousand small dry hinges of the cane turning to take the last light. They hear it better than you do, in fact, because it lives in a band the rollers never touched, and their hearing has closed in around it the way a plant closes around a wound. They know it the way they know the engine. They can tell you from it whether it will rain, and it will. Some of them, the old ones, will say that in the hinge-sound they can hear the strips by name — that Tallis's has a drier note than Wend's, that the eleventh strip, which is young, is almost silent — and they are not being fanciful. They have listened to it every evening of their lives.

Then someone hits the beam — *chock* — and the voices that had come up too loud go back down into the chest, and the hands that had opened close again around the diameter of a culm, and they go on. The cane goes on. The tanks, slowly, bubble. The mat under all of it thickens by nothing you could measure, and the town rises on it by the same nothing, and the dead in their strips come up green and are cut and carried and pressed and burned and breathed out over the water and come down again as rain on the leaves, which pass it down, and the people who live here would not call that a cycle, because a cycle is a thing you can stand outside of. They are inside. It is simply the arrangement. It is what lives here, and they are part of what lives here, and they are honest about it in the way the houses are honest about the works: they lean toward it, and they do not pretend to be leaning toward anything else.
