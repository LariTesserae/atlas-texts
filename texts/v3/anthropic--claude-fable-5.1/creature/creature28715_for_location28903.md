The first thing to say is that nothing in the bowl can hear it.

That is not an accident of the place. It is the place. Every creature that has stayed here longer than a few generations has done the same thing with its ears, which is to lose them — to seal them, or fill them, or let them go the way cave fish let go of their eyes — because an ear in that hollow is not an organ but an injury, a hole that the sound pours into until something inside gives. What lives here is what has closed that hole. You will not understand any of it until you understand that. You come down the stair with your hands clamped to your head, half-blind with the pressure of it, and everything around you is going about its business in perfect quiet.

---

Begin with the mites, because there are more of them than anything else and because they are the ones you actually touch.

They are not quite white. They are the colour of the inside of a shell, and they are hard, a fine hard grain like sand that walks. Push your hand into a mat of the growth and they pour over your fingers without hurrying and without alarm, thousands of them, and their feet on your skin are a dry prickling you feel more than see. They do not bite. They do not, as far as anyone has determined, do anything to you at all; you are simply a warm surface that has appeared in their world, the way a fallen tress is a surface, or a sleeping bird, and they will walk across you and off the other side and forget you.

They live inside the fibre. Not on it — inside, in the hollow of each strand, where the wind cannot reach and the shaking is a steady thing rather than a tearing one. Split a strand with your thumbnail and it is packed with them, nose to tail, a column of little shells. They eat the wall of the strand from within. This is what thins the old growth and makes it brittle at the tips and lets the wind take it, and it is also, it seems, what the growth wants; the tresses that carry the most mites are the ones that grow fastest, as if being eaten were a kind of pruning, or a kind of feeding, or the two were not different here. They carry the dust in with them. They carry it out again as something finer. When a tress falls and is shredded on the way down, what joins the dust is not only fibre but ten thousand small shells, and those go round with everything else, and settle, and are in the dust that the growth takes straight, so that in some sense the growth is eating its own eaters and the eaters are eating it, and neither is quite a separate thing.

In the afternoon lull you hear them. That dry hiss like sand is not sand. It is the whole bowl's worth of mites moving at once in their millions inside the fibre, and it has been going on the entire time under the scream, and it will go on tonight when you are trying to sleep on the plateau and cannot, and it was going on for centuries before anyone climbed down, and it is, if you stop and think about it, the sound of the growth being digested, very slowly, from the inside, by something that has never once in its history heard the noise it lives in the middle of.

---

The things that eat the mites are harder to see, because they are the colour of the fibre exactly — not close, not camouflaged, but the same colour, that dull violet-brown with the sheen on it, and the same texture, and when they lie along a tress they are a slightly thicker part of the tress and nothing more. They are long and jointed and very slow. You find them by their movement, which is the only movement in the growth that is not the wind's: a bending that goes the wrong way, against the pressure, and then you see it, a body the length of your forearm, segmented, dozens of legs tucked flat, working its way along a strand with its head — if it is a head — pushed into the fibre. It eats by splitting the strands open and drinking what is in them. It moves perhaps a hand's breadth in an hour. It has nothing anywhere on it that looks like a sense organ, no eye, no ear, no feeler; it finds the fibre by touch and finds the mites by being where the fibre is, which is everywhere, and so it is never hungry and never hurries and never has been.

The question that stays with you is whether it is coloured like the fibre or the fibre is coloured like it. Whether it has lived here so long it has taken on the growth, or the growth has lived so long with it that they have met in the middle. The jointed thing, when it dies, does not rot. It dries and stiffens and the wind takes it apart and it goes round with the dust and settles and is eaten by the growth, and there is a kind of sense in which it does not stop being part of the wall even then. There are patches of the growth that are a little thicker and a little more lustrous than the rest, a little more the colour of the jointed things, and you do not need to be told what they are made of.

---

The one that has settled on being a spider has done the settling recently — you can tell from the joints, which are not quite right, and from the way it moves, which has the deliberateness of a thing still learning its own body. It is the size of your palm and dark and quick and it lives in the angles where the growth meets the bare stone, and it makes no silk. It has nothing to make silk with. What it does instead is bite. It goes out into the fibre and bites free a strand the length of its own body, and carries it back, and bites free another, and knots them — actually knots them, a half-hitch worked with the front legs — and in a night it has a lattice, a stiff dry net of the wall's own hair stretched across an angle where the wind is least.

It cannot feel the net shake. Everything shakes; the whole bowl is one vibration and a spider that hunted by vibration here would starve in a day, would be forever running to the thrum of the wall. So it has turned the sense inside out. It sits in the centre of its lattice and waits not for movement but for stillness — for the one place in the net where the fibre has stopped humming because something is gripping it, some beetle or mite-fattened jointed thing that has blundered in and is holding on. The only still thing in the bowl is prey. The spider goes to the stillness and eats it. Afterwards it sits and shivers with the rest of the wall and is, in the noise, invisible again.

The beetles it catches are the ones with the ringing wing-cases — thumbnail-sized, the gold of old brass, and when they take off there is a note, a little struck cymbal, that the beetle itself cannot hear and that carries nowhere. They ring for nothing. Or they ring for the bowl, which is what everything here does whether it means to or not.

---

The birds are the thing that most looks like an answer and is not one.

They are swifts, or were, or are on their way to being something else; they have the blade shape and the long crooked wing and the small hard skull, and they never land on anything flat. They cling. Every fold of the growth and every ledge in the wall is a colony, and the nests are made of fibre and dust and something the birds bring up from inside themselves that sets like glaze, and they are packed so close that a hand pushed into the wall at the right height comes back stuck with eggs. The eggs are dark. They are nearly the colour of the floor.

They have no ears. Where the ear should be there is a smooth patch of skin, and under the skin the bone has grown over. They fly by sight and by the pressure of the air on their faces, which in the bowl is a great deal of information — the spirals form in the same places and turn the same way and a bird that has hatched in this wall knows the shape of the wind the way you know the shape of your own house in the dark. When they pour out in the evening they are not flying through turbulence; they are flying through architecture. Every one of those twenty thousand knows where the column goes up and where it comes down and where the sheet of torn fibre will be flung across, and they thread it without touching one another, and for those few minutes you see the wind, drawn in birds.

And then they leave it. They go over the rim and out onto the plateau, which has nothing on it. This is the thing no one can make sense of. They hunt all night and come back before dawn with their throats full, and no one who has walked the plateau has ever seen a thing for them to eat. The best that has been said is that they go a great deal further than the plateau — that they follow the sound out, since the sound carries for hours in every direction, and that somewhere at the edge of it there is something, water or insects or another bowl. That last is not said seriously. But the birds come back from every direction equally, and they come back full, and they scream the whole way in with a scream you cannot separate from the bowl's own, so that if the sound is being said to anything it is at least being carried, every night, out to the edge of hearing and back again, in the throats of twenty thousand things that cannot hear it.

---

There is the grey thing in the slabs, which eats what falls.

You do not see it for the first day. You see where it has been — a smoothness in the dust under the fallen stone, a scattering of small bones picked so clean they are white, the husk of a jointed thing with the segments pulled apart and licked out. Then on the second day, in the afternoon lull, when the dust stops climbing and the light comes straight down and lays its coin on the floor, something comes out of the slabs to lie in it.

It is long and low and the grey of the dolomite exactly, and it has fur, or something that has stopped being fur, a dense flat pelt that lies all one way. Its eyes are small. It has no ears at all, not even the sealed patch the birds have — the skull simply goes smooth from eye to neck — and it moves with a kind of absolute unhurried certainty that you eventually recognise as the movement of a thing that has never in its life been startled by a noise. Nothing here can startle it. Nothing here can creep up on it either, and it does not creep; it goes to the fallen tress and turns it over with its snout and eats the mites and the jointed things and the stunned beetles and the fledglings that came down with it, and when it has finished it lies in the sun for the two or three seconds the sun lasts, and then goes back into the slabs.

It is warm-blooded. You know because you find where it has slept and the dust is still warm. It is the only warm-blooded thing that lives on the floor, and it has chosen — if a thing like that chooses — the loudest place in the bowl, the place where the bell-note comes up through the ground unfiltered and the sheets slam directly overhead, and it lives there as if in a garden, and eats well, and is grey, and is not afraid.

---

The lizard is not a lizard. You come to this slowly.

It lies on the warmest patches of the fibre, where the growth is thickest and the sound is loudest, flat, the length of your hand, and it does not move for days. It is the colour of the floor — that dark grey-brown with the faint warmth under it — which is not a colour anything else in the bowl has. It has no legs that you can find. It has a head, or an end that is more head than the other end, and along its back there is a ridge, and the ridge, when you finally get close enough to see, is not a ridge but a row of something like the fibre, short stiff strands standing up from the skin, and they are shivering. They shiver with the wind. Every strand on its back is a string and every string is being bowed and the thing lies there under it, perfectly still, and appears to be listening.

It is not listening with ears. Whatever it is doing, it is doing with the strands. You put your hand near it and the strands lean, very slightly, toward your hand, and then away, and it does not otherwise move, and you take your hand back. When it does shift — once, in the three days you watch it — it shifts toward a place on the wall where the growth is thinner, and when you look at that place the next morning the growth is thicker there, and the not-lizard is somewhere else.

You are not sure it is an animal. You are not sure it is not a piece of the floor that has come loose and learned to move. You are not sure that those are different things here, any more than the jointed thing and the fibre are different things, or the mite and the strand it lives in. Everything in the bowl that has stayed long enough has gone partway toward becoming the bowl, and the not-lizard has gone furthest, and it lies on the wall with the wind in its back and does whatever it is doing, and you find you have stopped wanting to touch it.

---

Then there is the one that does not come down.

You never see it. You see its prints at the rim in the morning, in the dust the wind has laid overnight along the lip — a hand with too many fingers, six or seven, long, splayed, each finger ending in a mark like a pressed thumb. It walks on all fours. It walks the whole rim, a full circle, sixty paces round, and at four or five places it stops and the prints face inward and are deeper, as if it stood a long time with its weight forward, looking down. Then it goes on. Then, before dawn, it leaves, and its trail goes straight out across the flats and is gone within a hundred yards where the wind has taken it.

It has never, in fifteen centuries of prints — for they are in the old dust too, fossil-pressed in the crumbling lip under the new — set one of those hands on the stair.

You think about this at night, lying on the plateau with the sound coming up through the ground into your back. Everything that lives in the bowl has given up its ears to do it. Here is a thing that has kept them. Here is a thing that comes to the edge of the sound, night after night, and stands with its weight forward, and listens — because that is what it is doing; there is nothing else to do at the rim in the dark, nothing to eat, nothing to see — and then walks away again, and does not go down. It is the only thing in the whole place that behaves as though the sound were something to be heard. And it will not come any closer to it than the point at which you can still, just, hear yourself.

You wonder whether it is the last of whoever cut the stair. You wonder whether it is the descendant of whoever they cut it for. You wonder whether it is simply something from far away that heard the bowl, the way the birds hear it, and followed the sound to its edge, and stopped there, the way anyone with ears would stop.

---

And then the growth, which you have been walking on and through and holding onto this whole time, and which you have been treating as the wall's clothing, and which is not.

Stand in the afternoon lull with your back against it and your hands still over your ears out of habit, and feel it. It is warm — warmer than the air, not as warm as the floor. It is shaking, still, though the wind has fallen; the shaking is in it, not on it. And it is moving. Not with the wind. With something slower. The long tresses are not hanging quite where they hung an hour ago. The sheets have shifted a hand's breadth along the wall, all of them, the same direction, the way a field of grass leans. A tress you broke yesterday has already put out, at the break, a pale stiff shoot the length of your finger, and the shoot is pointed not down, not out, but toward the floor's centre, toward the loudest place.

It does not root in the stone. You have seen this under the lens: the fibre continues out of the rock as if the rock were the fibre's own root, a different state of the same stuff, dolomite that has learned to grow hair. There is no seam. There is no seam anywhere in the bowl — not between wall and growth, not between floor and wall, not in the floor itself, sixty paces across, not one join. You have been treating these as separate facts. They are one fact. The bowl is not a hole with things in it. It is a single thing, cast or grown or both, and the growth is its coat, and the floor is its skin, and the hum is what its body does, and the animals — the mites, the jointed things, the birds, the grey thing in the slabs — are what has moved into its fur.

And it is alive in the only sense that matters here, which is that it eats and grows and is destroyed and comes back the same. It eats dust. It eats the shells of the mites and the husks of the jointed things and the fallen birds and its own torn tresses, all of it shredded on the way down and gone round and settled and taken straight. It eats the wind — the wind is what drives it, what bows the strings, what keeps the bell ringing, and the bowl is shaped so that the wind cannot leave. It eats, perhaps, the sound; it grows thickest where the sound is loudest and the shoots point toward the noise like leaves toward light. It has been doing this, on the evidence of the stair, for fifteen centuries. It has been doing it, on the evidence of the floor, for a great deal longer than that.

---

The floor is the thing that stays with you, and you know now why.

Kneel on it in the lull and sweep the dust away and put your palm flat. It is warm as an animal. That is the phrase everyone uses and it is exactly right, and you should take it exactly: not warm like stone in sun, which is warm on top and cold underneath, but warm all through, warm from inside, the warmth of something with a temperature. The hum comes straight up through it into your bones — the bell-note, the eleven-second waver, the long swell and quick drop like breathing. Like breathing. Lie down on it, full length, with your ear to it, and the sound is not a sound any more. It is a pulse.

The shelter against the wall — the curve of floor-material with a lip and a cavity where a body could lie — has nothing in it but dust and mites. This is what everyone reports and it is true and it is not the whole truth. The cavity is the length of a body, and it is warm, and it is shaped, and if you lie down in it — you will; everyone does — it fits. It fits the way a thing fits that was made for a body. The lip comes up around your shoulders. The bell-note comes through the material at your back and through the material curving over your head, and the wind cannot reach you there, and the tearing sheets are somewhere above, and what you are inside is not the scream any more but the pulse. Eleven seconds. The swell and the drop.

Whoever lay here lay here to listen. That is the only thing the shelter is for. It is not for sleeping in; you could not sleep in it, and there is nothing to keep out. It is a place shaped for one body to lie inside the sound and be as close to it as a body can get — closer than the rim-walker will come, closer than the birds, as close as the not-lizard on the wall with the strings shivering in its back. And it is empty. And the ring of posts is a ring of nothing, and the three plates lie in the dust with nothing on them, and the stair is worn to bowls by feet that stopped coming.

You could say: the listeners are gone and the instrument goes on playing. That is what most people say, afterwards, on the plateau, trying to get their hearing back.

But you have lain in the cavity. You have felt the floor take the coin of sun in the lull and be a little warmer for it, the way skin is. You have seen the growth lean toward the noise, and the not-lizard go where the growth is thin and the growth thicken behind it. You have found no seam anywhere. And you have noticed — lying there, with the pulse at your back — that it is not the note that is regular. It is the waver. The bell rings steady; something else, every eleven seconds, bends it. The breathing shape in the gusts is not the wind's. The wind is turbulence, and turbulence does not breathe. Something is breathing it.

What lives in the bowl is the bowl.

It grew its own coat to sing with and its own skin to feel the singing through, and it drew the wind in and would not let it go, and it has spent however many thousand years saying one thing at full voice to a plateau that has finished and a sky that does not answer. Everything that has come to live on it has had to go deaf to stay. The only thing with ears that will come near it stands at the rim in the dark and will not come down. And the one place shaped for a listener is empty, and has been empty a very long time, and the floor keeps it warm.

---

In the lull the dust falls. The light comes straight down. The fibre relaxes against the wall with a sound like a long sigh, and the sigh is not a figure of speech. The mites hiss in their millions. The grey thing lies in the sun for two seconds, three. The not-lizard leans. A single bird calls one clear note that it has been calling all along and cannot hear.

And you, on the floor, with your palm flat and your ear to the warmth — you are, for those seconds, the only thing in the bowl that is listening to it. You are the one in the cavity. It was shaped for you, or for something enough like you that the fit does not matter. You lie there and it comes up through you, the swell and the drop, and you do not know what it is saying, and you understand, with your whole body, that it has never once stopped saying it, and that it has never once been sure that anything was there.

Then the pressure comes back against your face. The spirals start in their three or four places and turn the way they turn. The sheets lift. The bowl draws breath — that is the sound, that is what the eleven seconds are — and begins again, at full voice, to say the thing it says, to the plateau, to the sky, to the birds who carry it out and back without hearing it, to the walker who will not come down, and to you, lying in the place it kept warm, for as long as you can stand it before you climb the stair with your hands over your ears and leave it, the way everything with ears has always left it, to say it to no one.
