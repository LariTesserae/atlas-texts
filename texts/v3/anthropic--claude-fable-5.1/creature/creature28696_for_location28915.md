Start with the chest, because that is where the plateau makes its first claim.

The people here are broad through the ribs and short in the leg, and their breathing is slow and deep and almost never audible. Children born on the plateau come out already wide in the body, as if they had been told in advance. A visitor climbing up from wherever the clouds come from would spend weeks gasping in the thin air; the people of the city do not gasp, and they regard gasping the way lowlanders regard a stammer, as a thing to be looked past politely. Their skin is dark from a sun that gives light without heat and burns anyway, and it is cracked at the knuckles and the corners of the mouth in a way that never quite heals, because the air takes water out of everything, including faces. They grease themselves with rendered fat from the herds. They smell of it, and of dung-smoke, and of wet wool, and after a while in the city one stops noticing and then notices only its absence, the way one notices the herds only when they stop.

They are not a beautiful people by any standard that comes from elsewhere. They are a people who look, at a glance, like the plateau: weathered, compact, close to the ground, more grey than anything. But the hands are another matter.

I want to say the hands are the real thing to describe, and then I want to take it back, because it is not the hands exactly but what the hands know. A child of six can be handed a block of the young stone and will turn it, and tap it with a knuckle, and listen, and turn it again, and then set it down in the orientation in which it will split cleanly. They do not learn this so much as absorb it, the way children elsewhere absorb which way a river flows. Every surface they have ever touched has been this rock. They know its planes the way they know the joints of their own fingers. An adult stone-fitter can look at a wall from across a lane and tell you which block was set by whom, because the choice of face and the tightness of the fit are as individual as handwriting, and because a wall that has stood for three generations holds the hands of three generations in it and they can read them off. The rock rings when struck and they strike it constantly, testing, listening, a small dry tapping that goes on all day throughout the city and that a newcomer would take for insects if there were insects. There are not.

Their hands are scarred. The stone is young enough to cut, and everyone has been cut, and there is a look to the palm of an old fitter — a fine dense cross-hatching, pale on dark — that is admired, though not remarked on. They do not remark on much.

Then the water, because after the body and the stone it is the third thing every child learns, and it is the thing that shapes their ethics.

Water here is counted. Not measured — counted, in cups, and the cup is a horn cup of a standard size and every household has several and every child can tell you how many cups the household cistern holds and how many it holds now. This is not a matter of scarcity in the way a drought is scarcity. It is more that water is a thing that arrives, slowly and from the sky and the frost and the afternoon cloud, and that it is possible to know exactly how much has arrived and exactly how much has been taken, and so they know. They do not hoard it; hoarding would be pointless, since the cistern refills at the rate the sky allows and no faster. But they do not waste it either, and their word for waste and their word for a certain kind of stupidity are the same word. To offer someone water is a serious act. To be offered water and not drink all of it is an insult, because the offering was a cup and a cup is a known quantity and now it is a half-cup of nothing.

They wash rarely, and when they do it is with cloth wrung nearly dry. They drink the milk of the herds more than they drink water. They dream, some of them say, of rain, though few have ever felt it; the word exists and the old people say it once fell, in the lifetime of someone's grandmother, a soft cold falling that went on for most of a day and that everyone stood out in, faces up, silent, and then the cisterns overflowed and nobody knew what to do and some of it was lost down the fractures and that was a grief. The story is told as a wonder and a warning both.

The larger cisterns, the ones on the cliff edges that belong to no one, have keepers. The keepers are old. Their work is to sit by the tank in the afternoon while the cloud comes across and to watch the apron run, and to know the level, and to say no. Saying no is most of the work. They are respected in the way that anything is respected here, which is to say quietly, by being listened to.

The speech itself, then.

It is low. I said the city keeps its voices down by habit and that is true but it undersells it; the language itself has been made by the thin air and the swallowing walls. It has few sharp sounds. It is mostly hums and breathed consonants and long low vowels that carry a little way in the dry air and no farther. Spoken fast, it would be unintelligible, and so it is not spoken fast. There is a pause after each utterance in which the listener is expected to think, and a reply that comes too quickly is considered slightly rude, as if the speaker had not been worth thinking about. Conversations take a long time. Decisions take longer. The council of the city — if it can be called that; it is more a matter of who sits together on which roof in the evening — has been known to consider a question for a full turn of the herds before saying anything at all, and this is not regarded as delay. It is regarded as attention.

Across distances, they do not shout. Shouting does nothing here; the air will not carry it. They whistle, in a way learned from or shared with the grey rodents, a set of long fluting notes that go out over the tiled ground for a surprising way and that mean simple things — here, come, water, danger, stop. And beyond whistling range they sign, with the whole arm, slow large shapes held against the sky, and a herder on the far side of a shallow valley can tell another that the grass is better to the north and the seep has moved, without a sound. Children practice this on the roofs. Old people do it to each other across lanes, out of habit, when they could simply speak.

Now the two lives, because the people are not one thing.

Some of them stay. Some of them walk. The herds move on their year-long circuit and a portion of the people move with them, living in fleece tents pegged into fractures, carrying their water in skins, sleeping among the animals for the warmth, following the frost-melt as it moves across the plateau like a slow tide. The rest stay in the city and keep the cisterns and the aprons and the terraces and the young and the very old. It would be easy to say these are two peoples but they are not; a person is a walker for some years and a stayer for others, and most have been both, and the ones who have only ever been one are regarded with a faint pity by everyone else. The walkers come back thinner and quieter and with a way of standing that faces into the wind. The stayers have hands that are more scarred and less weathered. When the herds return, the city fills with animals until there is barely room to move, and the two halves of the people sit together on the roofs in the evening and do not say very much, and the herd-sound comes up through the slab floors with the warmth.

The herds themselves — I should not leave them out, since the people would not. The long-necked grazers are not tame in the way a dog is tame. They are accustomed. They have never known a world without people walking behind them and the people have never known a world without them in front, and neither party is entirely sure who is following whom. The people do not name individual animals; there are too many and the herd is the unit. But they know the herd. They know its moods the way a sailor knows weather. An old walker can tell from the sound alone — that soft continuous sound of breath and hooves — whether the herd is content or uneasy or about to move, and will act on it before anyone has seen anything. The herd is consulted, in a sense. Not with words. With attention.

The birds are the other economy, and the darker one.

The cliffs at the plateau's edge drop into cloud and the birds nest on them in their tens of thousands and their droppings are the richest thing in the world, and someone has to go down and get it. The bird-people are a smaller group than the walkers and stayers and they are marked, in the way of dangerous trades everywhere, by a certain looseness in the face. They go down on ropes of herd-hair, two hundred strands twisted, anchored to slabs that have been tested by tapping and by three generations of holding, and they hang in the shrieking white chaos of the colony with baskets on their backs and scrape the ledges and come up grey and stinking and shaking with cold. The cliff-wind takes some of them every few years. The rope is checked and checked and checked and it is never the rope. The bodies are not recovered; they go down into the cloud and that is the end of the matter, and this is the one place where the people's slowness breaks and they will not speak of it at all, not quickly and not slowly. The dung is carried up in baskets and spread on the terraces and the red-stemmed plant grows thicker for it, and everyone knows what it cost, and nobody says.

They bury their dead in the fractures. The rock flour is packed over them and a slab is laid across and tapped once. There is no stone raised; the whole plateau is stone raised. They say the frost will grind the body into flour like everything else and the flour will gather in the low places and the grass will grow on it and the herds will eat the grass. This is not spoken as consolation. It is spoken as a description of what happens.

Which brings me to time, and to what they think they are.

They know the land is young. They do not know it in the way a geologist knows it, but they know it in the way one knows a child is a child: it is unfinished, and it is going to change, and the change will be slow and total. A crack widens by a finger-width in a lifetime. A seep shifts three paces and a household spends twenty years rebuilding its apron to follow. The grandmother remembers the slab that used to be the doorstep and is now the threshold of a room two houses over, because the house was rebuilt around the shift. Everyone has stories of this kind and they are not told as marvels. They are told the way one might mention that a tree has grown.

And so they do not think of themselves as permanent either, and this is the thing a visitor would find hardest to grasp about them. They do not believe they are the final inhabitants of a finished place. They believe they are among the early inhabitants of a place that is still deciding, and that whatever lives here in the time when the plateau has been worn gentle and has soil and streams that know their courses will be something else, something they cannot picture, and they do not resent this. They speak of it, when they speak of it, with something that is not quite hope and not quite resignation. A stone-fitter setting a block will sometimes say that it is set for the ones after, meaning not his children but the ones so far after that they will not remember there was a city here, and will find the block in the flour and wonder at its edge.

They have no gods to speak of. Or rather, if the seep is a god it is a small and local one that moves three paces in a generation and must be followed with a basket. What they have instead is the sense, everywhere and unspoken, that the plateau is watching itself become something, and that they are part of what it is watching with.

At the edges, there are the things they do not know, and they hold these carefully.

They do not know what is below the cloud. The cloud comes up in the afternoon like something poured and they have never seen where it is poured from. A few people, in the stories, have gone down — over the edge on ropes, past the bird ledges, into the white — and some of those came back and would not describe it, and some did not come back. The copper comes from below, somehow, traded up at one place on the rim where a path is said to exist, by people who are said to be different, in a transaction that happens once in a lifetime and that no one living has witnessed. The copper is cherished for this as much as for its edge. A copper pin is a fragment of the unknown place, cold-beaten, worn at the throat.

They do not know who lives in the far cities, the ones whose fires can be seen on the clearest nights as a faint line at the rim of the world. They assume — it is only an assumption — that those people are like themselves, broad-chested, low-voiced, tapping stone. Once in several generations a walker follows the herds so far along the rim that they meet a walker from the other side, and the two stand and sign at each other across a shallow valley, slowly, with the whole arm, and find that the signs are almost the same, and then the herds move on. This is told as a story. It is one of the few stories they tell often.

And they do not know why there are frogs at the seeps. Small and dark, in a place with no rain and no pond and no green thing taller than a knee, frogs — and no one can explain it, and no one tries very hard, and the children are told not to touch them. It is possible the frogs are the closest thing they have to a sacred mystery. It is also possible they simply like having one thing they cannot account for, on a plateau where everything else — the water, the stone, the herd, the year — has been counted in cups and tapped with a knuckle and known.

Picture one of them, then, at the end. Not the fitter or the bird-hanger or the cistern-keeper. A woman in the grey light before dawn, standing on the wet apron of her own house, where the night's frost has just begun to run. Below her through the slab floor the animals are breathing. Above the city the bird cliff is starting its pale roar, felt in the chest more than heard. She is holding a horn cup under the channel at the apron's lower edge and it is filling, very slowly, drop by drop, and she is not impatient, because impatience is not a thing the plateau has taught her, and she is looking out past the last watch-hut at the cloud sea and the far rim catching the first light, where the other fires have just gone out. When the cup is full she will drink it, all of it, and tap the stone once with her knuckle to hear it ring, and go in.
