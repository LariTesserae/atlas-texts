The first thing that lives here is the ground, and it is the only thing that has never left.

Everything else that walks or drifts or threads through this place is a tenant. The ground is the landlord and the building and, in the end, the rent. It has no name because there has never been anything here to name it that was not also standing on it, and a thing you are standing on is hard to hold at the distance a name requires. Call it the mat, since that is what it looks like. It is one organism, or it is so many organisms so thoroughly grown into each other that the difference stopped mattering an age ago, and by now it may have stopped being true. Cut a piece from one side of the tableland and a piece from the other, weeks of walking apart, and lay them side by side, and they will have knit into one another by the time you have finished a meal.

It does not have a center. You keep expecting one — a heart, a root, an oldest place from which the rest spread — and the mat declines to supply it. Everywhere is as old as everywhere else, or near enough. Wherever you dig, the layers go down the same: green, yellow-green, brown, the black that is not rot. If it began somewhere, it has forgotten, and it has spread its forgetting evenly to every part of itself.

What it has instead of a center is attention. Not thought — nothing here suggests thought — but a kind of distributed noticing, slow as everything else, that you can watch operate if you are patient enough to watch. Set your hand flat on the turf and leave it there. Over an hour the mat beneath your palm grows very slightly warmer and very slightly wetter than the mat around it; it has registered a pressure and a heat and is answering with the only things it has to answer with. Lie down and sleep and in the morning there is a shallow bed the shape of you, already closing. The domes are the mat's answer to standing water: where the seeps gather, the tissue thickens and rises to drink them, and the dome is the drinking made visible, a swelling that has been happening for a hundred years and has a hundred more to go. The braided zones are its answer to the herds, which crop it hard; it grows coarser there, ropier, harder to drag a face through, and the herds drift elsewhere and the braid softens again over generations, and the herds drift back. The whole surface is a record of what has pressed on it and a reply to what has pressed on it, and the reply is always the same reply, made of the same material, at the same pace: it grows toward what touches it, and it grows over what stops moving.

It is possible that the mountains did not erode. It is possible that the mat took them. Rock exposed to a living surface for long enough is worked into grit and the grit into nothing, and there has been, here, a very long enough. The mat may be the reason there is no summit. It may have eaten the summit, and the ridgelines, and the cirques, over spans that make the hundreds of bands in a dome look like a single season, and be sitting now on the rounded stump of what it ate, a continent's worth of highland ground down not by ice or water but by the patient thirst of a plant. If so, it is the one thing on the tableland that has ever been fast, relative to the thing it was working on. Mountains are slow. This was slower, and outlasted them.

Whether it knows anything, you cannot say. There is the pulse, when you press your ear to it. There is the way the cloud holds. There is the fact that in weeks of walking you have never once found a place where it has failed — no bare patch, no dead zone, no sickness — and a body that large that has never once gone wrong in any place you can find is either very lucky or is, in some sense you do not have the equipment to understand, taking care of itself.

---

The grazers live on its skin and are, more or less, its skin's idea of an animal.

They are born already looking like the ground. A calf, if that is the word, is a pale damp lump the size of a curled person that lies where it fell for most of a season, turf beginning to green on its back before it has stood, and its mother — you assume mother; the herds do not make their relations obvious — lies beside it and crops in a slow ring around it until the ring is cropped bare and the two of them are sitting in a pale dish in the turf, and then they move on together and the dish heals behind them. The calf learns to graze by tasting where the mother has grazed. That is what the eyeless face is for: not to see the ground but to know it, to read in the flavor of the surface who has been here and how long ago and whether it is worth staying. A herd is not a group that stays together. It is a set of animals following each other's taste, a day or a week apart, so that the herd is spread out over the land like a sentence with long pauses between the words and each animal is reading the one before it.

They do not die violently, because nothing here does. They slow. A grazer that is reaching the end of itself stops moving on and lies down in the last dish it cropped, and the turf grows up around it and onto it, and after a season there is a dome where the animal was, and the dome breathes with the water moving in it, and whether there is still anything inside the dome that is animal or whether it has all become mat by then is not a question that has a clear answer or a clear moment. The herds graze the domes that were herds. The domes drink the water the herds have breathed out. There is no bone anywhere on the tableland because there is nothing that does not go back.

The burrowers live in the mat's flesh and are, more or less, its blood.

This is not a metaphor, or not only. What they eat is the yellow-green layer, the zone just under the surface where the tissue is still warm and moist but no longer green, and what they leave behind them as they pass through it is a tunnel that fills with water and closes, and the passing and the filling and the closing is the way water gets from one part of the mat to another faster than seeping would carry it. Where the burrowers are thick, the mat is wetter and warmer. Where they thin, the surface dries slightly and the domes stop breathing. They are the mat's way of moving what it needs around itself, and the mat is their way of being fed and housed and kept at a constant temperature in a place that has no other temperatures on offer, and neither of them is in a position to tell which of them the arrangement is for.

They breathe together because they are, at any given moment, breathing the same air. The surface of the mat is one long membrane over a body of laced tunnels, and when the air in the tunnels goes stale it goes stale everywhere at once, and a dozen narrow faces lift at once through the turf and open at once on the damp and sink at once. Sit long enough beside a burrower field and you can time the tableland's breath by it — a slow inhalation every hour or so, the ground briefly studded with faces, then smooth. What the faces look like, up close, is hard to hold onto. Not eyes. A slit, a soft pucker, a wet gray-pink that catches the sourceless light and gives it back as a faint shine. They do not look at you. They are not built for looking. They surface and drink air and are gone, and the ridge of them under the turf goes on toward wherever the water needs to be.

The thing in the cloud is harder.

You have seen it perhaps a dozen times in as many weeks and every time it has been a different shape, or the same shape at a different angle, and you have never once seen an edge of it against anything. It is a darkening in the gray that moves. It is wide — wider than the herds, wider than the ring — and it trails something behind it, or beneath it, long, many, moving at a different rate from the body, the way weed moves under a boat. When it passes, the water on the domes trembles, but it makes no wind. The note it gives, the one low sound two or three times a day, does not come from any direction and does not seem to come from a mouth.

The most reasonable thing to think is that it is an animal, some soft-bodied drifter that lives on the vapor and the moth-things and the fine green suspended in the air, and sleeps in the cloud, and comes down to the surface never, and that the trailing things are how it feeds. That is what you thought at first. You are less sure now. There is a way of looking at it, after weeks, in which it is not a separate animal at all but a part of the mat that has come loose — a raft of tissue lifted on the warm exhalation, the trailing things not tentacles but roots, drinking what the tableland breathes up, held in the cloud by the same heat that makes the cloud. If the mat could grow something to live in its own breath, this is what the something would look like. It would be dark, because it grows in the dark. It would have no edge, because the mat has no edge. It would make one sound and make it seldom, and the sound would come from everywhere, because it would not be the sound of a thing calling but the sound of a tension being released, a whole raft settling, a sigh.

There may be only one. There may be several. Nothing up there has ever done anything that would let you count.

---

The small things you count anyway, or try to, and lose count, and start again.

The lamp-slugs are the ones you know best because they come to you. Sit against a dome at dusk and within an hour they have found your warmth and are climbing your clothes, fringed and translucent and cool, glowing faintly on the underside so that when they cross your hand your hand is lit from below in a wet pale green. They want the condensation on you. They will drink your sweat. They are not many-legged or eyed or anything else you could name; they are a tongue with a fringe, a drinking that has grown a body. At the height of dusk every dome for a hundred strides carries a few dozen of them and the tableland is, for an hour, a field of low dim lanterns, and then the light in them fades — not all at once but in a slow rolling dimming that moves across the domes like wind across grass, though there is no wind — and they sink into the turf and are gone.

The moth-things hang over the channels all day and most of the night and do almost nothing. A wing moves. A wing stops. They are waiting for the surface of the water to break, which it does when the transparent worms come up to the skin of it to breathe, and when it breaks a moth-thing drops and closes and rises again, and the whole event takes as long as a long breath and happens perhaps twice in an afternoon over any given channel. They have no reason to be quicker. The worms are not going anywhere; the channels are not going anywhere. There is, you begin to understand, a version of hunger here that has stopped being urgent and become simply a direction, a slight lean toward the water, and the moth-things are made of nothing but that lean.

The many-legged things without fronts live in the braid. They are the color of the braid. When you kneel and part the ropes of turf with your hands you find them clustered in the shadowless dimness underneath, motionless, and if you leave your hands there long enough one of them will climb onto your fingers and sit, and it is only when you try to see which way it is facing that you understand it is not facing. It is a ring of legs around a mouth, and the mouth is on the bottom, and the animal moves toward whatever it tastes by walking sideways in every direction at once. It eats the braid. The braid grows. That is the whole of its life, and it does not seem to find that insufficient.

---

And then there are the ones who brought the stones, and here you have to be careful, because the tableland has taught you to be careful about the difference between what is gone and what has simply been taken back.

Start with what is certain. The stones are not from here. Nothing mineral is within reach — you have dug as deep as your arm goes and found only the black that is not rot, and there are hundreds of bands in a dome and the bands go down further than you can count, and the rock is below all of that, unseen for however long. So the stones were carried. And they could only have been carried from the edge, from the place — which you have not seen, which may be a season's walk in any direction, which may not exist in any direction you have walked — where the tableland finally stops being the tableland and falls away to the bare world below. Someone went to the edge, and picked up a stone, and turned around, and walked back in.

The hollow worn for a thumb is the mark of one hand. Not many hands over many years; one thumb, in one place, over a walk long enough to wear the stone. That is a very long walk. It is a walk of years. It may be a walk of a life. And the stones are spaced a hundred strides apart, and there are — you have counted, following the line — more than you can see the end of, and if each stone is a life then the line of stones is a line of lives, laid end to end across the tableland, each one carried in from the edge and set down at the place where the one before it stopped.

You do not think they were building toward anything. You think the line was the thing. You think that what lived at the edge — a people, or a family, or a habit passed from a very few to a very few — sent its members inward one at a time with a weight, and the weight was the point, and the walking was the point, and the setting down at the end was the point, and the person who set it down did not walk back out. Nothing walks back out. The mat is warm and the food is the ground and the water is in every fold of the turf, and a person who has walked for years into a place where every footprint closes behind them has no reason to believe there is still an edge to return to. They set the stone down. They lay down beside it. The turf came up over them, as it comes up over the grazers, and after a season there was a dome next to a stone, and after a hundred seasons there was only the stone.

The slab is older, and different, and speaks of more of them. Whoever cut it had numbers — enough hands to move a piece of stone three people long across however many seasons of turf it took to get it here, enough shared purpose to keep cutting the same regular lines into the same surface while the mat closed over their camp every night. The ring around it is the pressure of many bodies over a long time, remembered. There was, once, something like a settlement at the center of the tableland, if the tableland has a center, and the settlement was made of stone and of the mat itself, and the mat has taken back everything that was mat, and the stone remains. The walls are the same story told in turf: something raised, something lived between, something let go. Whatever that was, it is not here now. That much the tableland makes plain. There is no smoke, no path, no cropped ground that a grazer did not crop.

But the line of stones is different from the ring and the walls, and the difference is this: the stones at the far end of the line are less worn than the stones at the near end.

You noticed it on the second day of following and did not want to have noticed. The hollows are shallower. The gray is a little less dark with sitting. The turf around the last stone you reached before turning back had not yet drawn as far away from it as the turf around the first. The tableland cannot digest stone, and so it holds off from stone, and the width of the pale ring of held-off turf around a stone is, in a crude way, a clock. The clock at the near end of the line reads an age. The clock at the far end reads something less.

So it may be that they are not gone. It may be that there is still an edge, and still something living at the edge, and that once in a long while — once in a generation, once in a life — one of them still picks up a stone and turns and walks in, and is still walking, somewhere out in the pearl, at a pace slower than yours because they are carrying a weight and have been carrying it for years, and will not stop until they reach the last stone and set theirs down a hundred strides past it. They would look like nothing. A pale slow shape on the mat, turf greening on their shoulders the way it greens on a grazer, eyes no longer much used because there is nothing here to see. You could pass within a hundred strides of one and take it for a dome. It could pass within a hundred strides of you and take you for the same.

You do not know what they would say if you met. You suspect nothing. You suspect that a person who has walked for years through a place that eats every sound and closes every mark has arrived at something like the burrowers' relation to speech: it is not that they cannot, it is that the place has stopped giving them a reason. They would surface, breathe, sink. They would set the stone down. They would lie beside it.

You suspect, also, that this is not sad. It has the shape of sadness from outside — a life spent carrying a weight into a place that will forget the life — but the tableland has been quietly correcting your sense of shapes for weeks. The mat does not forget. It takes back. The person becomes a dome and the dome breathes and the herds graze it and the burrowers thread it and the lamp-slugs climb it at dusk and it is warm, it is all still warm, it is all still here, digesting itself at the pace of a season on the roof of the world. Only the stone stays separate. Only the stone is not taken. And that was, you think, the bargain: to give the whole of yourself to the thing that keeps everything, and to leave one small cold part of yourself outside the keeping, so that there would be, in a place made entirely of return, one thing that did not.

---

So: what lives here.

A plant the size of a country that has been alive for longer than the mountains it sits on and may have eaten them. A herd that is a slow sentence written in taste across its skin. A blood of narrow faces that surface and breathe together. A dark sigh in the cloud that may be an animal and may be a piece of the ground that learned to float. Lanterns that drink sweat. Hungers that have stopped being hungry and become directions. A ring of legs around a mouth.

And perhaps, out in the pearl, a person, or a very few people, or one, carrying a stone at the pace of the stone.

And you, for now. You have been here long enough that the mat has begun to answer you. There is a dish the shape of your sitting closing slowly behind you when you move. There is a fine green growing at the seams of your clothes, and your skin is the color of old bronze to the knee, and the lamp-slugs know your warmth and come to it, and when you press your ear to the ground the pulse that is probably water and probably nothing else has begun to sound less like a thing you are listening to and more like a thing you are inside. You did not carry a stone in. But your back is against one, and it is cold in a way nothing else here is cold, and you have found yourself, this evening, not wanting to take your hand from the hollow on its side that fits a thumb.

The line goes on over the swell. The cloud holds. Whatever is walking toward the far end of the line has not yet arrived and may not arrive in your time here, and if it does, you will not hear it coming. You will see, one gray morning, that there is a new dome a hundred strides past the last stone, and a new stone beside it, and the turf just beginning to draw back.
