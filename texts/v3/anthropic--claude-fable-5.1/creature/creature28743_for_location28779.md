Start with the plants, because they are what you can touch, and because for a long time they are all that anyone who goes down thinks is there.

There seem to be four kinds. People who have been down and come back up describe them in roughly the same way, which is worth something, since they are describing them with their hands.

The first is the cushion. It grows in domes a hand's breadth across, densely packed, so tight that a fingertip pressed into one meets resistance like a wet loaf. Each dome is made of thousands of upright shoots no longer than a grain of rice, standing shoulder to shoulder, each one tipped with a bead of water that runs into the next bead when you disturb it. The cushions prefer the floor and the lower walls. Where two meet they do not stop; they seem to pass into one another, the domes merging into a single quilted surface that dips and rises with the stone underneath.

The second is the trailing kind. It hangs from the ceiling in strands as long as your arm, sometimes longer, each strand a chain of small overlapping leaflets like the scales of a pine cone opened flat. They are heavy with water and they swing when you move through them and settle against your face and neck with a cold that is genuinely startling the first time. They are most numerous over the middle of the room, where the ceiling is highest and the drips have farthest to fall, and it is from their tips that most of the ticking comes.

The third is the flat scale, which would be a liverwort in the world above. It grows in overlapping plates, each one the size of a thumbnail and the thickness of a leaf of paper, lying against the felt so closely that you find them by texture alone — a place where the wall is suddenly slick and tiled instead of soft. They favor the ends of the room, the curved parts, and they cover the underside of the bench-stone almost entirely.

The fourth is the strap. These are the ones people remember. They stand out from the walls in ranks, each blade a finger long and a finger wide, thin and stiff and faintly grooved, and they stand so evenly spaced that a hand drawn along them ripples them in sequence — they bend and return one after another like the teeth of a comb, and the sound they make is a soft dry clatter that is the only dry sound in the room. They grow thickest on the far wall, toward the ring, and they all point the same way.

That is what is there. What it is takes longer.

None of the four kinds is a plant in the sense that would have satisfied anyone with a microscope, and a few people have brought one down. There is no green because there is no need for green: no chloroplasts, nothing shaped to catch light, no trace in the tissue of anything that ever did. The cells are there, in walls and rows, and they divide and elongate and die in an order that a botanist would recognize, but what fills them is not what fills a leaf. It is denser, more crystalline, and it is arranged. A blade of the strap kind, sliced across and looked at under a lens by lamplight, shows two distinct faces — a thin tough layer on the side toward the crack, and a thicker, more open layer on the side toward the ring — and between them a sheet of something laid down in fine parallel lines running from face to face, as if the leaf were a stack of wires standing on end.

That is the engine. Every leaf and shoot and scale in the room sits across the flow of heat with its two faces at slightly different temperatures, and the stuff between them runs on the difference. What it makes from the difference — what chemistry it drives, what it builds — is not known in detail, but the outcome is: it builds more of itself, slowly, and it builds it on the cold side. The warm face is the root, in the sense that matters. The cold face is where growth happens. Every living thing in the room is a small thermal cell wired between the world and the ring, and the ring is the drain that keeps the current running.

This explains the orientation, which otherwise looks like reverence. The plants are not facing the ring. They are facing the steepest part of the gradient, and the gradient happens to end at the ring. Where the current is strongest — the far wall, the last half-degree — the straps grow, because a strap is the shape that puts the most tissue across the most flow. Where it is weaker and the water is heavy, the trailing kind grows, letting gravity stretch it into the slower stream. Where it is weakest, in the corners and under the bench, the flat scales lie down and take what they can from a broad shallow contact. The cushions grow where nothing else is committed.

And that in turn raises the question of whether there are four kinds at all.

Because the forms do not stay put. A cushion at the foot of the far wall, followed upward with a fingertip, does not end at a border; it lengthens, the shoots stretching, until without any seam you are touching straps. The trailing strands, where they brush a wall and stick, flatten against it over a few years and become scales. Pull apart the felt anywhere and the dead layers underneath are all one material, indistinguishable, compressed to the texture of wet cardboard, with no sign of four lineages keeping separate company. The most careful person who has looked at the cells — and she was careful — could find no fixed difference between one form and another that did not come down to where it was growing. Her conclusion, which she offered without insisting on it, was that the room contains one organism, or one kind of organism, and that it has learned four shapes the way water learns the shape of what holds it.

If that is right, then the thing that lives in the room is roughly the size of the room. It is a single continuous body, about five meters long and a little over a meter through, hollow, lining the stone from end to end, its outer surface the living faces of a hundred thousand leaves and its inner mass the compressed remains of its own past. It has no front or back, no center, no organ that is not a leaf. It is as old as the felt is deep, which is centuries at the least, and it is not certain that any part of it has ever died in a way that would mean anything to it: the dead layers are not discarded, they are held, built upon, used as the medium the living layer stands in. Its roots go into itself.

Whether it thinks anything is not a useful question. Whether it senses anything is a better one, and the answer is yes, in the limited way that matters to it: it tracks the ring. In the strong years, when the pull deepens by whatever fraction it deepens, the far wall thickens — the straps lengthen, new ranks appear below the old — and the near wall around the crack thins as the tissue there finds less current to live on. In the weak years the pattern eases. It is not fast. A strong year adds perhaps a millimeter to the far wall. But it is consistent, and it is the whole body responding at once, and in a person that would be called turning toward.

It also, in some fashion, remembers. The felt is stratified. Cut down through it with a blade and the layers alternate, thicker and thinner, a dark band and a pale one — dark and pale only under a lamp, and only just — and the alternation matches nothing that happens on the surface, no season, no weather. It matches the ring. The body has been writing the ring's cycle into itself, layer on layer, for as long as it has been here, and the record goes down to the stone. Nobody has stayed long enough to count the cycles from the outside, but anyone with patience and a knife could count them from the inside.

It does not reproduce, or if it does, nothing has come of it. There are structures on the cushion shoots that could be spore capsules — tiny swollen tips that split when they are dry, which they never are. There are no spores in the water. The air comes down the crack and stops, and nothing that the body might release has any way up. If it makes seed, the seed falls onto the floor of the room and becomes felt. Every spore that has drifted down since the first, whatever it was, has landed on an occupied surface and either joined the body or been buried by it. The room is a mouth that closed on the first thing that entered and has been slowly swallowing it ever since.

Which is the other way of seeing it, and it is worth holding both.

The body lives on the ring, but the ring does not know this. The ring is not alive and does not do anything that could be mistaken for living. It takes heat and does not give it back, and it has been doing so since before anything grew here, and it would go on doing so if the room were scraped to bare stone. It does not need the plants. It does not tolerate them or resist them. It is not a gardener and it is not a host. It is a fact of the room, like the stone, and the body has fitted itself to the fact as closely as it fits the stone, and if the ring stopped — if whatever it does simply ceased one day — the body would not know why the current had failed. It would thin, over years, from the far end backward, the straps first and then the trailing kind, until only the scales and cushions were left in the last cool spots near the floor, and then the room would be four meters down and cellar-cool and quietly rotting, and the drips would keep falling on a felt that no longer grew.

That is not happening. Nothing suggests it will. But it is the shape of the arrangement: a thing that lives on a thing that does not live, in a room that neither of them made.

There is nothing else here. This has been said, but it bears saying from the inside. The water moves, and the water is not alive. The air moves, and the air is not alive. The felt holds no mites, no worms, no threads of fungus in the dead layers where fungus should be — the dead layers are not decaying, they are compressing, and that is a different thing, and it may be the thing that keeps the animals out. Whatever the ring pulls from the world, it pulls something that rot requires, or that a moving body requires, and the plants do not require it, and so the plants are alone. In several centuries of a room where everything is wet and nothing is ever cleaned, there is not one thing that has ever eaten another thing. There is only the one body, and the water it wears, and the drain it faces.

You are the only animal that has ever been in here, and you cannot stay.

When you do come — when you climb down the crack and stand crouched under the hanging strands with your breath going out of you white — you are the warmest thing the room has held in a very long time. You radiate. The current that runs through every leaf in the room steepens a little on the side that faces you, and for the half-hour or hour you are there the body is, in the only way it can be, aware of a source. It does not turn. Turning takes years. But the leaves nearest you take more than their share from the air you have warmed, and when you climb back out and the air behind you cools to what it was, the body settles again, having taken from you exactly as much as you left, and passed it on toward the ring.

That is the whole of its acquaintance with visitors. It has no other kind. Your hands ache on the way up because a small part of you is down there now, in the current, being spent. It is not much, and it was not asked for, and it is the closest thing to hospitality the place has ever offered anyone.
