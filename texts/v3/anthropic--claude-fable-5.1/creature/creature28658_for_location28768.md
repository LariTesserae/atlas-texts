The honest census is short, and the honest census is wrong, and it is worth doing anyway so that the wrongness has something to stand against.

There are shrimp. There are fish. There is a flatworm. There is an eel. There are three plants, if the white nodules on the bell count as a plant. That is the room. Nothing in it is large except the eel and nothing in it is numerous except the shrimp, and if you drained the chamber and laid everything out on the floor it would make a modest heap, smaller than the pipe, and it would all be dead within the hour, because nothing that lives here can live anywhere else. That is the first thing a census misses: not what is present but what is absent. There is nothing here that could leave. There is no air-breather, no seed that drifts, no larva that rides the outflow down the mountain and survives. The grille lets water out and lets nothing living out with it that goes on being living. The room has a population and the population has a single direction of travel, which is up, once, and then nowhere.

---

Start with the shrimp, because they are the sound.

There are more of them than can be counted and fewer than it seems, because their clicking multiplies them. Each is the length of a fingernail and nearly clear, with a smear of red where the gut runs and a faint grain to the shell that catches the green light and makes them, in the weed, look like beads of the weed's own sap. They are not snapping to speak. They are snapping to feed, a small violent closing of one claw that stuns whatever is smaller than they are, and there are so many of them doing it that the room is never quiet, and yet there is a pattern under it that has nothing to do with feeding: they click in the rests. They have learned, across whatever thousands of generations a shrimp's year allows since the first blind ancestor came up the pipe, that the stroke is coming, and they go still in the half-second before it and fill the pause after it with sound, so that the room's breath has a texture, a rough inhale and a hard beat and a rough inhale again. Whether this serves them, whether the stroke would knock a claw from its target, is not known. They do it. They have done it so long that the rhythm is in them the way the tide is in a barnacle.

They are not the inhabitant. They are too many and too brief for that. They are what the inhabitant's weather is made of.

---

The fish are the ones that have changed most and are the easiest to see changing. There are never many; eight, nine, a dozen when the shrimp are thick. They are the color of the lime crust seen through water, which is to say almost no color, and their eyes are enormous, wet black domes set in heads too small for them, so that a fish seen head-on is mostly eye. The eyes are recent. The rest of the fish is still built for the dark—the flattened snout, the whiskers, the lateral line running like a seam along the flank—and the eyes sit on that body like a repair somebody made in a hurry with the wrong parts. They see very little with them. Motion in light. The shrimp against the green.

They hang in the two bars of light the way a hawk hangs in wind, holding station against the current with small corrections, and they face the pipe. All of them. Always. It is not fear. A fish does not have room in it for fear that lasts. It is that the surge comes from the pipe and food comes up in the surge and the eel comes up in the surge and everything that matters in this room has the same source and the same direction, and a fish that faces the source is a fish that has noticed the one fact the room contains. Every fish in the room will be taken, eventually, from the front.

---

The flatworm is the exception, and exceptions are useful.

It is the length of a forearm and the width of two fingers and it is the deep brown-red of the alga it grazes, so that when it is still it is invisible and when it moves it looks like the wall thinking. It has been the same one, or the same few, for a long time; nothing eats it, it is too large for the shrimp and too slow for the fish to bother with and the eel does not leave the pipe for things that do not move. It travels in arcs across the north wall, always the same arcs, and leaves behind it a pale path that the alga closes over in a day, and then it comes back and does it again, and what it takes and what regrows are in a balance so exact that the wall has never been bare and never been unbroken.

It does not face the pipe. It does not click in the rests. It grazes at its own speed, which has nothing to do with the stroke, and it is the only thing in the room that lives here the way things live in an ordinary pond, indifferent to the machine, treating the warmth and the light as given rather than as gift. If the pump stopped tomorrow the flatworm would be the last to know and the last to die. It is what the room would look like if the room were merely a room.

It is not the inhabitant either. It is the proof that the inhabitant is something else.

---

So: the eel.

It is thick as a thigh at the shoulder and tapers to a blade, and it is mottled in a pattern that is not camouflage but accretion—the lime has been settling on it, faintly, for years, and it has grown a skin over the settling, and grown lime over the skin, so that it is the exact color and very nearly the exact texture of the pipe it lives in. Its eyes are small and old and probably useless. Its mouth is enormous and slightly open always, because the water moves through it and it does not need to do anything about that.

It lives in the throat, below the bell, where the surge is born. When the engine strokes, the column of warm water lifts it and it rides up until its head clears the rim and hangs in the light, jaw slightly ajar, the whole room's breath running through its gills, and for a moment it is a stone figure at the top of a fountain. Then the surge passes and it sinks, without effort, back into the dark. It does this every stroke. It has done this for longer than anything else in the room has been alive. It is not waiting. An eel does not wait. It is where the water is warmest and where the food arrives, and it has arranged its body along the one axis of the room that matters, and it stays.

When it strikes, it does not so much attack as continue. The surge lifts it; instead of stopping at the rim it keeps going, straight up the light bar on the momentum the pump has given it, and the fish it takes is the one that has drifted a hand's width too close to the source it faces, and the eel's jaw closes with a sound that is lost under the stroke, and on the very next lift it is back in the bell and the fish is gone and the shrimp have not stopped clicking. The room does not register a death. The room registers one less small motion in the light.

And here is the thing that matters about it. The eel is thick and the throat is not wide, and when the eel lies in the throat it fills most of it, and when the pump pushes, it is pushing against the eel. The tempo drifts. It slows when the eel is deep and settled and hurries when the eel rises and the throat clears, and this has been true for the whole of the eel's life, and it may have been true before that, because an eel that finds a warm throat full of food will grow to fill it and there has always been a warm throat here full of food. It is possible there has always been an eel. Not this one. One after another, each swimming up blind from the reservoir as a thread of muscle, each growing thick in the same dark, each becoming the load the engine labors against, each dying at the bottom of the pipe and going down the way it came so that the next can rise. The pump has perhaps never in its life pushed against clean water. The room has perhaps never once breathed without an animal in the breath.

---

That is close. That is very close. But it is still an animal, and animals are guests.

There is something in the room that was here before the eel and before the shrimp and before the light had anything to fall on, and it is the crust.

It is not one thing. It is the iron and lime coming out of solution and settling, which is chemistry, and it is the white nodular alga on the bell that hurries the settling and shapes it, which is biology, and it is the brown-gold film over every brick and every mortar line and every corner that is not a corner anymore, which is time. Whether the alga was here first and taught the water to build or the water was building first and the alga came up the pipe and moved into the work is not recoverable. What is recoverable is that they are one process now. The film grows a fraction each century. The corners round. The floor's bowl deepens where the current turns and shallows where the deposit falls. And the bell of the pipe, only the bell, grows taller, because that is where the alga lives and that is where the water is hottest and richest, and the white cauliflower nodules are laying down lime faster than anything else in the room is laying down anything.

This is the only resident with a future. The shrimp will be shrimp. The eel will be replaced by an eel. But the bell is rising, a finger's width in a lifetime, and the ceiling is where it is, and one day, not soon but certainly, the bell will meet the dome and the surge will have nowhere to roll and the room will breathe differently or will stop breathing. The crust is the only thing in the room that is doing something the room has not already done ten thousand times. It is building the end.

It does not know this. Nothing here knows anything. But if you want the inhabitant to be the one who has been here longest and will be here last and is changing the place rather than being changed by it, it is the skin on the walls, and it is white and warm and slightly rough and it is everywhere you put your hand.

---

And under the floor, farther down than the eel goes, is the engine.

It has never been seen and will never be seen, because the tower it lives in has become a mountain and there is no way into a mountain. But it can be felt through the soles of the feet as a shudder that comes just before the sound, and it can be reasoned about. It is old past reckoning. It runs on something—heat, water falling, a spring under pressure, a thing that turns—and whatever it runs on has not run out. It works against a load. The load changes; the tempo says so. It has been working against eels and lime and the sheer height of the column for so long that the labor has become its condition, and if you are inclined to read patience as contentment, there is nothing in the room more patient than the thing you cannot see.

Is it alive? No. Does it live here? It is the only thing that does in the sense of never having arrived. Everything else came up. The engine was set, and has stayed, and every other resident is a consequence of it. Take it away and there is no warmth, no surge, no light on anything worth lighting, no room—only a wet stone cell at the top of a ruin, going cold.

---

So the census fails, and this is where it fails: it wants a single body and there is not one. There is a body, but it is made of the others.

The engine is the lung, far down, doing the only thing it remembers. The pipe is the windpipe. The eel is the valve in the throat, an animal that has become an organ, that the lung labors against and that shapes the breath by being there. The surge is the breath itself, warm and iron-tasting, rolling across the dome and out through the grille. The crust is the skin, thickening, rounding, slowly closing over. The weed is hair, or gills, leaning toward the source. The shrimp are the noise of the body's own small violence, the sound the whole thing makes when it is feeding, which is always. The fish are what it feeds on, and they hang in its light facing the throat that will take them. Only the flatworm is not part of it, and the flatworm is how you know the rest of it is a body and not a room: there is one thing in here that does not keep the time, and it is the one thing that could live anywhere.

The body has one appetite. It wants water to come up. Everything it is, it is because water comes up, and every part of it is arranged toward the place where water comes up, and in that arrangement there is something that a person hanging in the light bar with the stroke going through the sternum will feel as attention. As if the room had noticed the surge. As if it were leaning toward it. It is not attention. It is that the surge has been here longer than anything that could notice, and everything that could notice has been shaped to lean.

What lives in the room at the top of the mountain that used to be a tower is a breath with an animal in it and a skin growing over it and a noise inside it, that has been drawing warm water up from the dark for longer than there have been eyes to see it arrive, and keeping some of what comes, and letting the rest go down to become cloud. It does not know it is at the summit. It does not know it is a summit. It knows one direction, up, and one event, the stroke, and one hunger, more, and it has made a whole small ocean out of these and filled the ocean with itself.

If a body came through the grille—if the grille's openings are wide enough for a careful body and a body were careful—it would be an arrival like any other. Something new come to the room by the wrong door. The shrimp would go still and then resume. The fish would not turn. The eel would rise on the next stroke and hang in the bell with its jaw ajar and settle, and the body would be either food or furniture, and the crust would begin, at once and very slowly, to close over whichever it was.
