What lives here is, first and almost entirely, the mat. Everything else in the valley — the bird, the hare, whatever turns over in the dark water — lives in it the way things live in weather, and it is better to understand the resident before the tenants.

—

Whether the mat is one thing or several is the kind of question the valley makes hard to hold onto. Cut into it anywhere and you find the same four or five kinds of plant, each with its own tissue, its own cell walls, its own way of dying. The cushion-plant browns and tightens. The sedge goes glassy and then splits along its length. The rope-creeper does not die so much as harden, and its dead columns stand for years inside the living ones like the heartwood of a tree. They are distinct. You could name them.

But none of them occurs anywhere else. There is no cushion-plant on the far side of the pass, no lone sedge in a crevice of ordinary rock. The lichen — already a partnership of two things pretending to be one — grows on nothing but the creeper columns and the passing ridges, and if you carry a plate over the pass and set it on stone it is dead within the week. They have been together long enough that asking whether they are one organism is like asking whether a hand is one thing or five. They behave as one. The level line on the walls is dead level along twenty kilometers of valley, which no collection of separate plants competing with each other could manage. The boundary at the pass is exact to the width of a stem and has been exact for as long as anyone has looked. The temperature in the lower layers is held, year in and year out, to within a degree. Whatever counts as an individual here, it is at least the size of the valley, and it is probably the valley.

It has no front and no face. It does not have a day, in the sense of a period of waking; it has instead a set of rhythms nested inside each other, and it lives in all of them at once. The second-long pulse of the trunk-lines. The daily shedding of frost, which begins at the edges of the leaves and moves inward and is finished by noon. The week-long migration of a ridge from the trough to the wall. The yearly creep of the line on the rock, up a meter in a warm year, down in a cold one. And under all of those, so slow that it shows only in the terraces high on the walls, the long shrink from whatever it was when it filled the valley to the summits and polished the rock with a substance that was not water. It is an old thing in its late size. It may be dying, at a rate that will take longer than the mountains have left. Or it may simply have found the size at which it runs best and be holding there, wearing in.

It attends to two things: heat and pressure. That is the whole of its nervous system, if it has one, and it is enough. Heat tells it where the dead are being broken down and where the columns need to stand. Pressure tells it where the trunk-lines are full and where the surface must be let go. Everything that is not heat or pressure is material — rain, sound, frost, the dead sedge, the dead hare, you. It does not notice you. It cannot, in any sense that would matter to you. But it is not indifferent, either, because indifference is an attitude, and the mat has none. It has a metabolism, and its metabolism is its behavior, and you are inside it.

What it makes is not known. The lichen plates are heavier than they should be, and when they spall the grit is crystalline and faintly metallic and very cold, and where it goes after that is down. Cut a trunk-line low enough — this is dangerous, the pressure is real and the fluid numbs to the bone — and the sap that runs out has a grain to it, a fine suspended glitter that settles in an hour to a gray film. The peat cores, when they come up from thirty meters, are black and fibrous and streaked with something that takes an edge from a knife. Whatever the plating beds precipitate is being routed into the floor of the valley and has been for tens of thousands of years, and the floor of the valley is very deep. Perhaps the mat is making the thing that lets it hold heat. Perhaps it is making the thing that once let it fill the valley to the summits, and is storing it, and will one day have enough. Perhaps a factory this old has forgotten what the product was for and goes on producing because production is the only shape it has. You cannot ask it. You can only note that it is very heavy, down there, and getting heavier.

—

The mat's real population, by any count, is in the dark.

Put your arm into a crevice past the elbow and you are in a different climate: wet, hot, black, and full. The heat that the columns vent to the sky is made here, by things that have never seen light and could not survive it — the bacteria and the fungal threads and the slow armies of decomposers that take the dead sedge and the dead cushion and whatever else falls to them and break it, in a day, into the fluid the pipelines carry. They are the furnace crew. They are also, almost certainly, the oldest residents of the valley: older than the plants, which came later and roofed them in and learned to feed them and to carry away what they made. Whether they are part of the mat or a nation the mat keeps is another question that dissolves if you hold it too long. They number in the trillions. They have never been counted and never will be. When the peat cores come up they are steaming, and the steam smells of turpentine and something else, sweetish, like bread, and in an hour the core is cold and the crew inside it is dead and the smell is gone.

—

Above the surface, three things live here that are not the mat, and a fourth that may or may not be.

The finches are small and dark and heavier than they look. They nest in the radiator cups, which are the only warm dry ledges in the valley; the eggs sit in a shallow bowl of dead creeper-fiber a hand's breadth above the bitter pool, and the column's heat does much of the incubating, so the birds are away from the nest more than any finch elsewhere would dare to be. There is nothing to fear from above. Nothing hunts here. The sky over the valley is empty of raptors, because there is nothing for a raptor to eat but finches, and the finches are few, and the light is shadowless, so that even a hawk's stoop would announce itself against the even gray. The finches fly low and slow and land often. They eat the grit that spalls from the lichen plates, and they work it in their beaks and drop most of it, and what they keep goes into them. Their bones, when one dies on the stone beyond the pass — which happens, occasionally, because they are the only thing in the valley that crosses the boundary in both directions — have a faint sheen that finch bones should not have. They are the mat's only export, and they do not know it, and it does not know it either.

They call to each other, but the calls do not carry, and over the generations the finches of this valley have become nearly silent. They signal with a flick of the wing instead, a quick white flash from the underside that shows well against the gray-green and costs nothing. A finch from outside, blown over the pass, calls and calls and hears no answer and is gone within the season. The residents flick, and land, and eat cold grit, and roost in the vapor.

The hare is a different case. There may be one. There are certainly not many. Its tracks are everywhere in the rime after a hard frost — long straight lines across the whitened floor, from crevice to crevice, from column to split cushion and back — and the number of tracks suggests a population that the number of sightings does not support. It is pale, very pale, the color of the frost, and in the flat light with no shadow to give it an edge it is close to invisible even in the open. It comes up when the frost comes because the frost is the one hour when the surface is stiff and quiet: the sedges lock, the cushions hold, the ridges pause in their migration, and a small animal can cross a kilometer without the ground letting go beneath it.

It eats the pale sweet interior of the split cushions, which brown and tighten within minutes and must be taken fresh. It drinks from the radiator cups, standing on the rim with its ears flat in the rising vapor. And it lives — this is the part that takes some working out — by never staying anywhere for long. The crevices are warm, and a hare in the cold wants warmth, and the mat offers it freely at a depth of about an arm's length. Below that the warmth becomes something else. The hare knows the depth. It must know it the way you know the height of a step, without thinking, because a hare that did not know it would be routed into the pipelines before morning, and the mat would not register the difference. It sleeps shallow and it sleeps light and it moves at the first shift in the fiber beneath it. Its life is probably short. Its lineage came over the pass at some point in the deep past and has never gone back, and has been shaped since then by a single pressure, which is the pressure not to stop.

Once — this is the third thing, and it belongs to the hare — a set of tracks was followed for most of a morning across a side-basin and ended at a place where a section had subsided in the night. The tracks went to the lip of the sag and did not come out. The sag was already overgrown, pale new sedge lifting through it. There was no sign, no fur, no sound. By the next frost there were tracks again, elsewhere, and whether they were the same hare's or another's there was no way to know.

—

The fourth thing is in the covered lakes.

In the deepest of the side-basins the mat has grown out over the meltwater and roofed it, and under the roof the water is warmer than any open lake in the valley — the heat from the lower layers is held in, and the surface is black and still and beaded with drops that fall from the roots above. The pipelines run through the roof and some of them have broken, and where they have broken the sap weeps into the water and clouds it, and there is a smell of turpentine so thick it is almost a taste.

Something lives in it. You see it, if you see it at all, as a disturbance — a slow turning of the water, a paleness that rises and does not quite surface, a bubble the size of a fist that breaks without a sound. Once it came close enough to the light at the cavern mouth to show a flank: smooth, colorless, the skin of a thing that has never needed color, with a texture that might have been an amphibian's and might have been the sheath of a root. It was large. It was slower than anything alive should be in water that cold, but the water is not that cold, and it may have all the time in the world.

If it is an animal it is the only one in the valley that eats what the mat makes — that drinks, directly, the grained sap from the broken pipelines and takes the glitter into itself and holds it in the dark. If it is not an animal it is the mat, in water, learning something new after all this time. It has never been seen to come out. It may not be able to. It may not be a single thing. When the roof over its lake subsides and the water shows steel-gray to the sky for a season, there is nothing in it, and when the roof grows back the disturbance returns, and no one has been able to say from where.

—

People do not live here. They come over the pass with cores and instruments, and they whisper within an hour without meaning to, and they leave. The mat has taken some of them. Not many — the ones who stopped too long in the wrong place, who lay down in the warm at dusk and did not feel the fiber shift. There is nothing left of them, not a buckle, not a bone; the pipelines are thorough. It was not malice. The mat cannot tell its own dead from anything else, and it does not need to, and this is the closest thing to a rule that the valley offers its inhabitants, all of them, the finch and the hare and the thing in the water and the trillions in the dark: keep moving, or be material.

The mat itself is the one exception. It does not move, in the sense that matters. It churns, from wall to wall, without ceasing, and has churned for longer than the mountains have had their shape, and the churning is how it holds still — the way a standing wave holds still, the way a flame does. At dusk the columns stand up out of the general gray with their vapor going straight up, and the frost comes in from the edges of the leaves, and under the frost the furnace crew works in the wet heat, and the trunk-lines pulse once a second, and a kilometer away something lets go and something else lifts. The hare is out on the rime somewhere, not stopping. The finches are in the cups. The water under the roof turns over, slowly, once. And the resident of the valley, which is the valley, goes on making whatever it is that it makes, and routing it down, into the dark, where it is very heavy, and getting heavier.
