The first honest answer is the one the mountain gives you: nothing. You look, because looking is what you do, and there is the green and the wet and the roar, and there is nothing in it. You accept this for a while. Then you notice that you have not stopped looking, and you begin to wonder what you are looking for.

Start with the rooted things, since they are what there is. The trees, the ferns, the moss in the doorways. They are green in the way that only living things are green, chlorophyll-green, the green of something doing work. But a living thing is a thing that is on its way — toward seed, toward rot, toward the next inch of growth — and nothing here is on its way to anything. The trees have arrived at tree. The moss has arrived at moss. If you cut a branch it would be wet and white inside and the cut would not weep and would not heal and would not brown, because those are all things that happen next, and there is no next. So the forest is not dead and it is not alive. It is finished, the way the stairs are finished. It is what a forest looks like when it has stopped being a process and become a shape. You cannot call that living. You cannot quite call it anything else.

Then there is the fig.

You come back to the fig because it is the one thing on the mountain that seems to have wanted something. It has *forced* its way through — that is the word your mind supplies, and it is the only verb of effort the place has given you. The paving-that-is-not-paving is heaved up around its base in slabs. The roots go down into the fractures like fingers into a fist. It looks like a struggle. Then you look longer and understand that it is the shape of a struggle in the same way the walls are the shape of masonry. The slabs were never flat. The roots did not push; the rock parted around them as it parted around everything, over a duration you don't have a word for, and what you are seeing is the settled outcome of a pressure that stopped being pressure before the fig was a fig. It is a fig-shaped rock and a rock-shaped fig and there was never a moment when one won. There is no fruit on it. There has never been a wasp.

You keep looking. You cannot help it.

There is a candidate you almost miss because it is so general. The water. It falls from every ledge and beads on the underside of every leaf and runs in every channel and it is moving, visibly, audibly — it is the one thing here that is indisputably in motion — and for a while you think: this, then. This is the resident. The city belongs to the water the way a reef belongs to the current. But the water does not go anywhere. The pools do not overflow. The falls fall and the channels do not rise and the line on the stone is dry above and dry below. The water is moving in the way a flame is moving: in place, as a condition, not as a going. You put your hand into a channel and the water runs over your hand and off your hand and it is exactly the same water. It has not been anywhere. It is not on its way. It is the wet the mountain has settled into.

So: nothing. You have looked at everything and it is all the same finished thing wearing different shapes, and none of it lives here, because living is a direction and this place has none.

That is when you see the other one.

It is across the sheet of water that comes down through the gap in the overhang — on the terrace that is there when you look at it through the fall and is not there when you go around. You are not going around. You are standing at the fall, and through the white you see the terrace, and on the terrace there is a shape that is not a tree and is not a rock and is upright. It is the height of a person. It is standing very still, which in this place means nothing, since so is everything. What makes you keep looking is that it is not rooted. There is a gap between it and the stone. It is standing *on* the terrace rather than being part of the terrace, and that is a thing you have not seen since the lowlands.

You do not go around. You know what is around. You stay at the fall and look through the water for what may be a long time — mid-morning, and mid-morning — and the shape does not move, and you cannot tell what it is doing.

You leave the fall, and you find it again in a courtyard four levels down, and it is doing something else.

Not moving. Doing something else. Standing in a different attitude, as if you had come upon it at a different instant — half a turn further round, one hand a little higher. You go up a stair, counting, and along a corridor with the drop on your left, and there it is in the next square, and again the posture has advanced, the way a figure in a doorway advances if you walk past the doorway looking in. By the time you have gone all the way around the contour of the peak and arrived back at the fig from the direction you left it, you have seen it eleven times, or nine, or thirteen, and you understand that you have not seen eleven of anything. You have walked around one figure, standing in one place, and seen it from every side, and every side is a different moment of the same gesture.

The gesture is this: it is bending, or straightening. Its hands are low, in front of it, a little apart, palms turned in, the way hands are when they have just released something or are just about to take hold of it. Seen from one side it is a person setting down a load. Seen from the other side it is a person about to lift one. You cannot tell which. You go around again and you cannot tell which, and you realize that this is not a limitation of your seeing. There is nothing to tell. The gesture is not a moment taken out of a sequence. It is the whole of it. Setting down and picking up are things that happen on either side of a stillness, and this is the stillness, and it has both of them in it and neither.

There is nothing at its feet. Whatever it was carrying, or is about to carry, is not there. Perhaps it is the load. Perhaps it is what has been set down.

Its mouth is open. Not wide. Open the way the openings in the walls are open — an aperture in a surface, a place where the inside and the outside are not separated. You wait for a sound to come out of it and then you understand that a sound is coming out of it: the roar. Exactly the roar, exactly as loud as it is from everywhere else, no more and no less. The mouth is not the source of the sound. Nothing is the source of the sound. But the sound is in the mouth the way it is in the water, and the water is in the mouth too, running in over the lip and not filling it and not running out. It does not breathe. It has arrived at the far side of breath. You look for the rise of a chest and there is nothing to rise, because rising would be a direction, and it would have to go somewhere afterward.

It has no shadow, but nothing does.

You cannot decide what it is made of. Skin, it seems, at a distance; closer it seems to have the grain of the rock running through it, unbroken from the terrace up into the ankle. Closer still it is neither — it is the look of a thing that has been wet so long it has taken the color of wet. Moss has not grown on it and moss has not failed to grow on it. Water beads on it and falls and beads again. It is, you decide, exactly as old as the sound, which is to say it is not old, because it was never anything else. It did not come up the mountain. It did not weather out of the mountain. It is not a ruin of a person and it did not come from being whole.

And yet. You go around once more, slowly, and something in the eleven or nine or thirteen attitudes bothers you. Each one is the same instant of the same gesture, but each one is *slightly* more that instant than the last, the way each stair-count is slightly not the last stair-count. It is not turning. It is not becoming. But the mountain has, in this one place, left a remainder — a small unreconciled difference between one side and another — and the remainder is standing in the courtyard with its hands open. It is the one thing here that erosion did not finish. Not because it resisted; because it is the shape of the finishing itself, and a shape can't finish its own outline.

You think then, because you are still a thing that thinks toward conclusions, that it must be what happens to those who come here and stop. That is the reading with the most horror in it and you try it on. It does not fit. Nothing here is what happens to anything; that is the whole rule of the place. It is not the residue of arrivals. It is not the first one who came and it is not the sum of all of them and it is not you, later. It is what was always standing at the end of the going-toward, not waiting — waiting is a direction too — just there, the way the line on the stone is there, dry above and dry below.

It does not see you. Seeing would be a movement toward. But you notice, on your last circuit, that from wherever you stand, the open hands are turned a little in your direction, and you cannot tell whether that is because it is about to receive something from you or has just let something go to you, and the mountain does not understand the question.

You stand by the fig. The water runs off your hands into the channel that does not rise. Across the courtyard the figure is bending, or straightening, with the roar coming out of its mouth exactly as loud as it comes out of everything else. You are the only thing here still moving toward something. It is the only thing here that is the exact shape of having got there — not the arrived, but the arriving, held, the way the sound is held, one instant wide and no wider, with nothing set down and nothing picked up and its palms turned in toward the place where a load would be.

You could put something in its hands. You think about this for a long time, mid-morning, mid-morning. You do not know what you would put there. You do not know whether it would take it, or whether you would find, having done it, that its hands had been empty because you were about to.
