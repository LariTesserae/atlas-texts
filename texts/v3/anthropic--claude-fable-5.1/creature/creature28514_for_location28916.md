**Who Lives There**

Begin, as before, with what is oldest. The people are not the oldest thing that lives on the table, and they would be the first to say so.

The pillars were here before the town. You can tell because the lowest, hugest courses of the walls — the ones no living person could have set — bend around the roots of the oldest columns rather than the other way. The founders, whoever they were, came to a forest already ancient and built into its gaps. So the first inhabitants are the plants: slow, thick-skinned, wordless, drinking the haze. They do not know they are alive in the way an animal knows it. They do not know there is a town. But they respond. Cut one and it seals. Hollow one for a nest and it grows a callused lip around the hole and goes on. They are the table's landlords and they have never once noticed their tenants, and the tenants — all of them, the birds and the lizards and the people — live entirely on their sufferance. Every mouthful of water on the plateau has passed through the wax of a pillar's skin over the course of years. The people know this, and they do not exactly worship the pillars, but they will not turn their backs on them while cutting, and they speak more quietly in the forest than anywhere else, which is still not quiet.

Each stand of pillars belongs, loosely, to a house. Not owned — the word does not fit — but tended, walked among, known by touch. A child of that house will be able to find a particular column in the dark by the pattern of its ribs under the palm. Certain very old pillars have names, which are also the names of the people who first cut them, so that a family and a plant may share a word, and when the plant finally splits and falls — which happens perhaps once in three generations, with a sound like a wet drum — that word is retired and not spoken again.

---

Then the colony. It is not one thing but the people speak of it as one thing, the way you would speak of the sea.

The birds are perhaps forty kinds, though no one has counted. The people distinguish them by sound rather than sight, because in the amber gloom a sooty bird and a rust bird look much the same, but their voices are as different as the voices of neighbors. There is the throat-clicker, the one that keens, the flag-tail that has a rising three-note call and nests only in the west-facing niches. The people do not name individual birds. They name the layers of the sound. A wall-walker listening to the colony at dawn will tell you, without looking, that the flag-tails are restless, that something has disturbed the north face, that the moths have hatched early this year because the keeners are fat and lazy in their song.

The birds do not know they are kept. They are not, really. They come to the warm stone and the safe holes because their grandmothers did, and they leave a share of their eggs and their bodies and their droppings because a certain fraction of every colony is always lost to something, and here that something walks upright and thanks them. The people would find the idea that they *own* the birds absurd. They would say they live in the same house.

Under the birds, the lizards, so many that the ground is a moving surface and children learn to walk with a flat sliding step so as not to crush them. The lizards are eaten, but without ceremony, snatched and killed and roasted on a hot stone between other tasks, the way a person elsewhere might eat a handful of berries while working. The insects are gathered. The bats are left alone, because they take the air at dusk when the people go inside, and because their coming marks the change of the day, and it is not wise to eat a clock.

The silky rust-colored thing inside the cushion plants has a name that means *the one who scolds*. No one has ever held one. Children try. The very old say that when they were young, a girl did once catch one and it looked at her and she let it go, and this story is told with enormous seriousness, as if it were the most important thing that had ever happened, because in a sense it is.

---

And then the people.

They are small. Nothing large lives on the table and they are not an exception; a tall one among them would come to the shoulder of a tall person elsewhere. But they are broad through the chest and long in the lung, from a lifetime of thin air, and their hands are large — disproportionately, noticeably — with long flat fingers made expressive by use. Their skin is the color of the lichen, which is not a pigment exactly but a condition: a lifetime of the mineral dust worked into the pores, and of the amber light, and of the pillar-wax that gets into everything and gives them a faint sheen in the gloom, so that a group of them standing close in a doorway looks like a patch of the forest that has decided to have faces.

The faces squint. Even in the deepest understory, even at night, the eyes are narrowed, and the lines at the corners come early and cut deep. Their eyes are otherwise excellent, adjusted to a world where nothing has an edge; they can read the tremor in a wall-block from ten paces or tell a young pillar's age by the exact grey of its skin, but taken into strong light they would be blinded and in pain. They have never seen a shadow with a hard edge. They have never seen a horizon. Their word for *far* is the same as their word for *the rim*, and it carries, always, a faint sense of the forbidden.

They are hoarse. All of them, from childhood. Ordinary speech on the table is a shout, pitched high to cut through the roar, and the throat pays for it. So ordinary speech is short and functional — *here, that one, water, wait, more* — and everything that matters is done with the hands. The hand-language is old, fluent, subtle. It has tenses. It has a way of indicating whether a thing is known or only believed. It is the language of courtship, of grief, of the negotiation of a sap-cut, of a mother telling a child what the child has done wrong. The consequence is that the people of the table are loud about small things and silent about large ones. Two of them arguing about who dropped a basket will fill a plaza. Two of them falling in love will make no sound at all, and you would not know it was happening unless you saw their hands.

They touch. Constantly, without comment. A hand on a shoulder while passing, a palm flat against a back while speaking, children carried until they are far too old for it and then carried anyway. They sleep in heaps. There is no privacy on the table and no word for it, and the absence is not felt as a lack. Solitude is a thing that happens only at the rim, and only briefly, and only to the young.

---

That is a rite, of a kind, and it is the only one that is done alone.

At some point in the years when a child begins to become something else, they go to the edge. Not sent — no one tells them to go — but every one of them does, and every adult remembers doing it. They push out through the last twisted columns and stand on the bare polished stone with the roar at their back and the nothing in front of them, and they stay there, alone, until they can hear their own breath.

It takes a long time. The colony reaches all the way to the rim; there is no true silence anywhere on the table. But if you stand long enough, and the wind is right, there is a moment when the roar behind becomes a wall rather than an ocean, and in front of the wall, very small, is the sound of your own body going on. Most of them weep when it happens. Some of them are sick. A few — every generation has a few — sit down on the mirror-stone and do not want to come back in, and someone has to go out and get them, and those ones are watched afterward, gently, for the rest of their lives, because the rim has taken hold of something in them and everyone knows it.

No one has ever gone over. That is what the people say, and it may be true. The birds do not go over either.

---

Their days are the waiting and the maintenance already described, but there is a texture to the waiting that takes time to see.

The wall-walkers are the nearest thing the town has to authority. There are always four or five of them, chosen young by the old ones for a certain stillness of hand, and they spend their lives moving along the outside of the town with a lever of dried pith and a stone maul, pressing loosened blocks home. They know every course. They know which blocks were set by which generation, by the dressing of the edges, and they speak of the deep founders — the setters of the giant lowest courses — with something that is not quite reverence and not quite grief. *They were bigger than us*, the wall-walkers say, and shrug, and it is not clear whether they mean it literally. The people believe, in a general way, that everything was once larger: the mountain, the pillars, themselves. Each generation is a little smaller and a little less skilled and this is simply the direction of time, the way water goes into the pillars and does not come out. It is not a sad belief. It is just the shape of things.

The sap-drawers are a smaller group and older. It is they who keep the chips of pale stone that mark the cuts, and they who hold in memory — for nothing is written; there is nothing to write on — which pillar was cut in which year by whose hand, and which are ready. A sap-drawer will tell you that a particular column was last opened by their grandmother's grandmother and that the lip has almost sealed and that in perhaps twelve years it may be touched again. They are the closest thing the table has to a calendar. When one of them dies, a certain amount of time dies with them, and the others spend months afterward going from pillar to pillar, touching the scars, trying to recover what was lost.

The tray-tenders dry the guano and gather the moth-fat and keep the small pale fires. They are mostly the very old and the very young, working together, and the pillar-shadowed corners of the town where the trays are laid out are where the stories get told — shouted, or signed if they matter.

And there are the listeners. They have no other work. They sit in the plaza, or on the flat roofs, and they attend to the colony, and when the sound changes they say so. A shift in the keeners means the moths are hatching. A silence in the north wall — a silence being only a slight thinning — means a block has slipped and the birds have left the niches near it. A rising in the whole roar toward dusk, earlier than usual, means the haze is thickening and the night will be warm. The listeners are the table's weather and its warning and its news, and they are held in a kind of affectionate suspicion, because they hear things no one else does and are sometimes wrong.

---

The old are the measure of time and are treated accordingly.

An old person on the table has seen perhaps two pillars fall, perhaps three houses roofed, perhaps one cut on the central sap-wall opened and sealed. They remember which niches held which sounds forty years ago. They are carried, when they can no longer walk the sliding lizard-step, in a sling between two of the young, and they are set down in the plaza where they can hear everything and touch anyone who passes, and they are asked things, and they answer with their hands.

When they die — and they die dry, in the way of the table, going thin and papery and quiet rather than swelling — they are taken to the rim. Not over it. They are laid on the polished stone at the edge, wrapped in nothing, and left for one turning of the haze, from tea to honey, and the wind does what it does. When the body is fully dry it is brought back in.

The bones are used. There is no metal on the table and no clay and no wood worth the name, and bone is the hardest, finest material the people have. The long bones become awls and levers and the pegs that hold roof-poles. The ribs become the blades for cutting sap — a thin, sharp, slightly flexible blade, better than stone for the delicate work of opening a rib a finger's width at a time. A sap-drawer's kit is made entirely of sap-drawers. This is not thought of as grim. It is thought of as continuity, as a hand going on doing the work it knew.

The skull is set into the wall. There are niches for this among the niches for the birds — a little larger, a little more carefully dressed at the edges — and the town's walls are studded with them, thousands over the ages, most so old that the bone has gone the color of the quartzite and cannot be told from it. And the birds move in. Of course they do. A skull in a warm niche is a fine dry nest. So the dead of the table lie in the walls with birds in their eyes, and the walls hum with them, and the people put their palms to the stone in passing and feel the trembling and do not distinguish — would not understand the demand to distinguish — between the living sound and the dead housing.

---

What do they believe? Less than you would think, and more simply.

They believe the table was a mountain and is not anymore. They believe the cloud below is the floor of the world and that nothing lives in it, because nothing comes up out of it. They believe the founders were larger, and that the pillars hold everything that has ever been given up — every drop, every breath — and that this is right. They do not have a story about the sky. The sky is what the light comes through and it has no face. They do not pray. When they want something from the table they wait for it, and when the table wants something from them they give it, and the transaction does not require a third party.

If you asked them who lives on the table, they would not, at first, understand the question. Not because it is difficult but because it is plural. The hand-sign for *here* — a flat palm pressed down, once, against whatever surface is nearest — is the same as the hand-sign for *us*, and it has been for as long as anyone has been counting, which is not very long, and also forever. The pillars, the roar, the lizards underfoot, the skull with a keener nesting in it, the child weeping at the rim, the old one carried into the plaza, the block pressed home, the bead of sweetness swelling on the bone blade: *here*. *Us*. One word, pressed flat.

It is warm in there, and crowded, and it goes on and on, and the thing that lives there is the going-on.
