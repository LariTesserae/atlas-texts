**The Census**

You try to take a census, and the census fails inside the first hour.

Not because there is too much—though there is—but because counting requires that the thing counted be the same thing at the end of the count as at the beginning, and here that is the one condition never met. You count eleven of the low many-legged shapes in a hollow, and by the time you have finished saying *eleven* there are seven, and a stone, and a place in the felt that is slightly warmer than the felt around it. You start again. You try kinds instead of numbers—*the ones with the salamander gait, the ones in the trunks, the ones that hang in the fog*—and the kinds bleed into each other while you are still naming them, so that you find yourself describing a single animal with four descriptions and are not sure at which point it stopped being the first and became the last.

So you give up on *what is here*. It is the wrong question. Everything is here, briefly. The right question is smaller and harder, and it takes you a long time to find the words for it, sitting on a warm slab with the drip landing on your shoulders.

Not what is here. What *stays*. What, of all this flicker, has managed to live on the shelf rather than merely occur on it.

That question has answers. They arrive slowly, and not in the order you expect.

---

The first thing you would name, if pressed, is the felt.

It is the only thing you have never seen change. The trunks move; the cliffs refold; the products come and go like weather. The felt is simply there, on everything, the same dark wet nap in every hollow and on every face, and where a product is taken back the felt closes over the place within a minute and the place is felt again as though it had never been anything else. It does not flicker. It does not become. It grows, but growth is not the same as revision, and it grows only one way—across, and over, and onto.

You begin to think of it as the one native. Everything else came up out of the rock or was pushed out of the works; the felt was waiting when the rock arrived. You cannot prove this. You only notice that when you try to imagine the shelf before the felt, you cannot, and when you try to imagine the felt before the shelf you can, easily: a dark warmth with nothing yet to cover.

Then you press your palm into it, as you did on the first day, and feel the blade underneath, and something in the picture shifts. The felt is not on the shelf. The felt is what the shelf is *wearing*. And a skin is not a resident. A skin is the thing residents are inside of. Whatever lives here lives in the felt, or under it, or is dissolved into it and grown out of it again—the felt is the medium, the bench, the wet cloth the products are laid on and taken up from. It is the most alive thing here and it is not an inhabitant at all. It is the room.

You set it aside. Not dismissed. Set aside, the way you set aside the air.

---

The fauna, then. The obvious ones. The dog-sized things and the bark-swimmers and the hanging clusters near the vents.

You spend a long time with these and come to a conclusion that feels like a betrayal of them: they do not live here. They *happen* here. Most of them last less time than it takes the felt to close a hollow. You watch one—the long damp salamander-shape with the mole's coat—for as long as it lets you, and what you watch is a subtraction. First the bird goes out of the head. Then the coat goes to scale, then the scale goes to something smoother, then the limbs shorten and the tail thickens and the whole animal, without ever hurrying, lies down and is a curl of warm dark stone with the faintest suggestion of a spine. It does not die. Nothing has ended. The shelf has simply stopped saying that particular sentence.

They are drafts. You knew that already; the ground told you. But watching it happen tells you something else, which is that the drafts are *trying*. Not deliberately—there is nothing in them to deliberate—but in the way a dropped thing tries to find the floor. They stop when you stop. You understood this at first as caution. It is not caution. It is imitation. You are the most persistent thing on the shelf: you have held one shape for the whole time you have been here, and nothing else has, and the drafts have noticed. When you stand still, the ones nearby stand still, and hold, and for a moment they are more themselves than they were. When you move they lose the thread. You are teaching them to persist, and they cannot learn it, and they keep trying.

This is unbearable for a while. Then it is simply what the understory is like.

---

Some of the drafts eat.

You notice this late, because eating here is so quiet. A thing with too many legs stops beside a fist-sized product in the felt and stays there and when it moves on the product is a different product, smaller, and the many-legged thing is very slightly larger, or has one more leg, or the same number of legs arranged differently. No tearing. No sound. Contact, and revision, and departure.

The eaters last longer than the drafts that do not eat. You are sure of this after a few days—you see the same eater, or an eater with the same manner, in the same run of hollows, and it has changed but it is still there. And you think you understand why. Eating, here, is what the shelf does. Take a form, alter it, release it altered. An eater is a small local instance of the works, running the works' own process on a scale of centimeters. The shelf has not built them for this—nothing is built here—but it tolerates what does its labor, the way a current tolerates the things that swim with it. The eaters are not fighting the revision. They are performing it. And so the revision passes through them more gently, or takes longer to arrive.

You come to think of them as the shelf's apprentices, and then correct yourself, because an apprentice is being taught and these are not. They are more like eddies. The stream turning, in a small place, on itself.

---

There is something in the canopy.

You never see it. You see the second lid of leaves bunch and slide and fold, and you assume, on the first day, that this is many things—birds, or what passes for birds, moving among the crowns. By the fourth day you no longer think so. The movements are too continuous. A shrug that begins above one trunk travels, unbroken, across two hundred meters of canopy and ends above another. When one of the great pistons sinks below you and exhales, the leaves overhead lift a half-second afterward, all together, the way a sleeper's chest lifts. When it rises again they settle. Something up there is *breathing with the ground*.

You try climbing. The trunks are smooth and the felt on them gives under your hands and you get perhaps four meters before the shelf, with no malice, tilts the trunk a few degrees and you are on the ground again in the ferns with the drip in your face. You do not try again. You lie in the ferns and watch the leaves move and let the picture assemble.

It is one thing. It is the size of the sky. It lies across the crowns of every trunk to the horizon in every direction, or the crowns are its underside and there is no it apart from them, and it is what collects. The leaves do not gather the light and the fog and pass them down—*it* does, and the leaves are its palms held up, and the beat you hear inside the trunks, the lift-and-pause of the chain hoist, is its swallowing. It is the resident you cannot check on. You will never see its face, if it has one, and you are almost certain it has not, because a face is a place where attention concentrates, and this thing does not concentrate. It is spread thin over the whole works, taking in the lid's weather, letting it down. It is the shelf's largest tenant and it is quite possibly not aware that it is a tenant, or that there is anything below it, or that the ground it breathes with is ground.

You call it the roof, in your head, and then stop calling it anything.

---

In the black water there are turners.

You see them on the second or third day, lying flat at the rim of a basin with the foam trembling an inch from your face and the dim bruise-colored light coming up from the bottom of the vortex. Between you and the light, arranged around the turning, there are shapes. You take them for fish. Then for leaves, caught in the current. Then—because they are translucent and the light comes up through them and they have no eyes and no ends you can find—for rings, or for a single ring, broken into segments, riding the slow rotation the way a bearing rides its race.

You watch for an hour and your understanding of them does not settle, and you realize, eventually, that it is not going to, and that this is not a failure of your attention. They are not a thing in the water. They are the water's edge given a little body. Where the vortex meets the still water there is a boundary, and the boundary is under strain, and the shelf has—as it does everywhere, with everything—tried out a form there, and this is the form that held: a translucent lip along the shear, alive enough to keep its place, no more. They do not swim. They are not carried. They are the seam between the turning and the not-turning, made just barely into creatures so that the seam will hold.

They will last as long as the basins last. Which is to say, as long as the shelf turns. You do not think they know they are alive, and you are no longer sure the distinction is one the shelf recognizes.

---

And then the watchers. You have been avoiding them, in the way that you avoid looking directly at the thing you most want to understand.

They sit at the rims of the basins and follow the turning with their heads. Heavy-bodied, hide the color of new rock, faces you cannot resolve. On the first day you saw two. By the end of the first week you have counted, or failed to count, dozens, and the reason you failed to count them is that a good number of the outcrops along the basin rims are not outcrops. You put a hand on one—a rounded shoulder of dark stone, warm as everything is warm—and it turned, very slowly, and the turning had a head in it, and the head continued to follow the water as if your hand were rain.

They are the most stable things here. You knew that. What you did not know, and learn by sitting beside one for most of a day, is *why*, and the why is not what you supposed. You supposed they had given up being anything in particular, and so the shelf had nothing to revise. That is half of it. The other half is that they are not watching the water. They are watching what is under the water—the dim light at the bottom of the vortex, the same color as the lid—and you understand, sitting there with the drip on your shoulders, that this light is the one place on the shelf where the works shows through. Everywhere else it is felt and fern and product. Here, at the bottom of the turning, it is just the glow. The watchers have found the one place the shelf is not pretending to be anything, and they sit there, and look at it, and want nothing, and the shelf leaves them alone.

Or not quite alone. Because the ones that have been there longest are harder to tell from the rim. Their hides are stonier. Their heads turn less. There is one—you are almost sure—that no longer turns at all, and is warm, and is a rock, and is still a watcher, in the sense that it faces the water and has never faced anything else. They are not resisting the revision. They are being revised in one direction only, very slowly, toward the one form the shelf never takes back: ground.

That is what living here means, you begin to think. Not keeping a body. Nothing keeps a body. Living here means finding the slowest possible way to become rock.

---

Near what you have been calling the edge, among the trunk-sized unfinished things leaning in the dark of the passage, you find a product you were not ready for.

It is on the ground, half in the felt, the length of your forearm. It is a limb. Not an arm; the shelf does not make arms. A limb, jointed twice, and at the end of it a spread of five or six or four slender pieces that close, and open, and close, and that are—there is no other word—*grasping*. Not at anything. At the air. Opening and closing on nothing at the fastest rhythm you have seen anything move on the shelf.

And it is flickering. Faster than any draft. It is stone, it is root, it is something with a wet gray hide, it is stone, it is root; the pieces at the end are twigs, are claws, are the fronds of a fern curling, are fingers, are twigs. It cannot settle for a second together. The shelf is saying it and unsaying it in the same breath.

A watcher sits a few meters off at a small black basin. As you stand there, it turns its head. It is the only time in all your days here that you have seen one look away from the water. It looks at the limb, and you cannot read its face because it has no face to read, but its head stays turned toward the thing for a long moment, and then it turns back to the vortex, and does not look again.

You understand this as well as you understand anything here. The shelf can make a stone, and a beast, and a leaf, and hold them for a while. It cannot hold a hand. A hand is the one product whose nature is to *make*—to take what is, and arrange it, and leave the arrangement—and the shelf has no place for that. Everything here occurs; nothing is made. The works will throw up a hand a thousand times because the works throws up everything, and a thousand times the hand will be the first thing it takes back, because a hand held for even a minute would begin to build, and a built thing would be a thing the shelf could not revise, and that is the one condition it cannot live under.

Whatever lives here, that does not. That is the thing that does not live here. You had it wrong on the first day—you thought the shelf had never known a hand. It knows them constantly. It just will not let them stay.

---
