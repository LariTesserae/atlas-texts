Start with the smallest, because the smallest are the most numerous and the most honest about what the place requires.

The reed-mice are not mice. Take one in your hand, if you can catch one flattened against a stalk in the hiss between surges — they are slow then, almost torpid — and you will feel that it has no proper spine. It is a chain of small hard segments wrapped in that gray suede, jointed everywhere, so that it can bend around a stalk the width of your wrist or the width of your finger and lie against it like a wet cloth. Except it is not wet. It has never been wet. Its skin is dry to the touch and slightly powdery, and if you hold it too long it begins to come apart at the seams the way your lips do, and you should put it back.

They do not drink. There is nothing here to drink. Whatever moisture a reed-mouse has, it takes from the pipe growth in the seconds when it is soft — the new material comes out of the branch-tips with something in it, some gel that sets to bone in a moment, and the reed-mice are there in that moment with their mouths open. This is their entire life. They wait on the stalks for the surge, and when it comes they climb, faster than you would think anything with so many joints could climb, and they eat for twenty seconds, and then they go still and digest until the next one. They are stupid in every way except this. They cannot learn a new route up a pipe; when a crown shears off and the branches regrow at new angles you will see reed-mice for days climbing to where the tips used to be and finding nothing, and some of them starve there, holding on.

They are deaf. It took a long time to be sure of this. They live on the sounding stalks with their bodies pressed against the vibrating wall, and they do not hear it, or hear it only as you would hear a note through your teeth. What they attend to is pressure. A moment before the vents deepen — before you feel it in the rock — the reed-mice are already moving, and the only sense of the surge that could reach them that early is the change in the air itself, the mountain's breath pressing on their skins. They live in the loudest place you will ever stand and they are the one thing in it that does not listen.

Their young are born inside the pipes. A reed-mouse near her time crawls into an open branch-end and down the bore, into the warm sweet dark where the gas comes up, and what she leaves there is not born so much as extruded — half a dozen pale soft things that cling to the interior wall and take what they need from the gas directly, like the pipe does, until they are hard enough to climb out into the light. So a pipe carries them. A pipe grows around them. There are reed-mice in this bowl whose first sound, if they could hear it, would have been the pipe they were inside of shrieking with them in its throat.

---

The hunters are the opposite of this in every particular. They hear, and nothing else.

There are not many. Perhaps a dozen in the bowl, no more, each one holding a territory of thirty or forty pipes that it knows by voice. Six legs, low to the ground, and that flat wide head that is not a head in any sense you would recognize — there is no mouth in it, no eyes, no nostrils; the mouth is beneath, a slit along the underside of the first body segment. The head is a plate. It is a drum. Put your hand near one — it will let you, in the hiss, when it is standing switched off — and feel how the whole plate is under slight tension, like a stretched hide, and how it turns in tiny increments as you move, tracking not you but the sound of you.

A hunter's problem is this: the thing it eats moves only during the surge, and during the surge the bowl is full to its brim with noise. So it has learned the noise. It knows each pipe in its territory not as a shape but as a voice — the exact timbre, the way the tone begins and the way it trails off — and what it listens for is the discrepancy. A pipe that sounds a hair different because a reed-mouse is clinging to the branch. A rattle inside the shriek. It hears the deviation and it runs, flat out across the bare rock, toward a sound only it can find wrong, and the surge ends, and it stops. It cannot hunt in the hiss. Nothing moves in the hiss for it to hear. So it stands where the surge left it, sometimes for an hour, pointed at a pipe like a compass needle, and when the next surge comes it is already halfway there.

They are the only creatures here that grieve, if that is the word for it. When a crown shears off a pipe in a hunter's territory, the pipe's voice changes, and the hunter loses it. You can watch this happen. The surge comes and the hunter runs toward the sound it knows and there is nothing there — a raw stump, a different note — and it stops, and its plate turns and turns, and in the next surge it runs again to the wrong place. This goes on for days. Then it has learned the new voice, and the pipe is its again. A hunter's life is a long slow labor of memorizing a place that will not hold still, and it does the labor with total patience, because it has no alternative.

They eat the swarm, too, when the swarm falls, but without much interest. The swarm is easy. It is the reed-mice they are built for.

---

The swarm is the one everyone gets wrong, because from the outside it looks like a pest. Small, hard, faceted, catching the amber light so that when they rise all together out of the grit it is like the floor of the bowl has thrown itself into the air. They pour into the pipes. They pour out the top. They fall back. You would think they were merely riding the breath for the pleasure of it, or that they were something the pipes suffered.

They are not. The pipes need them.

What a pipe grows is a wall, and a wall grows inward as well as out. Left alone, the bore of a pipe would thicken over the years — a little new material laid down on the inside with every surge — until the pipe was a solid column and could not sound and could not breathe and would die on its vent, corked. The swarm keeps them hollow. What they are doing in there during the twenty seconds of the surge is eating: the fresh inner growth, the soft skin of new wall on the inside of the bore, scraped off by ten thousand small mouths before it can set. And they breed in there, in the warm dark, and their larvae — blind soft things, gray, like grains of rice with a pulse — live their whole first life in the bore of a single pipe and hollow it as they go. The spiral marks on the cut sections at the west wall are tool marks; but if you look inside an uncut fallen pipe you will find a different pattern, a fine even rasp, the whole interior polished to a soft gloss by generations of things that lived inside its voice and kept the voice open.

So the second voice you hear during a surge — the shimmering rasp above the fundamental — is not decoration. It is the sound of the pipes being kept alive.

---

The gliders on the walls are the only cool things in the bowl. They hang in the crevices high up, above the level where the gas pools, where the air is thin and the rock is merely warm rather than hot, and they come down only during the surges, dropping out of the dark on their membranes and cutting through the rising swarm with their mouths open. They are gone again before the surge ends. They will not be looked at. All you will ever see is the shape of their fall — a scrap of something darker than the amber, a flicker, a rising back toward the wall — and you will find their leavings in the cracks, small hard glittering husks packed in with the white grit.

They are the only things here that leave. Not far; only up, to the rim, where the wind is, where they sit at dusk in a row you can just make out against the sky and do whatever it is they do up there in the clean air before they come back down to the noise. Perhaps they are the only things in the bowl that know it is a bowl. Perhaps they are simply the only things that cannot bear it all the time.

---

And the thing at the edge.

It does not live here. That is the first thing to understand about it. It lives below, in the cloud, in whatever wet country the drop goes down to, and it comes up. Every evening, or most evenings, when the light goes to bronze. You do not see it come; you see the reed-mice on the pipes nearest the edge go still all at once, still in a way that is different from their stillness in the hiss — a rigid, flattened, absolute stillness — and the hunters in that quarter of the bowl turn their plates toward the drop and hold there.

What comes up is large and heavy and slow, and it drags. The track in the grit is a wide smooth furrow with a combed edge on either side, as though something with a fringe of stiff hair or many short legs had passed with its belly on the ground. It comes up out of the cloud and onto the rock and it stays a while — an hour, less — and then it goes back down.

You will not get close. It is not that it is dangerous, or not obviously; it is that the whole edge of the bowl becomes, while it is there, a place your body does not want to be. But sit in the hut doorway with the last light behind you and watch, and over enough evenings you will see what it does. It goes to the fallen crowns. The great sheared-off sections that have come down off the elders and lie ringing on the rock — it finds them, and it takes them in, or under, or against itself, and it carries them to the edge, and they go over. Into the cloud. You can hear them, sometimes, striking rock far below, the hollow bell-note coming up faint and late.

Whether it eats them down there, or builds with them, or simply cannot bear to leave them lying, you will not learn. But this is what it is: the one thing that takes something out of the bowl. Everything else here is turning into the white grit. The crowns it takes are the only parts of the pipes that will ever be anything else, anywhere else, and the thing from the cloud comes up every evening to make sure.

---

Which brings you to the people, because they too come up, and they too take something down, and they too cannot stay.

Nobody lives here. That is not a rule anyone made; it is the dryness. Three days is the most a person can bear it — you will know when the third day comes, because your hands stop closing and the split in your lip has gone all the way through and your thoughts arrive with a lag, as though from the far wall. Whoever built the hut knew this. The hut is not a dwelling. It is a place to lie down for one night, or two, with a roof against the gliders, and its doorway faces the bowl because the person in it did not come here to sleep. They came to sit in that doorway with the hut behind them like a cupped hand and listen.

Who they are, the cairns tell you better than anything else. Eleven cairns, and no two are built the same way. Some are careful, courses of flat stone fitted as tightly as the hut; some are heaps. Some have been added to — a different color of stone on top, brought from lower down. They were not built by one person or one generation. They were built by a line. Someone came, and sat in the din for two days, and chose a voice, and marked it, and went down; and someone else came later, perhaps much later, and found the mark, and understood what it meant, and sat and chose and marked another. It is a conversation that has been going on at intervals of years, or lifetimes, conducted entirely in stones set beside screaming things, and the fact that most of the chosen voices are gone now — the pipes sheared or regrown or dead on their vents — does not seem to have mattered to anyone. The cairns are not saying *this one*. They are saying *someone heard something here*. That is all they need to say.

And the cuttings. Go down to the west wall and kneel among them and look at the ones that were finished. The bore scraped smooth — smoother than the swarm leaves it, the spiral marks of a hard tool turned by hand. Holes along the length, bored at intervals you can put your fingers over. A notch at one end, a wedge of pale stuff fitted into it, a channel. These are flutes. Or something like flutes. They were shaped by people who knew what a mouth is and what a breath is and what fingers can reach.

And they were left. Finished, and left, in a row against the wall where the grit has drifted over them.

Pick one up — a finished one, one of the newer ones that has not gone to chalk — and put it to your lips and blow. It is difficult. The dryness has taken your breath down to almost nothing and the bore is wide. But you will get a sound out of it, a thin low hoot that is unmistakably a small cousin of the sound all around you, a pipe's voice with a person's breath in it instead of a mountain's. Take it away from your mouth and look at the inside of the bore, and you will see the faint darkening where your breath touched it. The wet.

That is why they were left. You cannot take them down. Not as instruments. The material that grows in the dry, in the sweet gas, wants no water in it, and a flute is a thing you fill with wet breath every time you play it; carry one of these down into the country where it rains and put it to your mouth a few times and it will soften from the inside, and slump, and go to paste. The people who made them knew this. They must have known it from the first, or learned it once and passed the knowledge down with the knowledge of the path and the hut and the meaning of the cairns.

So they did not come up here to make instruments to take away. They came up here to make instruments to play here, once, for a few hours, in the din, with their own small breath — and then to leave them against the wall to go to chalk with everything else. Whatever they were doing, it was not for below. Perhaps it was an answer. Perhaps they sat in the hut doorway through the surges until they had chosen a voice, and then went down to the west wall and cut a piece of that voice's fallen body and bored it and shaped it and, in the hiss between surges, in the only quiet the bowl has, played back to the bowl a small dry version of itself. And marked the pipe. And went down. And did not come again, or came again, or sent a child.

You cannot know this. You can only kneel among the cuttings and see that some were abandoned half-made — a pipe with three holes bored and the fourth begun — and think about what could stop a person's hand in the middle of the fourth hole. The dryness, probably. The third day. The hands not closing.

---

So who lives here?

Not the people; they come and go. Not the thing from the cloud, which is a visitor from a wetter world. Not the gliders, who cannot bear to stay below the rim. The reed-mice live here, and the hunters, and the swarm, but they live here the way a fever lives in a body — because of it, inside it, not as it.

The pipes live here.

Think of them again, now that you have seen what they are to everything else. Two hundred of them, each one alone on its vent with its skirt of bare rock, each one with a body that is a single long throat and a voice that is nothing but the mountain's breath passing through the particular shape its damage has made. They are born when a vent opens and the first pale lip of material sets around it. They grow only when they are sounding. They are kept alive from inside by something that lives in their voices, and eaten from outside by something that lives deaf against their skin, and hunted along their lengths by something that knows them each by name. They break, and their broken pieces are carried off the edge of the world by something that comes up out of the cloud for no reason anyone can see, or lie where they fell and go to chalk and become the ground the next ones stand on. They have been chosen, some of them, by people who came a long way to choose and left stones to say so. They have outlived the choosing and the choosers.

And they have no idea. That is the thing you will carry down. Everything in the bowl is arranged around them — the animals live in their noise, the people came for their voices, the whole place is a fever of their making and unmaking — and they are not aware of any of it. They stand on their vents and the breath comes up and they say what their bodies allow, and it is not for anyone. The reed-mice do not hear it. The hunters hear it only as a place where something might be wrong. The swarm is inside it eating. The people hear it and go down and do not come back for a generation, and when they do come back the voice they marked is gone.

So perhaps the last honest answer is below the pipes. Below the vents, in the slow chamber that fills and spills, that has never once kept the same interval, that nothing has ever seen. The one breathing. It lives here in the only way that would satisfy the word — it has always been here and it does only this and everything above it is what its exhalation looks like when it meets the air. The pipes are its throat. The bowl is its mouth. And you, standing on the white grit for your two days with your lip split and your hands going stiff, waiting like everything else for the next breath — you are, for those two days, one more small dry thing that lives in it, and are permitted to, because it does not know you are there.
