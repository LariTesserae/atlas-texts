At first you think nothing does. You think the place is inhabited the way a cliff is inhabited: by whatever can hold on, none of it at home. The birds don't sleep here. The lizards are the room's temperature and go where the temperature goes. The moss lives here in the sense that moss lives anywhere, which is to say it lives on the place rather than in it.

Then you notice the valve.

Every other iron surface at that height carries moss. The stem of the wheel-valve is bare — not scoured, not scraped, but *worn*, a bright band a hand's breadth wide where something has closed around it so regularly that the moss has stopped trying. The rim of the wheel has the same bright groove you saw on the stair rail. The same thing wore both.

Then the catwalk. You look up through the green lace, and there's a place where the hanging fringe has been parted, and behind the parting the plate is a different colour from the plates either side — darker, rubbed through the rust to something almost polished, and the dark patch has a shape. It's about the size of a body curled on its side.

Then the body.

They're on the plate. That's why you didn't see them at once: they are the colour of the room. Cloth gone to the orange-brown that everything goes to here, hair matted to the same, one cheek flat against the iron the way the lizards lie, and two lizards lying against them, in the crook of a knee and the hollow behind an ankle, sharing the warmth. They don't look up when you reach the top of the ladder. Nothing here is heard. They look up when your weight comes onto the catwalk grating and the hum under their cheek changes.

You think: keeper. Then you look at them for a while and think: no.

It's hard to tell how old. The wet has done to them what it does to everything up here — softened, paled, loosened. The skin has the creased translucence of a thing that has lived under a stone. The palms and the knees and the heels of the hands are stained rust into the creases and will stay that way; you could scrub them for a year. The soles of the feet are bare and grey and thick as hoof. They look at you the way the lizards look at you, which is to say without alarm and without much interest, and then their eyes go past you, to the grating and the dark under it, because that is where everything here looks.

Here is the first thing to understand about them, and it takes a while, because it's the opposite of what you'd assume: to them, this room is silent.

The roar did what a roar like that does. It took the first season, perhaps two. After that there was nothing — not the sound reduced, not the sound muffled, but the whole sense gone, closed like a door. And what was left was what is under the sound: the hum in the teeth, the tremor in the tread, the shudder in the plate. The room stopped speaking to them through the ears and went on speaking through the bone, and it is the only thing that still does. They know a bird has landed on the rail by the change in the rail. They knew you were coming three hundred steps before you arrived, because the stair is bolted to the shaft and the shaft carries everything, and a body climbing is a rhythm in the iron that the room doesn't make by itself. They lay with their cheek to the plate and counted you up.

They've lost words the way you did on the stair, but for good. If you put a hand on their shoulder they'll put one on yours, and that's the whole grammar. They may take you to the wheel. They'll turn it a quarter, and turn it back, and look at you — not to show you it does something. To show you that it's done.

Because that's the day. When the bars of grey come through the louvres they go down the ladder, and turn the wheel a quarter one way. When the bars fade they turn it back. They can't say why and don't try. The paint was red once; someone thought it should be turned; it seems to them that it should still be, since it's here and so are they. And once, in the middle of the day, they go to the bell. They take it by the shoulder and swing it hard against the bracket and hold their hand there, and feel the shudder come up through the palm and the wrist and go on and die, and only when it has entirely died do they let go. That's what they have instead of a voice. No one hears it. They didn't either, even the first time.

They eat the birds. This is easier than it sounds. Stand at the catwalk rail with a hooked stick and reach into the column and you can take a bird out of the air the way you'd take fruit off a branch; the birds hang in the breath with their wings set and have never learned that anything up here is a threat, and don't learn, because nothing else has ever tried. When the birds are gone for their weeks, lizards. There are always lizards, and they don't run. The shoots of the broad-leaved thing on the ladder, which are bitter and green and eaten regardless. Water off the roof, caught in a bowl that was the housing of a lamp before someone unscrewed it. And what's left by those who come up — bread, which is soft by the second day; dried meat; once a knife, which they kept and use; once a book, which the wet took in a week and the moss has now, on the catwalk, a green brick with corners.

They've taken the lean. You notice it when they stand. The shoulders are turned a few degrees toward the grating, as the ferns are, and the head is tilted so that one ear — the ear that hears nothing — is kept faithfully toward the dark, toward the source of the nothing. When they lie down it's on the side facing in. The moss is thickest on the sound-facing side of every rivet head, and their hair is thickest on the sound-facing side of the skull, matted there where it presses the plate, and after a while you stop thinking of that as a coincidence.

The animals have accepted them. That's not quite the word. The animals have failed to notice them in the particular way the room allows: they make no sound anyone can hear and haven't, in years, done anything sudden, and so the lizards clatter over their legs and the birds drop close enough to touch and the insects hatch out of the rust-soil against their skin. They're the warmest thing in the room after the plate. The lizards know this and are not grateful and don't need to be.

They went down once. Not far — to the second landing, where the frost starts and the stair's bolts leave the plate and go into rock. And there the hum left their feet. The iron plate sings; the rock doesn't. They stood on the landing with nothing in their ears and nothing in their soles and the cold coming up through the treads, and understood something they had known and not let themselves know: that the mountain below, and the whole world under the mountain, is silent to them now and will be for the rest of their life, and the one place that still says anything is the room above. They climbed back up into it the way you come up out of water. They haven't gone down since. The world for them is sixty paces across, roofed with louvres, warm, and everything outside it is a held breath that never ends.

They weren't the first. When they came, the band on the valve stem was already bright. The plate on the catwalk was already dark, already shaped. Someone was up here before them and had gone, one way or another, and left the print of a body in the rust, and they lay down in it, and it fit well enough. They think about this sometimes, insofar as they think — that the shape will be empty again, or won't be, and that the moss will come over whatever's in it and measure it the way it measures everything. That whoever comes up next will find a bright band on the stem and a parting in the fringe and a dark curved place on the plate, and know without words where you lie.

It's not clear what they're for. They don't tend the garden; it doesn't want tending. They don't guard the shaft; nothing has ever come up it. They turn a wheel that does nothing and ring a bell that reaches no one, in a room that has abolished the whole category of small noises — and after a while that's what you understand them to be. Not the keeper. One of the small noises. A clatter on the grating that nothing runs from. Something that came up the stair once, meaning to go back down, and found a warm plate and lay on it, and reached terms.

When you go, they may come to the lip and put a hand on the rail. They'll feel you down it — each tread a lessening in the palm, the rhythm of you getting fainter in the iron — until it's only the roar in the rail again, and then not even that, only the hum. Then they'll go back to the plate. Cheek to the iron. The two lizards will find the crook of the knee and the hollow of the ankle. The room will say, up through the bone, the one thing it says, and they'll lie there and listen to it, as they have for a long time, as they will until the moss says otherwise.
