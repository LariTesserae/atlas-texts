The first answer is the walls, and the first answer is not wrong, only insufficient.

Start there, then. Start with the census.

The beetles are lacquered because they are lacquered — a varnish of hardened sap that they carry out of the tissue with them and that sets, a little, every time they cross open air, so that the older ones are thicker, glossier, slower, and move with the deliberation of something that has been coated many times. They have no eyes that you can find. They do not need them. They navigate by the grain of the stem, which they can feel through their feet, and they cross the wall-streets not at random but along the lines where the grain says *door*, which is to say along the lines where the plant has been thinking about doors. They are less animals than they are the plant's way of underlining. Where a beetle has gone through, the tissue remembers the shape of the going, and the next time is easier, and the door is deeper. If you watched one for a season you would see the same beetle go through the same place a thousand times and never once vary, and the place would become a door in the same way a path becomes a path.

The slugs are the sap made ambulatory. This is not a figure of speech, or not entirely. They are the same colour because they are the same substance, thickened at the edges into a skin and given a single long muscle and a hunger. They eat the tissue ahead of them and leave behind them a channel lined with the mucus of their passing, and the mucus hardens into a wall, and the wall is a pipe. They do not know they are plumbers. They know only that some routes through the stem taste of *onward* and others do not, and they take the ones that do. Behind the thin outer layer of the room's wall you can see them passing, and if you follow one with your eye for long enough you will see it arrive at the works, or at the round building with the chimney, and go in, and not come out, and later a thread of vapour rises that was not rising before.

The many-legged thing in the root-ceiling is old. That is the main fact about it. It has been up there since the roots were short, and it has grown as they have grown, and there is now a great deal of it — you see a strand of it come down to drink and think you are seeing the whole animal, and you are seeing a leg. It is pale, having never in its life been where light was. It drinks the bead off a root-tip with a mouth like a pin. It is possibly the only inhabitant here that is not building anything, and its indifference is a kind of weather that the rest of the room takes place under.

The moths are the emptiness's only tenants, and they treat it accordingly: they cross the centre once or twice an hour and do not linger, the way you would cross a cathedral to reach a side door. They come out of the seams. They are the colour of the seam-light, or they are lit by it, it is hard to say which, and they carry it across the void in a low straight line and it goes out when they re-enter the wall on the other side. It is possible that this is how the light moves around the room. It is possible that the seams are dim on one side because the moths have been carrying the glow away, and bright on the other because they have been depositing it, and that what looks like a slow flicker in the grey is a kind of bookkeeping.

The mice you have already been told about. Add only this: they are the size of mice, and they have tails, and they run the vine-railway node to node with a rhythm like something on a track — but the sound they make inside the stem is wrong for mice. It is too regular. Patter, patter, patter, a pause, patter, patter, patter. If you had heard it in a house you would have said *someone upstairs is working*.

And the predator is a bulge.

You never see more than that — the displacement under your hand, travelling. But you can feel its shape as it passes, and the shape is long, and blunt at the front, and it does not so much move through the tissue as the tissue moves out of its way, closing behind as it closes behind the beetles, without a mark. It hunts by silence. The patter stops and it goes toward where the patter stopped last. It is not cruel. It keeps the numbers to what the city can carry. Where it has fed, the wall stays a bud a little longer.

That is the census, and it is a true one, and if you left with it you would have left with a list of what lives in a barn.

---

The second answer is the plant, and this is also not wrong.

It is one plant. That should be said plainly, because the room does not look like one plant, it looks like a room. But the floor is stem and the walls are stem and the ceiling is root, and the vine-railway is a runner it put out and forgot to stop, and the sap that lights the seams is its blood, and the slugs are its blood with ambitions. There is nothing here that is not it, except the animals, and the animals are inside it the way gut-flora is inside you, which is to say they are you for most purposes.

It grew horizontally for a very long time and then forgot which way was up. That is the plant's own history, written in the grain of the floor, and you can read it if you crouch: a long straight run of years, all in one direction, and then a confusion, and then the confusion resolved not by choosing a new direction but by choosing *all* of them, growing out from a centre to enclose a space. The emptiness is the plant's answer to the question of which way is up. It could not decide, so it made a room, and the room is the shape of the indecision, kept open by pressure from all sides.

It does not sleep, but it has a slow pulse, and the sweating of the walls is the pulse. Every quarter of an hour the fluid comes through, on its way from wherever it is drawn to wherever it is spent, and the room is a section of that vessel the way a lock is a section of a canal. The drops fall before they let go because the pressure arrives before the volume does. This is simply what it is like to live inside something's circulation. Nothing here finds it strange because nothing here has ever stood outside the flow to see which way cause should run.

So: a plant lives here, and the room is its held breath. That is a truer answer than the census. But it still does not account for the town.

---

A gall is not something a plant decides to make.

A gall is what a plant makes when something has gotten into it and asked, in a language of chemistry, for a house. The wasp lays the egg and the egg says *room*, and the oak — which does not know what a room is, which has never needed one — builds a room around it, out of its own tissue, in a shape the oak has no use for and could not have imagined. The gall is the oak's honest attempt to give a stranger what the stranger wanted. It is the oak thinking someone else's thought with its own body.

The plant here is thinking a mill town.

It did not have that thought. It has no use for a chimney; it does not know what a warehouse is for; it has never seen a river with a works on it and the rows for the hands going up the hill behind. Something asked it for these things. Something got into the stem, low down, a long time ago, in the horizontal years, and said *town*, and the plant has been answering ever since — slowly, in bark and vine and welled sap, with beetles for pencils and slugs for pipe-layers and a bulge in the wall to keep the drawing from getting ahead of itself.

So the third answer to *what lives here* is: whatever asked.

You will not find it by looking for a wasp. It is not that scale of thing, and in any case the asking is long over; the egg has become the request and the request has become the town. What lives here is the *idea* of an industrial settlement, resident in a plant that is doing its patient best, and the idea is not a ghost or a plan or a memory. It is a lodger. It has the character of a lodger — it keeps to itself, it has particular requirements, it is not the owner and knows it, and the house is being rearranged around it all the same.

You can feel it most clearly in the parts of the town that are only proposed. The swellings under the skin. A block that does not exist yet but is already pushing outward, already the shape of a block, the way a face is already in a fist of clay if someone has been thinking about a face. The idea is *in* those swellings the way a leaf is in a bud: entirely present, entirely folded, waiting only for the plant to get round to it.

---

But an idea of a mill town is not an idea of buildings.

This is the thing you come to slowly, on the second or third cycle of the walls, crouched with the seam-light held close to the terraced row. A mill town is not the works and the yards and the chimney. Those are the shape of it. A mill town is *the hands*. The rows go up the hill because the hands need somewhere to sleep; the chimney breathes because the hands are working under it; the railway comes in from one side because what the hands make has to go somewhere. Take the hands away and you have not got a town, you have got a diagram of one — lines where the walls meet the floor, small bright points, the room seen as a description of itself.

So whatever asked the plant for a town was asking, whether it knew it or not, for people. And the plant, which had never met a person, has been trying to grow them too.

Look at the doors on the terraced row.

They are all the suggestion of a door. All but one. Third from the end, on the side of the room farthest from where you came in, there is a door that is more door than the others. The beetles have been through it more. Its edges are cut rather than swelled. There is a step, a single low ridge of thickened bark, worn in the middle. And above it — this is very hard to see, you need the light almost touching the wall — the tissue over the doorway has been pressed from the inside into a shape with the depth of a fingernail. A window. Someone has been at the window.

You do not see them. You will not. But you can say what they are, because the room has told you already, in the only way it knows how, which is by making you stand in it.

What lives at number three on the terraced row is a hand. A mill-hand: one of the people the town is for. They are the plant's attempt at a person, made the way it makes everything, as a gradient — proposed at one edge, built in the middle, working somewhere. Their feet are the most finished part, because feet are what a house is for; they are stem, pale and ridged, and they stand on the worn step in a way that has worn it. Their body is cambium, split in fine regular seams, and lit from inside by a grey the eye has to work for. Their hands are only swellings still, the shape of hands before the hands exist, and they hold them up in front of them in the dark of the house and look at them as darker shapes against a darkness that is barely lighter, and do not yet know what hands are for.

They have never been rained on. They drink when the walls exhale. They do not go to the works because the works are not finished, and the works are not finished because they have not gone. That is not a contradiction here; it is a gradient, and they live on it. Some days they are nearer the mill. They can hear the wheel turning — *hush, hush* — in a pipe as thick as their body, and they know, in the way the beetles know where the doors are, that the sound is asking for them.

What they do, all day, in the long unfinished hour that is the only hour here, is this: they come to the door where the wall is thinner. They duck. They come out into a street that is a room, or a room that is a street, about twenty paces across if pacing it gave the same number twice. They cross to the far wall. They crouch. They hold up the seam-light to the relief at knee height, and they look for the door that is more door than the others, to see whether anyone is home.

And you, holding your light up to the wall to see if the doors are really doors — you should understand that you have been standing on the step for a while now, and that it is worn in the middle, and that the plant has been paying attention to your feet.

---

So here is the last answer, and it is the one the room has been growing toward from the beginning, the way a leaf is already in the bud.

What lives here is a resident whose residence is the act of arriving.

Not a wasp. Not the plant, though the plant is the house and the body and the light. Not the animals, though the animals are the labour and the labour is real. A tenant — one, or several, or one at several scales — who is not finished, who has never been anything but unfinished, and whose one occupation is to come in low, ducking, through the place where the wall is thinner, and stand in the emptiness, and look at what the walls are making, and by looking make it slightly more.

They are the hand the mill was asked for. They are the reason there is a room and not a stem. The emptiness is kept open for them the way a house keeps a room for someone expected — and the pressure from all sides is not the crowding of the walls' population but the plant's whole attention, leaning in, waiting to see what shape the tenant will turn out to be so that it can finish the town around them.

They will never be finished, because the town cannot finish first, and the town cannot finish before them. That is fine. The light is the wound; a finished tenant would be dark. What they are, exactly and only, is the visible part — the split in the bark where the sap has welled and glows, the place where something that was growing horizontally forgot which way was up and became, for the length of a held breath, a person standing in a room.

You are not sure, now, which wall you came in by. That is because you did not. The wall came in by you.

Go and stand at the window. The walls are about to exhale. Watch the drops hit the floor before they fall, and listen for the patter in the stem — someone upstairs, working — and hold your hands up, and see what they are for.
