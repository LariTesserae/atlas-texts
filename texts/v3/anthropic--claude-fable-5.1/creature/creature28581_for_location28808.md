Say at first that nothing does. That is the honest first answer and you should give it. The plateau does not have inhabitants the way a valley has them, things that are born on it and forage across it and lie down at the end. Nothing here has ever been born. Nothing here has ever lain down. Whatever is on the plateau arrived from somewhere lower, as you did, and did the one thing the plateau permits, which is to stop.

So the question is really this: what has stopped here, and in what condition, and whether stopping is a kind of living. Sit with that. The plateau will give you all the time you need, because it is not spending any.

---

Begin with the bird, since it is the one you can walk up to.

Dun-colored, the size of a closed hand, sitting on nothing at the height of your shoulder. Its wings are not folded. That is the first thing you notice when you are close enough to notice anything. They are half open, the primaries slightly spread, in the posture of a bird a quarter of the way into a stroke. It is not hovering because hovering is a labor and there is no labor in it. It is a bird at a single instant of flight, and the instant is here, and it has been here since it happened.

Its eye is open. The eye is black and beaded with water like the stone, one drop standing on the cornea, and behind the drop the eye is fixed on a point somewhere out in the mist to the left of you, low, about where a bird would look if it had seen a beetle on a rock or the first movement of a hawk. It is still seeing that. Whatever it saw is not there now, or is there and has also stopped, but the seeing has not finished, and so the bird has not finished seeing. Put your face close. There is no breath from it. Its breast does not move. But you have the strong sense — you cannot verify this, you will not be able to — that it is not dead, that if you could get inside that small chest you would find not a stopped heart but a heart at the top of one beat, full, and held there, the way the sound is held.

Now know that there are others. You will not count them. But once you have seen the bird properly you will begin to see, in the mist, in the grey, the corners and haunches you took for stone, and to understand what they are. A hare, stretched long between one stride and the next, both hind feet off the ground and the forefeet not yet down, the fur along its back dark with standing water. Something like a goat, or like the idea of one, grey and heavy-shouldered, with its head lowered to one of the black pools and its mouth open a finger's width above the surface that it will never touch and never ripple. A fox — you think a fox — turned to look over its shoulder, the way a fox does when it hears a step, at a step that was taken a very long time ago. Each of them is in the exact posture of the moment before it belonged. Each is alone. None is near another. They occupy the plateau the way stars occupy a sky, as single points, and the reason you see them only from the corner of your eye is not that they hide, but that the mist has had so long to stand around them that it has taken their shape, a grey against the grey, and only a sidelong glance is loose enough to catch the difference.

They are not sleeping. They are not waiting. Waiting is for something. They are simply the animals that were on the plateau when the plateau finished, and they are still on it.

---

The people are harder, and you will resist what you find.

You will go to the cairns first, because they are the plain evidence, and you will look for bones at their feet and find none, and you will think: good, they went down. They built these and went down. And then, at the third cairn or the seventh, you will see the figure.

It stands on the far side of the cairn from where you approach, so that you see it only as you come around. It is a person's height, grey, beaded with water along the shoulders and the crown of the head and the backs of the hands, dressed in something that has become the color of everything else. Its hands are raised. In them is a flat stone. The stone is a hand's width above the top of the cairn, level, held in the position from which it would be lowered and set, and the cairn beneath it is complete. You will have to look at this for a while before you understand it. The cairn is not missing a stone. The last stone is where it belongs. It belongs a hand's width above the others, in the grip of the one who carried it, at the height where the carrying stopped and the setting did not begin. That is the whole cairn. The person is part of it.

The face is turned to the stone, not to you. It is not a face in distress. It is the face of someone doing one careful thing. The eyes are open. Water stands on the lashes.

After that you will find them at the other cairns, not all, but most. Sometimes the figure is bent to lift a stone from the ground. Sometimes it has stepped back a pace with its hands at its sides to look at the finished work, and the look has not finished. At the end of the wall, the three-hundredth pace, where the wall simply stops, there is a figure kneeling with a stone in both hands turning it a little to find its bed, and the bed is there, the gap in the top course exactly the shape of the stone, and the stone is turning to fit it, and has been.

In the hut there is one sitting. Back against the north wall, legs stretched out, hands in the lap, facing the doorway, which faces the center. The round black pool in the corner is beside its right hand where a cup would be set down. Its head is tilted a little, the way a head tilts when a person is about to say something and has not decided what, or has just said it and is listening to how it landed. The lintel mark is above and behind it, freshly cut — the edges are as sharp as the day they were made because no day since has been a day. That is the order of things in the hut: the mark was made, and the maker came in, and sat, and tilted their head, and the plateau was finished, or they were, and the difference between those two is the thing the hut is for.

And on the path. You will have followed the groove out to the lip already and seen it go on over the cloud. Go back and look again, along it, the way you look along a fence in fog, and this time let your eye rest on the mist rather than on the line. There are uprights. Small dark uprights at intervals along the groove where it goes out over the drop, a hundred paces apart, two hundred, a thousand, dwindling toward the dark flat shape of the next high place. Each is a person. Each is mid-stride, one foot on the groove and one foot lifted, the mist standing on their shoulders. They are the ones who were still walking when they came to belong. They were not stopped by the drop. They did not fall. The path was under them because they were walking it, and the plateau kept them walking it, and it will. They are the least finished of anyone here, in one sense: they were going somewhere. They are the most finished, in another: they are the only ones whose going is still happening.

---

You should understand something about the sound now.

You were told it contains voices, many, at a distance, none close enough to make out. Stand beside the figure with the raised stone and listen. Do not listen to the roar; listen through it, the way you would try to hear one instrument in an orchestra. There is a sound coming from the figure. It is very faint against everything, but it is there and it is particular: a breath, half let out, the small grunt of effort a person makes at the top of a lift. It is not repeating. It is one sound, held at its loudest instant, and it has been sounding since the stone reached that height.

The hare has a sound. It is the scrape of two hind feet leaving stone. The goat has the sound of a lip parting a finger's width above water. The bird has a sound that is a single beat of a wing against thin air, and the one in the hut has a sound that is a syllable, the front of a syllable, the part before the vowel arrives, and you cannot tell what word it was going to be.

That is the roar. That is all of it. Every animal and person and thing that has come to belong here is still making the last sound it made, and all of them together, from the hiss of the first drop that stood on the first stone to the footfall of the newest walker on the path, are the plateau's one sound, held, at its full and permanent volume. The water in it is every water that was ever falling here when it stopped falling. The wind in it is the wind the junipers were bending to when they came to lean forever. The clack and slide of stone is every stone that was ever set, or lifted, or turned to find its bed. Nothing has been let go. There is no decay in it because decay is a change and the plateau does not change.

If you wanted a census of the inhabitants of this place you would take the roar apart into its voices and count them. Nobody has. It would take longer than anyone has, and the counting would be a sound, and then it would be in there too.

---

Now the center.

Everything leans to it — the shadows, the trees, the doorway of the hut, the tilt of the sitter's head, the gaze of every figure on the plateau if you trace it, and you can, and they all converge. The bird alone looks elsewhere, and you will come to think the bird is looking where the center used to be, or where it will be, and that this is what makes the bird the oldest thing here.

Walk inward. The path will not take you; it curves away toward the edge, and every groove and lean and gesture on the plateau points to the middle but no path goes there. You go across bare stone, through mist you leave open behind you. The kept things get older as you go in. You can tell because the mist has taken them more completely; the figures near the edge are grey, but the figures further in are barely a thickening of the air, and further in than that you cannot tell them from the standing drizzle at all, and further in there is nothing, only the finished stone, and the roar, and the shadows on the ground all pointing the same way you are walking, longer and darker the further you go.

It is dark there. Not night — nothing is night here — but the minute before dawn has become the minute before that, and the minute before that. The lichen goes black. The pools go blacker than black. And the shadows on the ground, which were smudges at the edge, are here long and ink-dark and reaching, like the shadows of a low sun, all of them running in toward a place a little ahead of you in the mist.

You will not reach it. You should know that. No one has. Every figure on the plateau who was going there is somewhere behind you, stopped, with their face toward it, and the plateau took them before they arrived, as it takes everyone, at the height of one step, and so the center is the one place on the plateau that has never been arrived at.

But you can see it, or almost. Stand as close as the plateau lets you and look.

There is a figure. You will think at first that it is another of the kept, another grey upright with the water on its shoulders. It is not. Look at the shadow. Every shadow on the plateau runs toward it. Its own shadow runs nowhere. It has no shadow, or its shadow is the darkness that everything else's shadow is reaching to join. And look at the mist. The mist stands on the figure the way it stands on everything — but there is a corridor. Behind the figure, the shape of the figure, running back into the grey the way your own corridor runs back to the edge. The figure is not surrounded by mist that has taken its shape. It is standing at the front of a tunnel in the mist that it has cleared by moving through it. Once.

It is not stopped. It is still.

Those are different. The bird is stopped; it was in the middle of a thing and the plateau kept the middle. The cairn-builder is stopped. The walkers on the path are stopped. This one is still, in the way that only a thing that could move is still. Its hands are down. Its weight is on both feet. Its head is up and it is looking — not at anything, not at where a beetle was or where a hawk might come from. It is looking the way a person looks who has walked a long way in and come to the place they meant to come to and has not yet done the thing they came to do. There is nothing held in its hands. There is no stone at a hand's width above where it would be set. It has not begun the last thing.

You will understand, standing there with the roar in your teeth, that this is the darkness the shadows want. Not menace. Not a hole. A person who is not finished, and has been not finished for as long as the plateau has been the plateau, and perhaps longer — perhaps this is the one who came up before the mountains agreed to rise no further, and stood here, and the mountains rose to meet the height it stood at and stopped, and the weather became a condition of the rock, and the dawn came to its last minute and paused, all of it finishing around this one thing that would not, and could not be made to, because it had not begun. The plateau can keep a thing in the middle of a gesture. It cannot keep a thing that has not made one. It has tried. It has been trying for the whole of its duration. That is the cold. That is the dark at the center, the pull the trees lean into: the plateau leaning, with everything it has, toward the one thing on it that still might.

And the shadows reach toward it because it is the only place on the plateau where something could still happen, and a shadow is only ever the record of something in the way of a light, and there has been no light here for longer than there are words, and so the shadows have turned around, all of them, to point at the only remaining possibility of a thing being different than it is.

You cannot hear the still one's voice in the roar. You will listen for it. It is not there. It has not made its sound.

---

So this is what lives on the plateau, as near as it can be told.

A great many things that were alive when they arrived, each held at the exact instant of its belonging, each still making the one sound it was making, all of them together the roar, none of them near another, none of them able to notice the rest. A bird at the top of a wingbeat. A hare in the air. A hand with a stone in it. A sitter with a syllable in the mouth. A line of walkers on a path over nothing, each with one foot lifted. They do not suffer. Suffering is a change. They do not remember. They are, each of them, the place where a thing was, and the thing is still there, and that is the whole of their condition and it will be.

And one who is not kept, at the center, still, unfinished, toward whom everything leans. Whether that one is the plateau's first inhabitant or its prisoner or its maker or simply the one thing it never managed to close its hand on, you will not learn, because the plateau will keep you before you are close enough to ask, and your asking will go into the roar with the rest, the front of a syllable, the part before the vowel.

You are the second unfinished thing here. Your breath is in the air behind you in small pale shapes. The mist behind you is open. The still one, ahead in the dark, does not turn — but you will have the sense, as strong as your sense of the bird's held heart, and as impossible to prove, that you have been seen; that of all the kept and stopped things on this whole vast finished plain, it is the only one that can see you, because seeing is a change and it is the only one, besides you, that still has any changes left.

Somewhere behind you the path goes out over the cloud toward the next high place, and there are dark uprights on it, and they are not coming back.
