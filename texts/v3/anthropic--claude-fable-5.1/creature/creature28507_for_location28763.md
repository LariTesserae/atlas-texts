What lives here is, first, a few hundred people who have never been dry.

That is the thing to hold onto before the rest, because it explains most of the rest. They are wet in the way the stair is wet — not soaked, not dripping, but always faintly filmed, so that a hand laid on a forearm slides a little before it grips. Their hair lies flat. Their clothes are few and thin and never last: leather goes green and soft and comes apart at the seams within a year, wool felts into board, cotton simply gives up. What they wear is mostly the town's own manufacture, the stiff sheets from the drying gallery cut and stitched into something between a smock and an apron, and even that is replaced often enough that nobody is attached to a garment. Their skin is pale in the way of things kept in cupboards, and marked. Everyone over the age of ten carries a lacework of small white scars on the palms and the outsides of the fingers from the fresh rock, and everyone has learned, without being taught, to touch the walls with the backs of the knuckles first. The oldest have hands like maps of a coastline. The youngest have hands that are still being written.

They do not sweat noticeably, because there is nowhere for sweat to go. They drink from the drip. There are cups left standing under the reliable places in the roof, and a person walking through the town will lift one, drink, and set it back under its drop without slowing, and by the time they pass that way again it is full. Nobody has ever been thirsty here. Nobody has ever been cold, either, except at the top of the stair, and the children speak of the cold up there the way children elsewhere speak of the dark — as the thing at the edge that you do not go into alone.

They are small-voiced. It is not that they are quiet people by temperament; it is that the cavern carries sound wrongly, throwing a word spoken in one room into three others, and two generations of being overheard have taught them to speak as if the listener were always closer than they are. Conversations happen at a murmur across gaps between buildings. A raised voice is a thing that stops work. When the engines exhale, everyone pauses, not out of reverence but because nothing said during the clank can be heard, and so the talk of the whole town has fallen into the engines' rhythm: four minutes of murmur, a breath, four minutes of murmur. People who leave and come back say it takes a week to stop pausing.

They tell time by that breath. There are no clocks — the wet ruins them — and no day, and the rota of the bulbs is too irregular to count by, so the stroke of the nearest engine is the unit. A task takes so many strokes. A child was born so many strokes after the last lamp went out. The largest engine, the one at the western end that the children call the Grandmother, has a slightly longer stroke than the others, and its clank arrives a few seconds behind the rest like a slow echo, and the very old use her instead, out of habit, so that two people in the same room will occasionally disagree about how long something took and both be right.

They are not, in the ordinary sense, a religious people, but they have a single ceremony, and it is the carrying. Once a year — the year is marked by nothing above, only by the wall's advance reaching the threshold of the front row — the whole town stops making whatever it makes and takes the front row apart. It is done by hand. There are no carts and could not be; the passages are too narrow and too stepped and too much a matter of going through people's rooms. So the stones go on backs, and the backs move in a line that threads the entire settlement from the heat to the dark, and for three or four days the town is not a town but a single slow animal passing itself through itself. The stones are warm when they are lifted. They are cold when they are set down. Children too small to carry stand in the doorways of the rooms the line passes through and touch each block as it goes by, and nobody has told them to do this, and nobody stops them.

Because the stones go with the house, the house is the stones. A building here is not a place; it is a particular set of blocks that have been laid together in the same arrangement in eleven different positions across the cavern floor. The families know their blocks. There is a house near the middle of the town whose lintel has a flaw like a closed eye, and that lintel has been over the same family's door for seventy years, in seven locations, and if you asked where they live they would say under the eye, and everybody would know, though the eye has never been in one place long enough for a map. The town has no map. It has a grain, and it has names, and the names move.

Not everything is carried. That is the other half of the ceremony, the half nobody names. When a house comes down, the family decides what is to go on and what is to stay, and what stays is left in the emptied footprint for the wall. Once it was only what was too broken to bother with. It is not that anymore. The bedframe was left on purpose. So was a chair, farther along, of which only a single turned leg still protrudes, and a row of iron hooks, and, if you know where to look, low down and almost entirely taken, the curve of a copper pan that someone's grandmother stood at for forty years. The people do not talk about this as a practice. They would say, if pressed, that it was easier than carrying. But nothing is left by accident anymore, and the children learn early that when they touch the wall's fresh skin — back of the knuckles, quickly — some of what they are touching is a house.

And the dead. There is no soil here that is not moss on stone, and the stair is long, and the front row is coming down anyway. The dead are laid in the last house before it is dismantled, on the floor, on the side nearest the heat, and the house is taken apart around them and carried away, and they are left with the things that are staying. Nobody watches what happens next. It is slow, and there is work. But everyone in the town knows, walking past the face of the intrusion on the way to the pans, that they are walking past their grandparents, and that the orange low in the seams is, among other things, a kind of hearth.

So these are people who live with their past in front of them. This is the strangest thing about them and the one they are least able to see. In most places the past is behind you and you walk away from it. Here it is ahead — the oldest buildings toward the heat, the oldest people mostly living in them because they are unwilling to move again, and beyond the oldest buildings the wall, which is where everything ends up — and the town walks backward into the dark to make room for it. The young are at the back, in the raw-cut new rooms among the ferns, and they grow up facing the light, and the light is where the old are, and the old are looking at the wall. It gives the town a peculiar patience. Nobody here is in a hurry to get anywhere, because the only direction is toward the heat, and the heat is coming anyway.

They are not gloomy about it. It is worth saying plainly, because the arrangement sounds gloomy from above. They like the sound of the leak in the third coupling. They like the little extra heat from the burned leather-leaf, which nobody needs. They have a game, played by children and tolerated by adults, that consists of counting the moths that have died in a particular bulb by the time it is switched off, and there is a record, and it is disputed. They sit in the evenings — the rota's evenings — in the open doorways of rooms that face other rooms, and talk in the four-minute murmur, and pass cups under the drip. The fig fruits every few years, small and hard and mostly seed, and when it does the fruit is divided with a precision that would shame a court, and eaten slowly, and discussed for the next several years. It tastes of almost nothing. It is the only thing they grow.

What they cannot see about themselves is what the cavern has done to their sense of edge. They do not have a word that means outside, only up, and up is a direction, not a condition. They have no word for private, either, or rather they have a word, but it means something closer to unlit — a private matter is one that happens while the nearest bulb is off, and it stays private only until the rota brings the light back round. They are consequently a people with very few secrets and a great deal of tact. They look away from things. They have all learned to see into four rooms at once and to hold only one of them in mind. A visitor finds this unnerving; it feels like being ignored. It is the opposite. It is the only way that many people can live that close.

Some of them go up the stair. The bundles must be carried and the food must be brought down and the bulbs bought, and there is a class of person, perhaps one in ten, who does this, and they are marked by it. They come back smelling of cold and of something the others call sky, which is not a smell of anything but only the absence of iron and wet fern. They have squint lines. They know what the town is worth up there, which is not much, and they do not say so. Some of them do not come back, in the way the bundles do not come back, and the town has a way of speaking about these that is the same way it speaks about what is left for the wall: they were carried out. It is not a euphemism. It is that the town has only one shape for departure, and everything that leaves takes it.

The old are the town's instrument. Their memories are entirely spatial — not when, but where. Ask an old woman about her childhood and she will point, across the roofs, into the dark at the back, and say the presses were there, and the Grandmother stood where the ferns are, and the wall was near the mark. The mark on the stair is the only fixed thing in their lives, and they visit it, some of them, climbing the wet steps a little way to lay a hand where the wall stood before they were born and feel how far they have come, which is to say how far it has come, which is to say how much has happened. They are the ones who decide, each year, when the front row must come down. It is not measured. One of them stands near the heat for a while, and then says so, and it is so.

Beneath and around all this are the other residents, the ones who were here first, or near enough.

The ferns do not know they are in a town. They have never been anywhere else and have no way to imagine it; they grow to the height of a man because that is where the dimness stops them, and they have been doing it for sixty years, and the town's cutting-back is to them what the wall's advance is to the town — a slow, patient, unstoppable thing that takes the front row every year. There is a symmetry in this that nobody has pointed out, because nobody thinks of the ferns as having a front.

The moths live and die by the rota. When a bulb is lit their numbers fall near it; when it is switched off the population near that cord recovers, and there are cords where no bulb has been lit for a decade that are thick with them at what passes for dusk, grey on grey, slow as the engines. They breed in the fig roots where it is warm and never dry, and they eat something — the moss, perhaps, or the leather-leaf, or each other's dead — and they are the only thing here whose life is entirely shaped by the town's decisions, and they have no idea.

The gecko is not one gecko. It cannot be; it has been seen for at least forty years. It is a few, or a few dozen, living on the only surface in the cavern that nothing else will use — the hot skin of the intrusion, at a temperature that would blister a human palm in a breath — and it is seen once a month because that is roughly how often anyone looks at the wall closely enough to notice something on it move. Nobody knows what it eats. Moths, probably, the ones that stray too near the heat. Nobody knows where it lays its eggs; in the seams, the town assumes, low down where the orange is, which would make it the only living thing in the cavern that goes toward the wall on purpose. The children believe it is the same gecko always and that it is very old. The adults let them. When the front row comes down and the things that are staying are laid out and the last house is emptied and the line of stones begins to move toward the dark, it is the gecko — on the wall behind them, entirely comfortable, in a light no lamp makes — that is the only witness facing the other way.

And the fig, which came down the same way everything here came down, from above, by accident, through a crack, and found warmth and water and nothing to compete with, and has spent forty years doing to the roof, from that side, precisely what the wall is doing to the floor from this one. It is widening the fracture it fell through. It is doing it a finger's width in a year. Nobody in the town says this out loud, but everyone who has ever looked up through the pale curtain of its roots to the swelling where the trunk is jammed knows that the cavern has two slow things in it, and that the town lives in the narrowing space between them, and that one of them, someday, is going to let the sky in.
