Start with the people, because they're the hardest to see and the description already knows it.

Forty-some in the bungalows on a good week, thirty in a bad one. Most of them don't live here in any sense that would survive cross-examination. They're on rotation—six weeks down, two up—and they arrive in the company bus with a duffel and leave in the same bus with the same duffel and a rime on the zipper, and what they do in between is inhabit. They eat in the canteen. They drive the levee loop. They sleep with the air conditioner running and wake with salt in the creases of their elbows anyway, and they count the days on the wall calendar in the control building because nothing else in the basin will count for them. The sun won't. The sun is a coin behind frosted glass and it says the same thing at seven that it says at three.

But out of the forty there are maybe eleven who stopped rotating. Nobody decided this about them. At some point the two weeks up became one, and then became a doctor's appointment on the rim and back the same night, and then stopped. These are the ones who live here, and you can tell them from the others at a glance, though it takes a while to say how.

Partly it's the skin. Not tanned—there's no sun to tan under—but cured, a little. Fine white lines in the knuckle-folds that don't wash out. A texture at the temples like the inside of an eggshell. Their eyebrows have salt in them permanently and they've stopped noticing. Partly it's the voice, which is pitched low and flat, because in the haze a raised voice comes back at you from nowhere in particular a half-second late, and after enough years of that you learn to speak as if the air were a small room. Partly it's the way they move through the flies. A new hire flinches, waves, spits. A resident walks into the black hem at the water's edge the way you'd walk into weather, and the hem lifts and reseats itself behind them, and they never look down.

The man who grades the levees has done the same fourteen kilometers twice a year for twenty-six years. He knows where the washboard will form before it forms—there's a place four hundred meters short of the Pond Nine corner where the fill was never right, and it corrugates every spring in the same pattern, and he has come to think of it as the road's opinion rather than a fault. He drives the grader with the door open, which is against policy, because the cab fogs shut with the door closed and he'd rather be wet than blind. His hands on the levers are white to the wrist.

The control operator works in a building whose windows were painted over against the glare before she was hired. At eye level on the west wall someone before her scraped a slit in the paint the width of two fingers, and through it she can see the top third of the stack, and that's her weather report: if the plume goes straight up, still day; if it leans, the flies will be quiet on the lee shores. She watches a board of forty-year-old gauges and a newer screen bolted beside them that shows the same numbers in green. She trusts the gauges.

The clinic is staffed two days a week by a nurse who drives down from the rim and who has, in nine years, treated three things: heat, hands, and feet. Heat is heat. Hands are the salt getting into every split knuckle and cut and refusing to let it close; she keeps a drawer of the same ointment. Feet are the levee gravel through boot soles and the cracked heels of people whose socks are never dry. She has stitched one dog bite and one fox bite and set one wrist. She has never seen a snakebite or a bee sting or a rash from a plant. She says the basin is the healthiest place she's ever worked and the worst for the skin, and she says both without irony.

There is a cook. The cook is the closest thing the basin has to a mayor, because the canteen is the only room where everyone is at the same time, and because the cook feeds the dogs. Not scraps, or not only—actual portions, set out on the concrete pad behind the kitchen at shift change, so that the dogs have learned the schedule and arrive as the trucks do and sit in a rough line with their salt-white legs tucked under them. Ten of them, sometimes fourteen. Heavy in the shoulder, narrow in the hip, descended from something that was once a working breed and has since been working at nothing in particular for sixty years. They don't bark much. There's nothing to bark at that would care. They eat dead phalaropes off the levee crowns between meals and they sleep under the raised bungalows in the strip of cool damp between the pad and the floor joists, and when a truck comes down the grade from the rim they stand up all at once and watch it the whole way in.

And there are children. Three, currently. This is the thing visitors find hardest, harder than the flies. The children of the eleven grew up with the coin for a sun and the fryer-sizzle for silence and the ponds as a numbered sequence—they can tell you which pond the flamingos favor in April and which pond turned tomato early this year and why. They collect crystals. Every child who has ever lived here has collected crystals: the hopper cubes off the finishing pans, the ferny growths that form on a wire left in Pond Twelve for a month, the thin sheets that skin over a puddle overnight and can be lifted in one piece if you're careful. When they go up to the school on the rim in the autumn they say the green hurts. Not that it's beautiful or strange. That it hurts, the way light hurts after a dark room. They come back down at Christmas and their mothers notice they've stopped whispering, and by the end of the holiday they've started again.

Then the other bungalows, the thirty-odd with their doors open. Those are lived in too, in a manner of speaking. Go into one. The floor is frosted a quarter-inch deep and your boots print it. There's a bedframe, a calendar from a year that was a long time ago, a kettle gone to white fur. There's a fox den in the crawlspace under the bathroom, and the fox is in it—you can hear the shift of her when you cross the floor above—and outside the back door, in the damp strip where the pad meets the flat, there are gull pellets and shrimp casings and the bones of a grebe laid out very neatly, as if for inspection. Somebody lived here for eleven years. The salt has been living here since.

That's the human layer and its dependents, and it's thin. The description says so and it's right. The basin holds people the way a levee holds a puddle: incidentally, for a while.

Below it, the gulls. They're the only birds that stay. The phalaropes come for six weeks and spin on Pond Nine and go; the flamingos stand in the blood-pans from March to June and then some cue in the length of the light lifts them and they're gone in a single night, twelve thousand birds, and the pans are quiet and the shrimp rebuild. Those are tenants. The basin is a hotel to them, an enormous cheap hotel where the food is uncountable and the beds are salt. But the gulls have been here as long as the works and they have taken the works as their own. Every roofline, every levee crown, every fence-post cap at every pump station. When a station is decommissioned the gulls take possession within the season, and within a decade the building is a rookery under a crust of guano and salt so thick you can't tell where one ends and the other begins, and the transformer pole still stands beside it with its floodlight, which nobody ever turned off, so the gulls nest in a permanent artificial dusk. They eat what dies, and in a place with this many living things, plenty dies. They have grown, over sixty years, slightly larger than the gulls on the rim. Nobody has written the paper.

Below the gulls, the flies. And here the question of who lives here starts to come apart in the hand, because a single brine fly lives a matter of days, and so no fly you see this morning will be here next week, and yet the black hem along the water's edge has not moved or broken or thinned in living memory. The hem is the resident. The individuals are the hem's way of continuing. Same with the shrimp: the pink soup in the bucket is not a population so much as a standing wave, the water's own way of turning bacteria into something a bird can eat. If you asked the shrimp who lives in Pond Seven they'd have no idea what you meant. They're each of them passing through. Only the pinkness stays.

And then the bacteria, which are the truth of it. They were here before the levees. They were here when the basin was a natural playa that flooded and dried on its own schedule and the brine pooled in the low center and went red in the summers, and whoever passed along the rim looked down and saw the color and kept moving. The works didn't create them. The works are, without anyone having intended it, the largest cultivation of them on earth: two hundred square kilometers of graded, pumped, concentrated, temperature-controlled medium, moved from pond to pond in exactly the sequence that suits them, with a human staff of forty to maintain the plumbing. They live as color. Salmon, tomato, dried blood. They do not know about the conveyor or the rail spur or the war, whichever war. They know the concentration gradient. They were the first thing here and they'll be the last, and they don't experience the basin as heat or haze or salt or silence, they experience it as home in the plainest sense, the sense of the only conditions under which they can exist at all.

Which leaves the salt.

You could argue it doesn't live here—that it's the substrate, the product, the thing the whole apparatus exists to extract. But watch what it does. It climbs the pylons in tide-marks. It grows upward around the edges of the crazed plates as the water flees, a lip of crystal reaching for a surface that's gone. It frosts the floors of the empty houses and furs the kettles and builds, in the shallow pans, islands, on which the avocets lay their eggs. It's the only thing in the basin that accumulates. Everything else is subtracting or passing through: the people rotate out, the birds migrate, the flies die in days, the brickwork spalls to a thumbnail and stops, the trestles rust to a stable skin and hold. The salt gets thicker. It has taken the abandoned pump stations and it is taking the active ones at the same rate, a centimeter a century, without distinguishing. The whole basin is a machine for making it, and the machine is being made of it, slowly, from the outside in.

So who lives here. Eleven people who've gone to match, like the galvanized bracket. Three children who whisper. A dozen dogs who know the schedule and some foxes who know the dogs. Gulls in a permanent floodlit dusk. A hem of flies that never breaks. A pink standing wave of shrimp. Bacteria for whom this is the only possible world. And the salt, which isn't alive and is the only thing here growing.

In the evening the haze goes rose and then gray and the floodlights come up one by one across the flats, and someone in a bungalow turns on a radio. The description doesn't say who. Three kilometers off, across the brine, it reaches you as a single unidentifiable note. That's as close as you'll get. It's one of the eleven, or one of the three, or one of the thirty on rotation counting days, sitting in a room with the air conditioner running and salt in the creases of their elbows, listening to something from the rim. Then a truck starts. Then the flies drop. Then it's the flamingos, murmuring in the dark in water the temperature of blood, and under them the shrimp, and under the shrimp the color, and under the color the floor, crazed into plates and lipped with crystal, growing upward a very little, every night, toward the light that has no name.
