Start with what lives under the ledges, because it is the most honest.

The many-legged things are dark and flattened and about as long as a thumb joint, and they keep to the shadowed undersides of the rock where the drip gathers. If you turn one over with a chisel tip it will right itself without haste and walk back to where it was. It does not hurry because nothing here has ever taught it to. There is no dry season to run from, no bird that has ever taken one of its kind, no cold night. It eats the film of green that grows on the wet stone, and the film grows back under it as it eats, and so it does not really move across the rock so much as sit in one place and have the rock be renewed beneath it. You can count them. Under a given ledge there will be a number, and you can go along the ledge counting and come to the end and go back and count again and it will be that number. They do not mate, or if they do, nothing comes of it. They do not moult. They are not young or old. They are, as far as anything can be said about them, exactly the many-legged things that belong to that ledge, the way a certain quantity of water belongs to a certain cistern.

The snails are the same but you can watch them do it. A snail crosses a slab of wet stone and leaves its track, a shining line, and the wetness of the place takes the line back into itself behind the snail almost as fast as it is made, so that the snail seems to be towing a very short thread of light that never gets any longer. Where it is going you cannot say. Not toward food; the food is everywhere. Not away from anything. It goes, the way the water in the channel goes, at the speed it goes.

---

The herd you have seen. Come closer to it now, because it lets you.

Forty-three animals. You will find that number and it will stay. Their coats are black-brown and always wet, beaded at the guard hairs, and beneath the wet you can feel — because they let you touch them, they neither like it nor mind it — a dense dry underfur that is warm. Their eyes are large and dark with no white showing, and they have a way of looking at you that is not curiosity. It is attention without inquiry. A stone with eyes would look at you like that if it were interested in exactly the amount you were there and not at all in why.

There are three young ones. They are always three, and they are always the same size, small and long-legged and unsteady in a way that never resolves into steadiness. They nurse. You can watch them nurse. The mothers stand for it, and the young ones butt and pull and swallow, and nothing is being built in them by it. They are not growing. They are young the way the stone is new: they have not been given the time, and they will not be. If you come back they will be nursing, or standing, or lying folded on the moss with their heads up, and they will be that size.

Nine of the herd are lying down at any moment. Not the same nine. An animal will fold its legs and go down and at the same moment somewhere across the slope another will rise, and you cannot tell if there is an arrangement or if it is simply what the number does. They chew. The moss goes in and something comes out the other end and lies on the rock, small and dark, and the wet takes it and the moss takes it and it is gone, and there is no more or less moss for it. Their hooves click. That is all. They have throats and tongues and you would think they would call, a herd like this, in this openness, the young ones especially — but they don't. Perhaps they have nothing to say to each other that the being together doesn't already say.

---

The birds you cannot get close to. This is the first thing that is not quite balanced, and you notice it. The herd will let you lay a hand on it. The birds keep exactly the distance a bird keeps from a person anywhere — the small grey ones a few arms' lengths up in the branches, shifting as you shift; the dark heavy ones on the wall tops lifting off when you are ten paces away and settling ten paces further along, the wader stepping upstream ahead of you at your pace. They behave, in other words, like ordinary birds. They have the manners of a world where people are a thing to be wary of. It is the only wariness on the shelf, and it is odd to be the object of it, and it makes you think that somewhere, at some point, a person here did something to a bird.

Watch one open its beak. It is the small grey kind, sitting in a fork of one of the bearded trees with the water dripping around it. It leans forward slightly the way a bird does, and its throat works, and its beak opens and the tongue shows, and there is the moment where a note should be, and the moment passes, and the beak shuts. Then it does it again. Then it turns its head and looks at you and does it again, as if to show you.

You will try to decide which it is: that they have no voices, or that they have voices and the air won't hold the sound. Sit with it for a while. There is a difference between a bird that cannot sing and a bird that is singing and cannot be heard, and it matters which kind of place this is. But you can hear your own breath. You can hear the drip. So the air carries sound. So the birds are quiet.

Unless what a birdsong is, here, is something that would have to accumulate. A phrase. One note and then another and the second heard against the first. Perhaps it begins and the place will not let it go on into a second note, and so what you are watching is the first instant of a song, over and over, taken back each time at the rate it is offered. That would make the birds the most patient things here. They would be trying.

---

Now the figures.

You have seen them at the edges of things. Go toward one deliberately this time, across a terrace, with your eye on it the whole way, and watch what happens. It is standing at a channel, bent slightly, doing something with its hands at the waterline. It is dark against the green. It does not look up. You walk, and the distance between you does not close, and it is not that the figure moves away — it stays bent, it stays working — but that the terrace is somehow always the width it was when you started. You reach the channel where it was standing and there is the mark in the moss where it stood, a pale flattened patch already lifting, and the figure is at the next channel, one terrace down, bent, working. The distance has been conserved. Your approach was a process and the place balanced it.

Give that up. Go instead into one of the buildings. Duck under the single-stone lintel into the dry dark and stand until your eyes adjust.

There is one sitting on the floor against the far wall with its knees up.

It is a person. Let that be said plainly, because you will want it to be something else and it is not. It is a person of no particular age — you look for the age and the face refuses it, not young, not old, a face with weather in it but no years — with dark wet hair pushed back and skin the colour of the rock where the rock is dry, which is a colour you have not seen much of here. Its hands are on its knees. They are the hands of someone who has cut stone: broad, the nails thick and short, a whiteness of old callus along the edges of the palms, and on the back of the left hand a scar, healed and pale, the kind a chisel makes when it skips.

It is looking at you. You have been looked at by the herd and this is not that. The herd looked at you as much as you were there. This looks at you as if you might be someone.

Say something. Your voice is loud in the small room. The person watches your mouth and then your eyes, and its own mouth moves — you see the lips shape a word, you see the tongue — and there is nothing. Not a whisper. The chest rises for it and the breath comes out and shows in the cold air, so the breath is there, and the shape is there, and the sound is not. The eyes stay on you the whole time with a kind of steadiness that is not patience exactly. Patience would be waiting for something. This is not waiting.

Sit down across from it. Its breath shows and yours shows. Between you on the floor there is a copper chisel and a wooden mallet, set side by side the way you would set them if you meant to pick them up again in a moment.

You will want to know if it remembers building this. Whether it is one of the ones who cut the cisterns and dressed the walls smooth as oil and laid the single slabs across the fractures, who had all the time they needed and used it. Look at the hands and you will think yes. But then ask yourself what remembering is, and whether it could survive here. To remember you have to have been somewhere before and be somewhere after, and carry the first into the second, and let it pile up in you. Memory is silt. Nothing silts here. So if this person built the city, it cannot know that it did. It can only have the hands.

And yet it is looking at you as if you might be someone. Not as if it knows you. As if the knowing is a thing it is poised on the edge of, and the edge is where it lives.

---

Stay a while and you will see the work.

The person rises — not stiffly, not easily, just rises — and takes up the chisel and the mallet and goes out past you into the flat bright light, and you follow, and this time the distance holds at nothing; it lets you walk beside it. It goes to a channel. Along the channel's lip the moss has put out a soft green tongue over the stone edge, a finger's width, and the person kneels and sets the chisel flat against the rock beneath the moss and taps once with the mallet, a small clean sound, and the moss lifts away in a strip and the person drops it in the channel and the water takes it toward the edge of the shelf and the white.

Then the next finger's width. Then the next. It is not fast. It is the speed of someone who is not going to run out of channel and not going to run out of moss, and behind it, where it has cleared, the moss is putting out its tongue again, and by the time the person has worked the length of the terrace the beginning is as it was.

This is the work. Not building; the building is done and is always exactly as done as it is. This is the holding. The channels have to stay clear or the sheet of water would back up and the pools would rise, and the pools do not rise, and here is why. Somewhere on the shelf someone is clearing the spout of each terrace of the fern that would choke it. Someone is lifting the fallen leaves from the surface of each cistern at the rate they fall. Someone is setting back the one stone that the herd knocks from the top of each wall in the moment they knock it. It is not a city of ten thousand and it never was. It is a city of as many as it takes to hold the balance, and no more, and those are here, and this is one, and it has a chisel.

Whether it began as one of the builders and stayed on when the cutting was finished and the place went still around it; whether it came later and found the shelf and walked in over the fractured lip and did not go back out; whether the place made it, along with the fish and the orchids and the forty-three, as the kind of animal a channel needs — you will not be able to tell, and you should notice that the not-being-able-to-tell is itself part of how the place is made. A thing that has an origin has a before. Nothing here has a before that it can be caught holding.

---

But you have one.

That is the last thing to understand about what lives here, and it is the thing about yourself. You came in over the edge and you have been walking, and you are tired now in a way that you were not when you arrived, and that tiredness has been building in you the whole time, unbalanced, with nothing on the shelf to take it back. You are hungry. Your hunger has been getting larger and the place has not fed it, because the place does not feed things that grow. Where you walked across the moss the moss is still pressed flat behind you — go and look — and it is lifting, yes, but slowly, more slowly than the mark the figure left, and your prints are a line of pale ovals across the terrace pointing back to where you came from. You are the only thing here that is leaving a trail that lasts.

You are the only thing here with a before. It is why the birds keep their distance. It is why the person in the building looked at you as if you might be someone: because you might. You are carrying something they recognize the shape of and cannot hold.

The rule of the place is that nothing accumulates. You are accumulating. You are a small steady leak in the arrangement, and the arrangement has two ways to answer that. It can put you out — you can walk to the edge, to the clean fractured lip where the stone ends and the cloud begins, and go over into the white and be gone from it the way the water is gone. Or it can wait, at its own speed, for the tiredness and the hunger to reach whatever level they reach and stop rising, for the moss to start lifting behind you as fast as you press it, for you to set your things down on a ledge in a way that means you will pick them up again in a moment. It can wait for your before to run out. Then there will be one more at a channel with a copper blade, in the flat light, at the hour that is the only hour, and the count will have changed by one, and it will be the new count, and it will hold.

That is what lives here. The forty-three, and the birds that are trying, and the pale fish, and the people who have no number because the number is whatever is needed — and, standing among them at the moment you read this, a thing that still remembers coming in.
