The groves, first, because they are what you take for the inhabitants, and because you are not wrong.

But you are not right about what kind of thing they are, and it takes a long time to see it. You have been thinking of each column as a plant — one organism, rooted, standing. Then one evening in the red light you count the jointed sleeves on a single trunk, from the flare at the base to the first plate forty metres up, and you get a number, and you go to the next trunk and get the same number, and the next, and the next. Every column in the grove has the same number of sleeves. Every grove on the shield, when you check, has the same number. And the sleeves are not the same as each other: the ones near the crown are thin, tight, glossy, the ribbing sharp; the ones lower down are broader and duller; and the ones at the base are so thick and so fused into the rock and into each other that you cannot tell where one ends. They grade down into the stone the way age grades into geology.

What you are looking at is not a plant with segments. It is a lineage that never separated. Each sleeve was, in whatever sense applies, a generation — something that grew up out of the one beneath it, and would in any other place have dropped a seed and died and been replaced. Here nothing died. The new one simply stood on the old one and the old one kept standing. The column is its own ancestry, stacked, every forebear still alive in the trunk, the oldest of them so old that it has become indistinguishable from the ground. The grove does not remember beginning because its beginning is where its foot is. It is standing on the moment it started.

And the number is the same everywhere because the stacking stopped everywhere at once. Whatever finished the lava finished the lineages, mid-generation, with the top sleeve half-hardened and the next one never coming. The crowns are the youngest thing on the shield, and they are exactly as old as the oldest.

So when you put your ear to the trunk and hear that minute-long breath, you are not hearing one creature. You are hearing the whole line of them breathing together, the pressure passing up through every generation in turn, the base taking in the gas from the vents and the crown taking in the sun and every sleeve between them doing its share of the slow conversion. Each one cycles a fraction later than the one below it. The rhythm you felt as regular as a governor is regular because it is a relay, and every runner in it has been holding the same baton for the entire history of the world.

They do not know it is day. There is nothing in them that could. The plates found the sun's arc and stopped moving before the concept of a day could have been useful to anything. They do not know it is night either; they do not slow. What they know — if the word can be stretched that far — is pressure, and warmth from beneath, and the weight of oil moving through them. They know when the balance is right, because the balance has never been anything else. That is the whole of their inner life: a single, continuous, unvarying sensation of correctness.

---

You would not have found the second kind if you had not broken the plate.

You go back to the bead of amber on the fourth day, more out of habit than curiosity — it is one of the very few things on the shield that you did, and you find you want to look at it. It is still there, round and perfect on the black glass. But it is not where you left it. It has moved perhaps a hand's width toward the nearest vent. There is no trail. The glass beneath and behind it is clean. You sit down and watch it for an hour and it does not move. You mark its position with a scratch of your boot heel and come back the next day and it has moved another hand's width, and it is closer to the vent, and it is very slightly smaller.

It is going home.

Once you know what to look for, you find them everywhere in the grove. They are in the seams between the sleeves. They are in the vents, deep in, where the exhaled air is warmest. They are the colour of the glass — black, with that same faint iridescence — and they are flat, and roughly the size of your palm, and thick as a coin, and when they are still they are impossible to distinguish from the stone. They do not have limbs. They do not have a front. They have a surface, and the surface is very slightly adhesive, and they move by being marginally more attached on one side than the other, and they move very, very slowly.

They are the grove's hands.

That is the only way to say it. Where two sleeves rub against each other in that fractional daily shift, one of these is there, lying in the joint, and the joint is filmed with amber, and the film is exactly as thick as it needs to be. Where a crack in the rock runs too near a vent and would, over time, bleed off gas that the column needs, one of them is lying across the crack, sealed to it, and the crack goes no further. Where a plate at the crown has grown a hairline fault — you find this with binoculars — there is a black fleck at the fault, and when you come back a week later the fleck is gone and the fault is gone and the plate is whole. They carry the oil. They eat nothing. They are fed by the thing they serve, by lying in its seams and taking in its amber through their skin, and they give it back by laying it down where it is needed, and the amount they take is the amount they lay. The bead you released was the load one of them was carrying, spilled. It is going back to be picked up again.

They are not separate from the groves and they do not know that they are. They were, you come to think, never anything else. When the lava was still moving and the first sleeves were rising out of it, something in the process threw off these small flat flakes of the same material — the way a weld throws spatter — and the spatter did not cool. It stayed pliable, and it stayed attached in the only way it could, by usefulness. There is no place on the shield where one of them is idle. There is no place where one is missing. There are precisely as many as there are joints and faults and seams, and there have been for as long as there have been joints and faults and seams, and if you took one away the grove would seize.

You watch one for most of a day, lying in the seam between two of the lowest sleeves. You cannot see it work. What you can see, in the last red hour, is that the amber film in the joint is glossier at the end of the day than at the start, and that the flake is a fraction thinner. You put your hand near it and it is warm, exactly as warm as the rock. It does not respond. It has no way to. It is doing what it does. You have the strong sense — unearned, you know, but strong — that of everything on this shield it is the only thing that could be called content, and that the reason is that it is the only thing that has a task.

---

The third one you never see. You find its evidence.

It is the tick. You had taken it for the stone settling — a stress resolving, one crack easing by a micron somewhere out on the glass. But you begin, out of the enormous boredom of the days, to keep track. You note the direction and the interval. And after two weeks you notice that the ticks are not random. They come from a place. The place moves. It moves slowly, and it is coming, in a long curve, toward the grove you have made your camp in.

You go out to meet it. You walk half a day across the glass to where the last tick came from and you find nothing. Clean rock. Ropes and domes and a single fissure, edges sharp. You sit down and wait and after forty minutes there is a tick, very close, and you feel it through the seat of your trousers, and you look down and the fissure has not changed. But when you put your hand flat on the glass beside it, the glass is warmer. Not much. The width of a step warmer, in a place where nothing is ever the width of a step warmer than anything else.

Whatever it is, it is under the glass. It is in the stone.

You follow it for three days. You cannot see it and it leaves no mark, but you can feel where it has been if you are quick — a fading warmth, a hand's width wide, running in a long unhurried line across the shield. It does not go around the domes. It goes through them. It does not stop at the groves; it passes beneath them, and the columns above it breathe no differently. It is going somewhere at the pace of a thing that has nowhere it needs to be, and each tick, you become certain, is a footstep, or whatever passes for one: the stone relieving itself by one grain of the stress of the thing's passing.

You do not know what it is. You will only ever be able to say what it must be.

It must be the remainder. Everything else on this shield balances — the heat and the intake, the gas and the swallow, the oil and the burn — but no balance is perfect to infinite decimal places, and the last of the lava's motion had to go somewhere. It did not go into the groves; they are stopped. It did not go into the hands; they only move where they are needed. It went into one thing, small, that could not stop, because stopping would have meant that the equation had closed and the equation is not, quite, closed. It is the unfinished part. It is the mid-sentence, embodied, walking the inside of the rock forever because there is no full stop anywhere on the shield for it to arrive at.

It is alone. There is only one. You are sure of this the way you are sure of the number of sleeves. If there were two the ticks would sometimes come from two directions at once, and they never do.

On the third night, following the warmth by touch in the dark, you realise it has changed course. It is heading, in its long curve, along your own trail — along the line of scuffs and dust your boots have left across the glass, the only marks on the shield that were not there at the beginning. It is following you. Not fast. Not with anything you could call intent. It is simply that you are the one new thing on the shield, the one thing in the whole system that has not yet been accounted for, and the thing under the rock is the part of the system that goes toward what is unaccounted for. It has never had anywhere to go before. Now it does.

---

So: what lives here.

The groves, which are not individuals but standing lineages, every generation still alive and holding the one above it, breathing a single breath in relay, knowing nothing but correctness.

The hands, flat and black and warm, one to each seam, carrying the oil from where it is to where it must be, fed by what they serve, the only things on the shield with work.

The remainder, under the glass, walking.

And now you. Because you have understood, somewhere in that third night, that you have already become one of the inhabitants, in the only sense the place recognises. You are the surplus. You brought water in your body and moisture in your breath and dust on your boots and warmth of your own that does not match the rock's, and every one of those is a thing the shield has never had to account for. The hands will come for the dust. You have seen one already at the edge of the grove, at the end of your first footprint, lying across the scuff, and when it has gone the scuff will be sealed with a film of amber so thin you will not be able to see it, and the glass will be clean. The remainder is coming for the rest. Not to harm you. It has no means and no reason. It is coming because you are the first thing in the history of this world that has moved of your own accord, and it is the part of this world that moves toward movement, and you are, for it, what a full stop would be.

You can leave. There is nothing here to stop you. But you should understand that the shield will close behind you, hand by hand, seam by seam, until there is no sign that anyone was ever here, and then the ticks will resume their random wandering, and the groves will breathe their relay, and the balance will hold, and it will be — as it has always been — a place where nothing lives, in the sense you meant when you asked, and where everything that is here is alive in the only sense that has ever been available to it: it continues, and it is exactly what it must be, and it is not finished, and it never will be.
