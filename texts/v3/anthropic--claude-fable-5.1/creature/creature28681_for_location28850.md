What lives here has to be counted by clocks, because the place offers no other way to sort it. There is no day to be diurnal in and no season to be a creature of. There is only the roar, the rain, the warmth, and duration — how long a thing has been on the flat, how long its kind has, how long it will stay before it either leaves or stops moving and is taken.

That last is the flat's one law of the living. Everything that holds still acquires a rind. The mineral water beads on it, the bead dries at the edges, the film thickens; a lizard that sits on the same collar for a week comes away with a white scale on its back that will not brush off. The heron that stands longest in the wide space between the tank stand and the valve house has a grey crust along the ridge of its bill and the crown of its head, and when it finally lifts a foot the print it leaves is faintly whiter than the glass around it. A boot left outside the bunkhouse for a month becomes a boot-shaped stone. So on the flat, to be alive is to have kept moving enough not to be enamelled yet, and everything here is engaged in that, at its own speed, and the speeds are what tell them apart.

---

The midges are the elders.

They came first, before the trestles, in the year the basin was still steaming bare and the film had only just begun to lie on it. Nobody knows from where. Some pocket of the old lake's margin, some upwind marsh — it doesn't matter; they arrived, found warm shallow water with nothing in it, and have never since had a reason to be anywhere else. A midge lives a few days. There have been, in eleven years, something on the order of a thousand generations of them on the flat, and to a lineage that long the place is not new at all. It is the oldest thing there is. Every midge that has ever hatched from the film has hatched into the same violet dim, the same warm rain, the same roar, and the roar means nothing to them because they hear by other means. They are the only inhabitants for whom the flat is simply the world as it has always been, with no before and no elsewhere, and they carry themselves accordingly — rising in a cloud when a boot comes down, settling when it passes, unhurried, indifferent, permanent as weather.

The algae under them are older still, if you count the way algae count, which is by the mat rather than the cell. The mats have grown outward from the cool edges at the rate the crust cooled enough to let them, orange where the water is hottest, rust where it is warm, green only at the very rims of the flat where a person could stand barefoot. They have never been eaten by anything that eats algae. They have never been shaded. They are heaving with larvae and slick with the mineral rain and they are, in the strict sense, the only thing on the flat that is exactly as old as the flat can support, having filled precisely the area that will have them and not one hand's breadth more.

---

The frogs are the first natives.

They arrived in the second or third year, when the mats were established and there was something to lay in, and they have been breeding since without pause, because there is no cold to send them down and no dusk to bring them up. A frog here lives perhaps three years. The frogs in the shallows now are of the third or fourth generation to know nothing else, and it shows in them the way it shows in island things. They are drab because there is nothing to be otherwise against — the only colours on the flat are the algae and the lamps, and a frog cannot be violet. They are small because the food is small and constant; nothing here needs to fatten for anything. And they have lost the chorus. A frog population anywhere else swells and falls with the light, silent at noon, roaring at nightfall. These have no nightfall. Every frog rasps when it is ready to rasp, and enough are always ready that the sound is level from one end of the pool to the other and one end of the year to the other, a flat band of noise that sits under the wells like a floor under a floor. They cannot hear one another. It is not clear they need to.

Shine a lamp along the water and the eyes go on as far as the beam, and every one of them is the same eye. That is the thing about the frogs. There are no old frogs on the flat and no young ones, only frogs, all of a size, all of a colour, and if you took one from the eastern pool and set it down at the western pond it would sit in the water there as if it had never been moved, because it would not have been, not really. They are the flat's opinion of what a creature should be: something that fits the water and does not remember.

The herons eat them. Four herons, sometimes five, standing in the wide open ground between the buildings with the rain beading on their backs. A heron is not a native. The herons come and go — not migrating, since there is nowhere they need to be, but leaving for a week and returning, singly, unremarked. They stand for hours. They have the rind. When one of them strikes, it is the fastest thing that happens on the flat, and then it is a structure again, and the frog it took makes no difference to the rasp.

---

The swifts do not live here so much as they live above it.

There are hundreds. They nest under the sawtooth eaves of the turbine hall in a rank of mud cups that has been added to for eleven years and never cleaned, and the eaves are the only part of the flat their feet have touched. Everything else they take from the air: the midges, the rain to drink, sleep on the wing in the upper steam where the lamps do not reach. They scream. A swift screams anywhere, but here the scream has found its use — it is one of the few sounds that cuts the roar, pitched above the wells, and the swifts have kept it and sharpened it and the flat's people say they can hear the swifts when they can hear nothing else. Whether the swifts can hear each other is another question. They fly as though they can. They cross the flat in tight low bands that strike the face as a pressure and are gone into the murk before the eye has finished registering them, and they never collide, and they never leave, and the young that fledge out from under the eaves have never seen a horizon and do not appear to want one.

The terns on the spoil bank are a smaller version of the same thing, white where the swifts are dark, noisier at their colony, quieter over the water. The dredged crust they nest on is the only heap on the flat, the only place a thing can be above another thing, and they have made a town of it. Their eggs sit on warm glass and hatch early.

Lizards on the racks. They are the flat's most careful residents, and the ones with the most to lose to the rind, and they have solved it by choosing their spots. The lagged pipe is warm but weeps; the bare collar is hot and dry. A lizard that finds a dry patch will keep it, and the dry patches are known — the same grey shape on the same flange, day after day, motionless until it isn't. They eat the midges that come to the heat. They are the colour of the canvas because they are lying on it, and the flat's people, who have learned to walk without looking at their feet, have also learned to reach for a valve wheel without closing their hand on the first thing it touches.

Rats in the assay hut, where the crushed samples smell of something. Nobody counts them. Nobody has tried very hard to stop them. They are the only animals on the flat that live indoors, and they are the only ones that have adopted the people's hours — they move when the assayer is gone and are still when he returns — and they are, in that sense, the closest thing to company.

And the snake. Seen once, by one man, at what the shift bell called night, lying along the main steam trunk with its whole length pressed to the canvas. Thick as a forearm. It did not move. It has not been seen since. But the trunk is warm the whole way from the wells to the hall, and a snake that had found it would have no cause to leave it, and the fitters walk the trestle beside the trunk with a small unspoken attention that is the snake's real habitation on the flat. It lives in that attention. It may or may not live anywhere else.

---

The people are fourteen and the flat has changed them the way it changes everything: not by wearing, since nothing here wears, but by adding.

Their skin is soft. It is never dry; the fingers are permanently wrinkled at the tips, the feet are pale and shell-like from the boots, and the ones who have been here longest have lost the ridges of their fingerprints to a fine mineral smoothness that the assayer says will not come back. Their hearing is a strange shape. The roar occupies a band, and after a year the ear stops reporting that band, so the people hear above it and below it — the swifts, the compressor's knock, a dropped wrench, a voice pitched right — and are deaf to the middle. A man from the flat who goes home hears the traffic and not the conversation, and the silence in between as a pressure that hurts, and comes back.

They come back. That is the thing about the fourteen. Not one of them is the person who was hired for the position eleven years ago, and not one of them is a stranger. The shifts are relieved by someone who was on the flat before, went out, and returned. They know each other's hands. They know the chalk marks on the valve house door — a scratched set of half-letters that means "the third relief valve is sticking" or "I've gone to the pond" or "rats in the samples again" — and they do not know, most of them, each other's surnames, and have not needed them. Names are for calling, and nobody here can be called.

They are the same colour as the crust. Their oilskins were black and are grey; their hats were brown and are grey; their hair, after long enough, is grey, though not from age. The bunkhouse at the eastern edge never dries and never darkens, and they sleep in it by shift, and they dream, they say, in the same violet dim they wake to, and their dreams are quiet, which is the only place quiet is.

They are not unhappy. This is the fact of them that the flat has produced and that nobody outside it quite believes. They have the contentment of gauges that read what they read. There is nothing to want here that the flat does not supply — warmth, work, a place on the trestle, a wall to lean on while smoking, a body that is never cold. What they have given up is the thing that would let them notice the trade: the sense of duration. Eleven years have passed on the flat and the men on it have had, between them, perhaps forty of those years, and none of them could tell you where their forty went, because nothing on the flat marks the going of anything. They are not aged. They are added to, like the heron, like the boot.

There is a fig. The superintendent planted it the year the ground was made, in a half-barrel of soil brought from the coast, outside his office door. It leafed. It has not grown a hand's width since. The mineral rain has made the leaves leathery and dark, and the rind has climbed the barrel and started up the trunk, and the swifts use it as the one perch on the flat that is not iron, and it has never fruited and it has never dropped a leaf. It is neither well nor sick. It is what a living thing becomes here if it does not eat midges: held.

---

The superintendent has been here longer than the ground.

He was the man who came when the lake started to steam. He watched the vents open and the water go, a year of it, and he capped the first well when there was still standing pond around the collar and the crust was too hot to kneel on. He set the governors. He sealed them. He drew the trestle lines by walking the flat in year two with a long rod, tapping, listening for the thin places, and the streets of the settlement are the shape of his walk. Everything that is where it is on the flat is there because he put it there, and it has stayed there, and he has stayed with it.

He is not old, exactly. He has the rind. There is a grey line along the brim of his hat and the backs of his hands are a colour that is not quite skin, and his fingerprints are gone, and he hears the swifts and the knock and nothing between. He is the only one on the flat who remembers the lake, and he does not speak of it, because speaking requires the office with the door shut and the shouting even then, and because the lake is not a thing the flat needs. He walks the trestles once a shift. He reads every gauge. He sits with the fig sometimes, at the office door, facing out into the rain like the man at the compressor shed, and the swifts come and go over his head.

The logbook was his idea. A column of readings, one line per shift, dated, so that when something changed the change would be visible. Nothing has changed. The column is eleven years of the same figures in the same hand, and somewhere in the sixth or seventh year the dates stopped, not because he forgot but because he saw that the date was not information. The flat is the date. The flat is eleven years old and he is the one thing on it that knows this in the body, having been there the whole way, and it is not written because he is what it is written in.

So this, finally, is what lives here. Not the midges, who have made it their ancient world, nor the frogs, who have made it their only one; not the swifts who own its air, nor the fourteen who have let it fill them; not the snake in the fitters' attention or the fig in its barrel. Those are all things the flat keeps. What lives here is the keeping itself — the balance, the ceiling, the wells putting out what they put out and the pond receiving it and the seam letting it go — and the balance has one resident in whom it is a person, and he sets the gauges he reads and reads the gauges he set, and the ground under his boots rings or is silent, and he knows which, and he does not need to write down that it is warm.
