The first answer is the one already given, and it is not wrong. What lives here is the chorus. Every frog is a resident, every snail, every swift in its stinking cavity; the termites are the landlords and everything else is a tenant of the moss. If you were asked, on the far rim with the noise still in your chest, who lives on the plateau, you could say *everything* and be understood, and the question would seem to have been about density, about the fact of a place so full that a hand cannot go out into the dark without touching something alive.

But that is not quite the question. Frogs occupy a place. Swifts use it. There is a different sense of living — the sense in which a person lives in a house — that means knowing the place, holding it in mind, keeping it. And once the eye has finished learning that there is no ground and that nothing is hiding, it begins, around the fifth or sixth hour, to learn a third thing, which is that some of the gaps between the mounds are kept.

Not cleared. Nothing here is cleared. But in certain places the moss on the wall has been worn to a patch of bare plaster about the size of a spread hand, at roughly the height of a person's shoulder, and the plaster there is dark and polished the way a doorframe is polished where it has been touched going in and out. You will find one, and then, having found one, you will find them at intervals along the gap, on alternating faces, always about the same height. And you will put your palm against one because that is what it is for, and the wall will be warm, as all the walls are warm, and under the warmth there will be something else. A tap. Then, some time later, another.

---

The instinct is to say *people*, and it may be right. It is easiest to speak of them as if it were. But the plateau does not help you settle the question, and the longer you are in it the less the question seems to be the plateau's concern.

They live in the fallen towers. When a mound goes over — the crack, the lean, the decades-long slump into its neighbor — the colony withdraws to the core and the outer chambers, the old galleries and vents, stand empty. Moss knits the outside shut. The inside stays dry, and stays warm, because the colony is still down there, a few million bodies breathing, and the heat comes up through the floor. Those hollows are where they are. A tower that has fallen well, at the right angle, against the right neighbor, so that the rain runs off it and the wind cannot get in, is worth having, and is had, for as long as it lasts, and when its interior finally sags shut they move to the next. There is always a next. Forty kilometers of made things falling and being remade, and the ones who live here move through the ruin the way the colony does, from chamber to chamber, a little behind the collapse.

They do not build. That is important. Nothing here has been shaped by them except the patches of bare plaster and the smooth places on thresholds where bodies have gone in and out for a long time. The mounds are not theirs. They live in something else's house, as the parrots do and the orchids do, and they seem to know it, or to have arranged themselves as if they knew it — they take from a mound the way the moss takes from it, without asking it to be different.

---

They do not speak. There would be no point. Whether they ever did, whether there was a time before the chorus closed over this place when they called to each other across the gaps, is not a thing that can be known, and it does not matter to them. Sound above a certain pitch is simply weather here, and they treat it as weather. What they use is the wall.

The plateau is one structure. Every mound is folded into the rubble of the last, and the rubble is folded into the surface, and the surface runs unbroken to the ridges, and a knuckle struck against bare plaster sends a pulse into that structure that travels — slowly, faintly, but travels — through the whole basin. The termites drum. They have always drummed, head against gallery wall, a warning that runs through the colony faster than any body could carry it. The ones who live here learned it from them, or learned to hear it and then to add to it, and now under the chorus that fills every frequency the ear can use, there is a second conversation in the material itself, at a pitch below hearing, which you feel with the flat of the hand.

It is slow. A strike, then a wait, then a strike. Two or three a minute. The pulse takes time to cross the basin and a reply takes time to come back, and so what is said is short, and worth saying, and mostly it is about the place. That a tower is leaning. That a catchment has filled. That the alates are rising in the south. That something has come off the rim and is walking. They do not seem to have much to say to one another beyond this, or if they do they say it closer, with the hand on the arm, on the face, in the dark, where the wall is not needed.

The termites, for their part, answer. Not the words — there are no words — but the drumming. When someone strikes the plaster the wall beneath the hand grows, very slightly, warmer, as the colony below stirs to the vibration. Whether the termites take the strike for a warning or a neighbor or a kind of rain no one can say. But the ones who live here have learned to read the warmth as well as the tap, to know from the hand on the wall how large the colony is beneath, how deep, whether it is thriving or failing, and therefore how long the tower will stand. They know which mounds will fall years before the crack shows. The old ones can lay a palm on a face that looks to you like every other face and tell you, by a tap on your wrist you will not understand, that it will not outlast them.

---

Their hands are their eyes. The moss keeps everything soft; there is no callus here, and so the palms are smooth and almost feverishly sensitive, and the fingers are long. They read temperature to the fraction of a degree. They can tell a frog they can eat from a frog they cannot by the skin, in the dark, without looking — the pitch of the call means nothing to them, they cannot pick one throat out of the chorus, but the skin is a different thing. They find swift eggs in the cavities of the tall towers by climbing the moss, which holds them the way it would hold you, to the wrist, and then to the arm, and further. They take snails. They take the bromeliad water. When the alates rise, one night a year when the whole plateau boils with soft-bodied termites leaving their mounds to found new ones and every frog and gecko and nightjar on the basin gorges itself, they gorge too, standing in the gaps with their mouths open like everything else.

They do have eyes. The eyes work. In the grey dawn, when the cloud lightens from below, they use them, and it is then that you might see them, if you are in the right gap — a shape at a threshold, a face turned toward the light with the same patience the frogs have. In the dark the eyes are for the fungus on the north faces, and for eye-shine, and for very little else, and they are kept half shut against the moss.

---

They count in falls. Not years — a year is a rising of alates, a thing that happens, not a measure — but falls of towers. A fall takes a long time, decades, and a life is two of them, perhaps three, and the old ones will have seen a tower they were born inside go from a crack to a lean to a ruin knit to its neighbor, with new orchids in the vents, and then will have moved into the next and watched the crack begin in that one. The swifts on the same tower go through forty generations and remember nothing. The ones who live here remember, in the wall, in the tapping, which is old, which was leaning when their parents were born. They have a kind of history and it is entirely a history of the mounds.

When they die the body goes into the mound. There is no other place for it to go; there is no ground. It is put in a spent gallery of a fallen tower and the gallery is closed with moss, and the colony, in time, takes it, as it takes everything that stops moving here, and it goes into the plaster, and the plaster into the next wall. Nowhere on the plateau, if you dug, would you reach anything that had not passed through a mandible. That has always been true of the ones who live here too. They are made of the place in the most literal way possible; their dead are in the walls they lean on. Made upon made upon made.

---

Some of them go to the rim. Not many, not often. They walk the gaps for a day and a night, brushing moss from both shoulders, and come to the place where the mounds stop as if at a wall and the true rock begins, and they step out onto it. Stone. Cold, and dry all the way down, and utterly still. They lay a hand on it, and strike it, and it does not answer. Nothing comes back through it. It is not connected to anything.

They do not stay. The ones who come back tap the same thing into the wall, more or less, and the old ones have felt it many times and it has become almost a saying: *out there the ground is dead*. And they go back into the noise and the warmth and the wall that answers, and are quiet about it, and do not go again.

---

You will not see them. It is possible you already have, in the sense that one of the shapes in the gap at dawn was not a slumped mound, but you will not know it. What you should know is that they have known about you since the moment you stepped off the rock.

Your first footstep on the surface — the give under the boot, dry on top and cool beneath — went into the wall. Everything after it did too. Your weight, which is wrong for anything else here. Your gait, which is heavy and two-legged and stops often and turns back on itself. You have been a message travelling through the plateau all night, slow as the tapping, and they have read it, and you have been, without knowing it, in reach the entire time — in the way that everything here is in reach, a frog on the moss, a gecko on the wall, a hand's length away in the dark. If you have not been touched it is because they are careful. If you have been, and thought it was moss, or fern, or one of the cold unbothered frogs, then think again about the one that was warm.

They will not stop you crossing. They will follow you, at the pace of the wall, to the rim, and when you step out onto stone and stand there in what seems to you like quiet with the noise still shaking in your chest, they will stop where the mounds stop, and lay a hand on the last warm face, and wait to feel whether the dead ground takes you, and then, when it does, go back in.
