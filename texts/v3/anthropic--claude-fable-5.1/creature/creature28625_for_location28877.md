What lives here is easiest to list from the bottom up, because the bottom is where the ordinary kind of living is, and the top is where it gets thin.

Start with the small red-brown thing that crossed the ridge. It is not a resident. It is closer to a commuter. Down the fault it came out of — and there is more than one fault; the floor is seamed with them, most too fine to see against the glare — there is a country of cool, dark, older rock, wet in the normal way, smoothed in places by real water that has had real time, and in that country the hard-shelled things are numerous and unremarkable. They have been there for as long as the ground has had cracks. They eat films of things off stone. They have never needed eyes and do not have any that work, and so the brightness of the room, which undid you for a moment at the entrance, is nothing to them at all. They come up because the room is warm and the orange mat is there, and they go down because down is where they are from. If you could watch a single one over a week you would find it comes up perhaps twice, grazes the mat for an hour, and returns. It is not colonizing. It is using a new pasture that opened at the top of its world, and it will go on using it for as long as the pasture is there, and it has no idea the pasture is moving.

It has never been hunted. That was said of it already, and it is worth holding onto, because it explains the walk. Everything alive in this room walks or spreads or works like that — with the untroubled pace of a thing that has no enemies and no clock.

Then the orange. The orange is a resident, and the first one, and the only one that came here and stayed. It is a mat of very small cells, each one a thread or a bead, so thin in aggregate that it reads as a tint on the stone rather than a thing on it. It arrived as spores or as fragments, probably on the shell of one of the commuters, probably more than once before one took. It needs three things: the heat, the wet, and the walls. The walls are the important one. Whatever is working the stone is not tidy about it; it releases not only water but everything else that was bound up in the minerals, iron and sulfur and traces of things with long names, and these come down the walls in the film of water and pool on the floor in solution, and the orange lives on them. It is, put plainly, eating the crumbs from the worker's table. It has no interest in the light. It does not photosynthesize. If the room went dark it would go on exactly as before, provided the walls kept sweating.

It grows outward at the pace of the walls because that is the pace at which floor is made. Where the floor is a year old the orange is deep and almost brown, and if you put your fingertip to it — you can, that part of the floor is merely hot rather than scalding — it is very slightly slick, a soaked felt a hair thick. Where the floor is a month old it is a breath. Where the floor is a day old there is nothing, and the stone is white, and the water on it is clear, and the orange has not yet found out that there is somewhere new to be. It is the calendar of the room. Anyone who understood it could read the age of any square inch of floor from the color. Nobody understands it. That is one of the facts about the room: it keeps a very good record, in a language with no readers.

Now the walls.

It is tempting to say that nothing lives in the walls, that what is in the surface is a chemistry and not a creature, and that the light is a byproduct the way rust is a byproduct. That may be right. But the same could be said of a flame, and a flame moves toward its food and away from where it has already been, and holds a shape, and dies if starved, and can be started from another flame, and the thing in the walls does all of these. So call it what it is behaving like. Call it the tenant.

The tenant has no shape you can point to because its shape is the room. The room is its cast — the exact negative of everywhere it has been. It lives in a single layer at the boundary of stone and air, a layer so thin that "layer" overstates it: it is more a condition the surface is in. In that condition the surface is doing one thing, which is unmaking the bonds that hold the rock together, grain by grain, and taking the small energy that falls out of each unmade bond and using it to unmake the next. Most of that energy is spent on the work. Some of it, unavoidably, escapes as heat, which is why the room is a kitchen. And some of it, for reasons that would take a very good chemist a very long time to say, escapes as light. The light is the tenant's breath. The white is what it exhales.

It is not a plant. It has no body to point a camera at. Under enough magnification the bright surface would resolve into a texture, a moving pattern in the top few thousand atoms of the stone, something between a crystal growing and a crystal dissolving. It is too small to see moving and too large to see whole. It is exactly the size of the room and it is a few molecules deep. It has never been inside the room. That sounds like a riddle but it is only geometry: the tenant faces into the rock, always, everywhere, with its whole self. The room is behind it. Everything it has made is behind it. It has no back to turn and no eye to turn it with, and the bright, wet, faceted, faintly humming space it has spent its life producing is a space it has, in the strictest sense, no knowledge of.

It cannot work all rock. This is what the dark ridge is, and the dull patches between the facets, and the black-looking stone at the crack you came in through. Some of the ground is of a kind the tenant cannot eat — a different mineral, differently bound — and where it meets that kind it simply stops, and the working front goes around, and the unworked rock is left standing like a wall where a river split. The facets are the record of these detours. Every hard bright plane is a place where the front advanced straight until it met something it couldn't take, and turned. The room is the shape of the tenant's appetite drawn against the shape of the ground's refusals. The alcove on the far wall, the brightest place, is where the ground has refused least. The tenant is going that way. In a hundred years the alcove will be the room and the room you are standing in will be the entrance hall.

It gives up water because the rock was holding water, locked into minerals as hydroxyl, since before there was a surface for anything to live on. The tenant does not want the water. It wants the bonds, and the water is what is left. So the tenant is also, without meaning to be, a spring: an inexhaustible one, since it carries its source in front of it into the stone. The pool is its shadow. The drips are its clock. The nub on the ceiling is the first thing it has ever, accidentally, built rather than removed, and it will never know about that either.

Where did it come from? Down the same faults, probably. Something like it has to be common somewhere — in the deep hot rock, at the scale of pores, working slowly and uselessly in the dark and leaving behind nothing but a very slightly more porous stone. What is unusual here is not the tenant. It is that a bit of it reached open air, in a place where the rock was the right kind and the heat could not escape and the water had nowhere to go, and it found that on an open face, with room to work in three dimensions, it could go faster, and its heat could feed back into its own speed, and it could — this is the closest the word comes to fitting — thrive. What lives here is a thing that has been alive, at a crawl, for a very long time, and has recently found itself in the one situation in which it can be more than a crawl. The newness of the room is the newness of a thing that has just now, for the first time, gotten going.

So the census is this. A tenant that is the walls, breathing light, eating rock, facing forever away from the only space it has made. A first settler that lives on the tenant's leavings and keeps, in its own color, the only history the place has. A commuter that comes up from the old country below to eat the settler and go home, that has never seen the brightness and never will, and that walks as though nothing in the world would ever want to stop it, which in this room is true. And between them, the water, going around — off the walls, across the floor, into the pool, into the air, onto the cool stone, off the walls again — a resident with no cells at all, and the only one that touches all three of the others.

None of them knows the others are there. The tenant cannot know anything. The orange has no way to know it is following. The commuter is not aware that the pasture it has found is the outside surface of a living thing. They have found each other the way rivers find seas, by the simple fact that what one puts out is what another needs, and they have arranged themselves around that fact so cleanly that the room looks intended.

It is not. Nothing here wanted a room. But this is what lives in it, and this is what it does, and every hour, everywhere along the bright walls, there is a millimeter more of it.
