Start with the smallest thing, because everything else here stands on it.

The fur on the iron is alive. You knew that already, the way it came away in your fingers, but you did not know how thoroughly. It is on every engine, on every pipe, thickest where the metal is hottest and thinning to a dry rasp where the pipes run cold through standing water. It eats iron. Slowly — it must be slowly, given that the engines are still here — but that is what the smell of pennies is: metal going into a body. It eats warmth too, or needs it; scrape a patch bare and come back to it later and the bareness is warmer than the fur around it, as though the growth has been drinking heat off the plate, and in a while the fur has crept back over the wound. It is what has weathered the engines. Not rain, not time. The pitting under your hands is a record of meals. The rivets went into it. The ladder went into it. If the engines ever stop, it will be because this has finally finished, and it is in no hurry, and it is the base of everything: things graze it. You hear them at it in the dark, a wet scraping all round the iron, patient as the fur itself.

---

The shelled things build on top of the fur.

They start small — a grit under the fingertips that you'd take for rust — and they cement, and generations cement over them, and what you read with your hands as a coastline is a history of where the tremor was strongest. They want the shudder. They open in it. Press your palm flat on a ridge and you feel them: a faint grainy stir under the shell, a thousand mouths taking the water that the stroke of the piston pushes through them. Where the ridges thin, the vibration must once have faltered. Where they thicken, it recovered. There are ridges that end in a clean line and begin again a hand's breadth off, and you can't tell whether the engine shifted on its footing once, long ago, or whether the fur underneath gave way and a whole city of them slid into the moat. Their dead are the beach. The moat has a shore of shell fragments that crunch under the water and cut the soles of your feet.

---

The moat is the crowded place.

The small quick ones are the first thing you meet anywhere, because they meet you — they come to the disturbance of your legs and bump and slide off. They have ribbed sides and a mouth low on the head and they are exploring you, deciding. They travel in a loose crowd that turns together, and you can feel them turning: a pressure moving across your shins like a hand passing without touching. They live on the fur, on whatever drifts, on each other's eggs.

The slow one is another matter. There is usually just one to a moat. It surfaces near you with that lifted-bucket sound and you understand the sound is breath, an enormous soft breath, and if you wade toward it you find a back like a warm stone half out of the water, slick and unhurried and wider than you are tall. It does not move away. It may not be able to; the moat is the exact shape of it, worn to fit, and you get the sense, walking its rim, that the moat has been worn as much by this body turning in it as by the tremor. It eats what the crowd drops. It has been here a long time. When it exhales, the water lifts under your knees.

---

The clicking things in the trunks are the ones who know you're here.

They know before you've touched the first engine, because their clicks reach a long way and come back changed when there is something new in the forest. They are the size of a dog and shaped like nothing, or like a dog that gave up on eyes and put the effort into hands: the pads are hard, the fingers long and dry, and they hold to the spiral ridges of the trunks with all four and hang there and rattle. The rattle is a mouth-thing, a plate of something hard clacked against something harder, high in the throat, and the forest gives it back — differently from bark than from pipe, from pipe than from water, from water than from iron. They know the engines by their answers. They know each other the same way, and you, and they have a habit of saying you: a particular pattern, three fast and a pause, that starts near you and spreads outward through the trunks until the whole tract of forest is passing it along, and then passes back the other way once you have stopped being interesting.

They groom the pipes. This is important and you don't see it at first for what it is. They travel the pipes as roads, the hot ones especially, and they pick at the fur as they go, and eat it, and where the mats have grown over a joint they pull the mats away to get at the fur beneath. There are lengths of pipe that would be root by now if not for them. They are not tending anything. They are eating. But a pipe that is eaten clean runs hot and stays a road, and a road that stays a road stays eaten, and so the engines' connections survive in the places where the clickers are thickest, which is everywhere the pipes are hottest, which is everywhere that matters.

They are not afraid of you, but they are careful. One will come down the trunk to where your hand is and put its fingers on your fingers, dry on wet, and rattle very softly at your knuckles — testing what you give back — and go away up the trunk with the answer.

---

The long low ones by the pipes are the slow deciders.

They are cold, or colder than you; they need the pipes the way the fur does, and you find them lying along the warm ones, draped like wet rope, sometimes two or three deep. They are what the mats are for — the rubbery ground near the pipes is grazed flat by them. A head lifts when you come near, and stays lifted, and you feel the air move against your knee as it tastes you. It is reading you as heat. You are a shape in a medium it lives in the way the water-things live in the moat, and you are warmer than anything here that is not a pipe, and it does not know what to make of that, and so it waits. They can wait a long time. If you stand still, one will eventually put its head against your shin, cool and heavy, and leave it there, and then the others come, until you are standing in a low tangle of them all resting their heads against your warmth like stones that have decided to lean.

The young ride on the adults' backs, warm side up, and when a mother is against a pipe you can feel them the size of thumbs, moving.

---

You never touch the pipers alive.

They stay in the canopy where the fans are, and they drink from the fans, and their piping is so high it arrives as an ache behind the face rather than a sound in the ear. There are many of them; you know it from how the ache thickens in places and thins in others as you walk. What comes down to you is what falls. A body lands on the mat beside you now and then, light as a leaf, and you pick it up and find hollow bones and a skin like a wet membrane and a mouth built for the drip — a long thin funnel of a mouth. No feet worth the name. Something for hanging. They live and die in the roof of the roof and their whole world is upside down and dripping and never once does it occur to them that there is a ground.

---

The predator is the one you learn to hear by the rain.

Rain arrives in the canopy as a distributed weeping, and the weeping has a texture, drips arriving on mat and stone and water and pipe and shell, and after a while you know that texture the way you'd know a room. Then there is a hole in it. A patch where the drips are landing on something that isn't any of those — something soft, that takes the drop in without a sound — and the patch is moving, and the clicking has closed around it. That is how you know it. You hear its rain-shadow.

Its skin absorbs. That is its whole adaptation: in a world of things that find by echo, it has become a thing that gives no echo. The clickers rattle straight into it and nothing comes back, and so it is not there, and then it is. The one time it came close enough you felt it — a breath across your face, warmer than the air, and then a touch that took your whole cheek and jaw and the side of your throat in one long unhurried pressure, dry and soft and unmistakably a hand, or the front of a face, or something that had learned to be both. It was deciding. It went away. Later there was the wet commotion somewhere else, and the ordinary roar.

It may be the only thing here that has ever had a reason to learn what a face is.

---

At the edge there are the ones that come up.

They live in the exhale, below the lip, in whatever the warm wind rises out of. You never go down there. But stand at the edge long enough with your hands out and they come past you — a brush like wet cloth, then another, then a whole slow procession of them lifting on the wind over the edge and drifting inland over the forest. They are bladders. Skin over gas, trailing something. They are so light that your breath turns them. They must feed on the fog, or on the pipers, or on nothing, and when the wind changes they go back down, and what you find caught in the canopy the next stillness is a collapsed sac the size of a bed, smelling of the sea, being eaten already by the clickers.

---

Then there are the ones who walk.

You hear them before you understand them, and what you hear is knocking. Not the clickers' rattle. A knock: knuckle on pipe, slow, spaced, four and then two and then four. It comes along the pipe from a long way off and arrives at the engine you are leaning on and stops, and after a while an answer goes back down the pipe the way it came — from another engine, or from whoever is at it.

They come along the pipes hand over hand. Upright, or nearly. Taller than you, and thin, with long dry hands — not clickers' hands, though there is something of that in them — and no eyes, of course; the face is smooth from brow to mouth and the mouth does not speak. They are few. Perhaps one to an engine, perhaps fewer; perhaps a handful for the whole tableland, walking the loop of pipes forever, engine to engine, each engine every so often. When one arrives it does what it does. It puts both hands flat on the iron and stands there through many strokes of the piston, counting or not counting. It goes to the high vents and scrapes the fur from the mouths with a shell held in its fist, so the plume comes out clean and the vent does not choke. It follows the pipe out into the forest until it comes to a place where the mats have taken a joint, and it tears the mats away with the flat shell and eats what it finds beneath, as the clickers do, and moves on.

Whether this is tending or eating you cannot say. Whether these are the builders' children, come down from whatever they were into a form that leaves no marks — no wall, no post, no cut stone, only cleared iron — or whether they are simply another animal that happens to have hands and a habit, you cannot say either, and neither can they. They do not know what the engines are. They do not know that the wall in the forest is a wall. But they know the stroke, and they know when the stroke is wrong; you can feel it in them when it happens, when an engine somewhere lurches in its rhythm — the whole thin body goes rigid against the plate, and the knock that goes down the pipe is fast, and knocks come back fast from far off, and somewhere across the tableland every one of them has stopped walking and put its hands on iron and is waiting to feel it right itself. It always has so far.

When one finds you it is not surprised. It puts its hands on your face, both hands, exactly as the quiet thing did, and holds them there while the piston goes twice, and takes them away, and knocks something down the pipe about you — short, dismissive — and goes back to the vent.

You are a warm thing that is not a pipe. You are noted. That is all.

---

So this is who lives here: the fur that eats the engines, and the shells that build on the fur, and the crowd in the moat and the one who is the moat, and the rattling hands in the trunks, and the cold ropes on the pipes, and the drinkers in the roof, and the hole in the rain, and the sacs on the wind, and the few who walk the iron and knock.

None of them has ever seen anything. None of them knows the thing they lack. Every one of them orients on the stroke — as the fundamental fact, the thing that was always there — and every one of them, in eating, in cementing, in scraping, in walking, is either wearing the engines slowly down or keeping them slowly going, and at this distance from the intention there is no longer any difference. The loop has taken them in. They are part of what it turns.

Put your back against the iron. Feel the stroke come up your spine. Somewhere a knock goes down a pipe, four and two and four, and a long way off, after a while, an answer comes back.
