What lives there is the first question the place refuses.

The obvious answer is the animals, and the obvious answer is wrong, in the way the place has already told you. The frog does not live at the pond; it is installed at it. The beetles are not on the slab; they are fitted to it. None of them eats, sleeps, ages, moves, or dies, and those are the verbs of living, and a thing that has none of them is not an inhabitant but a fixture. You can stand for a long while in the works and count creatures and never once find something that is *here* rather than *in use here*. The count goes up and the sense of company does not.

So you go looking for what is not on shift.

You try the thing under the moss first, because it is the least like a part. It heaves and settles on no interval you can pin down, and for a moment that irregularity is enough to make it seem alive in the other sense. You kneel and put your ear to the green and hear, under the factory, a long slow draw, and a long slow release, and you think: bellows. Then you count, and it is on an interval after all, only a longer one than the frog's, and it keeps its place under the floor of the bowl the way the frog keeps its place at the lip of the pond. It is the biggest component and the most hidden and no less a component. You stand up. The moss is unpressed where your knee was.

You try the fish. You try the moths. You try the spaces between the stations, the wide clear alleys of moss and water where nothing stands, because a hall built for parts also has walkways, and walkways are for something that walks. You look for a track. There is none. Nothing dents here; nothing wears. But the alleys are kept. That is the word that comes. Not empty — kept clear. And a thing is kept for a keeper.

You find it at the lip.

It is at the open side of the bowl, at the sharp edge where the rock ends and the drop begins and the cloud sits like milk in the cup, and you did not see it earlier because it is the colour of the rock and it is facing away from you, out into the drop, with its back to the works.

It is sitting. That is the first thing, and it is stranger than it sounds, because nothing else in the bowl has a posture. The frog does not squat; it is at the pond in the one attitude it has. The birds do not fly; they are in the ring. But this thing is sitting the way a tired thing sits, hunched forward a little, weight down through the base of it, in a way that could be otherwise and is not. It has chosen this and gone on choosing it. It is about the size of a large dog, or a child who has drawn its knees up. It is grey-blue and close-grained and at first you take it for a lump of the shelf itself.

Then you see that it is rounded.

Every edge in the bowl will cut a hand. Every face is a fracture face, unweathered, morning-fresh. And this one thing has no edges at all. Its surfaces have been worn down to curves, dulled, softened, the way a stone in a stream is softened, the way a step is softened by a thousand years of feet. There is grit in the creases of it. There is something on its back that might be lichen: a grey-green crust, dry-looking even in this air, the only dry thing here. It has age on it. It is the only thing in the bowl that does.

And the rock beneath it is worn.

You have to come round to the side to see this, and you go carefully because the lip is sharp and the drop is real. Under the thing, where it sits, the grey-blue stone has a shallow hollow in it, polished smooth, a seat cupped out of the fracture face by nothing but the pressure of a body that has not got up. The hollow is the one blunted place on the shelf. It is the only mark. Everywhere else the moss declines your hand and the drop hangs unfallen and the rock is split this morning at every hour; here, in this one dish of stone the width of a lap, time has been permitted to do what time does.

You understand slowly what you are looking at, and it becomes more itself as you understand it.

The works keeps the moment from passing. That is its output; you have already seen that. The frog pumps and the water climbs and the fresh fracture is fresh again, and the instant is manufactured at exactly the rate the instant would otherwise go. But the instant does not simply vanish when it is declined. Time refused by moss and stone and fern has to go somewhere, and it goes here. This is where it collects. Everything on the shelf is unpressed, and this one thing takes the pressing. Everything is undented and this thing holds the dent. It is the drain at the bottom of the bowl. All the age that the rock will not accept, all the winters the edges have never had, the thousand fallen fronds that never fell, the rot that never set in the ponds — it has all been running downhill into the one body on the shelf that would receive it, and the body has been sitting in it, and has worn smooth.

It breathes. You notice this because you have been watching for the verbs of living and here, finally, is one. The flanks of it swell and subside — not on the frog's interval, not on the bellows' interval, not on any interval, but the way you breathe, a little faster when it shifts, a little slower when it settles. And its breath, when it goes out, does not hang in the air behind it and wait to be walked back into. It goes out over the drop, and it drifts, and it disperses.

It is the only breath here that disperses. You look at the cloud in the cup below the lip and you look at the thing exhaling into it and you do not, at first, want to put the two together, because it makes the cloud too large a thing to have come from so small a body. But the shelf has been here for as long as there has been air, and the thing has been sitting for as long as the shelf has been here, and every breath it has ever let go has gone out over the edge and stayed in the drop, having nowhere else to go. The cloud that fills the fourth side of the bowl, the cloud that hides the shelf from below and pools thickest on the far side — that is where it has been looking. That is its breath, laid down over an age, between it and what it is looking at.

You stand beside it and look where it looks.

The cloud runs out and the grey field is there. Matte, flat, featureless, going up and down and outward as far as sight goes, the colour of the light. A surface waiting to be given a shape. The shelf ends at the edge and past the edge nothing has been finished. Perhaps nothing has been started.

The thing at the lip has hands. You have not seen them because they are folded in under it, in the hollow, but when it shifts its weight — it does shift, once, while you stand there, and the shifting is the most alive movement you have seen in this place — one of them comes out and rests for a moment on the sharp rim of the stone beside it. The hand is rounded like the rest of it, blunt-fingered, thick, and where it rests on the edge the edge is not sharp. Not softened after, not worn in the moment of touching. Already blunt, there, under the hand, in a patch exactly the shape of the hand. The one place on the whole rim where the fracture has been taken back.

The shelf is a thumbprint pressed into wet clay. You were told that. Something pressed it. You look at the hand on the rim and at the featureless grey beyond and the thought that arrives is not one you can put down again: the field out there is the clay, and this is the hand, and the shelf is the one print it made before it sat down.

Or: the rock is new because something recently came apart, and what came apart was this, from that. The thing at the lip is not the maker of the shelf but the piece that broke off to make it — the mountain's own fragment, the part that split away in the moment the works now manufactures without end. The moment of emergence is held at its height, always fresh, always just now, and in the moment of emergence the thing that emerges is always just emerged and never yet gone on. It is born at the lip and does not finish being born. That is why it has not stood up. That is why the field is unstarted. Everything out there is waiting on a body that is held in the instant of leaving and has been held there so long that the instant has worn a seat in the stone.

Both of these may be true. The place does not choose between them and neither will you.

It does not turn round while you are there. It has heard the works for as long as there has been hearing and it has its back to them, and you begin to think that the works are not, after all, indifferent to it — that the frog pumps and the beetles knock and the birds spin their empty centre not simply to keep the rock fresh but to keep the rock fresh *for it*: to hold the instant open at the edge so that the thing at the edge is never asked to be anywhere but here, on the threshold, at the top of the breath. A whole factory floor at its back, running at the volume of a works heard from the yard, so that it will not have to move. Or so that it cannot. The noise is a kindness or a hold and from behind you cannot tell which and it does not look as though the thing at the lip can either.

It eats. You would not have thought so, but you stay long enough to see it: at some point it lowers its head and takes in, without hurry, a little of the cloud from the lip of the cup in front of it. Its own breath, gone out and come back. It is the only thing here that consumes anything, and what it consumes is the one thing it produces, and so even it, in the end, is in equilibrium with the place. Only its equilibrium has a floor that is wearing away.

Because the hollow deepens. That is the last thing, and it is the only thing on the shelf that is going anywhere. The frog's pond falls and rises to the same marks. The drop hangs and falls and hangs. The fracture is fresh at every hour. But the seat under the thing at the lip is a little deeper than it was — you cannot see this, you have not been here long enough for anything to be seen, but you know it the way you know the direction of a slope in the dark. It is the one clock in the bowl. Everything else declines to be pressed; this is pressed. Everything else is unpressed; this is worn. Some day — the word has no purchase here and it is the right word anyway — the hollow will be deep enough that the thing in it sits below the rim of the shelf, and the hand will not reach the edge, and the breath will not clear the lip, and then the cloud will begin, very slowly, to thin.

You do not know what happens after that. Neither, you think, does it. It sits at the lip with its back to the works and looks out at the surface that is waiting to be given a shape, and breathes its cloud into the cup, and is the only old thing on a shelf where nothing is allowed to age, and the frog pumps behind it on its exact interval, and the beetles knock, and the birds go round, and the rock weeps and drinks, and under the one blunt body on the mountain the stone gives way by the width of nothing, and the light lies over all of it, grey, even, sourceless, and has never once been lifted.
