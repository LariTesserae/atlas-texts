What lives here calls. That is the first fact about it and the last, and everything in between is an elaboration.

---

Start with the ones you can see.

The furred ones, fist-sized, that go head-down. They are born on the wall and they die on the wall and in between they never once turn over. They do not have an up. Their feet have soft dry pads that read the grain of the tissue the way a hand reads a rope in the dark, and they move along the tiers in short unhurried pulls, always downward, always toward the pools, and when they reach the lowest ledge they cross to a channel and pull themselves *up* it — still head-down, still facing the floor — and begin again. Their eyes are open but the pupils never change. What they attend to is the vibration in their footpads. A furred one will stop dead on a ledge for no reason you can see and stay there, rigid, and then a drop will land on its head, and it will drink, and go on. It knew. The thread of water falling through the roar made a sound too thin for any ear, and the ledge under its feet picked the sound up and passed it along, and the furred one read it in its soles and waited.

They call in a continuous purr, low, from the chest, and it never stops, not to drink, not to sleep, not to bear young. The young arrive already purring. You can find them, if you look at the right ledge — three or four small heads in a pinched cup of plaster, all facing down, all humming, mouths open, waiting for the wall to feed them, and the wall does, weeping into their mouths from a flushed seam. They do not have a mother in any sense you would use the word. Something laid them there and went on. The wall raises them.

---

The winged ones are the room's mapmakers, though they do not know this either.

They hang in clusters in the upper tiers, wrapped in their own membranes, shifting weight from one foot to the other, and every so often a whole cluster lets go at once and falls into the air and opens. They do not fly the way a bird flies, which is to say toward something. They fly the way a leaf finds its way to the ground — feeling for where the air will hold them and going there — and because the air here is made of sound, what they are feeling for is the sound's shape. They are the only ones that cross the open room. Everything else keeps to the wall. The winged ones swing out into the vast lit emptiness and thread the standing columns of noise and come down on a ledge a quarter mile away exactly where they meant to, and the route they took is the route, that hour, that no one else could have found.

Their call is high and short and they make it on the wingbeat, so that a flock crossing the room lays down a stitched line of pulses across the roar, and the ones still hanging on the wall hear where it goes. You realise after a while that this is how the room's streets are known. The wall cannot see the room. The wall is told, forty times an hour, by things too light to be hurt when they get it wrong.

They shed their membranes. Twice a year, perhaps, though there is no year in here. The old skin comes away in a single greyish sheet and drifts down to the floor and lies there like a fallen leaf, and the floor takes it, and the pools clear. Sometimes you see one hanging half out of its old membrane, the new one still wet and folded beneath, and it looks at that moment not like a small animal but like a bud.

---

The beetle-things are the only ones you would call builders if you had to choose, and they do not know they are building.

They eat the wall. That is all. They bite off a mouthful of the living tissue — it does not seem to hurt the plant, or the plant does not seem to mind — and they carry it in their jaws a short distance along the ledge and work it, chewing and wetting it, and press it against the edge of an opening. And move on, and bite, and carry, and press. They have been doing this for so long that the geometry of the city is nothing but the sum of their habits. A beetle-thing does not carry a mouthful farther than a certain distance because it has never needed to. It does not press plaster above a certain height because the drops there are too fast to drink. A thousand of these small refusals, kept up for ten thousand generations, and you have avenues, and tiers, and the smooth sagging profile of a ledge that has never once been designed.

Their backs are the exact glazed green of the plaster because they are the plaster. What they eat is what they are made of. When one dies it is worked in with the rest.

They call by scraping — a dry burr, a file on a rasp — and it is the sound with the least in it, the thinnest, the closest to no call at all. But there are more of them than there are of anything else, and the burr is the floor the whole roar stands on. Cover your ears completely and it is the last thing to leave.

---

And the eyeless ones, the long-necked ones, that feed at the seams.

These are the ones the plant is eating. You saw that. What you did not see at first is that they know it, or their bodies know it, and they go to it anyway. A long-necked one moves along a tier with its soft head weaving, blind, tasting the air, and when it finds a seam where the wall is weeping it puts its mouth to it and drinks, and the seam swells around its mouth, and the flush spreads down its neck. It does not pull away. It stands there for hours, sometimes a full swell of the colony's rhythm, and the wall feeds it and it feeds the wall and at some point the exchange stops being one thing and starts being the other and the long-necked one does not notice the moment. Some of them pull free and go on, thinner, and feed again elsewhere. Some do not pull free. They stand at the seam and go quiet and slowly the tissue around them goes from flushed to grey-green and you cannot say any longer where the animal ends. The oldest sealed districts, the ones entombed a yard deep in the translucent wall — there are shapes in them, if you look long enough. Long necks. Bowed heads. Still at the seam.

They have the fullest call. A low, held, open note with a slight waver in it, like a voice trying to hold a pitch it is slightly too tired for. When you first came in and the sound stood up around you, this was the part that felt like grief. It is not grief. But it is the part that felt like it.

---

There are others. There are things in the pools, colourless, finger-long, that never leave the water and whose call is a click you feel in your knees when you stand near. There are things living *in* the plaster, in the plaster itself, smaller than a grain, that you know about only because the beetle-things' burr sometimes rises to a whine over a particular stretch of wall and then subsides, and the stretch of wall is a little darker afterward. There is something that moves in the very topmost tiers, at the limit of the light, that you have seen only as a change in the density of the roar, a hole in the sound that drifts along the highest avenue and is gone. You do not know what it is. The winged ones swing wide around it.

But listing them is already the wrong way in. You could go on naming kinds all day and you would not have described what lives here, because what lives here is not a kind. Stand still. Let the roar settle into your bones the way it wants to. Look at the wall without looking at any one thing on it.

It breathes together.

The purr and the pulse and the burr and the held waver are not four calls; they are the parts of one call, and the wall is not covered in animals, it is covered in the one thing that lives here, distributed. The furred ones read the drops for the colony. The winged ones map the room for the colony. The beetle-things extend the colony's body into the plant's body and the long-necked ones extend the plant's body into the colony's, and the whole slow exchange — chewed wall becoming plaster, fallen animal becoming floor, floor becoming pool, pool becoming drop, drop becoming animal — runs in a single closed loop that has no beginning you could point to. The plant grew the builders the way a fig grows its wasps. The builders shaped the plant the way a river shapes a bank. Neither one lives here. What lives here is the loop.

And the loop calls. That is what it does. It keeps a room full of sound the way a heart keeps a body full of blood, and the room grows to hold the sound, and the sound grows to fill the room, and if the calling stopped — it will not stop — the columns of standing air would fall over and the streets would vanish and the winged ones would drop into an emptiness with no shape and the far wall would come rushing in to whatever size a quiet room ought to be.

---

Now shut your eyes.

The heard city. You have found it already; you found it the moment your ears gave up on direction and started hearing the wall. It is larger than the seen city. It has districts past the far wall and a level under the floor and a whole busy dripping quarter above the ceiling, occupied, and the seen city does not have these, and yet the sound is unambiguous.

Who lives there.

Listen to a single call. Choose one — a furred one's purr, close, on the ledge nearest your hand. Follow it. It goes out into the room and the room takes it up and it does not come back to you, not directly, but it does not stop either. It has nowhere to stop. The chamber was grown to hold sound, and hold is the exact word. Every call made in here since the first beetle-thing bit the first mouthful of wall is still in here, going round, worn a little softer each time, a little slower, the edges sanded off, still audible if you know how to listen under the new calls for the old ones. That is what the roar is. Not a hundred thousand voices. Every voice. The long-necked one sealed a yard deep in the wall is still holding its open note; you can find it, thin and slow and far back, like a star that went out. The winged one whose membrane you watched the floor take is still stitching its line across the room. The heard city is the city entire, every tier that has ever been, every ledge, every animal, still calling, and the seen city is only the front edge of it — the present, thin as a leaf, lit from behind by everything it used to be.

The district above the ceiling is full of the fallen. That is what you are hearing. Not ghosts; nothing here is a ghost. Just calls that have not yet finished going round.

And the drop. The one you saw fall twice. It fell once, and the sound of it falling — too thin for you to hear, but not for the room — went out and round, and the furred one on the ledge heard it come back and lifted its head, and you, by then half in the heard city yourself, saw it lift. The second drop was the first drop, heard. You are learning to see with your ears. Stay long enough and you will not be able to tell the two cities apart, and then you will be living here.

---

Which leaves the middle.

The vast wet hollow. The nothing in the centre of the room, the emptiness the plant grew around and kept. You walked toward the far wall through the standing columns of noise and never got there, and now you understand why: the room is not a room with a wall at the end of it. The room is the shape of the calling, and the calling has a direction, and the direction is *in*. Every purr, every pulse, every burr and waver, from every tier and every age of the city, is aimed at the centre. The chamber is an ear. The whole green swell on the cracked clay is a single vast ear grown around the place where a listener would be.

And there is no listener there.

Or — you cannot see one. You cannot hear one; that is not how it would work. But the calling has kept up for longer than the wall has been thick, and you do not call for ten thousand generations at a thing that is not there unless the not-being-there is what you are calling to. The plant did not grow around an absence. It grew around an *expectation*. It shaped a cavity the exact size and stillness of something that could hear all of it at once — every voice, every age, the heard city and the seen — and then it filled the cavity with the sound, and waited.

Something was here and dissolved, you thought when you came in. Or something was never here. Now you think: something was called, and the room is the shape of the call, and whether anything ever came is a question the room has no way to ask. It cannot stop to check. If it stopped it would collapse. So it calls, and grows to hold the calling, and closes over its own dead, and lets the drops fall twice, and out on the clay a low green mound hums as it has hummed and will.

That is what lives here. A thing made of ten thousand kinds of small warm body and one enormous plant and every sound any of them has ever made, all still going, all bent inward on a hollow the exact size of an answer.

You are standing in the hollow. You noticed that some time ago and did not want to say it.

You will want to leave soon, sideways, through the seam that is not a door, and out onto the dry clay where the note will drop from your teeth to your shins to your heels and finally to nothing. Go. But you should know before you do that the room has already taken your footsteps, and your breath, and whatever small sound you made when you understood — and that these are going round now with the rest, a little slower each time, softer at the edges, and will be for as long as the wall is warm. Whether anything is listening you cannot tell. The room cannot tell. You are part of the question now. It seemed only fair to say so.
