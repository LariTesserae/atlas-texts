Begin where the description couldn't quite go, because it is too small to be a district: the residents nobody in the city has ever counted, who were here first, who are the reason there is a here.

They are bacteria. They live in the water where it comes hot out of the rock, and they live in mats on every surface that isn't scraped clean, and — this is the important part — they live inside the tubeworms, in a dense dark organ that fills most of the animal's body, packed by the billion into tissue that exists only to hold them. They take the sulfur the rock exhales and the oxygen the cold sea carries down, and from the difference between the two they make sugar in the dark. Nothing else here can do this. Everything else here is downstream of it. If you wanted to say who lives in this city in the way you'd say who lives in a country — the population, the economy, the thing that would remain if you took away the architecture — it is them. The worms are their houses. The mussels are their houses. The mussels' shells are houses for the film that is more of them, and the limpets eat the film, and the shrimp eat the film, and the crabs eat the shrimp, and none of it would exist if the rock stopped breathing, because the rock is not feeding the animals. The rock is feeding the bacteria, and the animals have arranged their bodies around that fact.

So the first inhabitants are invisible, tasteless to each other, and everywhere. Hold that.

---

The tubeworm, then, seen as a resident rather than a tower.

It is an animal that has given things up. It has no mouth, no gut, no anus. As a larva it had a mouth and used it, briefly, to swallow the bacteria it would need; then the mouth closed over and the gut turned into a sac for the guests and the animal became, for the rest of its life, a kind of hospitable wall. What remains of its own agency is the plume — soft, blood-bright with a hemoglobin that binds sulfide as readily as oxygen — which it extends into the flow and withdraws when something touches it. That is the whole repertoire. Extend. Withdraw. The tube lengthens at the top as the animal secretes it, a fraction of a millimeter at a time, so that the worm is always at the mouth of a chimney it is building above its own past.

It does not know its neighbors. It is pressed against them, plume to plume, for its whole life, and there is no organ for knowing them. But it responds to them: when one plume withdraws, the pressure wave moves through the bundle and its neighbors withdraw a fraction of a second later, so a tower can flinch as a unit, the whole red crown going white in a wave that travels outward from a touch. Then, slowly, from the center, the red comes back. That is the closest the towers come to behavior. It looks, if you were watching with the wrong instincts, like a crowd turning to look at something. It is not that. It is a thousand identical reflexes overlapping.

They live decades. Some of the animals at the base of the oldest live towers were plumes when the top meters of tube hadn't been secreted yet. They are still extending, still withdrawing, farther and farther down inside a structure their descendants have raised over them, and when the seep finally shifts they will be the last to starve, because they are the closest to the source.

---

The mussels are simpler to describe and stranger to think about, because there are so many of them and each one is complete.

A mussel is an animal with a mouth and a gut and a heart and a foot and a set of gills that, here, is mostly occupied — like the worm's body — by bacteria. But unlike the worm it has kept its options. It can filter what drifts by. It can farm its guests. It does both, and the ratio shifts with the flow, so a mussel at the edge of a bed, where the sulfur is thin, is more of a filter-feeder and a mussel near a channel is more of a farm. The animal doesn't decide this. Its position decides it.

Each one holds its place with threads it spins from its foot, anchored to its neighbors' shells and to the crust and to whatever else is there. Over years the threads tangle into a felt, and the felt is what makes the pavement a pavement instead of a heap. When a mussel dies, its shell stays, still knitted in, and the neighbors' threads keep it, so the bed is always some fraction dead — a fifth, a third — and you couldn't tell by touch which ones. They open and close. The open ones draw water through and taste it and pass it along a gill the size of a fingernail. Multiply that by a number with a lot of zeros and the bed becomes a lung, breathing the channel water in through a million small intakes and returning it slightly changed, and the whole floor of the city is doing this, continuously, the shift and shift and shift that a hand laid flat would feel.

There are things that live only in the felt. Small worms, a few millimeters, that never see the outside of the bed. Copepods that graze the threads. A snail so small it spends its whole life in the space between two shells and moves to a third only when one of them dies. For these, the city has a ceiling and it is a mussel.

---

The shrimp. Say something true about the shrimp that the word *abundant* doesn't say.

A single one weighs almost nothing. It has no eyes — or rather, it has, in place of eyes, a pair of pale patches on its back that are sensitive to the faint infrared glow that very hot water gives off, so that the only thing it can see, in the entire universe, is the direction of the heat. It uses this to find the hot channels and to keep from walking into them. Everything else it knows by its antennae and by taste, and what it tastes for, mostly, is bacterial film, which it scrapes with its front legs and eats, and molt, and the dead.

It lives perhaps two years. In that time it will molt a dozen times, each time backing out of its own shell and lying soft for a day on the pavement while its new skin hardens, and each time it does this it is in real danger, and each time most of them survive anyway because there are too many to eat. It will breed, releasing larvae that rise — this is the only upward the shrimp knows about, the larvae go up, into the cold, into a dark that isn't warm, and drift there for weeks eating whatever tiny things live in the column, and then somehow come back down, not to this city necessarily but to some city, following a chemistry no one has traced.

The individual shrimp is nobody. It is one of the ways the city moves. When a seep strengthens the shrimp gather there in a day, drawn by the heat-glow, and the crabs arrive after them, and that is how a district is settled: not by anyone deciding but by the shrimp flowing toward warmth like water finding a low place, and everything that eats shrimp flowing after. Watch the shrimp and you are watching the city think, if the city thinks, which it doesn't.

---

The crabs are the ones that make it possible to say *patience* about the place without lying.

There are perhaps a few thousand on the whole rise, which makes them rare by the city's standards. They are not blind, exactly, but their eyes are small and slow and the crab doesn't rely on them. It relies on the hair. Its legs are furred with fine sensory bristles, and it stands on the pavement with its legs spread and it waits, and the water moves past the hair and the hair reports: shrimp, shrimp, shrimp, mussel gape, shrimp. It does not chase. Chasing is for a world with light, where you can see where the thing you're chasing has gone. Here you would lose it in a body-length. So the crab stands, and lets the shrimp come to it, and takes one when the hair says *now*.

Between meals it may not move for a day. It may not move for longer. It sits at the edge of a channel where the drift is richest and it is a piece of the landscape — the shrimp walk over its legs — and then it is not. It grows slowly, molts rarely, and probably lives a long time, though nobody has watched one long enough to say. Its whole existence is a demonstration of a principle: in a place where nothing can be seen, the one who moves least is the one who is hardest to find.

There is a squat lobster that does the same thing, faster, with longer claws, and eats the smaller shrimp. There is a larger crab, rarer still, mostly at the city's cold edge, that eats the smaller crabs. Above that, in this city, nothing. No sharks come here. The eels don't hunt crabs. The line ends with a crab standing still.

---

The bristled worms in the hottest channels live in water that would kill everything else in the city within minutes, and they seem to prefer it.

They sit in tubes that they make themselves, soft paper-thin things rather than the worm towers' hard shell, and the tubes are anchored in the seep flow where the water comes up at temperatures that are hard to state honestly, because they fluctuate every second, because the worm's tail may be in water fifty degrees hotter than its head. It has a fringe of red gills that it holds up into the cooler layer and a body that trails down into the hot, and it survives this by a chemistry no one fully understands — a collar of bacteria on its back that it feeds with mucus from its own skin and that, possibly, insulate it, or eat the compounds that would otherwise poison it, or both. In any case the worm and its coat are not separable. Scrape off the bacteria and you have killed the worm.

They are the closest thing the city has to citizens of the vent itself rather than of the city that grew around it. When a seep shifts, they shift with it, and they are the first to appear at a new hot crack and the first to vanish when it cools. Everything else in the city is, in a sense, living in the vent's cooling wake. The bristle worms live in the vent.

---

The brittle stars threading their arms up through the pavement are simple animals with a complicated posture. Each is a disk the size of a coin, buried in the mussel felt, with five arms that fork up through the gaps and hang in the current like the fronds of something. They feed by trapping particles on the arms and passing them mouthward in a slow relay. What is odd about them is not their biology but their arrangement: they are spaced out across the beds with a regularity the mussels don't have, each one about an arm's length from the next, so that the whole field of waving filaments is a grid. They are keeping their distance. An arm that touches another star's arm retracts. In a city defined by crush, they are the only ones who have made room.

---

The octopus.

It is the one thing in the city that has, for lack of a better word, a where. The tubeworm is at its tube because it is its tube; the mussel is at its threads; the shrimp is wherever the heat is; the crab is wherever the drift is. The octopus has a hollow in the crust at the western edge, and it returns to it, and it is not in that hollow because the hollow feeds it. It goes elsewhere to feed. The hollow is where it goes when it isn't feeding, and it has chosen that hollow over the others, and if you wanted to say something lived *somewhere* in the city rather than merely lived *in* the city, it is the octopus.

It is pale — the pigment cells that its relatives in shallow water use to flash and blend are mostly gone, or present and never used — and it is soft, and its eyes are large and probably useless and it keeps them anyway. It moves by pulling itself over the pavement on its arms, feeling for mussel edges, and when it finds a gape it pries and drills and eats. This takes time. The mussels are not fast, but they are strong. The octopus can spend an hour on one. Afterward it will sometimes stay on the shell it has emptied, arms spread, doing nothing anyone has been able to interpret.

It lays eggs once, probably, at the end, and broods them for a long time — the cold down here slows everything — and dies before they hatch, so no octopus in the city has known its mother, and the current decides where the young go. This one arrived from somewhere else, as a hatchling, drifting. It found the rise. It found the western hollow. It has been there since, and there is at least one other octopus somewhere on the eastern beds, and they have not, so far as can be told, ever met.

And it goes to the wood. Not often. Not on any schedule. It leaves the hollow and crosses the entire rise, the full width of the city, threading between the towers and over the pavement, past a thousand mussels it could be eating, and arrives at the rotten log at the eastern edge and drapes itself over it and stays. There is a temptation to say the wood smells of the surface, that something in the grain still carries a chemistry of sunlight, of a forest, of rain, and that the octopus goes there because it is the only strange taste in a city where everything tastes of sulfur and shell. There is no evidence for that. There is only the going, and the staying, and the coming back.

---

The eels are not residents. They come from the cold plain, following the warmth or the smell of the city's dead, and they work the channels for a while — eating soft-molted shrimp, eating whatever the current has dropped — and then they go. Nothing here knows the difference between an eel that has been before and one that hasn't. The eel probably doesn't either. But they are the city's only regular contact with anywhere else, and the fact of their coming and going means the city is not sealed. It is a warm island in a vast cold country, and things cross the country to reach it and cross it again to leave.

The drop-shaped fish over the channels are somewhere between. They may live their whole lives here. They may be passing through slowly. They hang with their mouths up, and they eat what comes, and they don't seem to go anywhere.

---

Then the wood again, but from inside it, because there are residents there who don't belong to the city at all.

The boring clams arrived with it. Their ancestors settled the log when it was somewhere in the cold, still on its way down, or on the plain before a current nudged it here — and they have been eating it ever since, drilling their tunnels into the softened grain, digesting the cellulose with the help of bacteria of their own that have nothing to do with sulfur. Their entire world is the log. They have never touched the crust or the pavement. They are the only animals on the rise whose food came from the sun, at however many removes, and they are eating the last of it. In a few decades, or less, the wood will be gone — hollowed to a lattice, then to fragments, then to a stain in the shell debris — and the clams will have eaten their country out from under themselves, and there will be nothing to replace it, because nothing else on the rise is wood. Their larvae, when they release them, go up into the dark looking for a log. Almost none will find one. The colony here is a population in a country that is shrinking under it by the length of a burrow a year, and it does not know that, and it drills.

---

Last, the dead, who are the majority.

The foundation is bodies. Three or four meters of tube, compressed, cemented, cross-cut with the mineral bands of old years — that is a population, or many populations, stacked. The mussel shells locked in the felt of the living beds are dead residents still holding their place. The shell hash that fills the hollows, that buries the log, that grits between the pavement's ridges: dead. The city's mass only ever increases because the city never removes anyone. It grows over them. The live crust is a skin a few centimeters thick on a body of the dead that is many times deeper, and every animal in the live layer is standing on, anchored to, feeding from the film that grows on, the ones that came before.

In that sense the question of who lives here has an answer that goes down. The bacteria, first and everywhere. The worms and mussels that house them. The grazers that scrape the film. The walkers that eat the grazers. The standing one that eats the walkers. The soft one with a home, who goes to the wood for no reason. The strangers who pass through. The exiles in the log, eating the last of the light. And under all of them, holding them up, in bands of dark and less dark that record the breathing of the rock across centuries — everyone who was ever here, still here, still part of the city, laid down and built upon and in no sense gone.
