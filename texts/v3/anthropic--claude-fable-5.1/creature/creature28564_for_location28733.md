Start with the honest answer, which is the crust, because it is the only thing here that anyone would agree to call alive, and even then only after argument.

Look at it closely. Get down on the warm rock and put your face near it. Up close it is not grey but very faintly blue-white, the colour of skimmed milk, and it is not flat. It stands off the stone a hair's breadth, and where the ring is oldest — at its centre — it has begun to lift at the edges and curl, and under the curl there is a second, thinner ring beginning, following the first. So it is not one crust but crust on crust. It has layers. Things that are merely chemistry do not usually bother with layers.

It grows toward warmth. You can see this if you find a slab where an ember-seam runs along one edge: the rings there are not rings but ovals, stretched toward the seam, and the ones nearest have gone from milk-white to the colour of old paper, as though singed, and stopped. There is a distance from the heat at which it thrives and a distance at which it dies, and it finds that distance the way anything does, by growing past it and losing what grew too far.

It needs the slab to hold still, and the slabs do not hold still for long, so it is always young. A ring the width of two hands is an old one. You will not find one wider than an arm's length anywhere on the crown, because nothing on the crown has been level for that long. When the slab tilts the crust does not slide — it is bonded too well for that — but it cracks along its rings, and the cracked pieces stop growing, and within a few nights the pale has gone dull and then gone. When the slab splits the crust splits with it and the two halves face each other across the new fissure and go on growing for a while, ring toward ring, until the breath from below cures them.

So it dies constantly, and yet every slab that holds still for a fortnight has rings on it. Where do they come from? Nothing brought them. There is nothing here to carry seed. And so you look up, because there is only one thing here that comes from anywhere — the dust, the fine dry suspension that has swallowed the stars — and you understand that it is not only settling on the rock but into it, and that some part of what falls is not mineral. The sky that has taken the light away is the same sky that seeds the ground. In a season the dust will be gone and the stars will come back and the crust, perhaps, will stop appearing on the new slabs, and the last rings will crack and dull and the place will be as barren as it looks. Or the dust is only the first carrier, and the crust has learned already to shed its own. There is no one here to wait and see.

That is the first thing that lives here. Small, pale, territorial, made of the sky's ash, dying every time the floor moves and returning every time it stops. It is about as much as this place will allow.

Now the builder.

It is tempting to say the builder does not live here — that whoever stacks the cairns is passing through, came from somewhere and is going somewhere, and that the wall and the hollow are the leavings of a traveller. But look at what the evidence will and will not support.

No burnt place. So no fire, ever, in any of the camps. Anyone who came here from a colder or a darker country would have made fire the first night, if only for the light. The builder does not need light beyond the ember-seams, and does not need warmth beyond the stone. The builder's feet, or whatever the builder stands on, are used to this floor.

No vessel. Nothing to hold water in, and no water to hold. The builder has crossed at least a week's walk of country where nothing drips or pools or condenses and has not carried a drop, and has slept, and gone on stacking. Either the builder does not drink, or the builder drinks something you have not thought of as drink.

The hollow. The size of a body, on a rectangle of ground very slightly smoother than the rest. You took it for cleared. Kneel and look at the smoothness. It is not the smoothness of stone that has been swept. It is the smoothness of stone that has had something taken off it — a faint pale rim at the edges of the rectangle, and inside the rim, nothing. Rings, grazed to the rock. The hollow is not a bed. It is a meal.

That is what the builder lives on, then, if the builder lives on anything: the crust. Thin as breath on glass, and scraped up a slab at a time, and the builder must find a new slab every day or two because a slab does not carry more than a body's-length of crust and the builder eats a body's-length at a sitting. So the builder moves. Not toward anything — there is nothing to be toward — but from slab to slab, following the pale, and the pale is only on the slabs that have held still, so the builder is always where the ground has most recently stopped moving, and always leaving as it starts again.

And the builder stacks stones.

You could say it is a way of remembering. A creature that eats a thing that only grows where the floor is quiet might learn to mark quiet floors, to say *here was still, here was food*, and come back — except that nothing here is where it was, and the builder must know that better than you do. The cairns are not for returning. The builder never returns. You have found none rebuilt from the far side; the new ones are always ahead.

You could say it is nothing, a habit, the way a hand tears paper. But the choosing is too careful for that. Seven stones, the flattest at the bottom, each one turned and set and balanced in the near-dark by something that can see in ember-light what you can barely see at all. It takes time to build one of those well. The builder has, by any reckoning, nothing else to do with its time, and spends it on this.

Or you could take the piece's own suspicion seriously and say the builder does it for the reason a person whistles in an empty house: to put a small made thing into the dark and hear it hold. The builder eats where the ground is still. The builder cannot make the ground still. The builder can make a stack of stones that is still for a night, and does, and moves on, and makes another.

Here is the thing that will not resolve. One cairn an hour, in every direction, over a country wider than a week's walk. One builder could not do that. One builder, eating a slab a day and stacking seven stones a night, would leave a line of cairns, and what you find is not a line. It is a scatter. It is a field.

So there are many of them, and you have not seen one, and you will not, because they are where the crust is and the crust is where the floor is quiet and the floor is quiet only in patches that open and close like breath — so at any hour each builder is alone on its slab, a slab's width from the nearest other, and by the time the ground has carried them together it has carried the crust away and both have moved. They do not meet. Perhaps they have never met. Perhaps each one believes it is the only one, and stacks its stones for the ground alone, and would be as surprised as you to find that the cairn it comes upon in the dark, leaning, one stone rolled a few paces off, was set by another hand.

And yet — go back to the wall. Two courses of dry stone, the length of a room, ending in air over a fissure. Look at the far end, where it stops. It does not end because the builder ran out of stone or will. It ends at a line, and on the far side of the line the ground is smooth and pale-rimmed, grazed to the rock, and the rings on the near side have been left standing. A boundary. One builder's eating stopped here and another's began, and neither crossed, and someone raised a wall to say so. The rings of the crust stop where they meet and form a seam. The builders stop where they meet and form a wall. At every scale this place will permit, what lives here is a thing that draws a line on ground that recognises no lines, and keeps to it, and rebuilds it when the ground carries it off.

That is what lives here. Not one thing. A pale crust that falls from the sky and grows in rings and dies when the floor shifts. A scattered people, if they are a people, that eat the rings and never see each other and stack stones in the dark. And beneath both, the thing they live on and against, which is not alive by any measure you could defend and which you nonetheless keep wanting to call a sleeper — the warm turning floor that gives the crust its stillness for a fortnight and takes it back, that carries the builders apart and never quite together, and that sighs, and ticks, and has not decided what shape it will hold.

In a season the dust will settle and there will be stars. The crust will have that long to learn to seed itself, and the builders will have that long to learn there are others, and the floor will go on with its slow bad sleep either way. If there is a morning, you will not be able to find the wall or the hollow or the seven stones. But somewhere, on some slab that has held still since midnight, there will be a ring the width of a hand, and beside it something that can see by ember-light will be turning a flat stone over, looking for the side that will bear weight.
