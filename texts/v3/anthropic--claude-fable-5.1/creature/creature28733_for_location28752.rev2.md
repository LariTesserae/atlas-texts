The first things that live here are smaller than you thought to look for, and they have been here longer than anything, and they are the reason the town exists at all.

Begin with the lichen. It is on the walls as it is on the shield outside — the same hard grey crust, close as paint, with a faint bloom of rust at the edges where it is putting out new growth at a rate of perhaps a millimetre in a good decade. On the shield it is ancient in the ordinary way. On the walls it is a clock. A patch the size of your palm on a chamber's outer face tells you that face has been standing, and dry enough to colonise, for something like a thousand years; and a few paces on, where the wall is pale and clean and the surface still soft enough to dent with a thumbnail, the lichen has not arrived yet, and that is how you learn which parts of the town are old and which are being made this century. The lichen does not live in the town so much as annotate it. It is not interested in the doorways. It grows straight across them, over the lip and down inside, and does not appear to notice they are there.

In the channels of the streets, where the water runs whenever it runs, there is a film of green so thin it is more a stain than a plant. This is the food. Everything else that lives here lives on it or on something that lives on it. When the sun reaches it — flat and grey though the light is, it is still sun, and at this altitude it is strong — the film photosynthesises, and in doing so it pulls dissolved gas out of the water and drives the stone out of solution, so that the green is always, very slightly, turning into white beneath itself. The algae builds the town without knowing it. Every channel is a little lower on the green side and a little higher on the white side, and that is how a street gets its kerb.

---

Then the snails.

You will have seen them already without seeing them. They are the size of a fingernail, and their shells are the same pale honeycomb colour as the walls, because the shells are made of the same mineral drawn from the same water, and they graze the green film with a sound you cannot hear but can, in the quiet between gusts, almost imagine — a dry rasp of ten thousand small tongues on stone. They move on the wet. In dry hours they withdraw into the porous walls, which are full of them, hundreds to a chamber, tucked into the hollow moss-tubes with their apertures sealed against the air by a dried skin of their own mucus, waiting for the next rain the way the whole town waits for it. When it comes they emerge all at once, and the walls seem for an hour to be perspiring snails as well as water.

They prefer the rooms. This is not mysterious — the rooms are out of the wind and the film grows thicker on the floors where the moss holds water — but it means that the only living things which mark this place mark it from the inside. Their trails on the dark cushion moss, silver in the right light, cross and recross and pass out through the doorways at floor level and in again, a lace of small decisions laid over the negative space of the marsh. And when they die, which they do in great numbers every winter, their shells fall into the channels and the water flows over them and in a season they are lost in the deposit, and the town is a fraction taller. Break a piece of old wall and look at the fracture with a lens: it is full of snails. Tiny perfect spirals in the matrix, thousands of them, generation on generation, the whole population of the place going down into the fabric of the place. They are the only inhabitants who are also the masonry.

---

The moss that made the walls is still here, in the wet corners, in the shadow at the foot of every north-facing face, and it is still making them.

It is a dark ropey kind, nearly black when wet, and it grows in tufts the size of a fist that reach up and out into the drip from the wall above. Every drop that hits it leaves a film of stone on the fronds. The fronds keep growing, because they must, up through their own crust, so that a tuft that looks like moss is moss for the top half-inch and stone for the six inches below, and the stone half is the exact cast of the moss half a year ago, and below that the year before, all the way down to the rock. The moss is not being buried. That is not how it would put it. It lives on its own roof, and every season it moves upstairs, and the house it leaves behind is what you are walking through.

Sometimes a tuft grows too far out from the wall and its weight pulls the crust away, and it falls into the channel and lands upright, and the drip finds it there, and it goes on growing, and in fifty years there is a new low wall across the street where no wall was, and the street divides around it. This is how the town changes its mind. It has no other way. There are places where you can see it happen at every stage — a fallen tuft still green, a stub of crust a hand high, a knee-high kerb with the moss long dead and the honeycomb plain, a waist-high wall with lichen on its shoulder — the same event at intervals of a century, standing in a row like a family photograph.

---

The birds are the largest thing you will see, and there are not many of them. Three pairs, perhaps four. It is hard to count them because they are all the same black, and they arrive from the same direction, and they go into walls.

They are choughs or very near it: the tapered wings, the fingered tips, the loose easy way they let the updraft carry them along the edge of the shield where the air comes up from the valley and brings insects with it. Their legs and bills are a red so clean and so out of place against all this grey that the eye refuses it at first, as though someone had painted them. They feed over the edge. They do everything else in the town. They nest in the widest of the hollow tubes, high on the walls of the oldest chambers where the honeycomb is coarse enough to hold a bird, and they line the tubes with the dark moss and their own down and the wool of some animal — there is wool in the nests, grey and coarse, and you should notice that, because there are no sheep on the shield and never have been, and you will come back to it.

Their young call from inside the walls. The call is a thin falling whistle, and it is the sound the doorways make, and after a while you cannot tell them apart and stop trying. Whether the birds have learned the town's pitch or the town's pitch was always a bird's, there is no way to say. Both have had a long time. What is certain is that no fox or stoat or hawk, hunting by ear across this featureless place, could ever find a nest here, because the whole town whistles like a nest, and the birds sit inside it as though inside a conjuror's trick. At dusk they come in low and fast over the shield and drop and are gone, and the walls have four more small warm things in them, and the drip goes on.

Their droppings whiten the lips of the nest-tubes and run down the walls in streaks, and the lime in them goes into the deposit like everything else. Even the birds are building.

---

You can go some time thinking that this is the answer — lichen, film, snail, moss, bird, and the water that carries all of them, which is the most alive thing here by any measure that counts eating and moving and making. The water arrives out of the west as cloud and settles on every cold surface and gathers itself into drops and finds its way down through the tubes and out into the channels and along the old braided course to the edge and over, and on the way it dissolves and deposits, dissolves and deposits, taking from one wall to give to another, and if you stood very still for a thousand years you would see the town breathe stone in and out the way a chest breathes air.

But none of that explains the doorways.

The film does not care where it grows. The snails come and go at the level of the floor. The birds use the tubes, not the openings. The moss makes walls where it happens to fall. And still the openings are at the height of your chest, and the width of your shoulders, and they turn to face the squares, and the flow that made them cannot have wanted any of that.

So stand in the square and be still, and stop looking for what is small.

---

It is the colour of the walls because it is coated in the walls.

That is the first thing to understand, and it is why you have not seen it, and why you may stand in a doorway for a full hour looking at it before you know that you are. Its hide is stone. Not stone-coloured — stone, a rind of the same pale honeycomb a finger thick over most of it, thicker on the back where the drip lands, cracked into plates along the flanks where it moves, and the cracks are dark and wet and warm, and if you put your hand into one you would feel it going in and out. The snails graze it. The lichen has started on its shoulders. Where the crust is oldest, along the spine, there is moss.

It is large — it fills the chamber it lies in, which is the largest, the one at the centre where the channels once pooled, and it lies there in the shape of the chamber because the chamber was made in the shape of it. Low. Long. A back that rises to about the height of your chest. Legs you cannot see, folded under or perhaps no longer distinct from the floor. A head that is only a heavier place at one end, toward the doorway, where two openings in the crust are kept clear by the passage of air, and out of these, when the west wind comes and the pressure in the town shifts, comes the great slow note.

It is breathing. That is all the note is. It has always been. Something the size of a room breathes once every few seconds and the room resonates, and you stood in the square and matched your breath to it without knowing what you were matching, and the walls carried it to you the way they carry the drip. The sleeper in the other room is a sleeper in the other room.

---

Here is how the town came to fit it.

When the springs still ran and the shield was a marsh, it was here already — one of many, then, or the last of many; the town does not say. It grazed the sedge. It lay in the pools because the pools were warm from the springs, and the moss grew up around the shape of it lying there, clump by clump, and the water ran over the moss and set down stone, and the stone took the shape of the moss which had taken the shape of the animal. When it stood and walked out, it walked the same way it had come in, along the channels, and the walls that were growing on either side grew no closer than its shoulders because it kept passing between them and knocking the soft new deposit off. Where it stepped over a low wall, the wall stayed low. Where it lay down again, another chamber began. The doorways are at the height of its back because that is the height at which it broke the walls as they closed, year after year, for longer than you can hold in your mind. The openings face the squares because the squares are where it turned.

The town is not a cast of a marsh. It is a cast of a marsh with something living in it, and the something went on living after the marsh had gone, eating the moss when the sedge failed, eating the lichen when the moss thinned, licking the sweat off the walls when the springs stopped and there was nothing else to drink. The cold here does not bite, it subtracts, and this is what is left when the subtraction has gone on for ten thousand years: something that has given up everything but the shape of itself, and is giving that up too, a grain at a time.

The wool in the nests is its wool. It sheds, still, a little, from the cracks in the plates. The birds know it well enough to take it from its flanks while it sleeps. They are the only things that do.

---

It moves in the cloud.

When the fog comes up over the edge and fills the streets to the height of the walls, and the doorways go soft and woolly and the town mutters to itself under the white, it rises. You will not see this. You will hear the note stop, and not understand why, and then a sound like a wall settling — a long low grinding, stone on stone, and the tick of small pieces falling — and then something in the fog going past the doorway you are standing in, close, at the height of your chest, so that the air moves and smells of wet iron and something warmer underneath. It goes to the edge. It stands there in the cloud, where the sound of the water falling comes up out of nothing, and it stays a long time, and then it comes back along the same street it has always used, and lies down, and the note begins again, and the fog drains, and there is a new scatter of pale crumbs along the channel floor where the crust broke as it walked, which the water will have back inside a month.

That is the oval in the moss near the square. It stopped there once, on its way, and lay down, and for how long you cannot tell — a night, a season — and the moss remembers it and will not stand up. That is all. It does not go there now. The route to the edge is shorter than it was; the walls have grown, and there are streets it can no longer use, and the number of doorways it fits through is smaller each century than the last.

---

The chamber at the centre is nearly closed.

The doorway there is narrower than the others. You noticed that without knowing what you were noticing. A tongue of new deposit hangs from the lintel almost to the floor, soft and cream-coloured, still wet; behind it the opening is a slot a hand wide, and through the slot comes the note and the warmth and, very faintly, a smell of animal, and the darkness in there is not the darkness of an empty room. The walls of that chamber are the thickest in the town. Rap them and they do not ring. They are not hollow. The drip on the inside has been landing on a back for so long that the back and the wall have made a single thing, and the water goes on falling through it, and it does not seem to mind.

It is not trapped. You should not think that. Nothing here is being done to it that is not also being done to the moss, the snails, the birds' white streaks, the town itself; it is only that it is the largest of them and the slowest, and so it is the last to go into the stone, and the going takes longest, and you are here in the middle of it. In the first hour of the morning, when the ice thaws and the town crackles, the crackling is loudest from the centre, because it is warm underneath and the frost comes off it first, and for a little while the note has a fine ticking in it, like a clock in a chest.

When the west wind comes and the whole town breathes, the drop that leaves the lip of the last doorway leaves it one grain heavier. That is the word the water is saying. It has been saying it a very long time and it has nearly finished, and when it has, the town will be quiet in a new way, and the birds will nest in the tubes, and the snails will graze the film, and the moss will move upstairs, and a shield of grey stone will lie under the sky like the lid of something, closed.
