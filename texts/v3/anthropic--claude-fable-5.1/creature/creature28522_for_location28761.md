The tenders will tell you nothing lives here. They say it the way people say a house is empty — meaning no one has signed for it — and then they spend the evening correcting themselves, one animal at a time, until the correction is longer than the claim. That is how you learn who lives on the plain: as a list of exceptions to an emptiness, each exception larger than the last, until the emptiness is the smallest thing on the list.

Start with the sky, because that is where you look first and where the most of them are.

---

**The drift**

The floaters are called drift, or lace, or nothing at all, depending on which tender you ask. Up close — and you can get close, they do not react — each is a membrane the size of an opened hand, stretched on a frame of something between cartilage and reed, so thin that the tea-coloured sun comes straight through and leaves a darker core at the centre like a seed in a slice of fruit. The core is the animal. Gut, gonad, a knot of nerve no larger than a lentil. The rest is a sail.

They were wings once. You can see the fold-lines, the ghost of a hinge along each side where the membrane used to beat. Now the hinges are trim tabs. A floater in the band does not fly; it *lies*, at the exact altitude where its density and the pressure agree, and it adjusts by fractions, and the field does the rest. It feeds through its own skin. The lifted water rides up the columns as threads and comes apart at the bands into a fine wet haze, and the haze carries dissolved stone, and the drift lie in it and drink it through their whole surface like a leaf drinking light. The tenders have found their outlines on the fresh rock in the mornings: dried films, faintly green, the shape of a hand with the fingers splayed. That is what a floater looks like when it loses trim and falls out of the sky and the ear-birds have not been quick enough.

They live for days. They breed by fraying — a floater in good condition thins at its margin and the margin detaches in a ring of daughters, each a coin-sized sail with its own dark grain of core, and the ring drifts apart in the slow rotation of the band and each daughter finds its own depth. Because the generations are so short, the drift have changed in the tenders' lifetime. The old logbooks describe them as opaque. Now you can read through them. They are larger and thinner every decade, and more perfectly held, and the oldest tender says that if you lie on your back under a band at the right hour you can hear them: each one at a pitch, very faint, the membrane ringing sympathetically with the chord, thousands of them together, a shimmer above the roar like light on water. She says the shimmer was not there when she came.

**The listeners**

Below the drift, in the wide near-silent volumes between bands, the ear-birds.

They are called listeners, or gulls by the tenders who came from coasts and want a joke. They are the size of a large hawk and built like something drawn by someone who had heard about birds. The wings are long and narrow and stiff along the leading edge, slotted at the tips, and they do not flap so much as *set* — the listener lies against the gradient the way a hand lies against a wall and moves by changing its angle to the pressure. The head is all ear. Two great cupped pinnae, ribbed, folding flat along the skull in a dive and opening wide on the glide, with the skull between them mostly hollow, a resonant chamber the size of a fist. Where eyes should be there are two dark pits that register light and dark and nothing else. A listener knows day from night. It does not know what a face is. It knows where you are to the centimetre and what your heartbeat is doing.

They roost on the undersides of the arms, hanging, or they do not roost at all and sleep on the wing in the antinodes where the pressure holds a body without effort, wings set, head folded, drifting in a slow arc a few hundred metres up. They nest in the fused spans. Where two arms have met and thickened, the moss grows deep in the join, and the listeners scrape pockets in it and lay two eggs, always two, and the eggs incubate in the vibration. There is no need to sit them; the span is warmer than blood and never still. A chick breaks its shell into the chord. It has never heard anything else and it never will, and by the time it fledges it can read the whole plain from the inside of a span the way you read a room by opening your eyes.

They do not call. There is nowhere in the spectrum to call. A flock of listeners communicates by shape: where each body sits in the gradient is a word, and the flock is a sentence, and when they hunt they rewrite the sentence at speed. The hunt is at the band edges, where the pressure tumbles a floater out into open air, and you can watch it as often as you like and never see the moment of decision. The flock is a loose net, then it is a fold, then one bird has dropped and the drift have scattered and the field has caught everything that was not eaten and put it back at depth. Whatever the listeners take that they cannot use, they drop, and the water threads carry it up the columns to the nodes. When a listener dies it falls, or it is set down — the tenders are not sure which — at the base of a column, and by morning it is in the moss, and by the next morning it is the moss.

**The ground**

Everything on the ground is deaf. Not hard of hearing. Deaf, in the way stone is deaf: born without the organ, or with the organ sealed shut generations ago behind a plate of bone. Down here the roar is not sound. It is the ground moving, and the ground moving is how they see.

The grazers are called slabs. They are the size of a table, flat, ridged, coloured the exact dark grey and faint green of the stone they crop, so that a resting herd is a set of slightly higher shelves. They have too many legs to count easily and each leg ends in a pad that reads the rock. A slab knows where the other slabs are by the hum they add to the ground; a herd is a texture in the vibration, and when one moves the texture changes and the others turn. They crop the moss in the rain-shadow of the columns, where the drip comes down as a soft constant rain of its own and the moss is a landscape — pillows, ridges, small green cliffs — and they never leave it. Fifty paces out, the stone is dry and warm and sharp and the field of the ground goes flat and featureless, and a slab that wanders there stops and stands and does not know what has happened to the world. The tenders have led one back with their footsteps. It followed the vibration of boots like a thing following a voice.

The slabs calve under the drip, in the deepest moss, and the calves are the size of a plate and soft for a day and then not. They are followed. At the second dusk — the true one, arriving on top of the plain's permanent late afternoon — something moves out from the black pools and the low fractures, and in the morning a slab is gone and the moss is pressed flat in a long track and the track goes back into the dark. No tender has seen it clearly. It is long, it is low, it has more legs than a slab or fewer, the tracks disagree. One of the tenders keeps a plaster cast of a print that could be a foot or could be the edge of a shell. They do not go out at the second dusk. They say this without embarrassment.

Standing in the pools, all day, motionless: the patient ones. They are the height of a person and shaped like a heron shaped by someone who had only heard about herons, and their bill is soft — a long grey lip, sensitive, that they lower into the black water and hold there. They feed by feel. What they feel is not known; the pools have not been sampled to any depth because the fresh joints go down further than anyone has had a line for, and because of what ripples in them. But the patient ones stand and wait and every hour or so the bill closes and lifts and something small and pale goes down the long throat. They are the only animals on the plain that seem to be at rest. They do not flinch when you walk up to them. They register your steps in the stone and dismiss them.

In the root-curtains, the climbers. Hand-sized, quick, a colour between bark and rust, running the columns in lines that never break — a procession up one root, a procession down the next, nose to tail, taking the flowers, taking the fruit. The fruit is new. The nodes began producing it within living memory, heavy dark things the size of a head hanging from the undersides of the lower arms, and the tenders did not plant it, and the tenders have not eaten it, and the climbers have been at it since the first one fell. What they do with it is carry it. Up the column, into the dark of the arch, into the spiral shaft. No one has followed them far enough to know where it goes.

And in the pools: the one that ripples. Say it is large. Say the ripples are the width of a boat and the pools are connected beneath the stone and it is, perhaps, a single animal, or a single animal's back, the plain's other resident, lying in the warm dark under the fresh rock and surfacing at the second dusk to breathe or to look. The tenders' theory — it is a theory, they say the word with care — is that there is one of it. That it was under the sea floor when the sea floor was decided, and that it has stayed.

**The pale ones in the quiet**

Stand in the exact cancellation point between four nodes and the roar drops to something you could nearly speak through. Step a metre to either side and it is back. The nulls are a metre across at most, hundreds of them across the plain, laid out on the grid like the empty squares in a pattern, and in each one lives a family of something that can hear.

They are the size of a thumb. Colourless — not white, but the colour of a thing that has never needed a colour — with soft bodies and blind puckered eyes and ears that are, in proportion, larger than a listener's. They sit in the null. They breed in the null, litters of three and four, and the young are born hearing and the first thing they hear is the chord at the one volume on the plain where the chord is bearable, coming in from every side, and they never leave. They cannot. A young one that wanders a metre is found at the edge of the quiet, curled, its ears burst, and its family does not go out to fetch it because they cannot go out either. They eat what falls into the null — a tumbled floater, the mineral rain, the rare seed the wind delivers. They drink the condensation off the stone. They are the most fragile thing on the plain and the only ones who hear it as music.

Because that is what they do. The tenders discovered it by lying down. If you lie in a null and put your ear to the warm stone the pale ones do not flee; they come closer, and after a while you understand that the thin sound you have been hearing under the roar is not a ringing in your own head. They are singing. Not a call — a matching. Small voices finding the intervals in the chord and holding them, the whole family together, tuning to the enormous thing outside and adding to it a part so faint that only the null could carry it. And the chord answers. That is the tenders' most guarded observation and they make it last and quietly. When a null-family thrives and its song thickens, the chord shifts toward it — not toward the listeners with their kilometre-wide hunting shapes, not toward the herds of slabs, but toward a metre of quiet and a handful of thumb-sized voices. The smallest things on the plain are its mouths. Every other animal is heard by the nodes as a footfall, a wingbeat, a ripple. The pale ones are heard as words.

**Inside**

The nodes are hollow. That has been said. What has not been said is that the hollows are inhabited by the nodes.

Walk in through the arch that grew itself an arch and the shaft goes up out of sight, ribbed like a throat, dim green, dripping, and every surface is alive. Not with animals — with the column. The walls are the same fibrous material as the outside, but softer here, unweathered, and they move. Not fast. A landing that was level when the tenders first surveyed it has a slope now. A doorway that was door-height is lower and wider. The tenders measured the interiors in the first decade because that was what they had come to do, and the measurements are the most disturbing document in their shelters, because the rooms were the right proportions for people when the survey was made and they are not now. The landings have broadened and flattened to the exact footprint of a resting slab. The high galleries have developed undercut lips that a listener could hang from. Certain voids in the spiral have narrowed to a passage a climber could take at speed, and the climbers take them, carrying the fruit up into the dark.

The city is measuring its tenants. It was made — if made is the word — for something the size of a person, and it has spent a lifetime finding out that no persons were coming, and it has begun to fit itself to who did. The tenders say this without deciding whether it is wonderful. One of them has stopped measuring. She says the rooms are not for her to measure any more, the way you would not measure someone else's house.

Up in the spiral, above where the light reaches, something lives that is not the climbers. The tenders have heard it — felt it, rather, through the wall, a movement in the vibration that is not the column and not the drip. It is small. It may be many. It may be what the fruit is for.

**The edge**

Nine people live within a day's walk of the plain, in shelters at the field's boundary that are mostly earth berm and double door and foam, and they are not residents. They will tell you so. They are tenders, which is a word they chose because *engineer* stopped fitting and *keeper* was too much. They came to build a city for people. They came in the second generation after the seeding, when the nodes were the height of trees, with plans, and the plans are in a drawer.

They shout. They have stopped noticing that they shout. The younger ones have hearing that comes and goes; the older ones read lips and put a hand on the table to feel who is talking. They keep a rota for walking the grid, with plugs and helmets and a limit of hours, and they come back with their teeth aching and their sight full of the tea-coloured light and they sit for a while in the quiet of the shelter, which is not quiet — nothing here is — but is enough. They keep a list of the animals. It is the worst-organised document they own. Species names in three hands, crossed out, arrowed, with margins full of arguments about whether the climbers are one kind or six and whether the thing in the pools should be listed at all.

Some have gone fond and some have gone afraid, and the two groups eat together and do not much discuss it. The fond ones have taken up sitting. There is a null a kilometre in that has no pale ones in it, and one of the tenders has begun going there without protection and staying for a day, two days, lying on the warm stone in the one square metre where his own voice exists. He says he is listening. His hearing is nearly gone and he says that is why he can do it now. The afraid ones say the chord has changed toward him — a few metres, the way it does for a new hunting line. He does not deny it.

Another keeps a garden at the shelter door. Root vegetables, herbs from the old country. The garden has begun to grow toward the plain, every stem leaning the same way, and she has stopped staking them.
