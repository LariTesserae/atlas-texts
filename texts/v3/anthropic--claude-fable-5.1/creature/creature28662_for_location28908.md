Start with the bootprint, because it is the only thing that will let you.

It is a good print. Deep at the heel, dragged slightly at the toe, the tread worn to nothing along the outer edge where a person who walks on wet brick all day learns to put their weight. The foot that made it is not large. The stride to the next one — there is a next one, on the flag beyond the leat, a dark shape on a dark surface that is already filling in — is short and even and unhurried. Whoever it was did not step over the channel. They stepped into it, on the silt edge, because that is where the foot goes and has always gone, and the water in the print is warm when you put a finger in it.

Follow the prints and they take you along the central alley to the engine house and stop at a door, and the door is shut, and behind it the beam rises and pauses and comes down.

---

They are one.

That is the first thing that resolves, though it takes a while. You had been picturing a crew — stokers, an engineman, someone for the lamps — because a works this size wants a crew, and because the sound is the sound of many things being done. But the peat stack is the size one pair of arms could keep up with. There is one rag on one valve-wheel. The path through the sedge in the north yard is one path, the width of one body, and it does not branch. The moss on the handrails is worn through in a single strip, and the strip is at one height. In the shed range there is a bench with one cup on it, upside down to keep the drip out, and one chair, and the chair's legs have gone into the soft floor to the depth of one person sitting for a very long time.

One at a time. Not one for ever — the mortar is a century old and the crazing in it older than any hand now living — but one, and then one, and then one. The works does not need more. The works, you begin to suspect, would not know what to do with more.

---

You see them the next morning, or you see the shape of them, because the fog has not thinned enough to let you see anything whole. They are on the walkway above the boiler house, moving along it with a can. The can has a long spout. They stop at each bearing and tip it and go on. They do not hold the rail. They know where the walkway sags and where it has been patched and where the fig has bent it, and their feet find the good iron without looking, the way yours find the stairs in a house you have lived in all your life.

They are not tall. The fog takes the top of them and gives back a coat — oilskin once, gone the same layered, rewrapped texture as the pipe lagging — and a pair of arms that come out of the sleeves bare and pale and shining, because the arms are wet, because everything is wet. When they reach the end of the walkway and turn to come down the ladder, you see the hands.

The hands are the thing.

They are the colour of the valve-wheels where the valve-wheels are gripped. Not pale in the way of skin kept from the sun, but pale in the way of metal kept bright by use: a worked colour, faintly luminous in the grey. The palms are broad and the fingers are thick at the joints and the nails are short and very clean, because nothing here leaves dirt on a nail — the water takes it. Across the backs of them and up the forearms there is no hair. There is, in the crease of every knuckle and along the lines of the palm, a faint darkness that is not dirt either, and when they pass beneath a lamp you understand that it is the same black film that catches the light on the weeping rock. Something that lives on wet surfaces and has found this one. They do not seem to mind it. They do not, you think, know that a hand can be otherwise.

---

They live in the boiler house.

Not in it exactly — in a room built onto it, brick, one door, one window with no glass in it because glass would only stream. The room shares a wall with the boilers and the wall is warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and against it there is a bed frame of angle-iron with a mattress that is mostly canvas and moss, and the moss is alive. It has come up through the canvas and along the frame and it is a deep, damp, springing green, and the shape of a body is pressed into it, and it is not a cold shape. This is where they sleep: on the works' warm flank, in the stroke of the beam, which comes through the wall as a slow pressure rather than a sound, four seconds, five, the pause, the descent.

There is very little else. A shelf with the cup's companion, a plate, a knife with a wooden handle gone soft and dark. A lamp in a cage, lit, bearded. Clothes on a line that never dry and are worn wet. On the sill of the glassless window a row of things that must have been picked up and kept: a brick with a maker's stamp, a fossil shell from the limestone, a length of copper pipe cut clean at both ends, a fern root the size of a fist. Nothing written. Nothing with a face on it. Nothing from outside that could not have come in on a boot.

And under the bed, on the floor, in the one place the drip does not reach, a coil of rope and a pair of long willow poles, and by the door a coat heavier than the oilskin, for going over the rim.

---

Because they must go over the rim. The works burns peat and the peat is cut from the fen, and the cutting is theirs — there is a spade in the shed range with a blade worn to a crescent and a handle polished to the colour of the hands. Twice a year, or three times, when the fog thins toward the middle of the day, the coat comes down off its hook and the door in the rim is opened and they go out into the reedbed with the spade and the poles and are gone for the length of the light.

What they eat comes from there too. Not from the bowl; the bowl gives nothing to eat, has no fish in its water and no fruit on its fig and nothing in its sedge that a person could live on. But the fen beyond the rim is a different arrangement, an ordinary one, with eels in its dykes and ducks on its meres and a scatter of rowan and elder on the old peat banks, and the tender goes into it the way a diver goes into the sea — briefly, with a purpose, holding their breath — and comes back with a sack, and shuts the door.

You would expect them to come back changed by it. The fen has birds. The fen has weather. They stand in it for a day at a time, under an open sky, with insects on their arms and the wind in their coat, and then they come down the causeway and the reeds close and the air changes and they are back in the grey and the wet and the stroke, and nothing about the way they move suggests the day outside has left a mark. They put the sack on the bench. They take the coat off. They go and look at the level.

It is possible that they find the fen loud. Not in sound — the works is louder — but in the other way, in event. Things happen in the fen. A heron lifts; a cloud passes; a fish turns over. In the bowl the only thing that happens is the engine, and the engine happens the same way every four or five seconds, and after enough years of that, a heron lifting might be an unbearable amount of news.

---

What do they do all day. You watch and you find that the answer is: the same things, in an order.

The oil can along the walkway at first light. The fires — there are three, and they are fed in turn, not to a schedule but to a sound, because the tender can hear a boiler dropping pressure before any gauge will show it and goes to it the way you go to a child who has stopped making the noise it was making. The lamps, which never go out but whose cages fill with condensation and must be tipped. The channels, walked end to end, a stick pushed into the silt at intervals to feel for a blockage. The valves. There are perhaps forty valves and each one is turned a quarter, or a half, or not at all, and there is no chart, and when you ask yourself how they know, the only honest answer is that they know the way the moss knows which pipe is warm.

And the level. Many times a day, in between everything else, they go to a particular place — the angle of brick where the main leat enters the engine house, where the water stands against a course of bricks that are paler than the rest — and they crouch and look. That is all. They look at where the water is on the pale brick. Sometimes they put a hand in. Then they stand and go and do the next thing.

There is nothing in the works that tells you what the level should be. There is no mark, no line scratched in the brick, no float or gauge. The level lives in them. It was put in them by whoever was here before, who crouched at this angle and looked at this brick with them beside them until the looking passed across, and it will pass across again when there is someone to pass it to, and if there is not, it will go with them into the moss and the bowl will fill or empty at last to whatever level water finds on its own.

They do not think about that. You are fairly sure they do not think about anything, in the sense of turning a thing over. There is nothing to turn. The works does not ask what it is for and neither do they.

---

You wonder, watching them, whether they are the inhabitant or the component.

Because look at it from the other side. The works has a beam and a flywheel and a governor and forty valves and three fires and a hundred yards of channel, and all of these are its organs, and it also has a pair of hands that go round in the morning with an oil can. The hands are warm and wet and worn bright at the grip like everything else it owns. They sleep against its side. They eat from outside and bring the eating in, and burn the fen to feed it, and in return it keeps them warm and keeps them at a level, and neither of them has ever been in a position to consider a different arrangement.

The tender does not have a name that anyone uses. There is no one to use it. When they speak — they do speak, rarely, a word or two, to a valve that is stiff or a fire that is sulking, in the tone you would use to a horse — the voice is low and hoarse and it goes into the sound of the works and is taken by it, and afterwards you cannot say whether you heard a word or a new hiss finding its place.

You might as easily say that the works lives here and the tender is how it reaches things.

---

But the works is not the oldest thing here, and it is not the most at home.

That is the moss on the warm pipe.

Go and look at it. It is on the run of two-inch iron that leaves the boiler house at head height and goes along the north alley to the shed range, and the lagging failed on it long ago, and the moss found it. It is not the moss of the channel walls, which is thick and dark and cushiony and suffers the cold. It is finer than that, and a green that has no business existing without sun — a green with yellow in it, the green of the first hour of a leaf. It grows in a sleeve around the pipe, complete, unbroken, and it is warm to the touch on the underside where the metal is nearest and cool on top where the drip lands, and between those two temperatures, along that gradient a quarter-inch deep, it has made an entire life.

It has been doing so for longer than the tender has been alive. Longer than the previous one. Perhaps since the lagging failed, which by the state of the canvas remnants was a very long time ago; perhaps it came in on the peat, a few spores in a brick of fen, and was carried past the pipe and felt it, in whatever way a spore feels, and stopped. It has never needed to be pollinated. It has never needed anything to eat it or carry it or make room for it. It needs warm, and wet, and a surface, and dim, and the works gives it exactly those four things and has never once in a century failed to give them.

There is nothing in the fen outside that is as content as this moss. There is nothing in the fen that has the same day every day.

If you want to know what lives here — not what tends it, not what runs it, but what has taken the place as it is and asked for nothing else — it is this. A sleeve of green on a warm pipe, extending along the iron by a distance nobody measures, in a direction nobody has decided, towards a shed it will reach in another hundred years and grow into and past. The tender walks under it every morning. The tender does not look up. The moss, in its way, is more the resident, and the tender more the weather.

---

And the fig, which came in on a boot and grew where it landed and has never set a fruit. And the willow, which is one tree — you are certain of it now — struck as cuttings all along the rim by some tender long back who wanted a windbreak, or wanted something that would lean in toward the warmth the way they did, so that the whole embankment is a single individual bent over the bowl, all its branches one sex, holding its grey lichen out into the fog like hair. And the purple-stemmed plant with the tomato smell, which flowers every year and closes every year unvisited and does not stop flowering, because nothing has told it to.

These live here in the way the tender does: they were brought, or came, and stayed, and the place closed round them. The moss lives here in the way the water does.

---

Then there is the thing that does not live here, and you have to describe it too, because its absence is a resident.

There is a shelf in the engine house, high up under the beam, where the swallows would have nested. You can see the ledge, the right depth, sheltered, warm from the boilers, over an alley busy with insects if there were insects. There is nothing on it. There has been nothing on it for so long that the brick is clean. Under every walkway there is a space between the grating and the pipe run where a spider would have strung its web across the drip, and the space is empty, and the drip goes through it. Along every leat there is a place on the silt edge where a heron's foot should have made a print beside the tender's, and there is only the tender's.

You look and look. In the still water of the north channel, on a day when the fog thins enough to see into it, you watch the bottom for an hour, silt and brick rubble and one white shell that turns out to be a fossil weathered out of the rock. Nothing crosses it. Not a beetle, not a worm-cast, not the shadow of a thing you didn't quite see.

It is not poison. The tender drinks the water; you see them dip the cup. The moss thrives in it. It is not the noise, or not only — birds nest in louder places. It is more that the bowl was full before anything came looking for room, and what it was full of was steam and green and one slow machine, and they had made an arrangement that had no gap in it the shape of an animal. Things came to the rim and looked down and found nothing they needed. A heron needs fish. Fish need something smaller. The something smaller needs a thing the works does not make. And the works has been not-making it for so long that the not-making has become a wall as real as the brick.

So it goes on inside the wall, with its three kinds of inhabitant — the one that was brought and stayed, the one that found the pipe and lay down along it, and the one that is not there — and the tender walks among them with the oil can and does not count.

---

Evening. The fog closes the alley and the lamps come up as points. The tender has done the last fire and stands in the door of the boiler-house room, not going in, the warm at their back and the drip in front. Their hands hang. In this light they are the brightest thing in the works, brighter than the lamps, the pale of a thing polished by a hundred years of the same grip — though these hands are perhaps forty, or sixty; the wet makes it hard to say and there is nobody here who would need to know.

The beam rises. The pause. The tender's breath, you notice, has fallen into it: in on the rise, held on the pause, out on the descent. They may have breathed that way since they were a child on a moss mattress against a warm wall, learning it before they learned to walk. They may have come from outside, once, and found it, and let the works take their lungs the way it took the fig's roots and the willow's lean.

There is no way to ask. There is nothing to ask with. When they go in and the door shuts, the print they leave on the threshold is already filling, and by morning it will be gone, and there will be another beside where it was, and the level on the pale brick will be where it has always been, and the moss on the warm pipe will be a grain further along toward the shed.

That is what lives here. Not one thing. An arrangement that has become a thing by lasting: a machine, and its hands, and a green that wants nothing, and a silence in the shape of everything else. It does not have a name because it has never had to answer to one. It has a level, and it keeps it.
