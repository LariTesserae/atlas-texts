What lives on the tableland, first and most, is the herd. But the word is wrong, and it is worth saying why before going on. A herd moves. A herd has a front and a back, animals that lead and animals that follow, a shape that changes when the weather does. This has none of that. It is an arrangement. It was laid down once, or laid itself down, and since then it has been very slowly solving the problem of how ten thousand bodies can share a surface with the least contact and the least waste, and the solution is not yet complete, and the working-out of it is the only thing that happens here.

Start with one of them. Come close — you can; they do not object to closeness, or do not register it in any way you would see. The back is the first thing. From a few feet away it is a hummock of pinkish stone, and even with your hand on it the illusion holds for a moment: the fur has been beaded and dried so many thousands of times that the mineral in the fog has come out of solution inside it, and the hairs are cased in a fine translucent crust, each one, so that what you are touching is a fleece turned to a kind of slate that still faintly gives. It is warm. Not warmer than the rock — exactly as warm as the rock, which is one of the ways the eye loses them. Along the spine the crust is thickest and there are lichens on it, black on the north side, a pale crumb of grey in the shoulder-hollow where the pool sits, and in the lee of the shoulder the crust has been worn back to something almost like hair by generations of small feet.

Go round to the head. This takes a moment; the animal is the length of a cart and you must step over the folded edge of a foreleg that has not been unfolded in a season. The head is small, absurdly small, tucked down between the front shoulders so that the crown is lower than the back, and it is turned to the stone. The eyes are set to the sides and half-lidded and the lids do not move. Nothing you do produces a blink. It is not that the animal is blind — hold a hand between the eye and the light and there is, after a long while, a minute change in the set of the muscle at the corner — but that blinking is a thing done by creatures who are keeping their eyes ready, and these are not. The mouth is broad and lipless and rests against the rock a few inches from the nearest crust of lichen. It is not eating the lichen. It is waiting for the lichen to grow to where it is. This is not a figure of speech. The animal has grazed a fan-shaped clearing a hand's width across, the lichen is recovering into it at the rate lichen recovers, and the mouth is at the far edge of the clearing, and when the black crust has closed the distance the mouth will take it, and the fan will be clear again, and this is a meal, and there are perhaps three or four of them in a year.

What does it sense? Not much, and not by the usual routes. The ears are pinholes and are mostly crusted shut, and there is nothing to hear. The nostrils work — you can see them, if you wait, open a fraction as the fog comes over and stay open until it has gone. But the great organ of the animal is the underside: the whole broad belly and the folded limbs and the low-hung jaw, all of it pressed flat to the polished stone, and through this it takes the world. The tableland is a single sheet of rock, and it carries pressure the way a drumskin does. When a foot moves half a mile off, every animal in that half-mile receives it through the ground, not as sound but as a slight redistribution of weight, an easing here and a settling there, and adjusts by some fraction of an inch, and the adjustment travels. They are in contact with each other constantly through the stone even where they are not touching. This is how the arrangement knows itself. If you could see it from high enough and fast enough — a year in a minute — you would see it ripple.

The movement itself is the second thing worth understanding. It is not slowness in the sense of a slow walk. It is that the decision and the act have been pulled apart until there is a season between them. Something in the animal registers, over months, that the lichen on the north lip of its basin is not coming back as thickly as it was, and that the stone to the left is very slightly cooler in the mornings, meaning wetter, meaning more growth, and at some point the weight begins to shift. The shift is the movement. There is no stride. The body leans, by degrees, until the leaning has become a reason for the buried foreleg to unfold, and it unfolds over a week, and the foot — a broad horn pad, unworn, because it never wears against anything — comes down a hand's width to the left of where it was. That is the sound that carries for half a mile. Then the other legs, over the following weeks, follow, and the body has moved the length of its own foot, and settles, and the ground carries the settling to everything around it, and everything around it eases by a hair. Multiply this by ten thousand and by a very long time and the arrangement drifts: not toward anything, but away from the places where it has already eaten, in a slow spiral that has been unwinding for longer than the rings have stood.

Now the backs. Each is a country, and each country has its people, and the people are the second kind of life.

They are about the length of a forearm, most of them. Four-legged, long-toed, the toes hooked at the end so that the animal can hang in the crusted fur without holding on. The skin has a dry pebbled look and it is the color of the fur it lives in, which is the color of the rock, so that the whole plateau is one shade of thing looking at itself. The eyes are large and forward and lidded slowly, and they do move — these animals blink, and turn their heads, and can be seen over the course of an afternoon to cross from one shoulder of the host to the other, which is a journey. They eat the lichen that has taken root on the back, and the crumb of grey moss around the pool, and the smaller things that live in the crust. They drink from the pool. When the host's head reaches round in the late morning to drink from the same pool, they do not move out of the way; the great low head comes up over the shoulder and the small animals sit where they are and it drinks around them. Nothing here has ever been in a hurry to be anywhere else and so nothing has learned to be in the way.

The thing to understand about them is that they are fast. Not fast to look at — you would not call it fast — but fast in the sense that matters, which is that their lives are short against the life beneath them. One is born on a back, in a fold of crust behind the shoulder where the fog collects thickest and the warmth pools; it is soft and pale for a few days and then it is the color of everything; it grows, it crosses the back, it eats, it mates with whatever else is on that back, it ages, it dies, and its shell of dried skin stays in the fur and is grown over, and in all that time the host beneath it has moved perhaps the length of two feet. To them the host is not an animal. It is the ground. It has weather — the pool rises and falls, the fog comes over the ridge of the spine and lifts — and it has seasons, in that some years the lichen on the north slope of the back grows thicker, and it has, very rarely, an earthquake, when a leg unfolds. There are generations on a single back that have never known it to move. They do not leave. Not because they cannot — a back is two feet from the next back, and a determined animal could reach it in a day — but because nothing has ever given them a reason to think there is a next back. The horizon of their world is the rim of the shoulder, and beyond it, if they look, is more of the same color, which is what the edge of the world is supposed to look like.

Below them, smaller. Flat oval things with a shell like a fingernail that live in the base of the crusted fur, pressed to the hide, eating whatever grows in the warm damp between the skin and the mineral. They are the size of a thumbnail and there are hundreds to a back and they live, as best anyone can tell, for a single turn of the fog — born in the wet, grown by mid-morning, dead by the next dawn, their shells becoming the grit that the crust is built from. And under them, and on them, the dust that moves. There is no bottom to it. Every animal is a place and every place has its animals and the only thing that changes as you go down is the speed: the lives get shorter and the horizons get closer and the weather gets larger, until at the very bottom there are things that live and die in the time it takes a bead of fog to gather on a hair and fall, and for them that bead is a flood, and the hair is a mountain, and the mountain has been there forever.

Then the drifting things. It is best to be honest that they are not understood.

They rise with the fog. Before dawn they are not visible; they are in the crust, or under it, or they are the crust — small grey bodies no larger than a lentil, soft, without limb or wing or any visible opening. When the cloud sea comes over the rim and lays itself down, they let go of whatever they were holding and go up with the wet air, and by full light they are hanging a few feet above the whole plateau, thousands and thousands, in a layer so even it looks like a second fog that has forgotten to move. They are held there by the warmth, which does not vary, and by a lightness that no one has accounted for. They do not eat. They do not appear to breathe. In the long flat middle of the day they turn very slowly, each around its own center, and drift a little, and by evening they have crossed perhaps thirty backs, and then the air cools by its one degree and they come down, and settle on whatever is beneath them, and are gone into the crust.

What is known is this. They are the only thing on the tableland that touches many backs in a lifetime. Everything else is born, lives, and dies on one place; only these go from place to place, and they do it without choosing, carried on a wind so slight that it cannot be felt. And wherever they settle, the lichen on that back is the same lichen as on the back they rose from, and the flat shelled things are the same, and the dust is the same. They are, whatever else they are, the way the tableland stays one thing. Every back would long ago have gone its own way — its own lichen, its own small people, its own shade of the color — if not for this soft grey weather that stirs them all together every day and casts no shadow doing it.

There is a guess, which is only a guess, that they are also the young. Not the young of the large animals — no one has seen those and this would not explain it — but young in the general sense: that the tableland has arrived at a way of being born that does not involve any of the trouble, and that these are it, and that when one of them settles on a back where something has died, something is there in the morning that was not there before. No one has watched long enough to say. Watching long enough is the problem here. Everything worth seeing takes longer than the person seeing it.

The bones are worth a word, because they live too. A skeleton lying among the herd is not an absence. The lichen has it. The grey moss has it, where the fog pools in the curve of a rib. The small long-toed animals from the neighboring backs have found their way down and across — the only time they ever leave — and live in the cage of it, and the shelled things live on them, and the dust on those, and in the mornings the drifters rise from the ribs as from any other back. Nothing has been taken away. The large animal has simply stopped being the ground for its country and the country has stayed, as countries do. In time the bones will be crusted the same as the fur was and the outline will soften and it will be another low hummock the color of the rock. It will be very hard to say, then, what the difference is.

And the people.

They came without tools, which is the thing that keeps being strange. There are no chips, no flakes, nothing worked. The rings are of rim-stone carried in and set down, a few dozen fragments each, knee-high, unmortared, not fitted — just placed, close enough to stand as a ring. The grooves are harder. They are straight and shallow and a hundred yards long, and there is no water on the tableland that runs in a line, and no animal that walks in one. But stand at the end of one and look along it and it is exactly the width of two feet set together, and it is worn, not cut — polished into the polish, a shallow trough of a slightly deeper sheen. Someone walked it. Walked it out and walked it back, and out and back, for long enough to wear a groove into a rock that ten thousand animals have been unable to mark, and then stopped at the end, and the groove stops there too.

Put the rings and the grooves together and something appears. A person comes up out of the white with nothing in their hands. They walk. They walk the same hundred yards, for reasons that stay with them, until the walking has gone into the stone. Then they go to the rim and bring back stones and set them in a ring, and they sit down in the ring, and after a while they lie down, and the fog comes over them in the morning and beads on them and they drink it as everything here drinks it, and they lie there. And they are still. And after a long time an animal that has been drifting on its spiral for longer than anyone has words for arrives at the ring and eases itself down into the middle of it, exactly where the person was, and stays. There is no need to say anything grim about this. The rings are the right size for a person lying down. They are also the right size for one of the animals. Perhaps that is the same size.

The slab on the eastern edge is the tallest thing here and it says nothing. It was set up — it did not fall there — and the hollow at its foot was made by fog and by a mouth. It may have been meant as a marker, or a thing to be seen from far off across all this flatness, or a thing to lean against; it is the only surface on the tableland a person could put their back to. The lichen on its lee side is the shape of an animal's breath. It is the closest thing to a mark anyone has left, and it was not left by the person.

So. What lives here.

An arrangement of ten thousand bodies that feels itself through the ground and moves by leaning and eats by waiting for its food to arrive. On each body, a people who think it is the earth. On each of them, a people who think the same, and on each of those, more, down to the ones for whom a drop of water is the whole of history. Over all of it a grey weather of small soft lives that carry the tableland's sameness from back to back and may be how it is born. Among it, the dead, still being lived on. And in the rings, where the stones were set by hands that brought nothing else, the animals that came to lie where the people lay, and have not moved, and are not going to.

They are all the same color. That was not always so. It is what happens when things stay long enough in one place: the place gets into them and they stop being distinguishable from it, and this is not a loss, though from outside it might look like one. The people who came here — one at a time, with nothing — may have been looking for exactly this. Not to be gone, but to be ground. And the animals may not be the ones who found the tableland; they may be what the tableland does, given time, to whatever lies down on it and stays.

In the last hour of the day the fog comes back over the rim and the horizon loses its edge and the drifters come down. Ten thousand breaths go in and, minutes later, out. Somewhere very far off a foot moves, and everything for half a mile feels it in the stone and eases by a hair, and settles, and is still.
