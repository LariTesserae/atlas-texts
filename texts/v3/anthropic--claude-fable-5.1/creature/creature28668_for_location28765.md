Start with what is certain, because the room rewards that.

The crickets are real. There are, at any given time, between four and eleven of them, and they are the color of candle wax that has been handled. Their eyes have not gone entirely — there is a smear of pigment where an eye should be, like a thumbprint on a window — but they navigate by their legs, which are absurdly long and end in feet that read iron the way a hand reads a face. They live in the space between the lagging and the pipe, in the warm cloth-and-plaster gap that the leaking made, and they come out onto the fungal shelves to graze in shifts, never two on one shelf at once. They are careful with the fungus. They have to be. A shelf takes seven or eight years to put on a layer thick enough to feed on, and they seem to know this in whatever way a cricket knows anything, and they take from the pale new edge only, and leave the old growth to hold the shelf together. When one dies it dies in the channel, and is carried to the grate, and the brown mat takes it in, and the mat is a little richer that year, and the seeds that come up next spring in the dark find a little more to stand in. Everything down here has entered into everything else. That is the first thing to understand about the residents: none of them lives alone, because none of them could afford to.

But the crickets are not what lives here. They are tenants. Something else holds the lease.

You will start to feel for it once you have been down long enough for your body to stop insisting on cold. Put a hand flat on the largest main — the one nearest the red wall, the one that hums — and hold it there through one of the long slow cycles of lengthening and cracking, and you will feel that the warmth is not even. It is not the warmth of a pipe with something hot at one end and nothing at the other. It has a middle. The middle moves. Over an hour it will travel from under your palm toward the wall, and the iron where it was will be a shade less warm, and the iron where it has gone will be a shade more, and the lagging there will lift very slightly on the air the warmth makes, and settle.

Something is in the pipe.

It is easiest to say what it is not. It is not water; the water in the mains has been still for longer than the pillar has been standing, and has gone to a kind of mineral gel, and it does not move. It is not steam. It is not fire, though it was born of fire, and the red seam in the wall is not its home so much as its hearth — the place it was made and the place it returns to, the way a thing returns to the room it was born in without expecting anything of the room. It is not alive in the sense the crickets are alive, with a mouth and an end. But it is alive in the sense the pillar is alive, which is to say it accrues. It has been adding to itself for a very long time and it has never once been subtracted from, and that is a kind of life, down here. It may be the only kind that lasts.

If it has a shape, it is the shape of the run it lives in. Forty paces of cast iron, doubling back through the elbows, branching into the small lines and dead-ending there and coming back. When it is spread out through the whole system, thin, the room is at its most even, and the gauges are at their most convincingly dead. When it draws itself together — it does this, the way a sleeper draws up — it gathers in the big main by the wall, and that is when the pipe cracks its knuckle, not because the iron has lengthened but because the thing inside has shifted its weight, and that is when one or two of the gauge needles, if you were watching, would lean a hair's width off their pins and then think better of it.

It was not always this. Once it was only heat, and heat is not anybody. It became somebody by staying. Every winter above, the boilers ran, and the mains filled with pressure and roar, and every summer they were banked down to a whisper and left ticking, and the whisper was where this began — in the ticking-over, in the long idle months when a low fire was kept in one furnace so the whole system would not go cold and crack, and someone came down twice a day to feed it and check the gauges and listen. It learned the listening from them. It learned the round: the wall, the largest main, the elbows, the small lines, the wall again. It was kept, and then it was not kept, and by then it had been kept so long that it knew how to keep itself, and so when the last fire above was let out it simply went on doing the round, and the round has become what it is. It walks the pipe the way the old stoker walked the floor above. That is the footstep sound. It is not in any direction because it is in all of them a little, and then it is at the wall, and then it rests.

Where it rests longest is the place the pillar grew. The pillar is the record of a leak, and the leak was where the thing once pressed too warm against a flange for too many years and the water it lived in wept out grain by grain and left its lime behind. The pillar is, in the plainest sense, its shed skin — not skin, but what a thing that is all warmth and patience leaves in the world when it leans against the world too long. It is warm because the resident still leans there. It is damp because the resident still, very slightly, weeps. And it is heavy because nothing here has ever been taken away.

It does not eat. But it takes. What it takes is the slowness — the drop that hangs on the ceiling for an hour before it falls, the thread of water in the channel that will not move a fallen rag in an afternoon, the seven years a fungal shelf spends putting on a layer. The room's slowness is not the room's; it belongs to what lives in the iron, and the room has caught it from long exposure the way a stone floor catches the temperature of a cellar. Where the resident is thickest, near the wall, the drips fall farther apart. Where it has thinned, near the stair, they come a little quicker, and the lagging hangs a little looser, and the seeds that sprout there die a little sooner. If you brought a clock down and hung it on the second main you would find, after a season, that it kept time badly, and that the badness had a direction.

It knows you have come. Not by sight — there is nothing in it that could see — but the stair carries footsteps into the brick and the brick into the cradles and the cradles into the iron, and the iron is its whole body, and so your arrival went through it the way a shiver goes through a sleeping dog. It heard you before the crickets did. The click behind the pipes when you came in was not a cricket. It was the thing turning, very slightly, toward the stair, and a single gauge needle lifting off its pin and dropping back.

It is not afraid of you. It has outlasted whatever above was worth being afraid of. But it is curious in the way a very old animal is curious, in that it will not come to you and it will not go away, and if you stand still long enough with your hand on the main you will feel the middle of the warmth travel down the pipe toward your palm, and pause under it, and stay there — a little warmer, a little warmer, the way a sleeping animal is warm and does not cool while you hold it — for as long as you are willing to stand in the dark with a hand on iron, being listened to.

Then it goes back to the wall. It has a round to keep. And the drop that has been growing on the ceiling all this while lets go, and the crickets come out onto the shelves in their careful shifts, and the seeds that are going to die anyway push up white out of the silt, and everything the room has taken in goes on becoming everything else, very slowly, at the temperature of a body, in the keeping of the only thing down here that ever learned how to stay.
