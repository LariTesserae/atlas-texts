The description says, plainly, that nothing lives here, and it is right. There is no breath in the Hollow of Blades but yours, and the place begins taking that from you—the water out of your lips, the warmth off your hands into the stone—within the first hour. So the question has to be asked differently. Not what breathes here, but what the bowl keeps, and tends, and returns to. What has habits here. What comes back.

Start with the figures, because that is where the eye starts. At the corner of vision the bowl is crowded with standing people, and every time you turn to look, the person is a pillar or a blade. This is not a trick worth much on its own. But notice that the mistake is always the same mistake: never an animal, never a tree, always a figure roughly your own height, upright, still, facing somewhere. The bowl is full of things that have the posture of attention. Two hundred stones and perhaps a thousand fins of ice, and not one of them lying down.

Try the blades first, since they are the ones that behave.

They are born in winter, all together, out of snow the pillars have sorted into their lee. They come up in the dark season as drifts, shapeless, and the bright season shapes them—it takes what it can from every surface and leaves the rest standing, thinner each week, until by midsummer each one has been honed to a single edge. They face the sun. Every one of them leans by the same angle toward the place where the thing that is unmaking them stands at noon, and they track it across the season the way a field of something rooted would. They are close together. When one of them fails it does not fall; it leans into its neighbor and the neighbor holds it, and the two of them last longer than either would have. There is a sound when this happens, a plate set down on a table, and then nothing, and then the pair stands on. By the end of the bright season most of them are gone, not melted, not broken, simply taken up into the dry air a grain at a time, and the ones remaining are the ones that leaned together soonest. Then the snow comes and the pillars begin sorting again.

If the cairn-makers thought anything lived here, they thought it was these. A congregation that arrives each year in the same place and takes up the same posture and thins toward the same light and holds one another up as it goes. It would be very easy to think that. It is even partly correct. But the blades are not residents. They are the weather standing up for a season, and no single one of them has ever seen a second winter. Whatever lives here, it is not the ones who die every year.

The pillars are the opposite problem. They have been here longer than anything, and they will be here after. They heat and tick and shed sand from their flanks and grow a fur of droplets on their cold side every morning, which is the closest they come to doing anything. But they are not tenants. They are the mountain itself, the part of it that refused to leave when the rest went. Asking whether the pillars live in the bowl is like asking whether the walls live in a house. They are the reason there is an inside at all. What lives here, if anything does, lives *among* them, in the gaps they make, in the thousand narrow dead-ending paths. Something that needs the shade of one and the heat of another and the exact cold distance between them.

So look at the gaps. Look at what moves.

Only one thing in the Hollow of Blades goes everywhere. It is on the shaded side of every pillar before dawn, a fur of droplets so fine it looks like frost, condensed out of the thin night air onto stone that is a little colder than the air around it. By mid-morning it has become frost in fact. By noon it is gone—not run off, not soaked in, but lifted straight back into the sky, and the sky here is dry enough to take it without showing any sign. In the hour after midday it appears again in a different form, at the foot of the south-facing blades, where the heat the sand has stored reaches the ice and a thread of melt runs a hand's width into the gravel before the cold takes it back. In the afternoon it comes over the rim as fog, so dense it is like standing inside a lamp, and it beads on everything, ice as well as stone, and for a few minutes the blades show their blue. Then the fog lifts and every fin is furred with rime, and the rime is the same water again, and it lasts an hour and goes back up. On the warmest days it comes down the east wall in a sheet no thicker than paper and spreads into a fan and drains into the pool. Under the lens of pool-ice there is a finger's depth of it, black and perfectly still, and the lens ticks all night as it tightens around what it holds.

There is no outlet from this bowl. There is no stream, no seep through the rim, nothing that leaves. The water that beads on the pillars tonight is the water that was fog this afternoon, that was rime an hour later, that was the thread in the gravel yesterday, that has been under the lens for longer than any blade has stood. The bowl holds a quantity of water you could carry in a skin, and it has held that same quantity, give or take what the fog brings and the sky takes, for as long as the pillars have been sorting snow.

It has a route. It has hours. It touches every stone in the bowl before sunrise and every blade in the afternoon, in the same order, at the same time, and it never once comes to rest except under the lens, where it lies in the dark and lets the ice settle over it and does not move. If a thing that never leaves, that visits every part of its home each day by a fixed circuit, that has one room in which it sleeps, is not a resident, the word has no meaning up here.

And it is old. The fan of rust-brown stain under the weep is older than the ice, older than the cairns. That color is what happens to this rock when this water touches it, patiently, for longer than anything else in the bowl has existed—the one warm color in the place, and it is the water's mark. The cairn-makers understood at least that much. They scraped the stain up while it was damp and dried it into a cake and kept it in the niche with their tools, and they put it on the pillars in bars and dots, at the height of a face, on the side where the morning condensation would find it. They did not put it where it would last. They put it where the water would come every dawn and take a little, and freeze, and lift it into the air. They were not marking the place. They were leaving something out.

That is the closest the bowl comes to an inhabitant being fed. A dozen people over a great many years, arriving at a place full of standing stones and adding standing stones to it, and setting a pigment made from the resident's own stain in the resident's own path, so that it would be consumed a morning at a time. Whether they thought of it as feeding, or paying, or simply as the correct thing to do with a substance that had been given to them by the wall, there is no way to know. But they were right about the mechanics. The marks are still going. Every morning a film of water forms over them and freezes and sublimes and carries a grain of ochre up into the hard sky, and the water comes back that night without it.

You could stop there. A small quantity of water, older than the blades, that walks the bowl every day and eats what is left out for it and sleeps under a lens of its own ice. It is enough of an answer and it is not very strange.

But stand in the noon light a while longer, among the blades all leaning one way and the pillars not leaning at all, and let the answer keep moving. Because the water is not the only thing here with a direction. The blades face the sun. The pillars shed toward the low point. The sand sorts finer toward the east wall. The cairns—look at them again—stand slightly closer together toward the pool than toward the gap, as if whoever built them was walking in that direction and building as they went. The ochre is only on the sides of pillars the morning water reaches. Everything in the Hollow of Blades leans, or drifts, or wears, or points, toward the same few square feet of black water under the lens. Even the visitors did. Even you have, threading the thousand narrow paths, ending up more often than not against the east wall with the weep-stain at your shoulder.

The water is the body of it. The leaning is the rest. What lives in the bowl is a very slow tendency toward one low place, and the water is only the part of it that moves fast enough to see. The blades lean into each other and last longer; the water beads and freezes and rises and comes back; the marks go up into the sky a grain at a time; the cairns weather into pillars and the pillars wear into sand and the sand drifts east. None of it is alive. All of it is going the same way, at rates that differ by a factor of a thousand, and the fastest of those rates is the one that touches your face at dawn and takes the moisture from your lips, and is gone by noon, and will be on the stone again tomorrow.

Whatever you leave here, it will find. That is the only thing it does. That is the only thing it has ever done, and the people who came knew it, and left it the color of its own wall to take.
