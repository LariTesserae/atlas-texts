The first answer is the heron, and it is not wrong. It is only the first answer.

Look at it longer than you want to. It stands with one foot in the water and one on the sedge, and the water around the submerged foot does not ripple, and the sedge under the other does not bend. Grey on grey. The eye is a pale ring around a dark point and it does not blink, and it does not track you as you move, and it does not need to, because wherever you move to, the heron is facing away. This is not avoidance. Circle it, slowly, through the horsetail, and you will come round to find the back of its head, the fold of the neck, the shoulders. It has a front, and the front is for something else.

When it stabs, it stabs at water in which you have seen nothing. What it brings up is dark and wet and thrashing, and you cannot say whether it thrashes because it is alive or because it is being brought out of the only place it could be. Then the beak tilts and the thing goes in and there is no swallow. No bulge travels the neck. The heron is not heavier. Whatever it was has ceased to be outside the heron, and that is all that has happened to it, and the heron resumes its posture, and the posture has not changed.

A fen this wet should be alive with midge. It is not. The air over the water is clean, and the heron is standing in every clearing, in the same posture, facing away.

So the heron is not what lives here. It is what lives here keeps a heron, the way a house keeps a door. It is a fixture, like the weeping rocks; it does its one thing continuously and has done it for long enough that the doing and the heron have become the same shape. Its croak arrives a half-second before its beak opens. Things here run slightly ahead of themselves, or behind, and the heron is one of the things, not the one who set them running.

---

The second answer is the sleeper.

You saw it once — the long swell under the sedge, thirty feet of ground rising and settling, alders tilting and righting as it went. Something with a back. Something that rolls over. Things that roll over are asleep, and things that are asleep are alive, and so here, at last, is a candidate: an animal, very large, lying under the peat, and the fen is its blanket and it has been under the blanket for a long time.

But consider what did not happen when it moved. The roar did not change. The reeds did not stop rattling. The water in the nearest pool did not slop. If the sleeper were the source of the sound, or the pulse of the water, or anything the fen depends on, then the fen would have flinched when it turned, and the fen did not flinch. The fen is not built on the sleeper. The sleeper is one more thing the fen has grown over, along with the fourfold trunk and the weeping stones and whatever the green fibrous shred in the split bark used to be. Perhaps it was what the place was made to hold — perhaps the water was taught its extra weight so that it would lie heavy on that back and keep it turning in its sleep rather than waking. Perhaps it is the maker, who lay down when the work was done, and found the work a good place to lie. There is no way to know and the fen does not offer a way. What is clear is that the sleeper does not live here the way a fish lives in a river. It is more that here lives on it.

---

So: the roar.

You have been treating it as weather. It is loud the way rain is loud; you speak over it, then shout over it, then stop speaking. But stand still on a tussock and put both palms flat and hold them there until your hands go the temperature of the water, and attend.

It is not voices. That was the first thing the description got right, that there is no living voice in it. But it has the *shape* of many things said at once. It rises and falls, not in rhythm but in cadence — the way a crowd behind a wall rises when something happens in the room you cannot see, and subsides when the thing is over, and rises again. Under the palms the tremor in the peat fibres has the same quality: not a pulse, not noise, but a texture, a thousand small actions overlapping. Something is being done here. Continuously. By many hands, or by one thing large enough that its parts do not all move together.

The moss is part of it. You have caught the script at the edge of your vision and lost it when you looked. Stop looking, then, and let the edge of your vision do the reading. The pattern is not fixed. It is being laid down and taken up — a line of tufts thickens over the course of the grey day and another thins, and by evening a passage that was there in the morning is ordinary moss and a passage that was ordinary is now, at the edge of sight, nearly legible. The fen is writing. Or the fen is correcting something it wrote, the same something, over and over, never quite to its satisfaction, and the sound of that correction is the roar, and the tremor is the pen.

The water is part of it. It routes. It goes somewhere at noon and comes back by evening, with no channel, and this is not hydrology. It is circulation. The rocks weep the fen's own water — the too-heavy water, the water that has been taught — and everything that water touches learns a little. Cup your hand under a weeping stone and your hand will be colder than it should be, for longer than it should be, and when the cold finally leaves it does not leave all at once but drains away toward the nearest pool, and you can almost follow it.

The thunder is part of it. Long, walking, from beneath the horizon, without lightning. Not weather. Punctuation.

---

Here is where the answer begins to become itself, if you let it.

What lives here is one thing, and it has no edge. It is not the fen, exactly — the fen is what it is made of, the way a body is made of water and the body is not the water. It is what was left when the engineering finished. Not the builders, who are gone, or asleep, or weathered into stone that has no seam. What the builders were building. Whatever it was for, it is complete, and being complete it does the only thing a complete thing can do, which is maintain itself and notice.

It notices. That is the sensation at the pool's edge with your shadow lying faint on the underside of the cloud. You are lit from below. Something below is looking up, and the light of its looking falls on you and passes through you and goes on into the felt of the sky, and your shadow is the small place in that regard where you are standing.

Its life is slow enough that you would not call it a life if you had not been told there was nothing else here to call one. It circulates its water and that is its blood. It grows the willow shoot a hand-length while your back is turned and that is its healing. It holds the same alder trunk across the same channel in four places because it can hold only so many shapes at once and it needs a crossing in each of those four places, and it is not troubled by the economy of this. It keeps a heron at the edge of every pool, facing away from wherever the visitor is, toward wherever the visitor is not yet. It keeps a sleeper. It keeps the rain in one part of the sky and the cloud in another and does not concern itself with whether this is how rain works elsewhere. It writes in moss and rubs out and writes again. Its day is the long grey pulse, and its year is longer than you will be here to measure, and it has been doing exactly this — not becoming, *being* — for so long that it has no memory of beginning and no expectation of an end, and if it has any equivalent to a thought, the thought is the roar, and the roar does not change.

That is one inhabitant. It is the only one that was ever meant to be here.

---

But there is the green shred in the split bark of the fourfold trunk.

It was cloth. You knew that the first time and un-knew it because the fen so plainly has nothing to do with cloth. Look again — at the third of the four trunks, or the first; it does not matter. Fibrous, weathered to the colour of the willow leaves, caught in the split as if someone crossing the trunk had snagged a sleeve and pulled free and not stopped to look. It is in the same place on all four. Whoever snagged it snagged it once and the fen holds the moment in four places, as it holds the trunk.

So there have been others. Not many. Over a very long time, a handful. Each one arrived as you arrived and stood on the ground that is a decision and looked for high ground and found only places where the water was slower. Each one picked out a stand of alders and walked toward it and arrived at a stand of alders. Each one turned and saw the stand they had left, at about the distance they had walked, exactly as it had been. Some walked for a day. Some for what would have been weeks anywhere that measured weeks. The fen did nothing to them. It never does anything. It is wet and cold and loud and dim and the paths return you and the light comes from below.

Eventually each of them sat down.

There is no violence in it. A body at the temperature of something that has been in water all night, sitting on peat that gives back some of the weight, with the roar in its hands. The moss is quick. The willow grows a hand-length. The peat beneath holds the shape a moment too long and then settles, not to where it was, but to where it should have been. No moment marks it — no more than a moment marks the point at which a dug channel becomes a river. At some point the person is no longer outside the fen. That is all that has happened to them.

Not dead. The fen has no use for the distinction and neither, after enough time, do they. Look instead at the pools that reflect. Most give back only peat and drowned stems, but some give back a sky that is not this sky — stars, or full sun, or copper — in the correct position for a reflection, with nothing above to account for it. Each of those pools reflects the sky over the place someone came from. The copper sky belonged to one. The full sun to another. The stars very clear and very close and not in any arrangement you recognise: that one came a long way. The water is slightly too heavy because it carries them, in solution, the way water carries salt — not their bodies, which are peat now and alder and the film of something on the ferns, but the weight of where they were from and what they were looking for when they picked out that first stand of alders. The heron faces away from you because it faces toward them. They are always in the direction you have not yet walked.

You may see one. At the very edge of the twenty paces beyond which everyone is alone, a figure standing at a pool's margin, grey, facing away, in the heron's posture. You will shout and the shout will go ten paces and stop. You will push through the horsetail toward it, and the horsetail will hiss, and you will arrive at a pool's margin, and there will be a heron there, or a grey willow leaning out over the water, or nothing at all — and behind you, at about the distance you have walked, a figure standing grey at a pool's edge, facing away.

---

So what lives here is one thing, and what lives here is several, and there is no longer any seam between them. That is what *ceased to be outside* means. The heron does it in a moment. The fen does it over a period of time that does not, here, feel like a period of time at all.

The place is not hostile. It should be said again, because everything above might make it sound otherwise. It has never pursued anyone. It has never closed a path or raised the water or sent the sleeper turning toward a visitor's feet. It is patient the way a pool is patient — not waiting for anything, simply there, and level, and cold, with time in it. It is finished and you are not, and finished things have a way of drawing unfinished ones in, not by force, only by there being nowhere else in the basin for a thing to go once it has stopped walking.

Toward what you take to be evening the thunder comes more often and from more directions until it is a floor, and the rain drifts slantwise out of the one bright place low in the west, and the pools rise, and the weeping rocks run faster into their shallow bowls, and the heron is at the edge of the nearest pool with its back to you and the water beside it full of close, clear, unfamiliar stars.
