The first thing to understand about the people is that they are wet, and have stopped noticing.

Not soaked. Nobody here is ever soaked, because it never rains. They are damp in the way the stone is damp: through, evenly, without complaint. Their hair holds beads the way the fern-fronds do. Their wool is heavy with it. Their skin has a particular softness at the knuckles and the backs of the hands, a pale puckered look, as if they had spent their lives at a washtub, and their feet — the feet are a subject of constant low attention, of wooden soles and oiled leather and the nightly business of drying them by whatever warmth can be found, which is the one thing the drying houses give freely. A child of the plateau learns before it learns to speak that a foot left wet will rot, and this is the first of the things that are understood here.

They are pale. Several hundred meters of water stand between them and the sun and have for generations, and the light that reaches them is the light of a room with the curtains drawn. Their eyes are large and a little slow to close, made for the grey. Their chests are deep, because the air is thin and there is no getting more of it except by taking more at a time, and so they breathe slowly and walk slowly and speak in short sentences with rests between. Someone brought up the cable from below — and this happens, once in a long while — is known immediately not by their clothes or their accent but by the way they gasp on the stairs, and the plateau people watch this without unkindness and do not comment. It passes in a season. The lungs learn or the person goes back down.

They are not many. A thousand, perhaps; nobody counts, or if someone does it is not spoken of. There is a sense, never quite said, that a count would be a kind of admission.

---

They are the color of their trades.

The cutters are stained to the elbow, a brown that goes into the skin and does not come out, and they smell of the beds — the sour, mineral, faintly sweet smell of things that have been dead for a very long time in the absence of air. They work in the shallow basins beyond the screens, in the open, in the cloud, with long blades and a rhythm that is a little like rowing, and they come back along the rails behind the low carts with their heads down and their shoulders rounded and say almost nothing until they have eaten.

The dryers are red. Their faces and forearms have the shine of skin that lives near a fire in a cold place, and they cough, all of them, a small dry cough that is the only dry thing on the plateau. The drying houses are the warmest rooms in the town and the most sought after and the hardest on the body, and the dryers age faster than anyone and are, on the whole, the most cheerful. This is not explained.

The pressers are hard in the hands and quiet even by the standards of the place, because the mills are loud by the standards of the place and they have learned to say what they need with a glance and a tilt of the head. The screen crews are the wettest, if such a thing can be measured — they walk the rows out beyond the last lamp, checking the wire for tears and the troughs for blockages, and the cloud comes through the mesh and through them equally. They are the only people who go out of sight of the town as a matter of routine, and they have a look, a way of standing, that is a little apart. The moss crews are green at the knees and green at the fingertips and are the only ones who joke, because their work is the only work that is openly futile and everyone knows it.

And there are the surveyors, who are few and old and are not really a trade so much as a kind of memory. The gradient that carries the water through the town is so slight that it cannot be seen; it can only be known, and the knowing was assembled over lifetimes by people lying on their bellies with water-levels and string, and it lives now in a handful of heads and in books that are kept wrapped in oilcloth in the driest room anyone has. When a channel silts or a wall shifts and the water hesitates, a surveyor is sent for, and comes slowly, and looks for a long time, and says a small thing, and the water moves again. There is a saying that the town is the surveyors' and everyone else is a guest in it. It is said fondly and it is probably true.

---

They wear wool because nothing grazes here — there is no animal on the plateau larger than the pale beetle, and so every fiber they wear has come up the cable from below, and they treat it accordingly. A coat is a serious object. It is oiled and waxed like the leaves of the low trees, and it is patched, and patched over the patches, and when it is finally beyond wearing it is not thrown away, because nothing here is thrown away — there is nowhere to throw it to. It goes on the fire, or it goes into a wall to stop a draught, or it goes out to the beds and is laid in the moss, where in a few seasons it will be moss.

They eat what comes up the cable, mostly — grain, salt, things in tins, and a hard dark bread that is baked here from flour that came from below — and what little grows: a few greens in the warm exhaust of the drying houses, a bitter leaf gathered from the tree ferns, a broth made from the moss itself in lean years, which is said to keep a person alive and is not said to do anything else. Their water they do not think about at all. It is the one thing they have more of than anyone, and they are careless with it in a way that would shock the lower world; a cup is filled and drunk from and set down and forgotten, and in an hour it is full again.

---

They keep time by the mills.

There is no sun to keep it by. The lamps burn always, the cloud is always the same, and so the day is the pulse — the groan and thud and hiss that comes through the walls — and its slowing at the changes of shift, and the long pause in the deep of the night when the pressers sleep and only the wheels turn. People wake to the first thud. They eat between the fourth and fifth. A child who has been out too long is told it is past the ninth and should have been home by the seventh, and the child knows exactly what is meant. There are clocks, brought up from below, and they hang in a few houses and are wound and consulted the way one consults a foreign guest, politely and without conviction.

They sleep long. The cold and the grey and the thin air ask for it, and there is nothing after dark to keep anyone from it, dark being a matter of degree. They sleep under many layers and near each other, and their houses are small and low and crowded on purpose, and the outer quarters, where the lamps have gone out, are avoided not because they are frightening but because they are cold and there is no one in them, and a room is only warm here if there is someone in it.

They speak little, and what they say is mostly about water. They have a great many words for the cloud — for its thickness, for the direction of its drift, for the fine kind that comes through the mesh without stopping and the heavy kind that clogs it, for the moment it begins to lift and the moment, more important, it begins to close again. They have words for the sounds of drip: the fast light patter off the ferns, the slow heavy plock off the pipes, the almost-silent one off the moss itself, which is not a sound so much as an increase in weight. They have a word for the grey that comes in the hour before the cloud lifts, and it is not a happy word.

They do not shout. There is no one far enough away to shout to. Everything is a few meters ahead and then it is here.

---

They are not, on the whole, curious about the lower world, and this is perhaps the strangest thing about them and the thing they would find strangest to be asked.

Things come up the cable. Wool, iron, glass, wire, salt, tins, occasionally a person. Things go down: the pressed blocks, bound and stacked, and occasionally a person. The cable crews at the rim work in the white with the drop under them and are the only ones who have seen anything of what is below — a glimpse, they say, sometimes, when the cloud thins at the very edge: a green that is a different green, a line that might be a river, a light at night that is not a lamp. They are asked about this by children and by no one else. The adults have the sense, not quite articulated, that whatever is down there is a place where things dry out, and that this is not to be envied; that it is warmer, and brighter, and that people there talk more and die of different things; and that they have no wish to go. When someone does go — and someone does, every few years, a young one usually, on the cable with a bag — it is spoken of the way one speaks of a death, briefly, with a small shake of the head, and then not again.

---

They lay their dead in the moss.

Not in the cutting basins. In a basin further out, past the last row of screens, where the moss is deepest and has never been touched, and where the walk is long enough that a body must be carried by six people taking turns and the carrying is the ceremony. There are no stones. Stones would only be moss in a year. The body is laid in a trench opened in the living moss with the same long blades the cutters use, and the moss is folded back over it, and the people stand for a while in the cloud and say very little, and walk back. In a season the place cannot be found. In a lifetime it is a meter down. In a long time it is peat.

They know this. They know what the peat is and what is done with it and where it goes. The far basin is not cut and never will be, and this is one of the things understood, but they are not sentimental about the deeper beds, the old ones, the ones the cutters open every day — those are so old that whatever lies in them is older than the town and older than anyone's grief. They burn it and press it and send it down without a thought, and the smoke goes up into the cloud and comes back as drip, and they stand in the drip and wipe it from their faces and do not think about it at all. Or they think about it the way they think about the cold. It is a fact about the place.

---

The children are the least wet things on the plateau, briefly.

They are born red and dry into rooms kept as warm as rooms can be kept, and they are wrapped, and for a while — a year, two — they are kept in, and they are the reason the drying-house quarter is where the young families live despite the cough. Then they go out, and they go green at the knees like everyone, and they learn the things. They learn the pulse. They learn that a foot left wet will rot. They learn which moss will bear weight and which will let a leg through to the thigh, and which of the low trees can be climbed and which will come away from the rock in the hand. They collect the pale beetles from the troughs in the mornings and lay them in rows on the stone to count and are told, always, to put them back in the water when they are done, because the beetles go down the channels and turn the wheels a very little and nothing here is wasted.

When the cloud lifts, the youngest cry.

This is well known and not remarked on. The sky comes down on them all at once, that hard pale blue with the small sun in it, and the far edge of the plateau shows for the first time in their lives, a line where the green stops and there is nothing, and it is too much. They are picked up and turned toward a wall. The older children go quiet and stand very still and look, because they have learned that this is what one does, and the adults go on working with their heads down and glance up once and away, and then the cloud comes back from the rim on every side and closes over and everyone lets out a breath they had not agreed to be holding.

---

The old sit by the wheels.

There is a warmth off the axles, a very little, and the sound is company, and there is nothing else to do. The plateau does not give an old person much: the cold gets into the joints and stays, the hands stop closing, the lungs that learned the thin air in youth begin to unlearn it. So they sit, in the passages, on stone benches worn to a hollow, wrapped in the coats they will be laid in, and they watch the belts run into the walls and they say a few words to anyone who passes and mostly they do not.

And the moss finds them. It is a joke, an old one, that the north side of an old person's coat will go green if they sit long enough, and it is a joke because it is true; the moss comes up the wall and along the bench and onto the wool, and the old person brushes it off, and it comes again, and after a while they stop brushing. Moss will grow on anything that stops. It is said of machinery and of people, and the people it is said of hear it, and understand it, and sit.

---

So those are the people. But they are not, if the question is asked honestly, what lives here.

What lives here is the moss.

The people are a thousand. The moss is beyond counting, beyond even the idea of counting, one thing and a billion things, a single body that covers the whole of the plateau and reaches into the town and up the walls and has been growing without pause since before the first stone was cut. Every hummock is a city. Every meter of depth is a century. It drinks the cloud and holds it and gives it up slowly to the channels, and in a real sense the screens and the troughs and the wheels are only a way of doing quickly and in iron what the moss has always done in itself, and the moss does not resent this, having no capacity for resentment, and will outlast it. Under the moss is the peat, which is the moss's own dead, and it is warm down there with the slow heat of things coming apart, and it moves — settles, bulges, lets out a breath of gas that finds its way to the drying-house flues and burns there with a soft blue flame that the dryers watch and do not name.

And in the moss live the small things, which are the true citizens: the springtails, in numbers that would be a plague anywhere else; the mites; the pale beetle, which the people call the trough-bug or the lamp-bug or simply the little one, and which comes out onto the mesh at night for reasons no one has asked and is found drowned in the morning in numbers that never seem to diminish. Nothing sings. Nothing here has a voice. The largest animal on the plateau, most days, is a person, and the person is quiet.

And there is the other thing.

The people do not say what it is. They say something lives in the far basins, and they say it the way they say the stone is old — flatly, as information. They say it is large. They do not say it is an animal. They have never seen it and they leave it alone and it leaves them alone, and when a child asks, the answer is a look, and a nod toward the cloud, and the word that means the peat is breathing. It may be the peat. It may be the dead in the far basin, all of them, a long time's worth, become one slow thing under the moss. It may be the plateau itself, which was here first, and finished long ago, and is patient. The people do not distinguish between these. They are not sure there is a distinction. They go out with the six carriers when someone dies and they lay the body in and they fold the moss over and they stand in the drip and they say almost nothing and they walk back, and behind them, in the cloud, the basin goes on being what it is.

---

At the far end of the town, where the water is let go over the iron lip and down the chute and out into the white, there is nearly always someone standing.

Not for any reason. There is nothing to do there. The chute needs no tending; the water finds it on its own, as the water finds everything here. But there is a bench, and a rail, and the sound of the whole plateau's harvest going over the edge — a full, steady, hollow sound, not loud — and people come and stand at the rail and watch the water go into the cloud and not come back, and after a while they go home.

Nobody has said why. If asked, they would say the sound is company, or that it is the one place in the town where something moves fast. They would not say that it is the only place from which the plateau lets anything leave, and that they come to watch it do so, and to be sure that they themselves are still standing on the near side of the rail, in the wet and the grey and the drip, where the moss is, where the wheels turn, where the pulse comes through the walls and tells them what hour it is and that it is time, soon, to go in.
