The first answer is the one you already have, and it takes some time to stop being unsatisfying.

What lives here is the floor. The floor, and the frames, which are the floor stood up. There is one inhabitant and you have been walking on it since you came up through the cloud, and everything you took for landscape is its body. It is warm because it is alive. It sweats because it is alive. The roar under everything is the sound of it staying alive, of a vascular system the size of a valley moving its blood at speed, and the slow thud you hear when you put your ear to a wall — too slow to be yours, too regular to be anything but a heart — is the one rhythm it has. You count between beats and lose the count and start again. Something over a minute. You cannot tell whether this is slow for it or whether it is racing.

It is not an animal. Nothing about it feeds, or excretes, or moves from where it is. But it is not a plant in the way you know plants, either, because plants do not lay courses, and plants do not carry load, and plants do not guess. You settle on the word the way the prompt did — a plant, the flattened body of one — and hold it loosely, the way you'd hold the wrong-sized rail on the spiral stair.

---

The second answer is the small pale thing, and you have to wait for it.

You sit where you saw the first one enter the low enclosure, and you watch the water threads, and eventually one comes. It is carried rather than walking, though it makes motions like walking — a rocking, a shifting of weight that has nothing to push against, because the thread of water under it is doing the work. It is the color of the floor but a shade paler, the way new growth is paler. It is perhaps the length of your forearm. It is soft-edged and damp and it glistens, and when it passes near enough you see that it is not finished either. That is the thing about it. It has a top and a bottom and a general orientation, and along its sides there are places where limbs are indicated — a bulge, a fold, a knuckle of something — and the indications do not agree with each other. One side has three. The other has two and a swelling that might become a third. There is a slight thickening at what would be the head end, and no face, or the very beginning of one: a slight concavity, as if something were pressing outward from inside and had not yet decided what.

It goes into the enclosure. You follow, this time, close enough to see.

It lies down. It fits itself to the curve of the wall, and to the sill, and to the floor, and it lies there the way a person lies down in a bed to see if the bed is long enough. It does not fit. It is too short for the space, or the space is too long for it, and the water films over it where it touches the floor, and the film thickens, and the pale body dims under the water the way a stone dims, and after a few minutes it is not there. Not gone in the sense of having left. Taken back in. The floor is level and clean where it lay, and the water runs on.

You watch four more over the afternoon. No two are the same. One is longer and thinner and has a distinct division halfway along, as though it meant to have a waist. One has something at the head end that might be the beginning of a mouth or might be a place where it was pinched off the material that made it. One is close — close enough that you feel it in your stomach — to a small crouched child, and then it turns in the water and the resemblance is gone and it is a lump with intentions. Each one goes into an enclosure, or up the single step onto the platform, or tries the width of one of the sideways openings in the long low building, and each one lies down or stands or presses itself against the sill, and does not fit, and is taken back.

---

You watch the columns and you find where they come from.

The gurgle that climbs the column and ends with the sound of a stopper being pulled — that is a birth. You see it happen. The wet terminus at the top swells, the way the drop under a lintel swells, and something pushes up through it that is the same material, paler, and it clears the tip and stands there glistening for a moment, and then it slips down the outside of the column on its own sheet of water and lands on the floor with no sound and is carried off along a thread toward the nearest frame.

So this is where the growth goes.

The water is delivered to the point of growth, over and over, and the point of growth does not take it — that was the puzzle. Now you see that it does take it. It takes exactly as much as would make the next course and spends it on this instead: on a small provisional body, a question shaped like an occupant, which it sends to try the rooms. The material goes up the column as a course-in-waiting and comes down as a guess and is read against the frames and returns to the floor as an answer. And the answer is always *not quite*. And *not quite* does not tell you which way to move. So the walls stay at shoulder height and the lintels stay at three heights and the sills stay too high to step and too low to be walls, and the plant makes another guess, and another, and the effort of a flood produces nothing but the next question. It is not that it cannot build. It is that it will not build until it knows what for, and it cannot know what for until it builds, and it has found the one place between those two things where it can hold.

---

They are not afraid of you.

You catch one. It is easier than you expected: you put your hand down across a water thread and the next one that comes along simply comes into your hand and stops, because the water has stopped, and it lies against your palm and is warm and gives the way the floor gives. It does not struggle. It has nothing to struggle with. You lift it and it makes its small walking motions in the air for a moment and then goes still, and you understand that it is not a creature in its own right, any more than a hand is. It is an organ that has been sent out. It has the intelligence of a hand — enough to feel with, not enough to want anything.

You hold it for a while. When you set it down, the shape of your palm is in it. Of course it is; nothing here heals. Four shallow troughs where your fingers were and a broader one for the heel of your hand, and it goes on along the water carrying this, into the nearest enclosure, and it lies down carrying it, and the floor takes it back with the print of your hand still in it. And you think: it has that now. Whatever it is that reads the guesses when they return — the slow thing behind the wall, the thud — it has the pressure of a hand that is not the hand it was building for. You have been entered into the record. You are one more *not quite*.

---

The bird is a different matter. The bird is the third answer and it is not an answer at all; it is the reason there needs to be one.

You see it twice more before the light would have gone if it went. Both times high, both times crossing in a straight line, both times without so much as a tilt of the wings toward the floor. It is a real bird — you are nearly sure — and it is real in a way nothing else here is, because it has somewhere to be. It comes from over one edge of the plateau and goes out over the other and the plateau is simply a distance to it, a long warm pale shape under the cloud that offers nothing. No branches. No roofs. No insects, because nothing rots here and so nothing breeds. No seeds, because the only thing the plant makes is guesses, and the guesses are not for eating. You realize you have not seen a single living thing on this floor that was not the floor. The plant is not an ecology. Nothing lives on it, in it, off it, with it. It is a body with no world around it, sweating and roaring and alone, and the only company it has ever had is company it has had to make, and the company it makes is only ever itself, sent out a little way and called back.

The bird knows this. The bird does not stop.

---

Then the question you have been avoiding, which is: where did it learn about rooms?

Because it did learn. Nothing arrives at a cambered lintel by itself. Nothing invents the spiral stair without having been told, at least, that there are creatures who go up. The three lintel heights in one wall are not the work of something that has never encountered a body; they are the work of something that has encountered several, or one at several ages, or one that it could only ever know by the pressure it made. And that, you think, is exactly it. That is what a floor knows. It knows feet as weight, distributed so; it knows a hand as this warmth, this span, this leaning against a wall at this height. It knows where bodies lay down by where the warmth stayed longest. It knows where they went in and out by where the wear was. It knows a door as a place in a wall where the pressure kept crossing.

So, one story: there were people, or something that stood in for people, and they came up through the cloud and lived on it, and walked on it and lay on it, and perhaps they even began to build — perhaps they were the ones who taught it that it could be laid up in courses, that if you took the pale wet material and set it down and let it firm and set the next lift on it, it would hold. And they left. Or they died and there was nothing to rot them. Or they were never really here and the plant dreamed them from the weight of rain. And it has gone on with the work as it understood the work, framing rooms for the shape it remembers as pressure, and sending out little pressures of its own to check the fit, and finding every time that memory does not contain what it needs.

Another story, no better and no worse: nobody taught it. This is simply what it does, the way a bowerbird builds without ever having been shown a bower, and it is a species of one, and the thing its bower is for has not yet been invented, and it will hold the frames open until the invention happens, and it is not waiting, exactly, because the frames are the whole of what it is.

You cannot choose between these. The plant would not know the difference either.

---

At the end — at what you decide will be the end — you do the only thing left to do, which is to lie down in one of the enclosures yourself.

The sill is too high to step and you climb it. The floor inside is warm and filmed with water and the curve of the wall is not a curve your back agrees with. You lie on your side. The roar comes up through your hip and your shoulder and the side of your face, and the thud is there in it, and you count it and lose it and count it. The water films over the parts of you that touch the floor, and you have a moment of something like fear — that it will take you back the way it takes them back — and then you understand that it will not, that you are not its material, that you have the wrong seams. You are only a guess it did not make.

After a while there is a sound at the sill and one of the pale bodies comes over it, on its thread, and lies down beside you.

It lies the way you are lying. Side, hip, shoulder, the head end turned toward the wall. It is closer than the others. It has a length that is closer, a fold where a knee might go that is closer, and along its top edge the beginning of something you would not have called a face an hour ago and now nearly do. It lies there and the water films it and it dims and it goes.

And the wall does not move. The sill does not lower. Nothing has been decided; you have only added a little to the *not quite*. But the next one, you think, will be a little more like you, and the one after that, and the plant will go on improving the accuracy of a body it cannot house, in a room it will not finish, for a guest it has just now met and will never see again.

You leave a mark on the wall before you climb out. It is the only finished thing on the plateau. It has a before and an after. The plant will read it, in its way, as pressure, and will send something to lie against it, and will learn nothing from that, and will keep it forever.
