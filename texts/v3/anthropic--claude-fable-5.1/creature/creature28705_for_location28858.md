**What lives in the basin**

Start with what is under your hand, because everything else stands on it.

---

**The pelt**

It is easiest to call it the growth, because the other words all fail in the same way. It is not moss; moss wants light. It is not fungus, though it has fungal habits — the fruiting bodies, the way it threads into the rock. It is not one thing. Pressed to the wall in the dark with your fingers sunk into it, you can feel that it has layers: a fine surface nap that is the youngest part, a dense fibrous body beneath, and under that, where your fingertips meet stone, a slick tough mat that is very old and half-mineral, as if the growth had been slowly becoming the wall it grows on.

It feeds on the thing that the rim does with light. Nobody knows what that thing is, but the pelt does. It grows thickest and fastest where the leakage is strongest, which is near the boundary — and so the most vigorous life in the basin has always been at its very top, in the warm band just under the dark, where the ropes hang thickest and the fruiting bodies cluster like frost. And that is precisely the band the descending lid takes away. Every century the pelt's best growth is left out in the day, where it whitens and crisps and sloughs off the rock in a season, and lichen takes the bare stone behind it. The pelt has been losing its crown for as long as it has had one. It does not seem to mind. It sends new growth up toward the warmth from below, reaches the boundary, thickens, and is cut off again — a green thing forever climbing toward a ceiling that is forever coming down to meet it.

Where it is healthy it moves. Not visibly, but under a resting palm, over a quarter of an hour, there is a faint travelling tightness, a slow flexing, as of something turning over in sleep. The ropes that hang from the shelves sway in the convection, but they also lengthen — perhaps a hand's breadth in a decade — and when they reach the growth below they fuse with it, so that the walls are webbed, in places, with living columns. You can climb on these. They hold.

The fruiting bodies come and go in waves that follow the rasp of the middle walls; there seems to be a relationship. They are cold, smoother than anything else here, and hollow, and when they burst they release a scent so sharply green it stings the eyes, and a fine dust that you feel settle on your wet skin. What the dust becomes, nobody has stayed long enough to see.

The pelt is the oldest thing in the bowl by an immeasurable margin. It was probably here before the dark. Something like it may have grown in the crater when the crater was open to the sky, and when the lid came down it was the thing that did not die but changed. Everything else that lives here arrived afterward, or was made afterward out of things that arrived. The pelt is the ground they arrived on.

---

**The ones in the cavities**

The pelt is full of mouths — pockets in the growth from the size of a thumb to the size of a head, warm inside, wetter than the surface, their walls grazed smooth. Something keeps them open. Put a hand in and it leaves, always, before you can close your fingers.

Once in a long while one is caught anyway, wedged at the back of a shallow hole, and then you learn that it is about the length of a hand and soft all over, with no hard parts you can find except a rasping ridge inside the mouth. It has a great many short legs, or a few long ones folded many times; in the dark, with the thing writhing, it is hard to say. Its skin is warm — warmer than the frogs, nearly your own temperature — and it is dry, the only dry thing in the basin, because it lives in the hottest part of the pelt and is always being baked. It smells like the fruiting bodies. It bites, weakly, and then goes still, and then, if you open your hand, is gone into the growth as if the growth had swallowed it.

They graze the inside of their pockets, keeping them clean, and the pockets are their houses and also, it seems, their nurseries: reach deep into a large one and you may find a warm cluster of something soft and adhering, like wet beads, that a parent will defend by clicking. It is a small dry click, repeated fast, quite unlike anything else in the chorus, and once you have heard it you notice it everywhere — a crackle in the walls, a faint continuous static behind the frogs and the rasp. There are thousands of them. They are almost certainly the source of the small bones in the drift. They are almost certainly what the flying things are hunting most of the time.

---

**The frogs**

They are not frogs. They have no legs to speak of — four short pads, more like a salamander's, useless for jumping — and they move by a kind of heavy walking crawl through the sodden growth of the lower walls, always within reach of seepage. But they call as frogs call, from a swelling of the throat, and the call is the bass of the whole place, so frogs they are.

Their skin is the coolest living thing in the bowl, only a little warmer than the water, and so smooth it is difficult to hold. There are no eyes and no marks of eyes except the two shallow dents. They are entirely without fear of touch; you can lift one and it will sit in your two hands, and after a moment resume calling, and the call is so loud so close that your teeth ache. But strike the stone with a boot, anywhere in the bowl, and every frog within thirty paces stops at once. Vibration through rock is the one warning they heed. Whatever hunted them long ago came heavily, along the wall.

The chorus is not random, though it sounds so. Each frog has a place in the pulse. If you sit for an hour you can hear it: a frog calls, and the frog nearest it calls a beat later, and the next a beat after that, so that the sound travels around the bowl in overlapping rings, like rain-circles on a pond, meeting and crossing. A frog that calls out of turn — because you have startled it, because you have moved it — is answered by a brief roughness in the rings, and then the pulse heals itself around the disturbance and goes on. Nobody knows what the pulse is for. Perhaps for nothing but the keeping of places. In a world without sight, a voice in the wrong position is a body in the wrong position, and the pulse may be nothing more than everyone saying, at intervals, *here*.

They eat what falls. The lower walls are where the dead pelt slumps down, and where the shed paper skins of the rasping things collect in the crotches of the growth, and the frogs sit in this and take from it whatever moves — there are small things in the decay, worms or nearly worms, and the frogs find them by the tremble. Their own young appear in the shallows of the pool in warm weather, if the basin has weather: gelatinous strings, hundreds of eggs together, wound through the rotted growth on the bottom. The larvae have gills and no mouths for a while and then mouths and no gills, and at some size they crawl up into the walls and start to call, thin at first, and find their place in the rings.

They live a long time. There is no way to know how long, but some of the largest, the ones the size of two fists, have skin that has gone slightly rough at the back, and their calls are lower than the others, and they sit in the same pockets of the wall year upon year.

---

**The rasping things**

They are the hardest of the basin's animals to know, because they will not be heard up close. Their sound is the surface of the chorus, the long shrill tide that rises and falls over the whole bowl, and it is made in the middle walls, above the frogs and below the hanging ropes, and it stops in a widening circle around anyone who approaches. You walk in a moving hole in the noise. When you stop, the hole holds; when you stand still long enough — a very long time — it begins to close, one rasp starting up somewhere behind you, then another, until the sound is on all sides again and you can feel it on your skin like heat.

That is how a few have been taken: by standing still until the wall forgets you, and then closing a hand on the nearest sound. What comes away is long — the length of a forearm — and weighs almost nothing. The body is segmented and flattened and feels like stiff wet paper folded many times; the legs are fine and hooked and grip the skin of your hand with a hundred small points. There is no head that you can find, only a slightly thicker end. The rasp is made along the flanks, one segment drawn against another, and a caught one goes silent and then, after a while, resumes, so that you stand in the dark holding a thing that is shrilling in your fist.

They eat the pelt. They must eat an enormous quantity of it. They graze in slow bands across the middle walls, and the pelt behind them is cropped to the fibrous layer, and by the time the band has circled the bowl the growth has come back. The waves of rasping that sweep across the basin are the bands themselves, each animal starting when its neighbour starts, so that the sound travels along the line of feeding like a rumour. In the quiet bands they are moulting. The shed skins — this is what the frogs sit in — are complete, legs and all, and so light they drift down the walls in the convection and gather in the low places like fallen leaves.

They are the food of the basin. Everything that hunts here hunts them, and there are always more. If the pelt is the ground, the rasping things are the grass.

---

**The flyers**

Nobody has ever held one, and the description that follows is built out of sound and the touch of moved air, which is the same way the flyers themselves know anything.

They are the size of a spread hand. They are soft; when one passes at arm's length the wind of it is muffled, downy, with none of the dry snap of an insect wing or the leathery flap of a bat's. They are warm — pass a hand through the air a moment after one has crossed it and there is a faint heat left behind. They emit a stream of clicks so fast the clicks blur into a hum that changes pitch as they turn, and by that hum you can follow one around the bowl with your eyes shut, which is the only way to follow anything here.

They live in the empty middle. The pelt is thick with life and the air above it is theirs alone. They cross and recross the open volume of the bowl at speed, in the total dark, over the pool and among the hanging ropes and along the walls a finger's breadth from the grazing bands, and they never touch anything. Not the wall, not the ropes, not each other, not the outstretched arm of a person standing on the stair. Watch — listen — for long enough and you understand that they are not avoiding obstacles. They are flying a map. The whole basin exists in them, wall and shelf and rope and platform, and they move through it the way a hand moves through a familiar room in the dark, not feeling for things but simply knowing where things are.

The map must be inherited, or taught. Young ones — there are young ones, smaller hums, less steady — fly nearer the walls and slower, and click more, and now and then one of them brushes a rope and the rope swings and the pelt gives up a shower of drops. The old ones never do. The old ones fly so close to the walls that the rasping things fall silent in a moving line behind them, and that line is the only trace their passage leaves.

They roost, it seems, in the doorway on the floor. At certain hours the hums converge on the western wall and go low and quiet, and if you crouch by the half-buried opening then, the passage breathes out warm air heavy with a smell like a bird's nest, and there is a dense soft clicking from inside, and if you put a hand into the opening you feel, for the first time in the basin, something *refuse* you — a stir of many warm bodies drawing back together, and a new sound, a hiss. Withdraw the hand. Whatever the passage was cut for, it is the flyers' now.

They take the rasping things off the walls, plucked in flight with a small sound like a page tearing. They take the cavity-dwellers when those venture out. They take, or try for, the frog larvae in the shallows; you can hear them skimming the pool. Whether they take the adult frogs is unclear — but something does, because now and then there is a frog-note that stops in the middle, and a thud, and the rings heal over.

They are the only things here that seem to know the lid is coming down. The map they fly is the shape of the bowl, and the shape of the bowl changes a finger's width in a lifetime, and they fly it a finger's width lower each lifetime, without error. The map is never wrong. It is simply revised.

---

**In the pool**

The water is warm and iron-tasting and the bottom is rotten pelt gone to slime, and things live in it that never come out.

Most of them are long and soft. Wading, you feel them slide against your calves — not fleeing, only passing, the way fish in a crowded pond pass legs — and if you reach down and catch one it is the thickness of a finger and as long as your arm and made of almost nothing, a tube of muscle and skin that goes slack in the hand. Its mouth is a ring at one end. It grazes the slime. There are hundreds. They come to the surface at intervals and take air with a sound like a kiss, and that is the small-waves noise of the pool: not wind, because there is no wind, but a thousand soft mouths breaking the surface and closing again.

There is something larger. It has never been seen, of course, and rarely touched, but it moves the water. Standing in the shallows you feel a swell come to your knees from the deep end, and a moment later the frogs on that side stop, and then there is the groan — low, drawn out, felt in the stone more than heard — and then the rings begin again. Once a wader felt something pass that was as broad as a person and smooth and cold and kept passing for some time. Once a hand under the water found, on the bottom among the roots of the pelt, a surface that was hard and ridged and moved away. The larger bones in the drift are probably not its bones; they are probably what it leaves.

It is the one inhabitant of the basin that is not in balance with the rest. Everything else here is a piece of a round machine — the pelt feeds on the dark, the rasping things feed on the pelt, the flyers feed on the rasping things, the dead go down to the floor and the frogs sit in the dead — and the thing in the pool is outside this, an appetite too large for the bowl, fed by something not understood. Perhaps it eats the slime and grows very slowly. Perhaps it was put here, like the stair, for a reason worn away. Perhaps it came in through the passage a long time ago and could not get back out.

The pool deepens, a little, every century. Whatever lives in the deep end has slightly more room each time, and everything else has slightly less.

---

**In the drift**

The floor away from the pool is soft dead pelt many body-lengths deep and hot inside from its own rotting, and there is a whole small country in there that is only known by hand.

Push an arm into the compost and it closes around you like a warm mouth. Things move against your skin — not many, and none large — and they are the hottest animals in the basin, fast small bodies that live at a temperature that would be a fever anywhere else. Some are legless and slick. Some have too many legs and a hard shell like a seed-husk and click when disturbed. Some are only a thread of muscle that coils around a finger and lets go. They are the ones who reduce the dead: the fallen pelt, the drifted paper skins, the frogs that stop calling, the small bones — everything ends here and is taken apart in the heat, and the warmth of the floor is the warmth of that work. Where the flyers are the basin's map and the frogs its clock, the drift-dwellers are its digestion.

They do not come out. The surface of the drift is cooler and wetter and they have no use for it. When a foot sinks into the compost the heat comes up around the ankle and something small runs across the instep and is gone below, and that is all anyone has ever known of them.

---

**What lives on the object**

Nothing. That is what makes it the strangest resident.

Every other surface in the basin is colonised. The stone is under the pelt; the pelt is full of pockets and grazing bands; the water is full of mouths; the drift is full of heat. Life here is thick as paint. And in the western drift there is a curled shape the size of a person on which nothing grows, nothing crawls, nothing settles. The compost surrounds it and stops. The pelt has approached it from the wall and stopped. Rasping-thing husks drift down and do not land on it, or land and slide off. Water beads on it but does not run.

It is exactly the temperature of the air. Living things are warmer or cooler than the air, always; even the frogs, who are nearly the water's temperature, are a shade above it. Only the object has no temperature of its own. Touch it and there is nothing to feel except the surface itself, glazed and faintly wrong, so that the fingers want to come away — not because it hurts but because the hand can find nothing there to be interested in, no warmth, no texture, no give, and recoils from the absence as it would from a hole.

If the basin has a heart, this is the least likely candidate and the only one. It might be the machine. It might be what the machine was made to hold. It might be a maker, or the shape of one, finished with a long task and set down. It has been here long enough that the pelt has grown around it and not over it, and that is either a very long time or a refusal, and there is no way to tell which.

It is not alive. But everything else in the bowl lives *around* it — in the literal sense, the compost curved to its shape — and has for ages, and will until the lid comes down, and the drift-dwellers and the frogs and the flyers all give it the same berth the pelt does, as if they knew something about it that nobody has managed to learn. The flyers' map has a hole in it where the object is. They do not pass over it. In the empty middle of the bowl, where they go everywhere, there is a column of air above one point on the floor that they leave alone.

---

**What lives on the band**

Outside, in the day, there is one more inhabitant, and it belongs to the mountain and not the bowl.

Between the old rim — the level where the stair begins, where the steps were cut into stone that has since been left in the light — and the present edge of the dark, there is a band of bare rock a little more than a person's height wide. It is the only part of the ancient crater that has ever been under the dark and then come out of it. When the lid sinks past a stretch of pelt, the pelt dies in the light within a season: goes white, then brittle, then blows off in flakes. Behind it, slowly, comes lichen. Grey and orange rosettes, a finger's breadth across, growing outward by a fraction each year on stone that is still faintly warm from the dark beneath.

The lichen is the basin's only heir. Everything below the boundary is going to be handed to it, a finger's width at a time, over a span longer than the mountain's remaining life. It is patient. It takes the warm bare stone and does nothing with it but live. And at the lowest edge of the band, where the lichen meets the dark, there is a line as sharp as a cut — grey-orange rosettes on one side, and on the other, a few handspans lower than they were when the stair was made, the black.

---

**What does not live here**

The makers. Whoever cut the stair and the platform and the doorway, whoever set the socket in the wall and took away what fitted in it, whoever left the object curled in the western drift — they are not here, and nothing here remembers them. The frogs do not sit on the stair; it is the wrong angle. The flyers roost in the doorway because it is a hole, not because it is a door. The pelt has covered the platform as it covers everything. There is no bone in the drift that could be theirs, or none that has been recognised as such.

They built a bowl that eats light and fills itself with warmth and moisture, and they built it mostly empty, and then they left — or stayed until they were gone, which from inside looks the same. What they left behind is a place that made a world without them. The world is loud and wet and blind and full, and it has no idea it was made, and it is closing, a finger's width in a lifetime, over the one silent thing at its bottom that might have known.

Climbing out, you hear all of it at once: the rings of the frogs, the tide of the rasp, the static of the pocket-dwellers, the kisses of the pool, the hums of the flyers turning through their perfect inherited map. It follows you up the stair. Then your head is out and the light is on your face and behind you the disk lies on the stone, roaring faintly, home to everything that has learned to live on the dark, and to one thing that does not need to.
