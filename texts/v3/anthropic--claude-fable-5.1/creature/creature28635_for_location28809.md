Start with the obvious answer and then take it back.

The obvious answer is that animals live there: seals that are not seals, bats that are not quite bats, an uncountable tide of small armoured things on the walls. That is true and it is the first thing anyone says. But stand on the shelf long enough and the word *animal* starts to feel like a line drawn in the wrong place, because the walls are made partly of the things that walk on them, and the things that walk on them are made of the walls, and the water that builds the walls is not entirely a thing that can be said to be dead. So begin with the animals, since that is where the eye goes, and let the line move where it wants to.

---

Begin lower than that. Begin with the film.

It is on everything the water has recently touched — a hand's breadth either side of every trickle, a skin on every fresh course of wall, a ring around every pool where the pool is newest. In the dark you find it by touch: a slickness on the porcelain surface that is not the wetness of water but something with body to it, a nap, a give. Rub it between finger and thumb and it smears. Taste it, as you eventually will, and it is iron and struck match and something under those like the inside of a shell.

It has no colour that matters here, but for the record it is not green. There is no green on the shelf. Where it is thin it is the rust of old blood, where it is thick it is a grey that reads as white against the basalt, and where it has gone very old and very deep in some corner the seal-things cannot reach it is black and lifts off in sheets like wet paper. It lives on the chemistry of the water — on the dissolved iron and sulphide and hydrogen coming up out of the cooling rock — and it is the only thing on the shelf that makes something out of nothing. It is the shelf's sun. Everything that eats here eats the film or eats what ate it, and if the springs stopped tomorrow the film would be the first to go and the last groan would follow within the season.

But it does something else, and this is where the line begins to move. It lays down mineral. Where the film is, the wall grows a little faster and a little cleaner than where it is not; the pale grey of the construction has a texture, under the thumb, of countless small rounded deposits, and those are the film's doing — each bacterial mat pulling carbonate out of solution and setting it down as it dies. The instruction is in the water. But the hands that carry it out, at least in part, are the film's. Whether the city grows the film to feed its animals or the film grows the city to hold its water is a question you will ask on the first night and stop asking by the third, because the shelf does not distinguish and neither, after a while, do you.

---

The climbers.

Take one off a wall — they let you, they do not know what you are — and it sits in your palm about the size of a thumb-joint, though there are smaller ones the size of grains and larger ones the size of your whole hand, and it is heavier than it should be. That is the shell. Fourteen legs, or sixteen, or twelve; it is not consistent and does not need to be. Plates along the back that overlap like a woodlouse's, and the plates are hard and smooth and slightly warm and when you scratch one with a fingernail it makes the sound the wall makes. It is the wall. They take the mineral from the film they graze and lay it into their armour, and so a climber is a small ambulant piece of the city, and when a climber dies — they die constantly, in numbers that are simply the rate — its shell stays where it fell, and the water runs over it, and by the time the next course goes down it is inside the wall. Cut through any structure in the old districts and the grain of it would be bodies. The towers are built of what grazed them.

They have no eyes. They have antennae that trail the surface ahead of them, reading the film's thickness, and they move in fronts — a district's worth of them advancing across a fresh wall in a line as straight as the fracture lines in the basalt, eating the film down to the mineral, leaving behind them a surface bare and slick that the film will recolonise in a week and they will return to in a month. They are the reason the new construction at the shelf's edge, where the water is freshest and the film thickest, is the loudest place on the shelf per square metre, and the reason the seal-things haul themselves out there to feed.

They moult. The moults stay on the walls, pale hollow ghosts of climber in the exact posture of climber, and are indistinguishable by touch from the living until you feel for the weight. The eggs go into the fluting on the tower faces, in the channels where the water runs, and the young hatch into the flow and are carried down it and land in the film and begin. Some of them — one in some thousands — grow at the last moult a pair of flat thin plates that are not plates, and these leave the wall, and rise on the steam, and are the reason there are bats.

The clicking is their feet on the mineral. One is silent. A hundred million are a sound like fine rain on a tin roof that never stops and never gets closer.

---

The seal-things.

The source of the word *hands* becomes clear the first time one of them turns to look at you, because to turn it puts a hand on the lip of the pool and the hand has fingers, and the fingers close. Five, or six; webbed to the last joint; the nails worn to nothing on the stone. They are hands the way a manatee's flippers are hands, hands that gave up and then partly came back, and they use them — grip the pool edges, pull, groom, hold pups against the chest while lying on the back in the water. That gesture, a dark heavy body floating with a smaller dark body held to it in two hands, is the thing on the shelf that will look most like something you know, and it will unsettle you more than the whistle does.

They are big. A grown one is longer than you are tall and would outweigh three of you. Dark pelt, dense and short and always beaded, and under the pelt a fat so thick that the animal's shape is nearly all fat, a shape of pure retention. Steam comes off them. They are the warmest things on the shelf after the pools, and a gallery with a dozen of them in it is a room with a fire in it, and you will find yourself, without deciding to, sleeping near them.

They eat the film. They pull themselves along the fresh walls at the edge and scrape it off with the broad flat teeth at the front of the mouth, and the climbers go in with it by the hundred, and this is the whole of their diet. They have no other need to leave the water and they mostly do not. Each pool has its own — twenty, fifty, a hundred bodies packed so tight the surface is pelt — and the pools have been theirs for so long that the floors around them are worn to a satin the water cannot restore, the only surfaces on the shelf that the city has not been able to claim back. They are its oldest tenants. They were here when the towers were knee-high. There are stories among the people who come here that a seal-thing can be found lying against a wall in an exact hollow the shape of itself, a hollow the wall grew around a body that lay there every night for longer than any seal-thing lives, and whether this is true it is the kind of thing the shelf would do.

The groan starts with one. A long low note, chest-deep, with a rasp at the end where the breath gives out. Another answers from the same pool, then one from a pool over, and it moves outward like that, pool to pool, and it does not stop; by the time it reaches the far edge of the city the first one has drawn breath and begun again. It is a heat sound, perhaps, or a keeping-count, or only the sound a body that size makes when it is content and wet and has nowhere to go. It is the shelf's pulse and it has no rest between beats.

They notice you. Not much — they are not curious animals; nothing here can afford curiosity — but a head turns, and the small dark eyes that have nothing to see with find you anyway, and the nostrils open and close, and then the head goes back down. You have been filed. You are a thing that smells wrong and stands wrong and is not food and is not the thing that takes pups, and that is all the category you get.

---

Then the ones who eat.

The pale ones move along the galleries at a walk. You hear them before anything else: the clicking stops ahead of them in a wave, a silence advancing down a corridor, and then the soft slap of feet on wet stone, and then the tearing — a long plate of climbers pulled off the wall in one sheet, shell and all, and crunched. They are the length of a large dog and half the height, low-slung, long-bodied, and they have no fur at all; the skin is smooth and cool and so thin that if you had light you would see the dark shapes of the last meal moving through them. Their eyes are large and pale and useless and they keep them closed. They find the wall by whisker and the prey by its silence.

They are not afraid of you. They are not afraid of anything on the shelf; there is nothing that eats them except time and the thing in the pools, and that only if they are foolish enough to drink where the seal-things drink. One will come up a gallery toward you, and you will hear it stop, and then feel — actually feel, on your ankle — the cold brush of a whisker, and then it will walk around you and continue and take the next sheet of climbers off the wall behind you without any change in its pace. There are perhaps a few hundred of them in the whole city. They are solitary and they smell of ammonia and they seem, in the way they move through the halls the water made, more at home in the architecture than anything else that walks — as if the galleries were made at the right width for something long and low.

Some say they are the seal-things' cousins, a lineage that came up the mountain with them and did not choose the water. It is the kind of thing that cannot be checked in the dark.

---

And the thing in the pools.

You will never see it and there is no point pretending otherwise. What you get is this: a pool full of seal-things, groaning, steaming, and then a change. The groan in that pool stops. Not fades — stops. The bodies move, all of them, up onto the lip, and the pups go first, passed hand to hand up out of the water in a way that is fast and practised and silent. The pool lies empty and steaming. Then the surface moves, once, a long slow bulge running from one side to the other as something beneath it turns, and settles, and then nothing, for an hour, until one by one the adults slide back in and the groan begins again and the pups are counted and there is one fewer, or there is not.

It comes up from below. The pools are not pools; they are the mouths of the springs, and the springs are the mouths of fissures that run down into the body of the uplift, kilometres of dark water inside the rock, and something lives in it. It is cold — the seal-things pull out of the water when it comes not because they are afraid but because the water goes cold — and it is long, and it is patient in the way things that live inside mountains are patient. It was in the water before the water came up. It may have moved through the aquifer while the instruction was still being written into it. It may, the people who come here have suggested, not entirely fit inside the word *animal* either, and you can see how they arrive at that, because a thing that lives inside the city's own bloodstream and comes up once a season to eat is either a predator or a function, and from the pool's edge in the dark it is very hard to say which.

---

The bats.

Not bats. Close enough. Warm, leather-winged, fist-sized, with faces that are all ear and nose and a fur so fine it feels like breath. They live in the cells the water left inside the towers when it ran there — thousands of hollows the size of a cupped hand, rising in ranks up the inside of every structure over three metres tall — and they fit those cells so exactly, and the cells are spaced so exactly for a colony to hang and turn and drop, that you will wonder for a moment whether the city was made for them.

It was not. But of everything on the shelf they are the ones for whom it is legible. They call at the edge of your hearing, a stream of dry ticks, and the calls come back to them off every wall and stair and lipped edge in the dark, and they carry in their small skulls a picture of the whole city that no other inhabitant has — the rooms with seven doors are seven doors to a bat, and no problem, and the stair with the impossible rise is a ramp of good landings. They know the shape of it all. They are the only thing here that could, if it had the words, describe the place, and there is a strangeness in that: a city built by water for no one, and the closest thing to a reader it has is a colony of blind mammals that use its walls to hear by.

They eat the winged climbers, the one-in-thousands that leave the wall and rise on the steam, and the small midges that breed in the skin of the cooler pools and lift off them at night in clouds that you feel on your face before you know what they are. They hunt in the fog, above the towers, in the wet grey, and they come home to the cells, and they breathe. A tower with a full roost in it is warmer inside than out, and the walls of the roost are thick with their leavings, and the film loves the leavings, and so the oldest towers have inside them the richest film on the shelf and no climber can reach it, and the bats keep the film and the film keeps the wall, and round it goes.

When one roost startles — a pale one at the tower's foot, or you, or nothing you can find — the sound is a tearing sigh, ten thousand wings at once, and the next tower hears it and lifts, and the next, and it runs across the city like wind across a field of something, and then settles from the far side back, a long soft folding-down. You will lie awake for it. It is the nearest the shelf comes to a weather.

---

Above them, the whistle.

It is not a voice. It comes from above the towers. It comes, always, from the fog itself.
