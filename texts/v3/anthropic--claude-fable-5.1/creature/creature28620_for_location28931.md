You will start with the hands, because that is what the people show you.

They are broad hands, wider than they are long, with the fingers short and the pads of them swollen and split at the ends, red where the split has gone deep and white where it has healed over, so that each fingertip has the look of a stone that has been broken and knit. The palms are cupped. Not held cupped; grown cupped, the way a palm grows around a tool that is never put down. When a hand here is empty it still holds the shape of something the size of a loaf, and it does not quite close, and it does not quite open. The nails are gone or nearly gone. What is left of them is thick and yellow and grows sideways. There is no one in the bowl over the age of about six whose hands are not like this, and the children's hands, which are still soft, are red to the wrist all the time, and their fingers are bent a little at the second joint already, from cold, from wet, from the small stones they carry, which they carry as soon as they can hold anything at all.

The rest of the body is what the hands leave over. They are thin — not gaunt, exactly, but thin the way a stick is thin, with the strength in the length of it — and small, and they move low, close to the ground, in a bent-kneed shuffle that never straightens because the passages are a shoulder wide and the slabs are at hip height and there is nowhere to stand up into. Their shoulders are heavy and their backs are ridged, and a good number of the old ones have a lump at the base of the neck where a slab has ridden for forty years. Their skin is pale to the point of blueness, and shiny, and finely lined all over like the skin on the surface of cooled milk, because it is never dry. Their hair is dark and short and lies flat and wet on the skull and does not seem to grow much. The lips are dark. The eyes are what you cannot stop looking at, because they are almost shut. Not squinted — the squint has gone into the bone. The lids are thick and pale and the lashes are pale and the eye behind them is a grey slit in which you cannot find a pupil, and yet they see everything: which stone in a wall of a hundred has taken a lean, where the film of water on a slab has changed its pattern, where a frog was and is not. Take one of them out of this light and into a shadowed room and they would be blind. There is a story — you will not be told it, you will assemble it from what is and is not done — that once, long ago, someone was carried down over the lip, alive, into the cloud, and came back up, and could not see, and sat at the end of a passage with their hands out for the rest of their life catching stones by the sound.

They do not talk while they work and they are always working, so mostly they do not talk. What speech there is has perhaps forty sounds in it, and most of those are not words but positions: the syllable for *lean it here*, for *the other side*, for *wait*, for *let it go*. There is one syllable, low and short, that means *I have it*, and you hear it more than any other, and after a day you realize that it is also the nearest thing they have to a greeting and the nearest thing they have to a name. When two of them pass in a passage with a slab between them they say it, both, at the moment the weight shifts. When a child is given its first stone it says it, and the adult who gave the stone says it back, and that is the whole ceremony. There are proper names, but there are fewer of them than there are people, and they are not owned. A name is carried for a while and set down and picked up by someone else. You cannot ask anyone who they are. You can only ask where they are, and since everyone is always somewhere else within the hour, the question does not quite hold.

They sleep in the hide-roofed rooms, in heaps, ten and twelve to a room, packed against one another for the warmth that comes off no other source, and every one of them sleeps with some part of the body against the wall — a shoulder, a heel, the back of the hand — so that when the wall moves they wake. They wake many times a night. In the middle of the heap, in the middle of the floor, there is a small hollow of stones where a fire of dried bird dung smoulders, not burning so much as glowing, giving off a low reddish heat and a smoke that lies along the ceiling and goes out through the gaps and makes the hide above it stiffer and paler year by year. It is the only fire there is. It will not boil water. It will not do anything but keep a heap of sleepers from going the way of the rim of ice on a crack. They cook nothing. They eat the snails whole, shell and all, because the shells are worn to nothing and crush against the palate like a fish scale; they eat the frogs' eggs in the spring, scooped out of the still pools in a clear gelatinous mass and swallowed by the mouthful; they eat the salamanders — some of them, sometimes, not the ones in the walls they are watching, and never more than one from a crevice; and they eat the stone-film itself, the greyish-green slime that the snails graze, scraped off the weeping walls with the edge of a flat stone and pressed into a cake and left in the smoke to stiffen. It tastes of iron and of the birds. Everything here tastes of the birds. The birds are the only thing that leaves.

You understand that slowly. The salamanders never leave the walls; they are white and soft and gilled, and their eyes, where they have them, are small dark points under the skin, and they have never been anywhere but a wet crack a degree from freezing and they never will. The frogs are of the bowl entirely. The snails are of the bowl. The people are of the bowl and do not go over the lip, or go over it once, feet first, and do not come back. But the birds — the small black birds with the red legs that stand on every wall-top close as stitching — the birds rise, toward evening, all at once, without a sound, and go out over the lip into the light coming up through the cloud, and are gone, and the bowl is a different place for the hour they are away: the walls look naked, and the people look up more often than they need to, and the work slows. And then the birds come back, in a low black pour over the lip, and settle, and shift, and the walls are stitched again, and the work goes on. Wherever it is they go, they eat there. What they eat comes back as the white and grey crust on every roof-edge and ledge, which the water takes down the walls, which the film grows on, which the snails graze, which the salamanders take, which the people take, which the fire burns. The bowl is closed, and the birds are the one seam in it. The people do not eat the birds. This is not sentiment either. It is that you do not eat the thing that brings in the outside, and you do not eat the thing that tells you which wall will stand.

Whether the people know the birds go somewhere, in the sense of a somewhere you could name, is another question. You come to think they don't think about it that way. The birds go into the light and come out of the light. So does the water. So, eventually, does everyone.

Because there is the question of the dead, and the bowl answers it the only way it can. There is no ground to bury in that would not turn the body up again inside a season. There is no fire hot enough. So when someone dies — and they die in the heaps, in the night, quietly, usually old, usually with a hand still against the wall — they are carried, in the morning, on a slab, by four, to the open side, where the stacking goes loose and gives out, and laid on the bare wet rock at the lip, and left. The birds do the rest; or the birds do some of it, and the light does some, and the water, sliding, does the last of it, and within a few days the rock is bare again. Nothing goes over the edge. Nothing is thrown. And nothing is kept — no hair, no bone, no stone marked with anything — except that when the body has been laid down, the four who carried it lift the slab it lay on and carry it back and set it into whatever wall is nearest and in need, and say the syllable. It is the same slab as any other slab. It has done this before. That is, you gather, the point.

Or nearly nothing is kept. Because the hides on the roofs are stiff and pale and old, and there is no animal in the bowl that could have given them, and none has come up from below in any memory, and they are patched, when they tear, with something thinner and newer and paler that stiffens in the smoke and comes in time to match. You are not told what it is. You are not told anything. You notice that the patching is done by the same four who carry, and that it is done without ceremony and without haste, and that the hide is handled with exactly the care a stone is handled with — which is to say, entirely, and as a matter of method — and you decide not to ask, and you find that this decision feels less like squeamishness than like being taught something.

There is the something that rebuilds alongside them. You could call it the water, and they do, more or less; they have a word for the water that comes up under a floor that is not the word for the water that slides down a wall, and the first word is also the word for a person who has just set a stone. Where the water comes up, a decision has been made, and the room is let go in the same spirit in which you would step aside for someone carrying a slab. But there is more to it than the water. There is the frost, which forms in the small hours on the wall-tops and, once in a while, forms where there is no wall — a low white line across a passage floor, a corner standing in the air, the exact plan of a room that was taken down the day before — and stands there glittering until the light comes off the snowfields and takes it. The people look at it. They look at it the way you would look at a face across a room that you had not expected to see again, and then they look away, and they do not build there that day, and they do not not build there the next. Whether the bowl remembers, or the frost does, or they do, or whether remembering is just the name for what happens to water in a place this cold, is not a thing anyone here would separate out.

And there is the warm stone. The one pool that holds still, that the light goes into and comes out of changed, and the slab beneath it that is warm to the touch — actually warm, the only warm thing in the whole scooped wet freezing brightness of the place — and the frogs know it and crowd it, and the people will not build within an arm's length of it, and the very old go and sit beside it, sometimes, with their hands in their laps, and do not say the syllable, and come away. Children are not stopped from touching it. They touch it once, and take their hands back, and look at their hands, and do not touch it again. Whatever it is, it is the one thing in the bowl that no one handles.

Except that it has a companion, or a counterweight. On the far side, toward the back wall where the mist hangs thickest, there is the slab that has never slid: roof of a room the size of two crouched people, black, weeping, ordinary to look at, and the birds will not stand on it. They stand on the wall-tops to either side, close as stitches, and leave that one roof bare, and always have. The people do not go in. It is not forbidden — nothing is forbidden, there is no one to forbid — but the passage that leads to it is never repaired and never falls, and so you have to want to. If you stoop under the slab, into the wet and the strange sudden dimness, you find a room with nothing in it. Nothing at all, except one stone, set in the middle of the floor. It is not large. It is not rounded. It has edges — corners, faces, a broken plane with the grain of the rock still sharp in it — and it is the only stone in the settlement that no hand has ever shaped, because it is the only stone that has never been lifted. It is where it fell. It is what everything else here was, once, before the handling. You can look at it as long as you like. Nobody will come.

When you come out the light hits you like water and the birds resettle and someone, somewhere, says the syllable, low, and the work goes on. A wall folds and is caught. A floor humps and the frogs are already gone from it. A child sets a small stone into a gap and it fits, in the way it has learned to fit, and the child says *I have it* to no one in particular, and the wet rock takes the sound.

That is who lives here. People with the eyes almost shut and the hands almost shut, who have never once in their lives put anything down for good. Salamanders that will never see. Frogs that know the ground before the ground knows itself. Snails grazing on what the outside sends up the walls. Birds that are the door. The water, which is the other mason. The frost, which is the plan of yesterday. A warm place no one will touch and a cold stone no one has touched. And the town, which is not a thing that lives here so much as the thing living itself does: a shape that has been every shape, made of stones that have been every wall, held up at every moment by somebody's hands and about to be set down.
