The first thing you notice about them is that they are all the same color as the place.

Not skin — skins vary, brown and olive and a few very pale — but the surface of them. Everyone carries a fine bloom of lime dust in the creases of elbows and knuckles, in the hair, in the folds of clothing, so that from the rim a person walking a party wall is hard to pick out until they move. When they sweat, and they do, the dust runs into pale streaks that dry again to a crust. Their clothes are undyed. Nobody has explained this; it may simply be that dye is not worth carrying down the ramp when the stone will whiten everything within a week anyway. The children are gray by midmorning. In the evening the whole city goes down to the sump in shifts and comes back darker and nearer to its own colors, and this lasts until the morning haze puts the pale back on.

Their hands take longer to see and are the real thing to see. The fingertips are broad and hard and slightly flattened, the nails ground short by contact and kept short deliberately with a stone. The heel of the palm and the outer edge of the hand are callused from tapping — not the calluses of gripping a tool, but of *being* the tool's counterweight, of a hundred thousand small blows delivered through a point held in the fingers and driven by the wrist. Older people's wrists are thick. Their knuckles do not quite straighten. When they rest, their hands lie half-open on their knees in the shape of holding a stone point, even when they are holding nothing.

They tap to speak, sometimes. Not a language, exactly, but a household on a lower bench will tap on the party mass and a household above will tap back, and the meaning is *we're here*, or *we're cutting this side today*, or a rhythm that stands for a name. Because the stone is one stone, undivided under everything, a tap travels. A child will lay an ear to the floor of a half-made room and hear the whole city knocking softly at itself.

---

They hear better than you do. This is the second thing. The bowl is quiet and they have grown up in the quiet, and so they hear the small water in the drains and can tell from its note whether a channel is clear. They hear a roller squeak on the far side and know whose block is moving. Most of all they hear the dawn reports, lying on their ledges in the dark, and they know — from the depth of the sound, the direction, the way it carries along the seam in the western wall — whose stone gave. They will say at breakfast, *that was the family below the fig*, and be right.

Every one of them has a first memory that is this sound. It comes through the ledge they are sleeping on, into the body before the ear. They do not find it frightening. It is the sound of a room becoming larger while they sleep, and it means someone poured water yesterday and went to bed, and it means the city is behaving as it should. When it does not come — when there is a still morning with no report, which happens in the dry half of the year when wood takes up water slowly — the whole bowl is uneasy until noon.

---

Time, for them, is the time wood takes to swell. Nobody here says *soon*. They say *a wedge*, meaning a night; *a dry wedge*, meaning longer than expected. Their patience is not a virtue and they do not think of it as one; it is a physical property of the material, like weight. You cannot make oak drink faster. You can only score the line well, cut the slots true, set the pegs, pour, and walk away, and they have all of them walked away from work ten thousand times with the thing undone. They are very good at it. It gives them a particular way of standing up from a task — straightening the back, looking once at the row of pegs, and turning without hesitation — that visitors mistake for indifference and is actually a kind of trust.

They do not hurry on the walkways either, which is why the walkways have no railings. A meter of flat stone with a drop on either side is not dangerous to someone who has never in their life had ground under them that would forgive a stumble. Children learn to walk on the party walls, holding an adult's finger, and by four they run on them. They fall very rarely. When they do fall it is remembered for a long time, and the place where it happened gets a scored line cut across the walkway, not to warn — the people who walk there know — but so the stone remembers too.

---

What they own is the space they have removed.

This takes a while to grasp about them, and it explains most of the rest. A family's holding is the absence they have made — the room, the ledge, the doorway that is a slot — and the stone they took out of it is not theirs. It goes up. It is lifted in pieces to the bench above and becomes the wall of whoever is building there, and in exchange the family below is allowed to keep cutting into the mass. Nobody calls this rent or tax; it is just what happens to a block. Wealth, therefore, is nothing. A rich household has more nothing than a poor one — a longer ledge, a second chamber, a cistern cut in the floor — and a poor household has a single scored outline and a lot of stone still in the way. You can tell status here by how much of a family you can see from above, because the more they have, the more roofless volume they occupy. The truly poor are still mostly solid.

Because the mass is shared, the people who draw lines are important. There are a handful of them in the city — older, mostly, with very steady hands and an unusual sense of where the stone is going. They walk the party walls with a straightedge and a hard pebble and they decide, when two households want the same face, where one absence ends and the other begins. A line wrongly placed takes a neighbor's wall. They are not paid, exactly; they eat at other people's fires and their own rooms are cut for them. They are the closest thing to authority the place has and they spend most of their time looking at stone and saying nothing.

Disputes are settled by tapping on the shared mass while both parties listen, and by the line-drawers, and occasionally by cutting the disputed stone out entirely and lifting it away, so that there is nothing left to argue over. This is considered a good outcome. The stone goes up and becomes a wall somewhere and everyone involved has a slightly larger room.

---

Wood comes down the eastern ramp in bundles on people's backs, and the people who carry it are half of the city and half of the plateau. They have relatives in both. Their skin is less gray. They are the only ones who see the grass every week, and they are asked about it — is it green yet, has the wind changed — the way you'd ask about a distant country. A wedge that has been used is dried in the sun on a southern terrace and used again, and again, until it splits along its own grain and is burned. The rollers are the most precious objects in the city, four to a set, straight-grained and kept oiled, and there are perhaps six sets for the whole bowl. A household waits for the rollers the way you would wait for a season. When a block is moving, everyone on that terrace stops and watches, not because it is interesting but because it is rare.

Copper is rarer. The few chisels are passed between households for the finest work — a doorway lintel, the last quarter-inch of a bed ledge — and they come back blunt and are hammered sharp again on a stone, and a chisel that has been re-hammered enough times is barely longer than a finger and is still used. Nobody would dream of cutting a room with one. Rooms are made with pebbles and wood and water and going to sleep.

---

The old live at the rim, in the stacked-block houses, and have stopped cutting. This is what old means here: not age, exactly, but the point at which you no longer make your room larger in the night. Their houses are the only places in the city with dust in the corners that nobody has swept into fill, and with a few edges that have begun, very faintly, to lose their sharpness — not from weather, there hasn't been weather enough, but from hands, from a threshold stepped over a thousand times. They sit in doorways and look down into the bowl. Some of them remember when it was shallow enough to call across. Some of them remember the plateau being where the sump is now. They are consulted about where old lines were drawn, since the lines are mostly gone — cut away, lifted, built into someone's wall — and only memory holds where a boundary used to run before it was removed.

They are also, quietly, the ones who know which reports never come. A room is scored and never wedged. A row of slots is cut and the family moves, or the pegs are set and never wetted, and the slots stay open. The old could tell you the story of every such slot. They mostly don't.

---

When they die they are carried up.

This is the one time the stone moves the other way — a body going up the ramp instead of a block, and nothing coming down in exchange. They are laid on the plateau under the grass, at a distance from the rim, with no marker, because the plateau will do what the city cannot: soften them, take them slowly, let rain round them off. The living have sharp stone and the newest cuts; the dead get time. Nobody in the bowl finds this sad. They speak of it the way they speak of walking away from a set of wedges. You have done your part; now something slower does the rest.

Small children are told that the grass line at the rim is where the plateau stops, and they believe it, and later they are told it is also where the city stops, and they believe that too, and the two beliefs do not seem to trouble each other.

---

The martins have the unfinished slots.

Wherever a row of wedge-holes was cut and never wetted — a household that left, a line that was redrawn, a face someone scored and then thought better of — the birds have found the openings, a hand deep and just wider than a body, and built in them. Dozens along the upper benches, all facing the sump, because that is where the insects rise off the water at dusk. So the city's abandoned intentions are all occupied. Every plan someone gave up on is a nest. The people notice this. They will not cut a slot they don't mean to use, in part because the stone is one stone and a wasted cut is a wound, and in part because the birds will move in, and then it cannot be finished without turning them out, and nobody does that. There are a few slots that were begun before the martins came and have martins in them now, and the families who cut them have simply built around. A doorway with a bird in it is a doorway with a bird in it.

At dusk the birds come off the water all at once and the sound is a soft turbulence, like cloth being shaken out, and then it stops. In the morning haze they do not fly. They sit in their slots with their heads out and watch the stone darken.

---

The thing in the sump has never been fully seen.

It is long and pale and its tail moves in the green water when the light is right. It is the color of the fresh stone underneath it, which may be why. Children lie on the newest bench with their chins on the edge and drop crumbs and watch the shape come up, not all the way, and take them. It eats whatever falls — insects, the martins' dead, once a sandal. It has been here as long as anyone remembers, and the sump has moved three times in that span, cut deeper and further toward the middle as the bowl deepens, and each time the pool was drained into the new channel and the thing was not in the old one and was, a few days later, in the new. Nobody saw it go. It lives at the lowest and newest point in the city, in the water everyone drinks, and it is treated with a kind of offhand courtesy. The people do not name it. They say *the one in the water*, and when the sump is to be moved again someone will go down the night before and tap on the stone at the edge of the pool, to say so.

The fox comes down the ramp and does not hurry either. It walks the party walls at night as though it has understood the whole system — that the walkways are the only continuous ground — and it looks into the roofless rooms with the same interest the people show looking into each other's, and it leaves before the reports. Sometimes a child on a ledge sees it pass overhead against the stars and says nothing, and in the morning says *the fox was on our wall*, and is believed.

Along the weeping seam in the western face there is the algae, and there are the ferns no longer than a finger, and they are the only things in the whole bowl that live at their own speed rather than the wood's. The people know exactly how many ferns there are. Eleven, this year. Someone checks.

---

So: what lives here is a people who have never seen a finished thing.

Not a room, not a wall, not a city, not a life. They are born in a cut that is still being cut, in a house made from the block that came out of a room below, above a room whose floor will be lowered tonight. The word they have for *done* means *walked away from for the day*. They do not seem to miss the other meaning. They have the green pool and the eleven ferns and the sound in the dark, and hands that hold the shape of a stone point when empty, and they stand very straight after they pour the water and turn without looking back. They are patient the way the stone is heavy, and they are quiet the way the bowl is quiet, and in the morning they will lie on their ledges and count, and know roughly whose.
