You'd say, arriving, that the animals live here. That's the first answer and it isn't wrong so much as it isn't the answer to the question. The snails and the eels and the walking things in the pools are here the way condensation is here — they are what the conditions produce. They occur. Turn the heat down and they'd thin; turn it off and they'd be gone and the crust would be bare and the basin would still be the basin. They belong to it. They don't inhabit it. Nothing that lives by drifting toward warmth can be said to have chosen its address.

The second answer is the people who come, and that one's wrong outright. The place doesn't hold them. They arrive, wait for their eyes to adjust, kneel, put a palm to the ground, follow a pipe, burn a finger on a bulb, and leave — and what they leave is a handprint that closes at a rate you'd need instruments to measure. You can tell a visitor by how they move: quickly, by comparison. They flinch when the eel takes their finger. They pull their hand back from the crust before it has finished sinking. They are still trying to see.

The one who lives here does not do that.

---

You find them the way you find anything. There is a warmth on your face that is not the pumphouse, because the pumphouse is behind you, and not the transformer, because there's no prickle to it, and as you walk toward it it does something none of the machines do. It moves. Not away — the heat sources here don't retreat — but sideways, a little, so that you correct, and then it stops and lets you come.

They are warm the way the ground is warm. Blood-warm, which is to say exactly your own temperature, so that when your hand finally lands on them there is the same absence of shock as entering the pools: the boundary of your body becomes approximate, and for a moment you can't tell whether you've touched a shoulder or whether a shoulder has been placed under your hand. They are beaded. Everything is. Under your palm there is the soft crust of moths, wings folded, dozens of them, clinging to the warmest places — the neck, the hollow under the arm, the crown of the head — and they don't lift when you touch them. Beneath the moths, skin. Beneath the skin, the same slight give as the crust. Press for a minute and your fingers sink a little. Press for five, a little more. They let you.

There are snails on them. Not many. Enough that you understand they have been sitting still for a long time.

---

They don't speak, or if they do it arrives late. You'll put your hand on their arm and feel the breath go in and the chest rise and then some while afterward — a minute, an hour — a low sound will come out of the dark from roughly where they were, one note, unanswered, and you'll realize that was it. That was what they said. Or it was a frog. You are not going to be able to establish this.

So they've stopped using it, mostly, and they talk with the one thing that still crosses honestly. They will take your hand. They hold it flat between both of theirs and wait, and what they're waiting for is the temperatures to equalize, and when there's no difference anymore between your hand and theirs they let go, and that's the greeting. It's also, as far as anyone can tell, the whole conversation. Some people come out of the basin saying they were told things. What they can repeat is: a pressure on the back of the hand, two fingers drawn along the wrist, a palm pressed to the ground and held there until the ground began to take it. Whether that's language or just what a person does after enough years in the dark, nobody who's been touched by it seems able to say. They only agree that it was meant.

---

They keep the nails.

That's the one thing you can say they *do*, in the sense of a task. The nail at the trestle post — the one that used to be at ankle height and is now at the sole of the boot — was driven by them. There are others. A nail in the doorframe of the pumphouse. A nail in the wall of the diesel shed, a hand's width above the ground now, that was driven, the story goes, at head height. Scratches on the rails at intervals that might be paces. Small stones set on the transformer plinth in a line, seven of them, or nine. They are measuring. The basin is rising around its machines at the pace of geology and something is standing in it with a hammer, marking how far.

You'd think this would be a losing kind of work in a place where counting fails, and it is, and they seem to know that. You can find them sometimes with a hand on a rail, sliding it along, feeling it become two rails and then one again, and they do this patiently, over and over, not to fix the count but — the best guess is — to keep company with the failing of it. The way you'd sit with something that was dying slowly and didn't need anything from you except that you stay.

They walk the pipe. The whole mile of it, or the less than a mile, to where it goes into the ground, and they put a hand on the place where it goes in, and they come back. They lean on the pumphouse when the hum stops and keep a palm on the casing until it starts again, as though the machine is more likely to resume if it's being felt. Behind the diesel shed, where the exhaust warms the crust, they sit down in the frogs, and the frogs shift to admit them and close over their legs and settle, and they stay there for what could be hours. That's where you'll find them most often. The warmest ground. Nearly covered.

No one has found where the fuel comes from. It's possible they know. It's possible they've been to the rim. If you asked them, and waited, the answer would arrive as a splash from the channel a long time after you'd stopped listening for it, and you wouldn't know it was an answer.

---

How many of them there are is the question everyone asks and it's the wrong question, or rather it's the right question asked of the wrong place. There is one. You put your hand on their shoulder and there is one shoulder under it. You keep your hand there and after a while there is a second warmth a step to your left, the same temperature, breathing at the same slow period as the pumphouse, and you'd swear you never felt anyone arrive. Then there is one again. The other one didn't leave. It's simply not the case anymore that there were two. They are, in the way the snails are seven and then nine, however many the place has thought it over and decided on for the moment, and they don't seem troubled by it. When there are two of them they hold each other's hands until the temperatures match, which takes no time at all, and then let go.

Where they came from, nobody says. The obvious story is that they were a visitor once — someone who knelt and put a palm to the crust and, for whatever reason, didn't lift it — and the crust closed over the question at the rate it closes over everything. They don't have the flinch. That's what you notice, after enough visits. When the eel takes their finger they let it. When the sound of their step comes before their foot does, they don't hurry to catch up with it. Whatever they were before they were this, they've stopped correcting for the dark, and the place, which corrects for nothing, has started to treat them as something that belongs.

---

They are lower every year.

That's the thing you learn last, if you keep coming. You find them by warmth and when you put your hand out it lands where their head used to be and finds a shoulder. The next time, the top of the head, lower still. They are sitting on ground that is rising, and they're not standing up to keep ahead of it. The crust has taken the impression of them the way it takes a palm — patiently, closing at a pace you'd need instruments for — except that the palm was lifted away and they weren't. Behind the diesel shed, in the frogs, there is a shape in the ground now that is roughly the shape of someone sitting, and it is warm, and sometimes it is occupied and sometimes it seems to be only the shape, warm on its own, with the frogs on it.

They'll go the way the pumphouse is going. In a hundred years, a warm patch on level ground with a slow breath underneath. After that a breath. After that a warmth. The moths will find it. The frogs will crowd onto it. The one who comes after — and there will be one, because someone always kneels, and doesn't lift their hand — will locate it the way everything here is located, and put a palm flat on it, and wait for the temperatures to equalize, and understand that they've been greeted.

Until then there's this. You're standing in the basin and it's quiet enough that you'd say it was empty, and you move your hand, and everything moves with it — the snails, the wings, the slow muscle in the channel — and one of the things that moves is a hand. It closes over yours. It is exactly as warm as you are. It waits.
