Start with the frogs, because the frogs start with you.

There are four, as you counted, and if you stay a week there will be six, because two of the calls only begin after full dark and one of those only after rain. None of them is large. The biggest sits comfortably on a thumbnail-sized snail shell without cracking it; the smallest could be mistaken for a drop of water that has decided not to fall. They are the color of what they sit on — the ticking one is slate, the humming one is the dark green of the algal stain and can only be found by watching for the throat to move, the whistling one is a mottled bronze that matches the leathery shrubs exactly and is never found anywhere else, so that each shrub, once you know to look, has one whistler and one only, and the whistler has, in a sense, a house. The fourth, the one that sounds like a stone into a well, you never see. You come to suspect it calls from inside the rock.

That suspicion is the beginning of understanding who lives here.

The frogs lay their eggs not in the sheets but in the joints, in the hairline cracks where the water is always exactly a finger's width below the surface and never dries and never floods. The strings of eggs are pressed into the fracture like grout. The larvae, when they hatch, are thinner than the eggs were, thin enough to move along the crack, and they travel inside the stone — inches a day, following the same weak gradients the water follows — until the crack opens into a pool, and there they widen, and grow legs, and climb out onto the rim, and begin to call. What you took for a plain of separate pools with frogs in them is a single body of water with a stone lid, and the pools are the places where the lid is missing, and the frogs are one population, not a dozen, moving beneath your feet along the tiling.

The fish confirm it. You wondered how they crossed. They do not cross. They are never out of the water, because there is no out. The translucent flickers you see in the deeper sheets are the adults, and they are as large as they will ever be, which is about the length of the last joint of your finger, and they spend perhaps a tenth of their lives in the open — the rest is spent in the fracture network, sideways, in the dark, in water that moves so slowly that a fish born in one joint may take its whole life to reach a pool and see the sky once and lay its eggs at the lip of the crack it came from. The pools are not their home. The pools are where they surface, the way a whale surfaces. Their home is the whole plain, three fingers deep, everywhere.

Hold that thought while you look at the snails.

There are hundreds in view and there are millions on the shield. Each one clears a path through the algal stain a few times its own width and a hand's length a day, and the stain closes behind it in a week, so that the plain's surface at any moment is a record of the last several days of snail travel and nothing older. They are not going anywhere. They are eating the film. Where the film is thick they linger and where it is thin they move on, and this is enough to keep them in perpetual slow motion across a surface that does not care. They do not seem to have predators, and then you find the piles.

The piles of emptied shells sit at the bases of certain stones and not others, and if you go back to one stone on three successive mornings the pile is a little larger each time, and if you go back at dusk and sit very still at a distance you will eventually see what does it. It is not the large bird. It is a rail — a walking bird, dull brown, barely larger than the frogs it stands among, with a bill like a pair of tweezers and legs that seem to be all knuckle. It comes out of nowhere, because nowhere is where it lives; it has no nest you can find and it is never seen crossing open rock, only arriving. It picks a snail off the stain, carries it to the stone, and with three or four quick taps on a spot worn faintly smooth by every rail before it, breaks the shell at the whorl and draws out the animal. Then it leaves the shell on the pile and walks — never flies — into the knee-high mist and is not there.

The stone it uses has been used for a very long time. The smooth spot is real. The pile beneath, if you dig into it with a finger, goes down further than the visible heap, into a cemented mass of shell fragments and algal grit that is, you realize with a small shock, the only soil on the plain. The rail has been making earth here. A thumb's depth of it, in ten thousand years, under one stone. The grass tuft nearest that stone is the largest for a mile.

The hinge call, the one you heard from far away, is the rail. It does not carry far. Far away was not far.

The large three-toed tracks belong to something else, and here the plain refuses you. You will find the tracks again, and again, always at the margins of the algal mud, always fresh enough that the edges have not slumped, and you will never see the bird. It walks at night, or in the thickest mist, or — you begin to think, in your third or fourth day — it walks in the haze itself, at exactly the distance where edges stop being edges, a grey thing among grey, keeping pace with you around the circumference of what you can see. Its stride is longer than yours. It eats, probably, frogs. It has been here, in some form, for as long as there have been frogs to eat, and the frogs have learned nothing from this, because there is nowhere to learn to go. It is not menacing. It is simply the part of the place that is not for you, and every place has one.

The dragonflies you already understand. They are the fastest things here and they are also, in a sense, the least present, because they never touch anything for longer than it takes to seize it, and they go where the light is. When the overcast thins toward evening they go with the gold, every one of them, so that the near side of the plain empties of them and the far side fills, and then the light goes and they are simply gone, down onto the undersides of the leathery leaves, wings flat, waiting for a morning that will be much like the last one.

Now the tree.

You walk to it on the second day because it is the only thing that could be called a destination. It takes longer than two hours because there is no straight line across water that is everywhere and nowhere. When you arrive it is smaller than the haze made it — perhaps twice your height — and older than anything you have ever touched that was alive. Its trunk is more root than trunk, a twisted grey hawser that has gone down into one joint and split it into a crevice you could lose an arm in, and that crevice is full of water, and the water is full of the fourth frog.

There it is. The one you never saw. Dozens of them, sitting in the dark wet slot the tree has made, fat and glossy and the color of the inside of a mussel, and every few seconds one of them drops a stone into a well. The sound comes up out of the crevice with a resonance the open rock cannot give it. This is where the well is. The call you heard everywhere was being carried through the fracture network, along the water in the joints, so that a frog calling into a crack at the base of the only tree on the plain can be heard from a crack two hours' walk away, arriving as if from directly below your feet. The plain is a single instrument and the tree is the sounding-hole.

Everything with wings comes to the tree. Not to live there — nothing lives there, not in the sense of nesting or staying — but to touch it. The low dark birds that click land in it for a breath and go. The wader comes and stands under it, once, at midday, and does nothing, and walks away. The dragonflies use its shadow, the only proper shadow for miles, and hang in it in the heat. The leathery shrubs, you notice now, are all downwind. The tree is the only address, and so it is the address of everything, briefly, and belongs to nothing.

The moss on the weeping shelf is next, and this is where you begin to lose your grip on the word *lives*.

The moss is a hand's width of green along an arm's length of stone, and it has been there — this patch, not this kind — for longer than the tree. You can see it in the groove. The drip that wore the bowl fell from the moss's lower edge, and the bowl is centered under where the edge is now, and the moss grows outward by a thumbnail a year; so the edge has not moved, or has moved and come back, for as long as the bowl has been deepening, which is a length of time you do not have a unit for. The moss is not old the way the tree is old. The tree is an individual that has persisted. The moss is a location that has persisted, and the moss is what a location looks like when it is alive. Cells are born at the drip line and die at the drip line and the line does not move, and something — call it the moss — occupies it continuously, and has, and will.

The algal stain is the same thing, spread over the whole shield. It is a single organism in the way a weather is a single organism. It has no edge except where the rock stands up out of the wet, and there is very little rock that does. Everything else that lives here lives on it, or eats it, or eats something that ate it. It is the plain's skin. When you put your palm on the dry-looking stone and lift it and find the print of wet, you are also lifting away a few thousand cells of the stain, and it is already closing over the place, and it will close over your footprints by the time you have walked out of sight of them, and the plain will have forgotten you the way a pond forgets a stone.

You go back to the pool where the turtle sank and you wait.

It takes most of an afternoon. When the dome comes up it comes up slowly, first as a change in the color of the water and then as a shape, and it is larger than you remembered, and the shell is not dark but a very deep green, and the green is the stain. The turtle has algae on it. Of course it does. It has been in this water, or water that connects to this water, for a length of time that is measured, for a turtle, in decades, and the plain has grown on it as it grows on everything that stays still enough. The head comes up at last, and the eye is old and gold and entirely indifferent, and it regards you the way the stone regards you, and it goes down again.

This is the only creature on the plain that you can properly call a resident. The frogs are a population. The fish are a flow. The rail arrives and departs. The moss is a place. The stain is a condition. But the turtle is one animal, in one body, in the same water, for longer than you have been alive, and it will still be here after you have gone, sinking when watched, surfacing when not, letting the plain grow on its back. If the place has an inhabitant in the way you mean the word — a someone who lives *there* — it is this, and it does not know it, and it would not care.

And yet you find, in the end, that the answer keeps sliding out from under the question. You came to ask what lives here, expecting to be given a thing to look at, and what you have been given instead is a way of looking. Nothing here lives *in* a place, because there are no places — only the one place, very large, very thin, tiled with hairline joints and roofed with mist. What lives here lives *as* the place: spread over it, three fingers deep, at a low constant concentration, surfacing where the lid is missing and going back under. You could say the frogs live here, or the fish, or the stain, or the turtle, or the thing whose tracks you keep finding, and each would be true and each would be the wrong size. The truest thing to say is that the plain is inhabited the way the air is inhabited by the drone — everywhere, a little, without gathering.

Toward evening the light leans, and the near side of the plain goes gold, and every living thing on it goes on doing exactly what it was doing. The rail taps a shell against its stone. The frog in the crevice under the tree drops another stone into the well, and the sound goes out along the water under the rock and comes up through your feet a two-hour walk away. The moss drips. The turtle is somewhere below, not coming up. The stain is closing over the last of your footprints. And out in the haze, at the exact distance where a shape stops being a shape, something with three toes and a long stride sets one foot down in water that has been standing here since before there were feet, and lifts the other, and keeps pace.
