Begin with the skin, because on the plateau everything about a person follows from it.

The water strips skin, and the air is the water in another form, so the people who live there have been being stripped since birth — gently, everywhere, all the time. The result is not rawness. Rawness is what a visitor gets: the split knuckles in the first week, the eyes that will not stop running, the tongue that tastes of soda for a month and then, disturbingly, doesn't. What the people themselves have is closer to a cure. Their skin is thick and smooth and faintly glazed, like the skin of a hand that has been in washing-water for forty years, and it does not sweat much, and it does not freckle, because the sun so rarely reaches it uncut. They are pale the way the walls are pale — not a fair colour but a deposited one. In the folds of the elbow and the creases of the neck there is often a fine white bloom that comes off on a fingertip and has re-formed by evening. Children lick their forearms without thinking, for the salt. The very old have white in their creases that no longer comes off at all.

Hands next. The hands of pan-workers are the most-described feature of the city in the accounts of people who came up and went down again, because a hand is the part of a plateau person a traveller is most likely to touch. The fingertips have no prints left. The nails are short and thick and ridged like the terraces. There are old burn-scars healed so flat they are found rather than seen. But the thing the accounts struggle with is not the hands themselves so much as the way they are held — slightly open, fingers a little apart, as if to let the air through — and the way a pan-worker sets a hand down on any surface with a small pause first, a hair's-breadth of hover before contact. Visitors read this as hesitancy. It is the opposite. It is the reflex of a lifetime of surfaces that may be hot, or wet, or a bird, and the hand is not asking permission; it is giving the surface a moment to declare itself.

The chest is deep, from the altitude. The voice is low, and this is the second thing the low country gets wrong about them, after the hands. Plateau people are called quiet, sullen, closed. They are not quiet. They talk constantly. But a high voice on the plateau goes up into the roar and is lost, and a low one goes under it and carries, so over generations the whole register of speech has dropped, and a plateau voice at ordinary pitch is something a low-country ear has not learned to separate from the ground-noise. Add to this that in the pan-quarters, where the steam takes faces at ten paces, a great deal of the talk is done with the hands — a compact signing that is not a language exactly but a working vocabulary, hot, wet, full, wait, mine, yours, bird — and a visitor can stand in a shed full of conversation and hear only the engines.

Their eyes have adapted to a world without shadows. They judge depth by silhouette and by sound, by the way the roar changes as it comes off a wall, and in their own fog they move with a certainty that looks like eyesight and is only partly eyesight. Take them down the mountain into hard clean afternoon light and they stumble on stairs. A shadow's edge reads to them as an edge in the stone. They squint for a week, and their eyes water — not from the soda, for once, but from the sun — and they say the low country is too sharp, everything has a black line drawn round it, how do you bear it.

They cannot smell the plateau. Every account says so and it is true. What they can smell is the low country, and to them it smells of rot — green rot, wet rot, the smell of things that die without being cured and lie there being soft. Plateau people coming down the switchbacks for the first time have been known to cover their faces at the tree-line. They are not being rude. They have never in their lives smelt a thing decompose.

The trades are old and they are families, more or less, though the families are porous and a child born in the rendering sheds may end up reading pans. The pan-readers are the aristocracy, insofar as there is one: the people who stand over a courtyard of boiling brine and can tell you by the colour of the crust at the edge what the ratios are and whether the vent under it is running hot or tired. It cannot be taught from a book, and there are no books. It is taught by standing beside someone for twenty years.

The scrapers go up the roosting cliffs on ropes in the dry weeks, when the condensation is merely heavy, and come down white to the eyebrows. The renderers work in the long low sheds under the reinforced roofs, and they are the ones the birds know best — the birds nest above the very fires that render their dead, in numbers so great that the roof-beams have been sistered and sistered again, and the renderers can name the individual birds on the beam above their own station and do. The pigment-dredgers are red to the elbow and stay red; the mat-stain goes into the glaze of the skin and does not wash, so that a dredger's forearms are the same colour as the shutters, the same colour as the water, the same colour as the flamingoes standing in it.

The iron-menders are the odd ones. Everything else in the city works on the scale of centuries, and they alone work on the scale of years, because iron on the plateau lives about as long as a dog. They walk the pan-quarters with hammers and lengths of stock, sounding pipes, and where a pipe rings wrong they cut it out and put in new and the new begins at once to redden. They are regarded, in a place where nothing much is regarded, with something like tenderness — the people whose whole work is with the one thing here that dies quickly. There is a saying that an iron-mender's hands are never clean, meaning never white; they are the only hands on the plateau that are the colour of the water rather than the colour of the walls.

And then the window-cutters, who are barely a trade at all — a few families, a few dozen people — but who are, in a city with no archive, the closest thing to its memory. Every few decades a house's windows have closed to slits and the cutters come with saws and take the accretion back to the frame, and the cut face shows the layers, and the cutters read them. They know how thick a century is on a north wall and how thick on a south. They can put a date to a lintel by its curtain. They keep the shoreline maps, and they are the ones who say the maps are boring, and they say it with satisfaction, because a boring map is a map of a place that has not failed.

The cart-drivers are the only plateau people who see the low country regularly, going down with soda and up with grain, and they come back tanned across the nose and full of news, and both fade within the week. They are pitied a little, and suspected a little, and listened to a great deal.

---

Children are born into the sound, and the sound is the first thing they cannot do without. A plateau infant carried down the mountain cannot sleep in the silence; it lies rigid and listening, waiting for the roar to come back, and the mothers who have to travel bring a wooden rattle full of dried peas and shake it steadily all night, which does not really work. On the plateau a baby is put to sleep by being carried to the shore, where the grumble of a million feeding heads is loudest, and it goes under in a minute.

They grow up on the roofs among the nests. Their first work is egg-gathering from the cold pans, where the waders lay in the drying brine, and it is there that they learn the fundamental skill of the place, which is not how to move a bird but how to move around one. You do not lift a bird; it will hate you and you will be bitten and the whole roof will go up in a shaken-cloth roar. You lean. You move your weight toward the bird slowly and steadily, without stopping, and the bird moves over, because that is what birds on the plateau have done for ten thousand years when something leaned on them, and the child moves into the space, and everyone is where they need to be. Plateau children lean on each other the same way. Plateau adults do too. Watch a crowd move through a pan-quarter alley at shift-change and you will see no one push and no one wait; the crowd simply displaces itself through itself, like the flies lifting ahead and settling behind, and comes out the other end sorted.

The old do not retire, because the pace of the work is already a retired person's pace. They slow, and the slowness is absorbed. A pan-reader at eighty reads the same pan she read at thirty and stands a little nearer the rail. The very old sit on the rails themselves in the warm fog with the birds on either side of them and are hard, at fifty paces, to tell from the birds — the same stillness, the same slight lean, the same white bloom — and the children going up to shift will touch a shoulder in passing and the shoulder will move over.

When they die they are limed.

This is the one thing the low-country stories get nearly right and wholly wrong. There is no ground on the plateau that is not crust, and nothing rots there anyway, so burial was never a possibility and no one has ever pined for it. The dead are laid out on the roosting terraces below the cliffs for a day, and the birds and the small dark mammals and the beetles do what they do to a dead chick, which is everything, quickly. What is left is bone, and bone is burned for lime with the eggshells, and the lime goes into the mortar, and the mortar goes into the walls, and the walls whiten. So the city is, on a literal accounting, partly the people who built it and lived in it, a few parts per thousand and rising. The low country hears this and finds it ghoulish. The plateau finds it merely tidy. A window-cutter, asked, will show you the layer in a cut face where a bad year is — a thin band, slightly greyer — and say, that was the fever when I was small, and there is no more grief in it than in a ring in a tree, and no less.

They do not believe the birds are souls. Pressed on souls in general, they become polite and vague in the way of people asked about a custom of somewhere else. If they believe in anything, it is level. The worst thing a person can do on the plateau is let something fall out of level — let a pan run dry so the vent scorches the pan-floor, let a rail rust through so the birds have nowhere to sit, let a window close over past the point where a saw can find the frame. These are not crimes. Nobody is punished. They are simply understood as the one true form of ugliness the place recognises, the failure to maintain, and a person who has done it is looked at, for a while, the way a pipe is looked at that rings wrong.

Their nearest thing to a prayer is the pause before the hand comes down. They would not call it that.

---

The ones who leave and come back are the test of the place, and the place passes it every time, and they do not.

A child who goes down the mountain and returns old finds the door where it was, with a deeper curtain round it, and the same birds on the same rail, or birds that are for every purpose the same, and for a few days finds that the city has done to them what it does to any visitor. Their skin has softened in the low country and it cracks. They cough. Their eyes run. And — this is the part they do not expect — they can smell it. For a week the returned can smell the plateau, soda and cooked mineral and the vast warm ammoniac sweetness of a million birds, and they stand in the alleys with their faces working, trying to hold on to it, because they understand that this is the only time in their lives they will ever know what the place is like from outside. And then one morning it is gone again and they have simply come home, and the crust starts to form in the creases of their elbows, and within a season they lean on a bird without thinking and the bird moves over.

---

So: what lives there.

From the switchback road, an hour below the cloud-line, what lives there is a sound — a broad grey roar with no direction to it, which a traveller takes for wind until he notices the air is still. From the edge of the fog it is a population: the birds standing so close the water looks feathered, the flies furring the shore, and somewhere in it, indistinguishable at three hundred paces from the roosting terraces, the pale rounded districts of the city with their rust-pink doors. From that distance the people are one more niche, filled to the brim and slightly over, one more thing the plateau has cured and glazed and set to turning over in its slow equilibrium alongside the bacteria and the bats. That view is not wrong. The people themselves would not argue with it. They know they are limed into the walls.

But go in, past where the silhouettes become bodies, into a pan-quarter alley at the end of a shift, and the scale changes and the population comes apart into hands. Here is one — a pan-reader, sixty, red-armed from a season on the mats before she moved to the pans, white at the elbows and the neck, going up the steps to her door in a light with no shadows, in a roar she cannot hear and could not sleep without. She reaches for the rail. Her hand hovers, a hair's breadth, and the rail is a bird, and the bird, without looking at her, moves over, and her hand comes down on the wet white iron, and she goes in.

It has been happening on that step for eleven hundred years. It is also, and entirely, just her, and just that bird, and the door closing, and the next person coming up the steps behind.
