Take the small things first, because they are honest, and because the town rewards patience with them.

The lichen is the eldest and will outlive everything, including the walls. It is the same hard grey crust that grows on the open shield, but here it has found the one thing it never had out there, which is a vertical surface out of the wind, and on the leeward faces of the walls it has spread into plates the size of hands — dull, cracked at the edges, growing at the rate of a fingernail's breadth in a human lifetime. It does not eat the wall. It sits on it, the way it has sat on everything. If you scraped a plate off, and you would need a knife and a long afternoon, you would find the stone beneath pale and perfect, unweathered, the honeycomb sharp as the day the water set it down. The lichen keeps the wall. It is possible it is the only thing in the town that means to.

The snails do eat the wall. They are grazing the algae, the green film in every wet channel, but they take the stone with it, a grain at a time, and they make their shells from it, and when they die — inside the chambers, mostly, in the sheltered corners where the moss is thickest — the shells lie there for a season, thin as parings, and the drip finds them and dissolves them and carries them back into the wall. Nothing is lost. Look closely at the lip of a doorway where the new deposit is forming, soft and white, and you will sometimes see the ghost of a spiral in it, a shell not quite gone before the stone closed over. The walls are made, in some small part, of the animals that eat the walls. The snails do not know this. They are small and blind and cold and they go about their grazing with a steadiness that is the closest thing in the town to purpose.

In the tubes there is a fauna you will never see. Springtails, pale as the stone. A blind white something with too many legs that lives in the standing water at the bottom of the cisterns and has never felt light. Mites in the moss. A moth — one species, grey, the size of a thumbnail — that rests on the walls by day with its wings flat and is indistinguishable from lichen, and rises at dusk in the hundreds, silently, and is gone by full dark, and no one has found where it goes. Put your ear to a wall and some of the patter you hear is not water.

The birds you have met. There are seven, or nine; the number is hard to fix because they come and go from the same few tubes and you cannot tell one from another. Red legs, red bill, a black so deep it looks wet. They nest in the high honeycomb where the tubes are widest, and line the nests with the dark ropey moss from the corners, and raise their young in the ringing of the walls, so that the chicks — if you could see them, which you cannot — grow up inside a drum. They do not seem to mind. They have the loose tumbling flight of things that have never had to hurry, and when the wind is high they let it take them up and over the edge and out into the grey air above the long fall, and hang there, and come back. They eat the moth, presumably. They eat the snails, certainly; the shells lie broken in the squares. They are the only things in the town that ever seem to be enjoying themselves, and it may be that they are not.

---

That is what lives here, if living means what it usually means. It does not take long to list. The town is a room for a great deal of nothing, and the nothing is most of what is in it.

But there is another way to say it, which is that water lives here. Not passes through — lives. It arrives through the doorways, all of them, at the height it always has. It keeps to the streets. It goes from room to room. It sleeps in the cisterns, in the dark, for days at a time, and comes out changed, carrying stone. It made the walls in the image of what stood in its way and is still making them, and when it has finished them it will have nowhere to go and will simply stand in the closed rooms in the dark and slowly become the rooms. That is a kind of dwelling. If you wanted an inhabitant and were prepared to be strict about it, that would be your answer, and you could leave with it and be more or less right.

---

You will not be satisfied with it, because of the oval.

It is in a chamber on the north side of what you have been calling the square — one of the larger rooms, with its doorway facing in. The moss on the floor is worn: not scuffed, not torn, but pressed, the way a bed is pressed, into a shallow depression about the length of a person lying on one side with the knees a little drawn up. The moss is perfectly healthy. It is not dead in the oval; it is simply lower, denser, as though it had grown under a weight. It grows back in a season. The oval is still there.

You will go and stand in that doorway, at some point, on some grey afternoon with the cloud coming up, and look at the oval for a long time, and here is what you will notice.

The doorway is at the height of your chest, and it is the width of your shoulders. Not approximately. You have to turn a little to go through.

The room is colder than the street, which is wrong; the other chambers are warmer, being out of the wind. The cold in this one is not moving air. It is the cold of something large and wet that has been lying still for a long time.

The sound changes. In the doorway the whistling and the hum and the throb go on exactly as before, but inside they are soft, muffled at the edges, the way sound is in a room with a body in it. An empty room rings. This one does not quite.

And the sweat on the walls runs differently. It beads and runs on every wall in the town, but here, at about the height of your own chest and the height of your own hip, there are two bands where the drops do not form — where the stone stays faintly dry, the way a window stays dry where someone has been leaning against it and warming it from the other side.

---

Now let it become what it is going to become, slowly, because it is slow, and to name it too quickly would be to lose it.

It is grey. That much can be said. The grey of the shield and the sky and the wet cloth in the air, and not the grey of any surface but of a volume — the grey that fog is, or breath on a cold morning, or the inside of a cloud. It is roughly the length of the oval and roughly the shape the oval implies, but only roughly, because it does not hold a shape so much as consent to one. Where the room is, it is the room. Where the doorway is, it is the width of the doorway. If the water had cast it, as the water cast the sedge, the cast would be this town.

It is made of what the walls are made of, at an earlier stage. Not stone. Not yet. The saturated air, the mineral in solution, the cold that does not bite but subtracts — it is these, gathered, the way a wave is water gathered, and it has been gathered for so long it has taken on the habit of a form. It sweats, as the walls sweat. Put your hand into the room where it lies — and you can, nothing prevents you — and your hand comes out wet, colder than the air, with the faint chalky taste on the skin that the whole town has, and a fine pale grit under the nails that you did not have before, that is not on the walls, that came from somewhere.

It lies down more than it stands. When it stands — rarely, when the west wind comes and the great note sounds from the centre of the town, which is where it goes when it stands — its chest is at the height of the doorways. The water did not care about doorways. It cared about what was standing in the flow, and what was standing in the flow was sedge, and this, and it has made the openings to fit the one as exactly as it made the rooms to fit the other.

It breathes. The slow note is not something it makes; it is something it does, as the walls are not something the water makes but something the water does. One breath every few seconds when the wind is west. You found yourself breathing in time with it. Everything in the town does. The birds' wingbeats settle to it. The drip in the tubes, which has no reason to, falls on the beat more often than off. The moss, over years, grows in rings that follow it, though you would have to cut a wall open to see.

It listens. This is the closest thing to a fact about it the town will give you. The whole place is an ear — a hundred chambers tuned close, a thousand tubes, the deep cistern at the centre, all of it carrying sound inward along the streets the way the streets once carried water. And at the middle of it, in the north room off the square, the thing the sound arrives at. What it is listening for you cannot say. The water has been saying its one long word since the springs failed.
