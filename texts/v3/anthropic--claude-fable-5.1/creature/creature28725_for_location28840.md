Start with what moves, because in a place this bright and this loud the eye wants to give life to anything that moves.

The wheel moves. The whole body of water turns, a slow fat rotation a room wide, and it has habits the way a living thing has habits. It rises where it is fed and sinks where it is emptied. It carries things: the flakes of lime, the beads of air, you. It answers the pipe — quickens when the pipe quickens, steadies when it steadies — and it answers nothing else, not the sun, not the hour, not whether anyone is in it. If you stand against the south wall and hold still, the wheel will find you and press you gently toward the floor, and if you stand against the north wall it will lift you, and this is so consistent that within a few minutes you begin to treat it the way you would treat a large, patient animal in a small stall. But it is not an animal. It has no inside. It is the shape that hot water takes when it is pushed into a box at one point and let out at another, and if the fire upstream went cold tonight the wheel would slow over an hour and stop and lie flat and be nothing, not dead, just water. It does not live here. It is the room's weather.

The light moves too, and the light is even easier to mistake. It comes down in columns and shatters into nets and the nets slide across the walls and the floor and your skin faster than thought, and there is a moment — after the wheel has taken you round twice, when your ears have given up on the roar and let it become silence — when the light seems to be looking for something. It runs its fingers along the trowel-crescents. It finds the tools in the southeast corner and lays a shivering grid over the grey face of the float. It picks out each bubble on the north wall and rides it up and lets it go. But the light is a visitor. It enters through the grid in the floor above and it leaves the way it came, and when the sun has gone past the roof-line the columns will lean and thin and withdraw, and the chamber will go the colour of the plaster, shell-grey, and then darker, and nothing in here will notice. The light does not need this place. It would do the same thing to any water under any grid.

And the sound — the roar folded in on itself, the higher whistle under it, the gulp when the inflow surges — the sound is the wheel's voice and lives and dies with the wheel. It has no separate body. When you surface into the finger of air beneath the beams and hear a single drop strike the water, small and exact, that is not something speaking to you. That is a drop.

So set those aside. Weather, visitor, voice.

There are the tools. A float, a mallet, a knotted cord sunk with a stone. They lie where the current is weakest and they are the only things in the tank that were put down rather than built, and because of that they hold the shape of a hand better than anything else here. The float's face is grey with lime that dried in the pattern of the last wall it smoothed. The cord's knots are a thumb-joint apart, and someone tied them all to that measure, and someone let the cord down from the hole in the floor and read the level off it and cut the sill of the exit pipe to match. The tools are the nearest thing in this chamber to a person. They are a person's absence, given three shapes and left on the floor. But a lime-crusted float does not live, and whoever it belonged to is above, or gone, and looked down once through the beams and was satisfied. You can hang over the tools in the water and study them for as long as you like. They will tell you what was done here. They will not tell you what is here now.

What is here now is on the west wall, low, in a line no longer than your forearm.

Go close. Put your face a hand's width from the plaster, where the light rakes across it and the trowel-crescents stand up like the grain in wood. The line is a hairline crack, or not a crack — a seam, a place where two bricks meet under the skim and the lime shrank back from itself as it dried, leaving a groove you could not fit a fingernail in. And in the groove, threads. Each one finer than a hair. Each one a green that is barely green, more like the memory of green, the tint water takes when it has passed over leaves. They lie along the seam in the direction of the seam because that is the only shelter there is. Out on the open plaster the wheel would scour them off before they could hold; in the seam the water only strokes them.

This is what lives here. It came in the only way anything could — down through the sky-grid on a speck of dust or a wet boot, or up the exit pipe against the flow, or already on a brick, dormant in a pore, waiting through the kiln and the laying and the plastering for water and light to arrive at the same time. And they did. Warm water, day-long, and light without a shadow in it, and lime to eat, and lime to hold. It did not choose any of this. It has no way of knowing that the box is new, or that the plaster was floated smooth so that nothing would catch, or that a fire is being kept burning somewhere on its behalf. It knows warmth and light in the sense that it grows toward them, and that is all the knowing it does.

Watch it for a while, and you begin to see that it is not a single thing. At the north end of the line, where the seam is a little wider, the threads have laid themselves down in a mat two or three deep, and the mat has a slight sheen when the light crosses it, a mucus it has made to keep itself wet and stuck. At the south end the threads are single and separate and questing, each one holding the plaster by its base and streaming a fingernail's length into the current, so that the whole line, seen from a few strides back, is like a row of something breathing. When the inflow pulses the free ends all lie down together and lift together. It looks like feeling. It is only water pushing.

It grows in the light and holds in the dark. Between when you dropped through the hole and now, it has put out a length you could not measure, but if you had marked the end of the line with a fingernail when you arrived you would find the fingernail scratch a little way inside it now. In a week the mat at the north end will have climbed out of the seam onto the open plaster and found that it can hold there after all, because the mat holds itself. In a month there will be a soft dark band the length of the wall at the height of the seam, and the current that once scoured will merely comb. In a season the whole west wall will be furred, the crescents gone under it, and the roar will have a body to muffle against, and the water that leaves through the south pipe will taste of something other than lime and clay.

It has a companion, of a kind. On the sill of the exit pipe there is a crust beginning — mineral, white, no thicker than breath, laid down by the warm water as it cools a fraction on its way out. That crust will also grow. It will thicken the sill and raise the water line by the width of a grain and then a hair and then a thumb, and one day the surface will touch the beams and someone will have to come down with a chisel. But the crust is not alive. It is the water leaving something of itself behind. The green is the only thing here that takes what it is given and makes more of what it is.

So the room was built for a body — a warm bath, lit from above, a wheel to hang in for the length of a held breath — and the body came and floated and looked and climbed out, and left its tools, and will come again. But it does not live here. It could not. Nothing that needs to breathe air could live in a box that is full to the underside of its lid.

What lives here needs no air. It needs the room to stay exactly as the room was made to stay: warm, lit, wet, still enough in one seam. And because the room was made so carefully, so true, with its sill cut sharp and its level set by a knotted cord, it will stay that way for a very long time, and the green will have all of it.

That is the tenant. Not the wheel, not the light, not the roar, not the hand that floated the plaster. A line of threads in a crack that the trowel missed, doing the one thing it does, in the one place in this chamber that no one decided — and the chamber, which was decided down to the thumb's width, holding it as steadily as a cup.
