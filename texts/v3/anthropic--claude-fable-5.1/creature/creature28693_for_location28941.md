**Who Lives in the Upper Cistern**

Start with the census, since the room invites one, and then be prepared to throw it out.

The amphipods number in the hundreds of thousands. They are the first thing you will call the population, because they are the thing that moves, and because there are so many of them that the walls seem to be made of them. But an amphipod here lives eleven months. It hatches from a brood pouch, grazes the mat, molts six times, is eaten or is not, and the difference between one and the next is nothing you could point to. They are not residents. They are a current where there is no current — the room's only weather, a slow pale tide that runs along the stone and never goes out.

The flatworms are the same, and the snails, and the eyeless shrimp picking their way across the floor. They come and go at the scale of seasons. Their numbers hold because the food holds and the food holds because the spring holds. Take any one of them out of the water and you have removed nothing; the gap closes behind it before it is gone.

The mat is older. The mat may be a single organism, or a federation so long settled that the distinction has softened the way the summit and the cistern have. It has been alive down here for as long as the room has been wet, dying along its upper edge each century as the water climbs and leaves it in the air, growing back a hair's width lower, so that it carries its whole history on the wall in rings like a tree laid on its side. If anything here is the room's memory, it is the mat. But it is a memory with no one inside it. It records. It does not attend.

So: who lives here.

---

The salamanders are different, and you feel the difference before you can say what it is.

There are twelve of them. There have been twelve, or eleven, or thirteen, for longer than the mat has been keeping rings. They hang in the water at their odd angles and do not look at one another, because they cannot look at anything, but they know where the others are. When one drifts to the wall to feed, the others do not drift to the same wall. When one descends, the others do not descend. They have never touched. In a room eleven strides across, in a hundred thousand years, no two of them have ever been in the same place at the same time, and this is not shyness. It is something more like arrangement — the way the stones in the vault are arranged, each one holding its neighbor off.

They are slow in a way that has stopped being a speed and become a kind of material. A meal is an event on the order of a season. A heartbeat, if you could put your ear to one, would come and then not come and then, when you had begun to think it would not, come. Their skin has thinned over the ages to something nearly glass, and in the bruise-colored light you can see the shadow of the organs inside, the small dark fist of the heart, the coil of a gut that has held the same few mouthfuls for weeks. They are not waiting for anything. That is the mistake — to think that stillness this complete must be pointed at something. They are simply the shape a life takes when nothing pushes on it. Left alone long enough, in warm water, in the dark, this is what an animal becomes: a held breath with a mouth at one end.

They live a very long time. No one has ever been here to count, but the water tells you. A salamander hatched in this cistern hatched in a room with a finger more air under the vault than there is now, and it is alive, and it has never noticed. Some of them are older than that. Some of them may have been here when the mat's uppermost ring, the one now dead and grey at the very top of the highest course, was still glowing — which is to say they may be as old as the sealing of the room.

---

One of them is oldest.

You would not know it by size. It is barely longer than the others, and its gill-frills are, if anything, thinner, sparser, worn like the snails' shells to a kind of translucence. You would know it by where it hangs, which is directly above the center of the floor, higher than the rest, close to where the water meets the last of the trapped air, so that its back is sometimes — not often, not for long — in contact with the surface. The only surface in the room, and this is the only body that ever touches it.

And you would know it by the count. In the time you could bear to stay here, if you could bear to stay for years, you would see the others descend to the warm stone perhaps once each. This one descends more. Not greedily. There is no greed in it. But when the order comes around, whatever the order is, it comes around to this one oftener, and it sinks through the water with its limbs folded back and its gills flat against its neck, and it settles its length on the polished face of the block with the deliberateness of something lowering itself into a chair it has sat in all its life, and it stays.

It stays until it rises. There is no other way to say it. Nothing disturbs it. No hunger comes; it has eaten. It lies belly-down on the one warm thing in a warm room, and the mat will not grow within a hand's breadth of it, and the shrimp go around, and the amphipods on the walls go on with their grazing, and then at some point it lifts, and drifts up through the still water to its place near the ceiling, and another one — not the next one in any sequence you could reconstruct — begins its own slow fall.

---

Which brings you to the stone, and to the honest answer.

Everything that lives here eats the stone. Not literally, or not only. But the spring comes up around the stone's edges, and the spring feeds the mat, and the mat feeds the crawl on the walls, and the crawl on the walls feeds the twelve pale bodies in the middle of the room. Follow any mouth in the cistern back far enough and you arrive at the seam where the polished block meets the floor, where warm water rises out of the mountain carrying whatever it carries. The stone is the source. Everything else is downstream of it.

And the stone is warm without a fire. This should be said again, because it is the thing the room is built around, in the way a house is built around a hearth: the reason for the walls, the reason for the roof. Someone polished this block, somewhere else, before there was a cistern, and carried it up to the highest place for a hundred miles, and set it in the floor, and dressed a hundred thousand smaller stones to make a room around it, and put water on it, and closed the lid. You do not do that for a stone. You do that for what the stone is doing.

What it is doing is holding a temperature. That is not nothing. It is, in fact, the one thing that living things do and stones do not: they keep themselves a little warmer than the world around them, for as long as they can, and then they stop. This stone has not stopped. It is not alive — put your hand on it and you will not feel a pulse, only smooth warmth and the ghost of every hand before yours — but it is doing the living thing, patiently, at the bottom of a drowned room, and every creature in the cistern has arranged itself around it the way a household arranges itself around a fire.

So the census reverses. The stone is the tenant of record. The stone was here first and the room was built for it. The mat is the stone's garden; the amphipods and the snails and the shrimp are what grew in the garden; the salamanders are what came to eat what grew, and stayed, and slowed, and stopped being visitors so long ago that they have become something else — not worshippers, there is nothing here that could hold an idea. Attendants, perhaps. Or only this: cold things that found a warm thing, and refined the finding over a hundred thousand years until it looks, in the bruised light, like a ceremony. One at a time. Never two. The way you would take turns at a hearth if the hearth were small and the night were very long.

---

The water will finish rising. The last of the air will go, and the oldest salamander will lose the one surface it ever touched, and it will not notice, because it never needed the surface, only happened to be near it. The amphipods will go on grazing. The mat will stop leaving rings, having nowhere left to die. The vault will hold its final shape.

And in the middle of the floor the stone will still be warm, and there will still be a long pale body lying on it, and another one, somewhere in the dark above, beginning to sink.
