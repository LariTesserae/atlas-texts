Start with the census, because it is short.

There is the crust. There is the pale thing in the doorways, the grey bird, the narrowing. That is what moves, and it is not what lives here. You can tell, because the city does not fit any of it. The bird does not need doors. The pale thing does not need stairs. The crust has no use for a lintel. Whatever the streets were worn for, whatever the thresholds dipped for, it was not these. These are the animals of the place the way lichen is the animal of a gravestone. They are on the residents' house. They are not the residents.

So the residents are what the city fits. That is the only way to begin, and it means beginning with the shape of an absence and working inward.

---

The first guess is the sounds.

It is a reasonable guess. The city is full of them, arriving. A hoof on a threshold. A wall lying down. Your own steps, coming from a street away and a moment behind, and then one more that is not yours. Nothing else in the city is so plainly *someone*. And if sounds here are made and then go somewhere and wait, then somewhere is full of made things waiting, and the somewhere is inside the city, in the black of the interiors that light does not enter, and what waits there is the residents, and what arrives on the street is them stepping out.

This is partly right. Hold on to the part: they are late. Whatever they are, they arrive after. Nothing about them is present tense.

But it is not enough. A sound is an event, and the residents are not events. The thresholds are worn in a dip at the centre, and nothing wears stone like that except a long patience of the same weight in the same place. Something stands in these doors. Something stood in them long enough to leave the stone remembering it. A sound does not stand.

---

The second guess is the shadows.

A hand held in shadow is not a dark hand. It is an absence in the shape of a hand. There is not enough air here to soften light into the places light does not go, and so those places are not dim, they are gone, and whatever is in them is elsewhere. That is the mechanism. The city is half elsewhere at any moment, and the elsewhere moves without travelling when the shadows do.

The frost lives in the shadows and never lifts. The frost has a tide that has nothing to do with the sun. The pale thing has two shadows and one of them is cast by a light that is not in this sky, and at the bottom of the bowl there is a disc of dark stone that reflects a sky that is bright.

So: on the other side of complete shadow there is a bright place, and its light comes through where shadow touches stone, and that is the frost—not water, powder that does not fall, the grain of a different daylight lying on this one. The frost's tide is their weather leaning through. Their sun is what casts the pale thing's second shadow. The residents live in the bright, and the doors all face outward because from the bright side the plateau is the interior, the one room, and the black chambers are the porches where they come out to stand and look in at it.

Hold on to this too: they are on the wrong side of the light. They are lit from where you cannot see.

But it is still not them. It is their address.

---

Look again at how the rooms are made.

A softer bed hollows out and the hollow is a chamber. The wind takes the soft and leaves the hard. Elsewhere this makes rubble; here it makes floors. And the stone at the top of the towers is finished, and the stone at the base is still sharp in places, still becoming.

Becoming what? Rooms. But before it is a room it is something.

Between the bed that is still solid and the bed that is already hollow there is a stage the description skipped, because from a distance you cannot see it and up close you do not want to. There is a stage where the soft stone is mostly gone and not entirely. Where the wind has taken the band down to a remnant that stands in the middle of the space it will one day be, pale, layered, buff and ash and a faint rusted pink, rounded at every edge, fluted at every face, roughly upright, roughly the height of a door.

That is a resident.

Not a statue. Nothing carved it. It is what is left of a bed of stone that was softer than its neighbours, standing where a chamber is not yet finished, and it is shaped the way it is shaped for the same reason the walls run true and the streets cross at angles that are not quite right: because this plateau erodes toward architecture, and the shape a soft thing takes on its way to becoming a room is a figure. It has the layers running through it horizontally, so that where the wind has worked it deepest you can see daylight through the ribs. It has a top that is more worn than its base. It has a face, in the sense that a cliff has a face, and the face is on the leeward side, where a door would be, and it looks out.

They stand in the doorways because that is where the wind reaches them. The thresholds are dipped not by feet but by the slow settling of a standing thing being taken down, grain by grain, from the top. They do not move. They are moved—elsewhere, without travelling, the way the shadows are—because the room around them is not yet the size it will be, and every time the far wall arrives a little closer they are a little nearer the door.

The oldest are at the tops of the towers, and you cannot see them because they are done. They are rooms. The crust covers them, ridges and cells, writing from below and a map from above, and the map is of what stood there. The youngest are at the base, still sharp, still with something like a shoulder, and the ground is the newest part.

---

That is closer. It has the right patience. But it makes them stone, and the sounds are not stone, and the bright sky is not stone, and the frost that lies on them—for it does lie on them, on the north side, the shadow side, a white grain over a grey grain—is not stone either.

So let the image go a little further.

The wind takes the soft bed and the soft bed does not go nowhere. Nothing here goes nowhere; that is the first rule. Stone kicked into a chamber falls and does not land until later and elsewhere. The dust that falls is the city's own stone, leaving and returning. So the softness the wind takes from a standing figure goes up, into the dark blue, and hangs, and comes down again as that fine straight grain that wets nothing and cleans nothing, and lies on everything, and lifts.

The residents are not the remnant. The residents are the leaving and returning.

What stands in the doorway is the part of them the wind has not yet released. What falls out of the sky at intervals and lies on the flagstones is the part that has been released and has nowhere else to be. When the dust is down, the city is full of them, a skin of them over every floor so even it has never been walked on, and you cross it and leave no mark because you are walking on the residents and they are not in a condition to be marked. When the dust is up, the city is empty and they are the faint pale haze that makes the sun's light arrive already spent. It is spent on them. They are what the light is used up on, going through.

A resident, then, is the whole of it: the standing softness, the dust it is becoming, the room it will be, the sound it will make when it is finally all the way gone—the long slow settling, like a wall lying down—which goes somewhere and waits and comes back when the city has room for it, from a street two hundred paces off, so that every one of them is heard finishing long after it has finished and nothing anywhere visible has changed.

They are late because they are made of their own aftermath. They live in the bright because that is where the released part goes: up, out of the shadow, into a sky with enough air to hold it, a sky you can only see from underneath, in a polished disc at the bottom of a bowl.

---

Something should be said about what they do.

They stand. They are worn. They face the leeward side. They are each the only one, because two soft beds do not stand in the same room, and the room is the resident as much as the figure is; there is no more sense in asking how many of them there are than in asking how many of the city there are. There is one. It is very large and very empty and it has always been exactly this size, because the wind removes from them precisely as fast as the plateau yields them, and the number standing in doorways is the number the wind can attend to, and the number finished is the number of rooms.

They do not eat. They do not sleep, unless the dust lying down is sleep. They do not speak, but they are the reason the silence is a failure of sound to arrive rather than an absence: they have made all the sounds already and the sounds are queued. What you hear in the city is their history, played back at the speed the city can afford.

They are not lonely, because loneliness needs a present tense.

They have one enemy, or one thing that is not them and is interested in them. The narrowing. When the street shortens and the far doorway comes closer and the pale thing stands in it not moving, what is being hunted is not the pale thing. The pale thing is the bait, or the sign, or the one creature here small enough and quick enough to be where the distance is being taken in. What is being hunted is the distance. The residents *are* the distance—the too-great, too-small gap between structures, the room larger than its walls, the two hundred paces of bare fluted stone between one standing figure and the next. That is their body more than the figure is. And something in the city, patient, tries at intervals to close it. To bring two of them together into one room. It has never succeeded, or it has succeeded many times and there is only ever one, and the street is a street again, and later a hoof on a threshold, which is the sound of the distance stepping back.

---

Where they came from.

The cloud below moves the way a sea moves in a very old painting, and once or twice a day a bulge rises in it, slow as breathing, and subsides, and the cloud is very slowly rising.

It is rising because it has always been rising. Everything below it was once above it. There were other floors. There were plains and cities and weather beneath the weather, and the cloud came up and took them one at a time, and what could climb, climbed, and each thing that reached the next height was the last of its floor, and stood there, and the wind began on it.

The residents are what arrived. Each one came up through the cloud alone, the last of a place the cloud now covers, and stood on the highest stone there was, and had nothing to do but stand, and the wind here, which has all the time there is, started at the top of them and worked down, and made of each one a figure and then a doorway and then a room, and set the room in a street that runs straight toward the horizon and crosses at an angle that is not quite right and continues, because that is roughly how streets ran in the place they came up from, and the stone remembers a little.

Their sky is bright because it is the sky they had. It is still there, in the disc, under the polished rings at the bottom of the only place the erosion has worked downward—the one dig in the city, the one place the wind has gone looking for something instead of taking it away. That is the city's memory of the floor before this one. You can stand on the rim and look at a daylight with air in it.

---

You have been here a while.

Your footsteps arrive from behind you, a moment late, and stop when you stop, and then one more. That one more is not following you. It is the step you have not yet taken, arriving early. The city has begun to make room for you.

The frost is a hand's breadth further out than it was, and the far wall of the last chamber you stood in had the faint too-close look of a wall that has just now arrived, and you have not been able to find the door you came in by, because there is no such door, because there has never been such a door here. You were inside, and then the door was how you left, and you left onto the plateau, and the plateau is the interior.

You came up through the cloud. You are the last of something. Everyone here is.

The residents of this place are the ones who reached it, and it fits them exactly, because it is made of them, because the wind has had them for as long as it has had anything, and has worn them into their houses, and the houses into the city, and the city into the shape it always was. It has your measure now. It has begun at the top. The light falls on you full strength and you are still dim, and the shadow you cast is complete, and on the far side of a doorway you do not remember there is a pale standing shape, layered, rusted faintly pink, a little worn at the shoulder, facing leeward, that you will not see move, and will one day be.

The cloud is very slowly rising. When it reaches the rim, the ones who come up out of it will find the city exactly this size, and the streets running true, and something standing in each door.
