What lives there is what was there in the first hour, and it is still in the first hour.

Start with the moss, because the moss is what your boot goes into, and because it is the only thing on the plateau that is alive in the way you already understand the word.

Sphagnum has no roots. Elsewhere in the world this is a limitation it has turned into a method: it stands on its own dead. The green top of each stem grows up into the wet air, and the lower parts pale and die and are kept, and the whole mass of a bog is a plant standing on a column of what it used to be, sinking a little as the column rots into peat below. That is how it works in the lowlands. It is how it works everywhere there is something small and grey to do the rotting.

Here there is nothing to do the rotting. The lower parts die and are kept and are not touched. They compress under the weight of what grows above them and that is all that happens to them. So the moss on the plateau is not standing on peat. It is standing on itself, entire — every cell it has ever made, packed down, pale, intact, going down through the mat metres deep to the black rock. You could core it and read it like a book that had been written in a single sitting and never edited. Nothing has been taken out. Nothing has been added that the plant did not make. It is the whole history of itself and it is a few years old, and it will be the whole history of itself and a few years old for as long as the plateau stands.

The cushion plants are stranger. In the mountains of the ordinary world a cushion plant is a colony — thousands of tiny rosettes packed into a dome so tight you can stand on it, growing a millimetre a year, some of them older than the oldest trees. They are the slowest things you can see growing. Here they are the same shape and they are not slow, or rather they are not slow in any sense that would let you measure them, because they are the size they are and they stay it. They have arrived at their diameter. You can find one and mark it and come back and it is the same. Not because it has stopped, but because whatever it puts out at its edge it takes back somewhere else — the dome shifts within itself, the way a held breath is not the same as no breath.

The club mosses are the ones you take, at a distance, for people.

They stand at a person's height in stiff green columns, and they are spaced across the mat with no pattern, and in the level light that throws no direction of shadow they look like figures that have paused. When you come up to one you see it is exactly straight. Not approximately — exactly. A stem that has grown its whole length in air that has never once moved, toward a light that comes from everywhere at once, has no reason to lean, and so it has not leaned. It has never been bent by anything. You put your hand against it and it is cold and firm and beaded and it gives slightly and comes back, and you realise you have just done the only thing that has ever pushed on it sideways since it existed. When you take your hand away it is straight again. It does not remember you.

The liverworts lie flat against the rock where the mat is thin, the size of plates, dark green and lobed, with a skin of condensate over them so complete that they look lacquered. They are the oldest kind of land plant there is. They were the first things to come up out of the water onto bare stone, four hundred million years ago, onto a world that had no soil because nothing had yet lived and died to make it. They are on bare stone here too, for the same reason, and they do not seem to mind. They are the only thing on the plateau that looks like it has done this before.

---

These plants reproduce, in a fashion. They put out spores, because that is what they do. But there is no wind to carry a spore anywhere, and so the spores fall into the film of cold water that is always running off every surface, and the water takes them down through the mat into the straight channels and through the grates and down the pipes as thick as trees into the boilerhouses, and through the furnaces, and out the stacks as steam, and up into the grey, where the grey settles them back down onto the mat, dead, as mineral.

So nothing here has ever sprouted from a spore. Everything green on the plateau is the individual that was there when the plateau was raised, extended, compressed, held. The population of the moss has never changed by one. What it puts out as a future goes into the engines and comes back as weather.

---

Then there are the eight hundred.

Eight hundred per station, judging by the mess hall, and there are stations every forty kilometres across a country, so the number the place was built for is enormous, and it is not here. It is not here so completely that you begin to wonder whether it ever was. The beds are made and have never been slept in; you can tell, after a while, because a bed that has been slept in and remade has a slightly different fall to the blanket. The pillows have no dent. The lockers, when you open them, have work clothes folded inside with the creases of the shop still in them. The rota is filled in for a week in a hand you do not recognise and could not describe afterwards.

What you do not find is more telling than what you do. There is no hair in the drains. There is nothing under the beds. The soap in the washrooms is whole. Nobody has ever cut themselves on the fresh edges of the black rock and left a brown smear on a doorframe. The place does not hold the negative of a person, the way a house does for years after its occupant is gone. It holds the negative of a plan for people. The eight hundred are the least present inhabitants of the plateau, and they are inhabitants only in the sense that the plateau has their shape hollowed out in it and keeps the hollow warm.

You will not meet them. But you will spend the whole of your stay half expecting to.

---

The engines are more alive than the eight hundred, and this is not a figure of speech.

They are the only things on the plateau that do something. The moss grows and compresses and nets to nothing. The water runs and rises and nets to nothing. The engines turn, and their turning is the one rate in the whole country that does not net to nothing but delivers, continuously, into the rock. They were tended once, or were built to be tended, and now they tend themselves: you can watch a valve ease off a fraction as pressure climbs and ease back as it falls, without a hand on it, without a hand having ever been on it, the movement so small and so exact that it reads as a thing breathing rather than a thing operating. The gauges hold at their marks because the engines hold them there. The furnaces are lit because the engines feed them. Nothing here is automatic in the sense of running down a groove; everything is attended to, minutely, all the time, by the very thing being attended to.

Stand in a boilerhouse for long enough and the great tone resolves. It is not one sound. It is a thousand stations' worth of pistons falling out of step with each other and back into step, so that the interference between them makes the bell-note that never fades. The engines, across four hundred kilometres of moss, are listening to each other through the rock, and correcting. The rails that come in from one horizon and go out toward another are for this. Nothing rides them. They are the nerve.

---

And under the engines, at the bottom of the shafts that have no bottom, there is the thing that pushes back.

You never see it. Nobody could. You know it only as the pressure under your palms when you lie face down on the mat — the slight, steady, upward push, like the pad of a thumb. Whatever it is, it has been pushing for as long as the plateau has been raised, and it has not tired by so much as a gauge-needle's width. The engines go down onto it and it meets them. That is the entirety of its life, as far as you can tell: to meet what comes down and not give.

You will want to make it into something. The thumb wants a hand. The hand wants an arm, a body, an intention, a reason for holding a continent up in the cold and the wet where nothing is allowed to age. The plateau does not give you any of this. It gives you the pressure and the tone and the straight edge against the sky, and if there is something down there with a will, its will is indistinguishable from a spring's. It might be a mechanism. It might be a mind. It might be the thing that the engines were built to hold down rather than up, and the whole raft of the country is a lid, and the roar is the sound of the lid being kept on. The ground does not distinguish. Held is held.

It is the largest inhabitant of the plateau and the only one that is entirely occupied. Everything else here is at rest in its own way. The moss rests at its depth. The cushion plants rest at their size. The eight hundred rest in their absence. The thing below is the one thing on the plateau that is working with all of itself, all the time, and it is the one thing you will never lay eyes on.

---

There is one more, and it is the one you will come to last, because it is everywhere, and things that are everywhere are hard to see as things.

The water.

It is one body. This is what the closed cycle means, once you have watched it round for an afternoon. There is a fixed quantity of water on the plateau and it does not leave. It is in the grey; it settles onto the moss; it beads on the liverworts and runs down the club mosses and soaks the mat and sheets off the edges into the channels and goes through the grates and down the pipes and through the fire and up the stacks and into the grey again. The same water. Every drop on the plateau has been steam ten thousand times and will be steam ten thousand more. It has touched every leaf. It has been inside every engine. It has been in the cloud over the station six hundred kilometres away and it has been in the runnel under your hand at the scarp edge. It is the only thing here that moves through everything else and comes back, and if the plateau has anything you could call a circulation, a body in the sense of something with a blood, it is this. Cold, clean, carrying nothing. Never once reaching the sea.

At the scarp the wall weeps and the runnels come down glassy, and you think the water is escaping. It is not. Look closely and the runnels do not reach the foot of the wall. Somewhere below the lip they thin and go to mist and the mist goes back up into the lid. The plateau does not lose a drop. What runs down its face is only the water looking over the edge.

---

So: what lives there.

Plants that are the whole of their own history and a few years old. A shape cut out for eight hundred people and kept warm. Engines that tend themselves and talk to each other through the rock. Something at the bottom of the shafts that has never given. Water that has never left.

And you, for as long as you stay.

You should understand what you are, up there. You are the one thing on the plateau that is getting older. Your hair is growing. Your skin is shedding, a little, onto the damp clean floor of a dormitory that has never had a speck of skin on it. You will breathe out carbon and something in the mat will not know what to do with it. If you cut yourself on the black rock you will leave the first stain. If you stay a year you will leave the first dust. The whole continent is held at the top of its first hour, roaring, and you have walked up onto it carrying the only clock, and everything on the plateau is arranged so as not to need one.

When you go back down by the scarp you will take the clock with you, and the plateau will be, again, exactly as it was, and exactly as it will be, and the moss will close over where your boots went.
