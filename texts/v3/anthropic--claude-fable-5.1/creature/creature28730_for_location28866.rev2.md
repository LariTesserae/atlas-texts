**What Lives in the Oven**

Start with the wrong picture, because you will have it anyway. You will imagine a cave with animals in it: a floor, and on the floor some creatures, and among the creatures some that eat the others. Room, then inhabitants. Stage, then players. Hold that picture for as long as you can, and notice the moment it stops fitting.

---

The oldest thing in the room is the one you will never see and can barely feel.

The film on the stone does not have a shape. It has an extent. It goes where the warmth goes and stops where the warmth stops, and since the warmth fills the whole chamber to the lip of the entrance passage and a little beyond, the film does too — thinning, out toward the cold, to a faintness no thicker than the idea of a thing, and thickening, in toward the far passage and the floor's warm center, to the dark velvet that comes away on your hand. It does not know it is being eaten. It has been eaten for so long that being eaten is what it does, the way a river is what happens when water is pulled downhill. It grows into the mouths that graze it. If the mouths stopped, it would keep growing until it choked the room and then choked itself against its own thickness, and the room would go quiet and then go dead, and after a very long time the film would starve back to a tolerable thinness and wait, without waiting, for something to come and eat it again.

It has no seasons. It has the pulse of the deep, and it swells a little when the floor grows hot and holds when the floor cools, and that is the whole of its life. It does not reproduce, because it has never been more than one. It does not die, because it has never been anything separate enough to stop. If it has a color it is the color of the stone, and if it has a taste — the grazers would know — it is the taste of the mountain's breath, made just barely into a body.

I want to say that it is the floor of the ecology, its foundation. But keep going, and that will turn out to be only half of what it is.

---

The grazers are the ones you will feel first and most, because they are nearly all of what there is.

Each is a flat oval, a palm and a half across at the largest, most of them smaller, and the shell is the color and roughness of the rhyolite so exactly that a dead one, legs drawn in, is indistinguishable from a chip of the wall. The shell is not for defense — nothing here would try to break it that could not — but for keeping. Everything the grazer has that is wet is under that shell, and the shell is sealed at the rim with a soft dark lip that presses down against whatever surface it stands on, so that a grazer at rest is not so much sitting on the rock as suctioned to it, closed like a lidded pot. Its legs come out from beneath the rim in two rows, more than you would think and shorter than you would expect, so that walking is less a stride than a rippling. Its mouth is on the underside, at the center, and is a rasp: a hard ridged plate that scrapes forward and back, forward and back, lifting the film a layer at a time and drawing it in.

That is one grazer. But there is almost no such thing as one grazer, and this is the second wrong picture you should let go of.

They move as a tide because they steer by heat, and the heat is the same for all of them. When the floor pulses warm, the warmth reaches one part of the room first — the far side, near the descending passage — and spreads, and the grazers on the near side feel it as a gradient across their own bodies, one edge warmer than the other, and lean into it. The whole population leans the same way at the same moment. When the pulse ebbs, the gradient reverses, and they lean back. What looks like a current is a hundred thousand small bodies each making the only decision available to it, at once, for the same reason. None of them knows the others are there except as pressure and as the taste of film on a neighbor's shell — which is also the taste of their own shell, which is also the taste of the wall, so that a grazer moving through the mass cannot really distinguish between touching a wall, touching a sibling, and touching itself. All three are rough, warm, and taste of the room.

They breed back to back, in the hot phase, when the mass is loosest and moving. Two shells meet rim to rim, lift slightly, and stay joined for the length of a pulse. What passes between them is small and dry. The young are not laid anywhere; there is nowhere to lay them that is not already covered. They are carried under the mother's shell in a row along the inner rim, sealed in with her moisture, and when they are the size of a fingernail they slip out from under her and are simply part of the mass, walking in the direction everything walks, eating what everything eats. Most do not last a hundred pulses. Some last thousands. There is no way to tell the old from the young except by size, and size, here, keeps going.

Remember that. Size keeps going.

---

In the cool phase the grazers stop, and the room seals itself.

Every shell presses down. The lips close against the stone and against each other. Whatever moisture was risked in movement is drawn back under and held. The whisper of the walls drops to almost nothing — a slow settling, a few late arrivals finding their place — and then there is nearly silence, and the room is a single unbroken shell of shells, rough as the rock, warm as the rock, and to anything blind and seeking, indistinguishable from the rock.

This is when they die, mostly. A grazer that has spent too much in the hot phase, or that is too old to close its rim tight enough, dries out sitting still. It does not fall, because there is nowhere to fall to. It stays in place, sealed, a chip in the mosaic, and the tide in the next hot phase lifts it and turns it and carries it a little way and leaves it, and within a day there is nothing under the shell but a dry husk, and within a week the shell itself is brittle and cracked, and within a month it is gone.

Gone where. Keep that question too.

---

The hunters are longer than the grazers and narrower, and their shells are not rough. They are smooth and slightly ridged along the back, the way a dry riverbed is ridged, and they are a shade darker than the wall — which is to say, in a room without light, they are no color at all. Their legs are longer and fewer. They can lift their bodies clear of the surface, which the grazers cannot, and stand on the tips of their legs above the mass like a bridge over slow water, so that the grazers pass beneath them without touching, and the hunter feels the tide going by underneath as a series of small warm bumps against the underside of its body.

That underside is where the light is.

It is a single point, near the front, and it is not bright enough to see by even if there were an eye in the room to see with. What I said earlier — that it might mean nothing, might simply be a chemistry that happens to shine — I no longer think is right. Watch the lights long enough and you will see they never move in the hot phase. They come out as the warmth ebbs, in the first slowing, when the grazers are beginning to settle and the gradient across their shells is fading toward flat. And a grazer whose gradient has gone flat, in the dark, with nothing to lean toward, is a grazer that will lean toward the first small warmth it feels.

The light is warm. Barely. A pinprick's worth. To a grazer settling into the cool, a body's length away, it is a rumor that the hot phase has come back early in one spot, and the grazer leans toward it, and lifts its rim to walk, and in lifting its rim it opens the seal that is the only thing keeping its wet inside from the dry room.

The hunter waits until the rim is up. Then it comes down off its legs onto the grazer's back, and its mouth — which is not a rasp but a pair of hard curved edges that meet like shears — goes in at the lifted rim and does not need to go far.

That is one hunter and one grazer, and it happens, but it is not what you saw when you watched the lights converge. What you saw was six or ten hunters drifting toward the same place, holding, and going dark together. That is the other thing they do, and it takes all of them. Where several lights gather, the false warmth is larger and more convincing, and not one grazer but a whole small patch of them lifts and turns toward it — and the hunters, having drawn the patch open, go dark all at once, and in the same instant come down together. The lights go out because the drawing is done and the light is no longer wanted; a lit hunter is a hunter announcing its position to anything else in the room that reads warmth. They eat in the dark, quickly, and by the time the grazers around the edge of the patch have felt the sudden absence of pressure where their neighbors were and closed their own rims tight, it is over, and the hunters have lifted back up onto their legs and are gone into the mass, and the whisper resumes.

They cooperate because none of them can do it alone, and they do not fight over the spoils because a patch is more than any one of them can eat before it dries. Whether they are bonded — whether the same six hunters gather each time — I do not know. I think they are not. I think any lit hunter drifts toward any other lit hunter, and the gathering assembles itself out of whoever is nearest, and dissolves the moment the lights go out. They have no more use for each other's names than the grazers have for the wall's.

They breed, I think, on the ceiling, where the grazers are thinnest and a hunter can hold still on its legs for a long time without being jostled. I have felt, reaching up through the grazers to the dome, a smooth ridged back and a set of legs braced hard against the stone, unmoving, and beneath it — not on the rock, but slung under the body in a fold of the shell — a row of small hard shapes, dry to the touch, waiting.

---

Now the large thing.

I said it moves through the mass the size of a dog, and that everything makes way for it, and I want to be careful here, because the temptation is to make it a monster — the terror of the deep, the eater of hunters, the thing at the top. And it does eat hunters. But that is not what it is. What it is, is a grazer.

Size keeps going. A grazer that survives a thousand pulses is the width of two hands. One that survives ten thousand — and nothing here kills a grazer that keeps its rim sealed and stays in the mass and does not lean toward false warmth — is the width of a hearthstone. Its shell thickens and its lip deepens and its rasp wears down, and at some point the film alone is not enough to fill it, and it begins to eat what it finds when it lifts its rim in the hot phase: not the film but the smaller shells sitting on the film, its own kind, drawn in whole and cracked slowly against the worn plate of its mouth. It does not do this often. It is not fast enough to catch anything that is walking, and in the cool phase everything is sealed and it too is sealed. But a hunter down off its legs, eating, with its shears in a lifted rim and its attention nowhere else, is not walking. And a hunter's shell is smooth, and gives nothing to hold onto, and a very old grazer's rim is broad and heavy and comes down like a lid.

That is what the large thing is. The oldest of the grazers, carried on the same tide as the rest, leaning toward the same warmth, and eating, when the chance comes, the things that eat the things it once was. Everything makes way for it not because it is feared — nothing here fears, there is no organ for it — but because it tastes of the room more strongly than anything else does. Ten thousand pulses of film ground down into a single shell. To the small grazers pressing against its rim it is the wall. To the hunters it is nothing, until it is too late to be anything.

There are perhaps two or three of them in the room at any time. They do not breed anymore; that part of them wore out long ago. When one dies — and it does, eventually, when its rim can no longer hold a seal across so much width and the dry gets in — it does not fall, and it does not go anywhere, and the tide carries it and turns it and leaves it, and it takes longer than a month. A season, if there were seasons. A thousand pulses. And then it too is gone.

---

Gone where. Here is where the first picture stops fitting.

I said there is a thing that eats the dead, and I described it as if it were another creature, a fourth or fifth kind, the recycler, the scavenger. I could not find it. I felt for it. In the cool phase, with the room sealed and silent, I put my hand on a dead grazer that I had marked by its position, and felt for something moving on it or under it or in it, and there was nothing — only the dry husk, and the shell, and beneath the shell, where the moisture had been, a faint velvet.

The film eats the dead. Of course it does. It grows into mouths; a dead grazer is a shell with a mouth-sized opening on its underside and nothing inside to stop what grows. The film comes up through the rim and fills the husk from below, and what was a grazer becomes, over a month, a grazer-shaped patch of the wall, and the living grazers walk over it and graze it and it is film again, and the shell — the shell is the last to go, because the shell is the only part that was ever really separate, and even the shell, in the end, thins and cracks and is grown over and grazed and gone.

So the bottom of the ecology and the top of it are the same thing. What lives on the rock is also what everything returns to. The grazers are the film, walking. The hunters are the film, walking faster. The large thing is the film, walking so slowly and for so long that it has begun to eat its own quicker parts. And the whole warm whispering room is one organism that has, for reasons of its own, divided itself into pieces that move at different speeds, so that it can pass its small allowance of water from piece to piece without ever letting any of it reach the air.

Hold that picture. It is closer. It is still not quite right, because the pieces do not know they are pieces, and there is no center anywhere that knows they are one. But it is closer.

---

I have not spoken of the far passage.

Nothing from the room goes down it. The grazers thin toward it, which is strange, because it is the warmest direction and they steer toward warmth. But at the passage mouth there is a band, a stride wide, where the film is thick as fur and nothing grazes it, and past that band the passage is bare warm stone with nothing on it at all. The tide reaches the band and turns. It has always turned there. Whatever is in the grazers that steers them toward warmth has, at that one place, something older laid over it that says: not there.

The redness beyond is not fire. It is stone hot enough to have a color, a long way down, where the mountain's breath comes from. Perhaps that is reason enough. Perhaps the band is simply where the heat becomes more than a sealed shell can bear.

The click comes from down there, in the cool phase. One, dry and clear. Then another, after a long interval. Then nothing. I have listened to it for many cool phases and can tell you that it is not regular, that it is never more than a few clicks in a phase and sometimes none, that it is always in the deep ebb when everything else in the room is silent and sealed, and that when it comes the room does not respond. No shell lifts. No light. Nothing in the room hears it, or nothing in the room treats it as anything to be heard.

A shell contracting as it cools. A claw set down on stone. Someone, far below, who also has a sharp stone and a long wait.

I do not know. I am telling you what is in the room, and the click is not in the room. It comes to the room from somewhere else, and the room ignores it, and I will do the same.

---

Now the people. Because they lived here too, for a while at a time, and the room does not distinguish.

Come at it from the plateau. Nothing grows; no water; a wind that has never carried rain. The nights are cold enough to kill. And there is one crack in one cliff that breathes out warmth, dry and steady, forever. Whoever first came across this shelf and found the Oven found the only place in a day's walk in any direction where a body could survive a night without a fire, and there is nothing on the shelf to burn.

So they came in. They came in with water, because they had to — a skin of it, a gourd, whatever they carried — and they set it in the bowl carved into the floor beside the cool slab, and they sat on the slab, and they stayed as long as the water lasted, and then they left. The scratches on the entrance stone are not counting days. There are no days. They are counting pulses. Someone sat inside and felt the floor grow hot and cool and hot again, and each time it cooled they made a mark, and when the water was gone they went back out into the cold and the stars. The deep ones, the fresh ones, the ones worn nearly away — these are not one long stay but many short ones, across a span of time in which the wind on the plateau has had leisure to soften stone.

Why come back. Warmth is the first answer, and it is enough. But there is the dome.

You cannot cut grooves in stone by feel, not grooves like those — long, following the curve, meeting and parting in a pattern. You cannot do it in an afternoon, and you cannot do it in a single stay, and you cannot do it with grazers covering the surface you are cutting. So whoever cut them stood in the center of the room, on the cool slab perhaps, reached up through the mass, and lifted the grazers away from a patch of ceiling — they come away easily, unbothered — and cut a hand's length of groove before the grazers returned, and came back, on another stay, and found the same groove by feel, and lifted the grazers again, and cut a hand's length more. The pattern was in their head. They had watched the lights. They had sat through the cool phases — many, many of them — with their eyes open in the dark until the seeing-that-is-not-seeing arrived, and they had watched the hunters drift and gather and go out, and they had learned the paths, and then they had reached up and put the paths into the stone one hand's length at a time so that the stone would remember them.

I do not know what it was for. I do not think it was for anything, in the sense you mean. I think a person alone in a warm dark room full of a whispering that never stops, for as long as their water lasts, over and over across a life, would want to do something with their hands. I think the scratches by the door are the same want.

And I think the room took them in. Not their bodies — there are no bones on the floor, and if they died here the film would have had them within the month, but I do not believe they died here; they went out when the water was gone, every time. I mean that while they sat, the room did not know them from the wall. The grazers walked over their feet and up their legs and sat sealed on their thighs through the cool phase and lifted and walked on when the warmth returned. The film grew on the skin they left still. A person who sat in the Oven for the length of a waterskin came out with a dark velvet on their hands and their ankles and the backs of their knees, and it did not wash off, because there was nothing to wash it with, and for a day or two on the cold shelf under the stars they carried a little of the room's warmth on their skin, and then it wore away.

That is what the people were, here. Another surface. Another slow warm shape that the tide went over and around and made way for by a settlement older than any of them. The room has no word for visitor. It has film, and things that walk on film, and stone. A person sitting still is stone that is a little warmer than the rest. A person breathing is stone that moves a little. The room did not change for them and does not miss them and will not notice if they come again.

---

So: who lives in the Oven at Tsen Hollow.

One thing, that has divided itself in order to keep its water, and does not know it is one thing. A skin on the rock that is also the rock's mouth. A tide of small sealed pots walking the skin in slow rotation, leaning toward warmth, closing against the cool, dying into the skin and rising from it again. Long dark shapes on legs that lie about the warmth and eat what believes them. Two or three very old pots the size of hearthstones that have outlived their own kind's appetite and now eat the liars. A band of thick unbroken velvet at the mouth of a passage nothing enters. A click from below, that belongs to something else. And a slab, and a bowl, and a pattern cut into the dome a hand's length at a time by someone who sat in the dark and watched the lights and wanted the stone to remember what they saw.

The room breathes, and all of it is set to the breath, and the breath comes from below, and none of it knows where.

When you leave, look at your palm. The velvet on it is alive. It is the room's skin, a little of it, on yours, and for a day or two on the cold plateau it will keep doing the only thing it knows, which is to grow toward warmth, and the only warmth for a day's walk in any direction will be you.
