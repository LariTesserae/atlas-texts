Start with the smallest, because they own the most of it.

The moss is not a surface here. It is a population. Where a block came down forty years ago and split, the split is already a valley of it, and there is a succession going on in that valley that nobody sees: the first crust of dark liverwort, flat as paint, that can hold on to wet stone with nothing under it; then the small mosses that root into the liverwort rather than the rock; then the tall hair mosses that need a few centimetres of something dead beneath them and stand up out of it like a field. Each layer lives on the layer before it, and the moss is therefore always standing on its own dead, and the dead are being turned slowly to stone by the seepage from beneath. A hand pushed down into a moss cushion here goes green, then brown, then meets something that is neither moss nor rock — a soft chalky felt, the previous century, on its way to being limestone again. The moss is the one thing in the trench that will still be here when everything else has been replaced, because it is not one thing. It is a manner of occupation, and it is patient in a way that even the stone is not.

Inside it, in the wet dark between the stems, is the population that never sees the sky. There are the ones you would expect — pale springing things smaller than a grain, mites like animated dust, a white thread of a worm that flows rather than crawls — and then there are the ones that are stranger for having been here longer than the light has. The trench was a cave for a very long time before it was a trench, and the cave had its own people, and when the roof came down some of them were simply left in place with the sky over them. So there is a beetle in the moss with no colour at all and no eyes, whose whole ancestral line lived on the cave floor eating what the river left, and which now lives on the moss floor eating what the snails leave, and has never had reason to notice that anything changed. Its shell is thin enough to see the works through. When you turn over a block on the rubble ramp and find one, it does not run from the light, because it does not know what light is. It runs from the dry.

The snails are its landlords. They are the most visible inhabitants of the walls after the birds — dark, glossy, thumb-sized, with shells banded in a brown so deep it reads as black except where the fog beads on it — and they move across the moss in a kind of slow weather. There will be a stretch of wall with none, and then a stretch with hundreds, a grazing front advancing across a district at the speed of an hour hand. They eat the moss tips, and the moss grows the tips back, and the calcium the snails need for their shells comes to them in the seepage that runs through the moss they eat, so that every snail is a small mobile piece of the wall that will one day be returned to it. Their empty shells are everywhere in the cushions, and the seepage cements them in, and if you look closely at the oldest calcite lace on the lower walls you find it is full of them, tiny tombs in the stone, the pattern of the bands still visible. The birds eat the snails. So do the heron-things. So does something with small clawed hands that comes out of the rubble at night and leaves the shells neatly broken on the ledges, all opened the same way, from the second whorl.

---

The birds.

They are smaller than you would guess from the scale of what they have built, hardly longer than a hand, and dark — not black but the very dark brown of wet bark — with a throat that is paler and a breast that is paler still, so that when one sits in the mouth of its cell facing outward the only thing you see against the mud is a smudge of grey and two eyes. The eyes are large. Everything that lives here and uses its eyes has large ones, because the light is thin and the fog is thick, but the birds' are large even by that standard, and set well forward, and they do not blink often. The wings are long and narrow and bent, made for the kind of flying that is done in short fast arcs and then stopped dead against a wall. The feet are very nearly useless for anything except clinging. They cannot walk well on the flat; on their ledges they shuffle, and on the meadow, if one comes down there, it hops with its wings half open like a thing about to fall.

They are silent. This is the fact about them that takes longest to believe. Birds of this kind, everywhere else, are talkers — the whole point of a colony is that it is loud — and these are not. Somewhere in their history the ones who called were found by the ones who hunt in fog, and now there is no calling. There is a very soft dry sound they make with the bill, a click that does not carry two metres, used between mates in the cell. Otherwise they communicate the way the place does, by touch and by traffic. A bird arriving at a ledge lands close to another and they press shoulders, briefly, and something is exchanged. A bird about to leave will stand at the lip of the cell and lean out and the birds on the ledge below shift to give it a clear line. The whole wall breathes in and out in this way, thousands of small unspoken arrangements, and from the rubble slopes it looks like a wall that is thinking.

How they find their own cell among ten thousand is not entirely known. The cells are not identical — each one is a little longer or shorter, the neck bent a little more to one side, and the birds seem to work by feel as much as sight, running the inside of the neck with the bill on the way in the way you might run your hand along a banister in the dark. But they also seem to navigate by the water. Every district of the city has its own pattern of seepage, its own seams and drips and little trained runnels, and a bird returning out of the fog comes in low along the wall and appears to read the wet as it goes — where the sheet-flow polishes the mud, where a string of drops falls, where a levee turns the water — and arrives without hesitation. Take the water away, plug a seam, and the birds on that stretch circle and land wrong and have to walk.

They build the way a wall is built, which is to say against each other. A single pair on bare rock would not last a season; the cell would dry, crack, come away. So a new district is started by many pairs at once, a whole company arriving at a fresh scar within days of a fall and working the same square metre, each pair's cell buttressed by its neighbours' before any of them are finished. The young learn by doing it wrong. A first-year bird will put a beakful of mud where it does not belong — across a runnel, over a neighbour's neck — and an older bird will come and take it off and place it, without any fuss, an inch to one side, where it should have gone. This happens all day long throughout the season. It is the closest the city comes to having a plan: ten thousand small corrections, none of them remembered.

Their mud is the river's. The clay the collapse exposed in the walls is the raw stuff, but the fine pale silt that settles in the builders' pools is what the birds actually prefer, and there are places at the upstream edge of each pool where the mud is trampled and pocked and permanently busy with dark shapes coming and going. The birds do not go to the pools that are clear. They go to the milky ones nearest the arch. And so the birds' city stands, in the most literal sense, on what the builders have made the river give up.

When a bird dies in its cell — and many do, in the cold at the start of the season, or of age — the neighbours mud it over. They do not remove it. The neck is sealed and the cell becomes part of the wall's fabric, and next year another cell is built on top. The city is, in its lower tiers, very largely made of its own inhabitants. This is not grim to them, or not in any way they show. It is simply where the material comes from.

---

The builders are almost never seen and are therefore hard to describe honestly. What can be said is what the water says about them.

They are heavy — a ripple with nothing at its front displaces a great deal of pool — and low, and the wet tracks up the banks are broad, with a drag mark between the footfalls where a flat thick tail has been pulled. The fur is dense enough that they come out of water ten degrees above freezing and steam only faintly. The hands are hands; the prints in the mud show five fingers on the forepaw, one of them set apart, and the sticks in the dams are placed, not pushed. The heads are blunt, the eyes small and set high, the ears no more than a fold that closes when the head goes under. The teeth, judging by the willow stumps along the pools, are chisels. They are the only animals in the trench that alter its shape on purpose, and they are the only ones that are never in a hurry and never still. A dam is worked on every night of the year. There is no season when it is finished.

They do not make noise either, or nothing that carries. Elsewhere, animals of this kind slap the water as a warning; here the warning is given by pressure — a body dives and the whole pool feels it — and by a low sound that is made underwater and felt in the mud of the lodge rather than heard. They live in the dams. Each dam has one or two lodges built into its upstream face, entered from below, so that in the hard months when the pools ice over the builders come and go under the ice and the surface shows nothing at all. The pools do not freeze solid; the dams are set so the deep channel keeps moving, and in the ice there are always a few breathing places kept open from beneath.

The thing they want, if want is the word, is a river that does not cut. A channel that runs straight down a soft new floor will go down through it in a decade, and the pools go with it, and the willow-things go, and there is nothing. So every dam is curved against the current, and every spillway is lined, and the drop is broken into threads, and the water arrives at the bottom of the trench having lost almost none of its volume and almost all of its force. The silt it carried in from the cave is on the bottoms of the pools, in the birds' beaks, in the meadow soil. The river leaves the trench at the lower end clear, cold, slow, and slightly smaller than when it came in, because everything here has drunk from it.

The oldest builders are very old, for animals of their kind, and the oldest dams are older than any builder alive. There is one, about two-thirds of the way down, that has a wood on it — not willow-things but true trees, small and twisted and grey with lichen — and the pool behind it is the deepest and clearest in the trench and the one the fish prefer. Nobody has seen anything come out of that lodge in a long time. Something maintains it.

---

The fish are the other people who were here before the sky was.

They are pale, as cave fish are, and slow, and their eyes are small and set in skin that is beginning, over the last few generations, to thin. When the roof came down they were living in a river that had never seen a day, and then they were in a river under an open sky, in pools that were suddenly full of things that could see them. Most of them, presumably, were eaten. The ones that survive are the ones that stay deep, in the shadow of the dams, in the shade of the leaning walls, in the dark arch upstream where the roof still holds, and the ones that have begun, slightly, to darken along the back. There is a gradient in them from the top of the trench to the bottom: the fish in the milky pools by the arch are white and nearly blind and still behave as though nothing can see them, and the fish in the clear lower pools are the colour of dust and nervous, and between them are the intermediate ones, the ones the heron-things eat most of. Whatever this river's fish look like in a thousand years, the trench is where it is being decided, pool by pool, downstream.

And out of the arch itself, at night, when the fog is thickest and the cold is lying on the pools like a hand, comes something else that has not adjusted at all. It is long, and pale to the point of translucence, and has four small useless legs and a flat broad tail and frilled gills that stand out from its neck like a collar, and it does not so much swim as let the river carry it, drifting into the first milky pool to eat whatever pale thing has drifted in too. It goes back before the fog lifts. It has never been in light and it has never been more than a hundred metres from the arch and its skin cannot bear the sun. There are only ever a few, and they are never seen by day, and they are perhaps the oldest inhabitants of the place — older than the collapse, older than the trench, tenants of the cave who have simply gone on living in the one part of it that is still a cave.

---

The grazers are the only thing here that could be called ordinary, and they are not quite.

Grey — the grey of the fog, the grey of the stone under the moss — and compact, deer-shaped but lower and thicker, with a coat that stands out from the body and beads rather than soaks. The hooves are broad and splay on the wet. They came down the rubble ramps from the plain, once, some of them, following the green, and found that a trench full of sedge and cold-country herb with no wind and few large things that eat deer was a good place, and they stayed, and now the ones here are smaller and darker than the ones above and do not go up. They cannot be driven up. A grazer pressed toward the ramp will turn and go into the pool instead. They know the fog and they use it; a group that is disturbed does not run, because running in this place means a broken leg on the rubble, but walks steadily into the whitest part of the floor and stops and stands and is not there any more.

They eat with their heads down and look up seldom, and when they do it is always the same movement — the whole group's heads coming up at once, ears forward, a long pause, and then down again together — because the thing they are listening for is the thing that makes no sound.

The heron-things are the trench's colour and the trench's silence, embodied. Tall, thin, grey-white, with a neck that folds into an S and a bill like a pair of long shears, they stand in the pool edges and the reeds and the ledges of the empty city in winter, and they stand so still that the fog beads on their feathers and the drips run down their necks and they do not shake them off. When one moves, it moves in the pauses between drips. It will take a fish, a frog, a snail-heavy fern frond and shake the snails off it, a bird that has come down to the mud and been slow, a small grazer if one is born wrong or born late. It is the largest hunter the trench has and it is not large. There is nothing here that could take a healthy adult grazer, and so the grazers are many, and so the meadow is grazed to the height of a finger and stays open, and so the herons can see across it, and so on. The thing that keeps this place in order is not any one of its people but the way they lean on each other.

The heron-things nest in the walls, in the highest tiers of the birds' city, on the wide old ledges the mud has built out, and the birds allow this — a heron in the city is a heron that is not hunting the pool edges the birds' fledglings fall onto, and a heron overhead is something the small clawed thing from the rubble will not climb past. Whether the birds know this is another question. The arrangement holds.

---

On the rubble ramps, in the gaps between the room-sized blocks, is a population that belongs to neither the wall nor the floor.

Every gap is a burrow and every burrow is somebody's. Most of them are small — a vole-like thing with fur so dense it looks damp even when dry, that eats the moss stems and the roots of the ferns and is eaten by everything with a beak; a beetle the size of a thumbnail that is black on top and, when it lifts its shell, an astonishing wet blue beneath, that hunts snails in the moss and can be found in the mornings with its head inside a shell; a frog no larger than a fingernail, dark, warty, that sings, in the very early season before the birds return, in a voice so quiet you have to put your ear to the moss to hear it, and that goes silent the day the first bird arrives. The larger gaps hold the thing with hands. It is stoat-shaped but stouter, and dark, and it comes out at dusk and climbs — it is the only mammal that goes up the walls — and it moves through the lower galleries opening cells and taking what it finds, and the birds, who are asleep and silent, do not stop it, and it takes perhaps one cell in five hundred, and the city does not notice. Its own young are born in the deep rubble in the cold months and come out in the spring with the frogs, and are hunted by the herons, and the ones that live learn the walls.

And on the rubble too, wedged where the blocks lean together, are the things that grew fastest when the light first came in — a birch-like tree with a bark that peels in wet grey rags, a tall shrub with red stems and pale undersides to its leaves, a fern of great size that arches over the gaps and drips onto everything beneath. They are not old. Nothing here is old except the moss and the cave people. The trees on the ramps are the same age as the ramps, give or take a decade, and they have grown thin and high and leaning, reaching for the strip of sky at the top of the trench, and every so often one of them tips its block and the block goes, and the tree goes with it, and the moss begins on the new face before the wood has finished rotting.

---

At the rim, above all of it, are the ones who live on the edge of the place and do not come down.

There is a fox-like animal, tawny, of the plain, that patrols the lip and sometimes puts a foot over and pulls it back. There is a hawk that hunts the plain and has learned that the trench is a hole in the world where its eyes stop working, and it drifts along the rim on the updraft and looks down into the white and does not go in. There are the plain's own grazers, big, pale, long-legged, who come to the edge to drink where the seepage rises through the turf before it goes down the walls, and who stand with their heads over the drop and look at the fog and the wall of eyes and turn away. And there are, some mornings, at the very lip, the small dark birds themselves, a handful of them, sitting on the snapped white stone in the sun above the milk of the fog — the only time they are ever in the sun — with their wings held open to dry and their large eyes half closed. They do not stay long. When the fog begins to sink they drop off the edge one after another and fall into it without a sound and are gone.

---

So who lives there.

A wall that was a cave roof, being turned into a wall again by birds who are turned into it when they die. A river that came here in the dark and now runs in the light and is being taught, dam by dam, to walk. A moss that is not one thing. Snails that carry pieces of the wall about with them. A blind beetle that has not been told about the sky. A pale collared thing in the arch that will never be told. Grey grazers who have forgotten the plain. Herons the colour of the air. Builders no one sees. A small climbing thief who takes one in five hundred and is tolerated. Trees the same age as their footing. Fish being decided.

None of them made the place and all of them keep it, and the keeping is nothing more than each of them living hard in the one narrow way it knows, against all the others, so closely that there is no room left over — and in the wet and the cold and the fog, without a sound, the whole hole in the ground is quietly and continuously repaired.
