The first answer is the obvious one, and it is wrong in an instructive way. The rattail, the sea cucumber, the brittle stars: they are the only things in the basin with faces, or the nearest thing to faces, and so they are what the eye—if there were an eye—would settle on. But they do not live here. They occupy the place the way a traveler occupies a road. The rattail was spawned somewhere with more oxygen in it and will drift, over the years, toward some other plain that is no different from this one. The cucumber's grandparents were in another basin. These animals are guests of the floor. They sit on top of it. When the water shoves, they hold on, which is what a guest does.

Go lower. The bacteria in the ooze are more nearly residents; they were here before the floor was flat and will be here when the walls are gone entirely. But they are citizens of everywhere. The same ones live in the mud of a harbor and in the gut of the rattail and in the black wood of the sunken trunk. They do not belong to the basin any more than cold belongs to it. They are a condition, not an inhabitant.

What lives here is in between: too large to be a condition, too slow to be a guest.

It is a single cell. That is the first thing to hold onto, and it is hard to hold, because the cell is the size of a hand—sometimes the size of two hands laid palm to palm—and it has no shape that a cell is supposed to have. It is a soft branching mass, a body more like a root or a frost than an animal, and it has spent its life doing the one thing that the basin itself never does. It chooses.

The plain mixes. Everything that arrives here is lifted and set down and lifted again until it has been averaged into everything else; the whole valley floor is a demonstration of what happens when nothing selects. But this cell, lying at the surface of the ooze, extends fine threads of itself into the mud and feels over the grains one by one, and it takes some and rejects others. It likes a certain size. Some of its kind like a certain kind—only the flecks of mica, only the glass needles left by dead sponges, only the round grains that were once a beach—and they will pass over ten thousand particles to find the one they want. Nobody has explained why. In a place with no light, where all grains are the same color, in a medium where every cubic centimeter is full, something has been quietly sorting the world for longer than there have been eyes anywhere.

It cements what it chooses. It builds itself a house, or a skeleton, or a shell—the words all fit badly—out of the floor: a delicate, crumbling structure of grey-brown particles glued together with a mucus that is the same temperature and nearly the same density as the water. Inside the structure the living matter runs in strands, and alongside the strands it keeps its waste, packed into dark threads and never discarded. Nothing is removed. It has taken the rule of the basin and made it a body. It eats what settles on it, the dust of dead things from the lit world, and it grows so slowly that a decade adds a rim you could not see. If there were light, and you shone it at the floor, you would not find this thing. It is made of the floor. It differs from the mud around it only in arrangement, the way a word differs from the letters spilled beside it.

There are many of them. That is the second thing. Across the plain, at the same intervals as the brittle stars but far outnumbering them, the ooze is studded with these arranged places—here a low hummock, there a soft frilled crust, there a thing like a small collapsed sponge—each one a single cell that has sat where it is for longer than the rattail has been alive and has done nothing but sort and glue and wait. The floor that is described as flat is in fact very slightly textured by them, and the texture is the only pattern the basin has that the basin did not make.

Now the storm.

It does not hold on. It cannot; it has no grip, no muscle, no knot to draw itself into. When the heavy water arrives and the floor comes up, the cell comes up with it. Its house, which was built of suspended silt, becomes suspended silt again. The threads of living matter tear and are carried, and the carefully sorted grains go back into the general brown and are un-sorted, and everything it did for thirty years is undone in an afternoon, not violently, not even noticeably, just dispersed into the substance that closes behind whatever passes and leaves no trace.

Or it does not come up. Some of them are on the lee of a bank, or in a slight hollow that is not a hollow by any reasonable standard, and the mud comes down on them instead. A hand's width of new floor. A house buried is a house in the dark under the dark. And here is the thing that makes it a resident and not a casualty: it does not die of this. It sends a strand upward through the fresh mud, feeling as it goes, tasting the grains, keeping some, and in a season there is a new frill at the new surface, and the old structure below it is now part of the sediment record, a fossil in the making with its maker still attached and still working on the story above.

So it lives by being remade without being changed, which is exactly what the place does. It is the plain's own habit, grown a body.

It has certainly touched the stone. Not this individual, perhaps, but its kind: in the long intervals when the sinker was buried, the fine threads went over it in the dark, tested the groove, found the grain too large and the surface too smooth, and moved on. It rejected the only shaped thing in the valley. It did not know there was a hand in the groove. It knows nothing; it has no organ for knowing, no eye to be useless here, no nerve. And yet the thing that a hand did to that stone—chose it, worked it, made an arrangement—is the thing this cell does all day, forever, without a hand and without a reason, and it is the only one down here doing it.

When the flow weakens and the finest clay hangs and then comes down, and the floor is flat again, the survivors will already have begun. Somewhere a thread is pressing through the new-laid dust, closing around a grain, letting another one go. The plain will be full of them again in a decade, though not the same ones and not in the same places, and the mud will be very slightly sorted, in patches, by something with a preference. That is what lives here. Not a creature so much as a preference, dressed in the floor it prefers, waiting for the next thing to fall.
