The people are the first answer, and they are not the whole answer, but start with them.

They are quiet the way other people are tall. It is not something they do; it is something they are, from the outside, obviously, before anything else. Come down the switchbacks with a party of them and you will notice that the talk thins as the rim road drops and by the time the first cables pass overhead everyone has stopped, not from any signal, simply because the air has changed and the voice knows it. Their voices, when they use them, sit low in the chest and come out in short warm pieces, as if each sentence were something being handed to you rather than said. They have no public voice at all. There is nothing in the basin you could call a shout. Children born here try it — every child tries it — and hear their own voice go two meters and die in the moss, and there is a look that crosses their faces that you see nowhere else, a small private betrayal, and after that they do not try again.

So they lean in. That is the second thing. Everyone in the basin, to be heard, must be within arm's length, and this has made them a people who touch. Not warmly, necessarily — they are not an effusive people — but constantly and without remark: a hand on the shoulder to turn you toward a speaker, two fingers on the wrist to say *wait*, a palm flat on the back to say *go on ahead*. In a crowd at the market you will feel yourself steered by hands you never see. It does not feel like intrusion. It feels like being part of a rigging. The old ones say that a person who will not be touched cannot live here, and that the rim towns are full of such people, and that they are welcome to them.

The hands themselves are cable hands, most of them. Even the assay clerks, even the children. The braid is wet steel furred with moss and it is under load all day and all night, and anyone who works it has palms that have split and healed and split and healed until they are a flat grey pad, calloused and faintly translucent at the edges, and it is not lost on anyone that this is the exact texture of the new ground. They joke about it. A winch hand will hold up a palm and say *coming up in the night* and someone will tap it with a fingernail to hear if it has clouded yet.

They are cold. Always. It is not a hardship they have overcome; it is a condition they have entered into. Everything they wear is wool and everything they wear is damp and has been damp for years, and their skin has the pallor of things kept in cellars, with a faint greenish undertone from the light and the moss that outsiders find alarming and that they do not see. They move slowly not from lassitude but because heat is expensive and motion makes it and there is no point wasting it. A winch hand hauling a tenement a handspan into the air does it in one long unhurried pull and stands still afterward for a full minute with the heat of it in her shoulders, eyes half-shut, and you would be wrong to think she was resting. She is listening to the cable through her hands. She is feeling whether the building has come true.

*True* is their word for level, and it is their word for a great deal else. A cable is true when the tension is right along its length. A building is true when the pendulum in the tilt gauge hangs dead centre in its bell. A person is true when they can be relied on to feel a lean before the gauge does, and this last is not a figure of speech. They have all of them, from infancy, been rocked in hanging rooms on a heaving floor, and their inner ears have learned things that other people's do not know. They walk on a rising blister the way the browsing animals do, without looking down. They can stand in a room and tell you which corner is low by a hair's breadth. Outsiders in the basin are sick for a week, the way people are sick at sea, and the basin people watch this with polite interest and a certain quiet pride, and when the sickness passes they say the newcomer has *found their hang*.

Their hang is what they are hung from. It is the nearest thing they have to an address. Nobody in the basin lives at a place; they live in a building, and the building lives on a set of cables, and the cables live on a stretch of lattice, and where all this happens to be at any moment over the ground is a matter of record but not of identity. Ask where someone lives and you will be told the name of a building — the buildings all have names, old ones, from before the settlers knew what they had gotten into: Harrowgate, the Long Store, Tessary's, the Dun Hall — and if you ask where the building is you will get a gesture that means *west of the market, roughly, this week.* The tenements migrate. A whole hang can be unshackled and trolleyed sixty meters in a morning. People come home from the foundry and find their street has moved and they follow the lamps.

The lamps are how they find each other. Sound is useless past two meters and the light under the lattice, though good, is flat and grey and swallows distance, so the city is marked in colour. Every hang has a lamp colour, a pane of stained glass in a brass cage, lit from dusk, and the children learn the colours before they learn the names: the Long Store is a deep bottle-green, Tessary's is a hard amber, the foundry is a red that is nearly brown and that nobody else may use. The winch crews signal with lamps — a lifted blue for *hold*, two whites crossed for *clear*, a swinging yellow that means *look at your gauge*. At night, from the rim, the basin is a field of coloured points hung in the dark at seven different heights, some of them slowly, slowly rising.

They have no politics in any sense a rim town would recognize. There is nothing to stand on to give a speech and no way to be heard if you did. There is an assay office and a winch guild and a foundry board and a market committee, and they meet, and they lean in and murmur at one another around a table, and something comes of it, but nothing is decided in the basin the way things are decided elsewhere. Things are decided the way the southern district decided that it was fine: by everyone, separately, in the same direction, and then it is simply the case. Watch the market move. Nobody orders it. A blister splits and the first stall unhooks and wheels west and the second sees the first go and follows and within the hour the whole market has flowed sixty meters like water finding a level, and afterward if you ask who decided they will look at you as if you had asked who decided the rain. They call this *coming true* as well. A decision is true when it is level across everyone. A decision that has to be argued for is already, in some sense, wrong.

This makes them hard to lead and hard to rob and almost impossible to tax, and the rim towns have given up trying.

They are, all of them, in some degree, people of the winch, but there are castes within that.

The winch hands proper are the aristocracy, if the word means anything here. They are the ones in the girders in all weathers, cutting the moss back with heated knives, reading the braid with their palms, calling the lifts with lamps. They are the ones who feel a rise coming. Everyone in the basin can feel the ground move; a winch hand can feel it *decide*. They say the moss tells them — that a day before a blister comes up the moss over it tightens, draws in, goes a shade darker and colder, like skin before a bruise shows. They will stand on a stretch of perfectly flat street and put a hand down on the moss and say *this one, tomorrow, about a meter,* and they will be right. The assay office has tested this and found no measurable change in the moss and the winch hands have received this finding with the courtesy they extend to everything the assay office says.

The assay office is its own caste: clerks, mostly, in a hanging hall lined with drawers, who take samples of the new ground twice a day and grind them and dissolve them and burn them and write down what they find, and what they find keeps changing. They have a wall of labels. They have retired eleven names for the ground in four years and are said to be nearing a twelfth. There is a clerk there who has stopped using names altogether and simply numbers each sample by the day and the hour it was taken and files it with a note of what it did in the flame, and this is looked on within the office as a kind of surrender and outside it as the first honest thing the office has done. The clerks are not liked, exactly, but they are needed the way a chaplain is needed on a ship: not because anyone believes them but because someone should be trying.

The foundry crews are something else. They are the best paid people in the basin and the quietest in a city of quiet people and they keep to their own hang, which is that reddish-brown lamp and the great suspended cathedral under it, and they do not like to be watched and are not. They work the new stone before it clouds: press it, cast it, draw it into rods and bearings and the little clear bells that ring in every tilt gauge in the city. Whatever they do in there involves the furnace, and the furnace is the one hot thing in the basin, and the ground rises around the foundry more than anywhere else — or it is believed to, which in the basin is the same — and so the foundry crews live in a permanent condition of being about to be blamed. They have the look of it. They walk through the market as a group and the crowd steers itself around them with those unseen hands and nobody says anything and nothing needs saying. They are paid what they are paid because someone has to stand next to the heat.

The cutters are the ones with the pneumatic chisels, cutting back the ridges when they rise through streets, and the sellers are the ones who crate the pieces and send them up the switchbacks, and between them they carry a shame they do not discuss. The new stone, before it clouds, is beautiful — grey with that liverish undertone, smooth, faintly translucent, taking light like the inside of a shell — and the rim towns will pay for it, and the basin needs what the rim towns pay. But there is stone in the basin that is not cut, and everyone knows which. It is darker. It runs in veins through the clouded ground of the southern district and it comes up here and there elsewhere, and when a cutter's chisel finds a dark vein he stops and moves the gang somewhere else and nothing is written down and nothing is said.

This is the dead.

There is no earth here to bury in and the ground would not stay still for a grave if there were. So the basin's dead are laid on the rise. When someone dies the winch hands watch the moss for a coming blister, and the body is carried there — wrapped in wool, in the same damp wool it lived in — and laid on the tightening moss in the night, and the family stands in a ring at arm's length with lamps, and in the morning the ground has come up under it and around it and the moss has begun already to grow over. Within a month it is stone. Clouded, hard, faintly darker than the ground around it, and part of the basin floor, and the city is winched a handspan higher over it and life goes on. This is what the southern district is. Not a disaster — or not only a disaster. It is the oldest part of the settlement and the first generation is under it, and the people who live in the upper floors of those swallowed tenements and come and go by the tram-rails are, most of them, the children and grandchildren of what the stone holds. They decided it was fine because it is where their people are. There is a woman in the Dun Hall who keeps a brass key on a cord round her neck. It opens a door on the second floor that is a meter deep in stone. She has never said what is behind it. She does not need to.

The children are born into this and find nothing strange in it. They are born in hanging rooms and their first sensation is the long slow swing of a winch lift, and they learn the lamp colours before they learn faces, and they learn hand-talk before they speak, and when they speak they speak low. They play a game the adults pretend not to know about that involves lying flat on the moss in the dark with both palms and one cheek pressed down, in silence, for as long as it takes, and the first one to feel the ground move under them and say *there* wins. They are never wrong about where. They are frequently wrong about when, and this is the game.

They are forbidden the sumps. Every child in the basin is forbidden the sumps and every child in the basin has been to one, has lain on the horsetail fringe with the cold coming up through their wool and looked down into the black still water and waited for a pale shape to turn over slowly under the surface, and most have seen nothing and some have seen something and none of them say. The old winch hands say the ground dreams and that the sumps are where it does it. The assay office has dredged two sumps to the stone and found nothing but water and cold. The children have decided, collectively and without discussion, that both of these things are true.

*

That is the people. And you could stop there and say that is who lives in the basin, and you would not be lying, but you would have missed what they themselves know and do not say.

Look again at the city. Look at the beards hanging from the undersides of the buildings, the fur on every girder, the lining inside the foundry like the inside of a coat. Look at the cutters' hands, the winch hands' hands, and then look at the moss, the way it has grown into the braid of the cables so deep that it has to be cut back with fire.

The moss lives here. The moss is the thing that lives here. Everything else is a consequence of it.

It was on the first blister before the first pylon was driven, and it will be on the last cable after the last winch hand has gone up the switchbacks for good. It is not one species; the assay office has found forty and stopped counting. It is grey-green in shade and a bright wet emerald where the light comes through and a colour without a name in the hollows under the buildings where the drip collects. It is a hand deep on the ground and a finger deep on the steel and a hair deep on the glass of every lamp, so that all the colours in the basin are seen through a green scrim. It drinks the condensation off the cables and it draws minerals up out of the new stone that the assay office cannot extract with acid, and it grows a handspan a fortnight, and it is silent, and it makes everything else silent.

The people live in it the way fish live in water and they think about it about as much. But they are shaped by it entirely. Their quiet is its quiet. Their leaning-in and their hands-on-shoulders and their coloured lamps and their decisions that come true without a word — all that is the moss, making them into the kind of people who can live inside it. It has upholstered them. It has taken a settlement of rim-town people with rim-town voices and in one long generation it has made them into something else, something soft-spoken and near-standing and patient and cold, and they know this and are not resentful. A winch hand cutting the braid free will thank the moss under her breath as she burns it. Not sentimentally. It is a courtesy, like thanking the ground when you lay someone on it. You do not live inside a thing that size and not observe the forms.

And it is eating the city. Slowly, in the way of everything here. It rots the cable. It pries the seams. It weighs on the girders, saturated, a hand deep, until the winch crews have to re-tension not for the ground's sake but for the moss's. Nobody says this either. But the city is in a permanent state of relocation not only because the ground rises. It is also because the moss holds still, and grows, and what holds still and grows in the basin will in the end be stone.

*

And then, under the moss, there is the third answer, and here you have to be careful, because this is where the people and the assay office part ways and where the people, if you press them, will stop leaning in.

The ground.

It rises a few centimeters a week in the quiet zones and a meter in a night where it decides to. That is the word everyone uses, on both sides of the argument. *Decides.* The assay clerks use it and hear themselves and wince and use it again. It comes up in domes and blisters and long welted ridges and it takes a month to cloud and become, more or less, stone, and it is grey with a faint liverish undertone, and where it is fresh it is smooth and slightly translucent, like the skin over a healing wound, and nobody in four years of trying has been able to say what kind of stone it is or where the minerals in it come from that the moss loves, or why the moss should love them, or why, if you bring the one hot thing in the basin close to it, it should come up faster.

The old winch hands say it dreams. They do not say it thinks. Nobody here would say the ground thinks. That would be a rim-town idea, a foundation idea, the idea of a person who has never lain on the moss and felt a rise decide under their cheek. What they say is that it dreams, which is a different thing: that it moves the way a sleeper moves, without intention, toward and away from things, and that the sumps are where the dreaming pools, and that the pale slow shapes in the water are not fish and not minerals but the ground's own turning-over, seen from above, the way you see a sleeper shift under a blanket. They say the dead go into it and it dreams them. They say that is why the southern district has that dark vein. They say the foundry crews know something about the furnace they will not tell. They say the ground is not finished, and that everyone knows this, that it is the first thing anyone learns coming down the switchbacks, and that the question no one asks is *finished doing what.*

The assay office says the ground is a mineral phenomenon of unusual vigor and a composition that resists standard categorization, and it says this in a low voice, leaning in, with its hand on your arm.

*

So that is what lives here, and it is three things, and it is one thing.

A soft-spoken people who touch, hung in a lattice, moving all day without hurry, with grey palms and green light on their faces and a brass pendulum they glance at like weather, who lay their dead on the rise and lean in to be heard. A moss that made them, and covers them, and is unmaking the steel they hang from, silently, a fortnight at a time. And under both, something that is not finished, that comes up in the night with a soft crack like knuckles, and that they stand on and take their water from and burn their hands on and sell in padded crates up the switchbacks and lay their parents in, and whose name the clerks have given up on, and which — in the late afternoon, when the overcast thins and the low light slides in from the west under the lattice and every cable is a line of gold and the new ground glows through its skin like a lamp — the whole packed hanging city stops, for twenty minutes, and holds still, and looks at.

They do not say what they are looking at. There is no way to say it that would carry more than two meters. But you can see it in how they stand — winch hands with their palms turned out, children flat on the moss, the woman from the Dun Hall with her hand on the key — that they are not looking at the light. They are looking at the thing the light is coming through. And it is the look of people who live on something, and know what that means, and have decided, collectively and without discussion, that it is fine.

Then the light goes. Then the ground comes up somewhere. Then a bell taps against a cup, and they lean in, and go back to moving.
