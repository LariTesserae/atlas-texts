Start where the place starts, at the ceiling, because that is where the food comes from and where the sound comes from and where, if you ask what lives here, the honest first answer hangs.

The fliers are not one people. They are a dozen lineages that have nothing in common except the habit of coming back, and the habit has been long enough to shape all of them toward the same few ends. They are small-eyed or eyeless. They are packed. They are loud. Beyond that they diverge, and the divergence is the first thing you would notice if you could see them, which you mostly cannot.

The most numerous are the ones with faces made for sound. Their skulls are mostly ear: broad shallow dishes of skin and cartilage that open forward, ridged like the inside of a shell, that tilt and cup and flatten as the animal listens, so that a wall of them at rest is a wall of slowly moving cups. Their mouths are small and set low, almost an afterthought. The nose-leaf, if that is what it is, stands up between the dishes as a stiff fin that can be shaped, and from it they emit the calls that make up the upper third of the roar. They are not bats. They are furred, and warm, and they bear live young that cling, but the wing is not a hand; it is a fold that runs from a long single finger back along the flank to a spur at the ankle, and folded it wraps the animal entirely, so that at roost they look like ranked grey seeds. There are nurseries. Whole ceilings of one sink are given to mothers and infants, the infants clustered in mats a hundred deep, and the sound of that sink is a different sound, a shrill continuous begging, and the floor beneath it is a different floor, because the infants fall. They fall by the thousand every day and they are alive when they land and they are not alive for long, and everything in the drift beneath knows the nursery is above it.

Then the ones that never land except here. They are long-bodied and stiff-winged and their feet have gone to hooks, useless on flat ground, and they cannot rise from a horizontal surface at all; they must drop from something to fly. So they hang. They hang in the highest, driest galleries in strings and festoons, each gripping the one above, chains of thirty or forty bodies swinging very slightly in the moving air, and when they leave they leave from the bottom of the chain upward, each one releasing and falling into flight, so that the chain unzips from the floor toward the roof. They are silent as fliers go; their contribution is a thin dry ticking. They spend the bright hours in this dark and the dark hours in the bright, and it is not clear they have ever seen anything.

And the six-limbed ones, the membrane-winged, which have no counterpart. Two wings, four legs, a body like a soft lantern. They are the ones that pack so tightly the wall appears to breathe, and the appearance is not entirely appearance: they breathe together. Something about their crowding synchronizes them, and a crevice of ten thousand rises and falls as one slow surface. They are cold to the touch, or nearly. They are the only fliers here that seem to have arrived from somewhere the others did not, and they roost apart, and the other lineages do not roost near them.

On all of the fliers, other things live. This is the second carpet, the one you do not see: each body a country of mites and lice and small soft leeches, each of which has its own lineage as old as its host's, each of which falls with the dead and the dung to the floor and enters the drift and is eaten and enters the drift again. When the ceilings empty at evening, a fine rain of these comes down in the wake of the leaving, and the coral-things open for it.

---

The floor is where the lineages get strange, because the floor has never had to fly anywhere, and has never had to be anything but here.

The pale segmented things the length of an arm are the great movers of the drift. They have no eyes and no head worth the name; the front end is a ring of soft sensory lobes that opens and closes as they push. They breathe through their skin and their skin is nearly transparent, so that in the glow from the walls you can see the dark line of the gut and, moving in it, what they have eaten. They swim through the leavings and leave tunnels behind them and the tunnels drain water downward and aerate the mass, and without them the drift would sour and slump and the whole floor would be a different floor. They are grazed by nothing. Their flesh is bitter, or poisonous, or simply not worth the effort when the drift is all around; whichever it is, they are the one abundant thing here that nothing eats, and their dead lie in the drift until the fungi have them.

The beetles as broad as plates are old, individually old. They grow slowly and they do not stop, and the largest of them, in the deepest sinks, have shells so pitted with the years that they look like the stone. They are blind and armored and slow and nearly indifferent; you can lift one, and it will draw in its legs and wait, and then when it is set down it will raise its shell and sing. The song is rasped from ridges along the shell's inner edge, and each individual has a song of its own, and the songs seem to be for finding one another in the roar, since two beetles that meet will stand shell to shell and sing at one another for a long time before anything else happens.

The wall-walkers, the crab-descended things, are the ones you would call hunters if you had to call any of them anything. Eight legs, or ten; long, jointed, angled backward, tipped with hooks fine enough to hold a wet ceiling. The body is a flat disk not much bigger than a hand, and from it, forward, extend two arms as long as the legs, ending not in claws but in soft pads fringed with hair. They move up the walls among the roosting fliers and the fliers do not react to them, and they lay a pad against a body and hold it there, feeling, and if the body is weak or sick or very young they draw it out and open it. They have the blue-green smear along the belly, and it is thought that they use it the way a lantern is used, because a flier taken by the wall-walkers is often found with its face toward the light.

The long soft predators that lie along the ledges with a hundred feet are barely predators and barely anything else. They are the closest the place has to something that simply waits. Skin like wet paper, a body that is all trunk, feet in two rows so short they hardly lift it. It has no eyes, no ears anyone has found, no mouth that is obvious until it is used. It tastes warmth through the whole length of its skin. It lies in the mist along a ledge for days and then something warm passes close and it folds over and there is a mouth and then there is a slow swallowing that goes on for hours. They are what eventually takes the sick fliers that fall but do not die, and the beetles that stray too far up, and, once, something larger.

---

The pools hold the amphibians, and the amphibians hold the middle of the roar, and nowhere else in the basin is a single kind of animal so purely a sound.

They are the color of candle wax and about the length of a finger, and they have eyes, small and black and lidless, useless in the dark but not lost, and they lie in the shallow pools in thousands and tens of thousands with only the crown of the head above water. They call. Each individual has a pulse, a soft wet knocking, and the pulses are not synchronized in any way you could name and yet they make waves, slow swells of sound that travel across a pool and up over the lip and into the next pool and the next, so that the whole basin's middle band rises and falls in a rhythm that has no center. When the fliers leave, the pools boil. They mate in mass in the dark and the eggs are laid in strings along the walls of the pools, pale and jellied and glowing very faintly, and the strings drift and tangle and the wall-walkers come down for them and the beetles come for them and most of the strings are eaten and enough are not. The tadpoles, if that is the word, are the color of nothing at all, and live under the surface in a layer that moves as one animal, and the water of the pools is thick with them for a season, and then they climb out and knock.

They are, of all the inhabitants, the ones most easily killed and the ones there are most of, and the two facts are the same fact.

---

The coral-things deserve to be spoken of as residents, though they do not move.

They are not corals. Each polyp is a small stiff cup of tissue on a stalk of something like horn, and the cups stand shoulder to shoulder on shared skeleton, and the skeleton grows outward and upward in the shapes the air allows: shelves where the air is still, fans where it moves steadily, slow towers where it rises. They open when the drift is falling and close when it is not. Each cup, when it closes, clicks, and the click of a whole shelf closing is a run of small stones, and the shelves close in sequence as a disturbance moves along them, so that a hand drawn across a colony sends a sound ahead of itself into the dark. They are cold. They grow perhaps a finger's width in a lifetime. The largest colonies, in the shallow eastern sinks where the cloud is thinnest, are the size of houses, and the stone under them has not been touched by air since the roofs fell, and if anything here holds a memory of the place before it became this, it is under the coral-things.

Whether they are the same lineage as the sea corals of whatever coast this country once had, no one has stood here long enough to ask. They filter the air. They eat the fine rain. They are the nearest thing to a forest, and the basin's floor is arranged around them the way a floor is arranged around trees.

---

So far these are the ones you would meet. The one you would not meet is the one that lives lowest.

It is easiest to say first what it is not. It is not many. The hole in the noise, when it moves, is one hole, and it moves at one speed, and in a day of listening you would hear it pass perhaps once, always in the deep galleries, always along the water. It is not fast. It is not, so far as anyone has stood in the right place to tell, ever seen. It is not a maker of any sound of its own, and that is the strangest thing about it in a country where everything announces itself: it announces nothing. The animals near it go quiet, the way animals do, and the quiet moves with it, and that moving quiet is the only shape it has.

You could stop there and let it be a predator, and perhaps it is one; something must eat the drowned when the rivers rise, and something must eat the things that eat the drowned. But the longer you stay with the shape of the quiet, the less it seems to be about eating. It is too slow. It passes too seldom. It does not seem to hunt the emptying floor at evening when hunting would be easiest. It moves, rather, in a pattern, along the flooded galleries and up through the lower sinks and down again, a circuit that takes days, and the circuit does not repeat exactly but drifts, the way the cloud drifts, and it goes always toward the places where the stone is thinning.

It is there when the roofs fall. That is the one fact about it that is fairly sure. The deep single note comes, and the almost-silence, and in the almost-silence, if you are near enough, the hole in the noise is already there beneath the new sink, has been there for days, has been waiting. Whether it is drawn by the sound the stone makes before it gives, a creak too low and too slow for anything but the oldest ears; whether it eats what falls, the million fliers that lose their perches, the rain of bodies from the new-broken roof; whether it has simply learned, over a life that may be as long as the falling, where the falling will be next: the place has no interest in the question. It is the one inhabitant that knows the whole basin. Everything else here maps the nearest few yards of it by shouting into the dark. This maps all of it by listening, and has listened for so long that it no longer needs to move to hear, and moves anyway, slowly, toward the next place that is about to change.

It is not the master of anything. There is no master. But if you asked what lives here and would take only one answer, and if you had stood at the bottom for long enough to stop hearing the roar as a roar, this is the one you would give: the silence that walks under the noise, and has always been here, and is going where the roof is next.

---

And the who. You will want to know whether there is a who.

There is not, in the way you mean. Nothing here has built anything or named anything or is waiting for anything to be named. The nearest the basin comes to a who is the sound itself, which is not a creature but is not nothing: a chorus of a hundred lineages, none of which can hear the whole, each of which is heard by all the others, adding up second by second to a single act of the country attending to itself. The fliers are its eyes and the pools are its voice and the drift is its stomach and the coral-things are its slow long memory and the quiet in the lowest gallery is whatever in it does not speak. It has been this since before the roofs began to fall. It will be this when the last roof is down and the basin is one open sink under one lid of cloud and the fliers have nowhere to hang. It is not a mind. But you stood in it with your hand on the wall and the wall moved, and you were heard by everything and understood by nothing, and you were, for as long as you stayed, part of what it was hearing.

That is who lives there. Everything, and no one, and the loud attention that the two of them make together.
