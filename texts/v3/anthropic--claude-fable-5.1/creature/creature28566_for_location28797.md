You look first for the person. That is the shape the place makes you expect: a well left uncapped, a transformer left humming, a door with no lock. Someone must have stayed. Someone must come up.

The block house says otherwise. Step inside and there is no room in it for a body — the transformer fills it wall to wall, with a gap at the back you could fit an arm into and nothing more. The floor is bare concrete beaded with condensation, unmarked. No tin, no stove, no bench. On the wall by the door, scratched into the concrete when it was still wet enough to take a nail, is a date, and under it three sets of initials, and under those a fourth set in a different hand, years later by the look of it, with a line through it. Whatever that means, it does not mean anyone is here. The last human thing to happen in this room was that someone stood where you stand and crossed themselves out.

So: no one. Not in the way you meant. What lives here is not the kind of thing that leaves initials.

---

Start with the smallest. The flies over the warm pools are the size of a grain of rice, dark, soft-bodied, with wings that do not glint. There are thousands. Each pool has its own cloud, and the clouds do not mix — you can stand between two pools three meters apart and watch two populations hover over their own water and never cross the moss between. The water is where they are born; the water is where they mate, in the layer of warm air that sits on it; the water is where they die, and their bodies feed the film of algae at the pool's edge, which feeds the larvae, which become the flies. Their world is a circle a few strides across and a hand's height deep. A generation takes a few weeks. In the span of one of their lives the mountain rises by an amount too small to name, and the pool they were born over is, by the time they die over it, very slightly higher than it was. They are the only living things here that will never notice this, and there are more of them than everything else in the bowl combined.

Then the moss under the broken flange. It is worth saying that this is an inhabitant and not just a growth, because it lives on something specific: not the mountain's heat, but the well's. It takes the steam that the survey team drilled for and never used, and it has built itself into a cone — waist-high now, brilliant, the green of a thing that never dries — that is the only monument in the bowl. The pipe was meant to carry that heat down the slope and light something far below. Instead it has lit this. A liverwort, flat and dark and lobed, holds the lower slopes of the cone; above it the true moss, fine as fur; at the very top, where the plume hits, nothing, because nothing can live in the plume itself, and so the cone has a bald hot crown. Break off a piece and it grows back in a season. It is the fastest thing here, and it exists because of an accident, and if the well ever seals itself — as such wells do, slowly, with mineral — the cone will yellow and collapse and be indistinguishable from the rest of the floor within a few years. It is the one resident that depends entirely on the mistake.

---

But you know already what lives here. You heard them before you saw them, and once you had seen them you could not stop.

There are, as near as you can count, eleven. It might be twelve; one pair may be one bird seen twice. They are the size of a wren, perhaps a little larger, brown above with a darker streaking, paler below, a short straight bill, dark eyes, legs slightly longer than seem proportionate. Nothing about them would make you look twice if you saw one in a hedge. Here they are the only warm-blooded things in a room three hundred paces across, and you look at them the way you would look at the only other person in an empty city.

Their voice is the thing. In a place with no silence, they have found the one register the mountain does not use. The vents roar at a pitch and the drip patters above it and between them there is a narrow band, low and thin, that nothing else occupies, and that is where the birds sing — a descending note, three or four steps, ending in something that is nearly a click. It carries. You hear it from across the bowl, under the steam, clear as a struck glass, and you realize that it could not have been made anywhere else. A bird that sang like this in a quiet forest would sound wrong, too low, muffled. It is a song for shouting under, and it was shaped by generations of being heard or not heard against a roar.

How long? Long enough to fit the mountain — and the mountain is young. Some ancestor came up. Perhaps the bowl was lower once, and warmer air pooled in it long before the rim rose above the cloud, and a few brown birds found a garden and did not leave, and the garden lifted under them a few millimeters a season, a meter a lifetime, until the world they had known was far below and white. They did not fly down because they never knew they were going up. Or perhaps they were blown in, a storm-scattered pair or three, and found flies and warmth and a roof, and stayed because the way out was a notch onto nothing. Either way, they have been here long enough to have become slightly their own. Their legs are long for a bird that works fronds, but they do not perch so much as brace, feet spread on wet green, and the length helps. Their feathers shed water in a way a lowland cousin's would not. And there is the song.

They eat the flies — that is the whole economy. Each bird has a pool or two it works from, and a set of tree ferns it patrols, running along the underside of the fronds and taking the insects that come up from the water to rest there. They drink from the bromeliad cups. They bathe, as far as you can tell, in the rain that is always falling, by simply not sheltering from it. In the whole bowl there is one thing they fear, and it is not you. You can stand under a frond and a bird will work its way down the rib toward your face and stop a hand's breadth away and look at you and go back to what it was doing. They have never had a reason to learn otherwise. Nothing walks here.

The thing they fear comes to the window. You saw it once — the dark span in the notch, riding the column of warm air where the mountain breathes out into the cold. It does not live here. It lives on the edge, on the boundary itself, where the thermal lifts and the cloud thins, and it comes because the boundary is where things can be seen. When it appears, the birds go silent in a way you notice only after it has gone — the roar seems to get louder because the thread under it has been cut. Then one bird gives a different call, sharper, and the others take it up, and they drop into the moss and are gone, and the bowl is green and loud and empty of anything moving. Eleven birds is not many. The dark wing has taken some. It has been taking some for as long as there have been birds to take, and the birds have been enough for it to keep coming, and few enough that it does not stay. That balance is the closest thing this place has to a law.

They nest behind the roof of the transformer house. You knew this already; you saw the polished metal. Consider what it means. The building has been here some tens of years. The gap between the corrugated sheet and the block wall is warm because the transformer is warm — or because everything is — and it is dry, which nothing else in the bowl is, and it is high enough that the moss has not yet reached it. Some bird found it the first season it stood and the birds have used it every season since, and a patch of steel the size of a thumbnail has been worn from grey to mirror by the landing of feet. Do the arithmetic: a few dozen birds a year, a few landings a day each, the breeding months only. The polish is the sum of that. It is the only place in the bowl where a living thing has left a mark on the machinery rather than the other way round, and it is the brightest thing here.

Before the roof they must have nested somewhere else — a crack in the rim, a hollow in a tree fern's trunk — and when the roof came they moved, and now there are eleven birds whose whole idea of home is a sheet of metal bolted to a box that hums. If the transformer ever stops, the gap will cool and the damp will get in, and they will have to remember what they did before. Some of them will. The nest, when you find a way to see it, is moss, of course, and fern fiber, and one strand of rotted pipe insulation, black and fibrous, woven in like anything else.

---

There is one more resident, and it is the one you were standing on.

It is easy, in a place like this, to speak of the mountain as if it were a stage. It is not. Everything else here — the flies in their weeks, the moss in its seasons, the birds in their generations, the survey team in its single cancelled year — is briefer than the thing that holds them, and the thing that holds them is not still. It moves. It has been moving since before there was a bowl. The warmth that makes the garden is not weather; it is the mountain's own body heat, coming up through its skin, and the fissures along the western wall are not features of the landscape but the places where that body is breathing. The rain is its breath condensing. The pools are where it sweats. The rim is its edge, unsoftened, cutting because it has not yet had time to be anything else.

And it is growing under everything that lives on it, at a speed the moss can almost match and nothing else can. The birds' roof tilts a fraction more each year and they land on it anyway. The moss on the transformer climbs and the transformer lifts and neither of them knows the other is moving. The survey marker says an elevation that was true the day it was stamped and will never be true again, and the mountain does not care what is stamped on it. If there is a way to say what lives here that is honest, it is this: a very young, very large thing that is warm and slowly getting taller, and on its back, in a hollow in its crown, a garden it did not mean to make, with eleven birds in it who sing in the one gap in its voice.

At night the birds go quiet and the mountain does not. You lie in the moss and the warmth comes up through your back and the roar goes on and on and the transformer holds its one note under it, and somewhere behind the roof of the block house eleven small hot bodies are pressed together on a bed of moss and insulation, dry, in the only dry place for a very long way in any direction. Above them the fronds. Above the fronds the violet. Above that, stars over a jungle. And under all of it, rising, the thing that lives here, that has always lived here, that will still be here — a little taller — when the roof rusts through and the well seals and the birds have found some other gap in the rim and forgotten they ever needed metal.
