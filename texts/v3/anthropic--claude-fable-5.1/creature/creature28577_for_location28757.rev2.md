Start with the choughs, because they will start with you.

They meet you at the throat before your boots are on the ladder — six or eight of them riding the updraft where the bubble's warmth spills out into the killing air, hanging there on the thin wind with their wingtips splayed like fingers, watching. They have red legs and yellow bills and an expression of frank appraisal. By the time you are three rungs down, one of them has landed on the rebar above your head and is considering your jacket zipper. By the tenth rung it has tried for the zipper twice. These are birds that live at the ceiling of the world by being interested in everything, and the pit is the most interesting thing for a hundred kilometers, and they behave as though they were consulted on its construction.

Most of them were hatched here. That is the thing to know about the choughs. The colony that first dropped into the bubble may have come up from some real cliff, some honest limestone face with weather on it, but their descendants have never nested on anything but angle iron. They build in the gantry joints, in the elbows of the ducting, in the crook where a cable bundle passes over a brace, and they line the nests with stolen things. When the crew pull a gantry — and everything in the pit is pulled eventually, because everything in the pit is eventually bent — they find the hoards: washers, cotter pins, a lens from a headlamp, a spoon, the foil off a hundred packages, and once a ring that a man had lost two seasons before and given up for gone into the dark below the lowest catwalk. The choughs do not need these things. They want them. They are the only creatures in the pit with the surplus for wanting.

They have learned the machines. Not the way a dog learns a door, but with some precision. An amber indicator on a jack means it is coming up to bleed, and a bleed means hot fluid venting, and hot fluid means the small flies that breed in the grease sumps rising in a sudden cloud, and so before the crewman has even reached the valve there are a dozen black birds lined along the handrail above him like a jury. They have learned that a welding arc means don't look. They have learned the difference between the tremor that is nothing and the tremor that is a plate letting go, and they are in the air a half second before the crew look up. If you want to know whether to worry, watch the choughs.

---

The crew you see next, and you see them the way the description promised — in glimpses, in pieces, a headlamp moving along the third tier, two figures and a chain hoist on the fifth. It takes a while to see them properly because they don't hold still for it and because they are, all of them, the color of the pit. Not painted; ingrained. The dust that has never been wet finds the only water in the mountain, which is a human body, and it settles in sweat and dries there and is sweated into again, and after a month a crewman's neck, the backs of his hands, the creases at the corners of his eyes are a dark gray-brown that no rag will lift. They do not wash. Water is drunk; it is not spent. They wipe with cloth and the cloth goes black and the skin under it stays the color of the walls. The first rain this rock has ever known falls inside somebody's collar.

It gets into the lips. Everyone's lips are cracked — the air does that within a day — and the cracks fill with the fine glassy grit and heal over it and crack again, so that the crew carry the mountain in their mouths, a little, and taste it constantly, a flat mineral taste like a struck match. They stop noticing. Newcomers notice, and spit, and are told not to waste it.

They are, most of them, from high country to begin with, born above three thousand meters with the broad chests and the slow deep breath that that gives you; the ones who weren't are quieter for a different reason, which is that they can't spare the air. Nobody talks much either way. What they do instead is tap. Every prop in the pit, every screw jack and cross-brace, has a note. Steel under the correct load rings one way when you rap it with a wrench; steel under too much load rings duller and shorter; steel that is about to fail makes a sound that is not a ring at all but a sort of complaint, a *tunk*, and once you have heard that sound you do not forget it. So the crew walk the catwalks tapping as they go, the way a farmer walks a fence line, and the pit is full all day of these small flat notes, *tik, tik, tik*, a kind of inventory. They can tell you which brace is which from two levels away by its pitch. They have names for the big ones — the seven jacks that hold the waist of the bubble, where the compression is worst, have names the way boats have names, and are spoken of with the same wary affection. When one of them has to be pulled there is a feeling in the pit like a death in a small town, brief and practical.

They sleep on the third tier, in a gallery bolted into the lee of the largest fumarole, where the wall breathes out at something like the temperature of a cellar in October and the steel of the bunks is warm to the touch. They sleep in shifts. The mountain does not sleep and so somebody is always awake and walking, and there is never a moment when the tapping stops entirely. The food comes up the ladder in sealed drums along with the water and it is dried — meat, mostly, and something like biscuit — and the bones and rinds and cut-offs go down to the lowest gantry, for a reason that will come.

What they are for, they can tell you: the walls move, and the props must hold. Why the walls must be held is not a thing they discuss. The oldest of them arrived to find the first jacks already set and already groaning, and the ones who set those jacks are not here to ask. There is a board on the second tier where the colored lamps are switched — a grammar of green and amber and a certain rare blue — and the one who sits there is the closest thing the pit has to authority, and even she will tell you only that the racks must be tended and the ducts must be kept clear. The racks she'll show you: fist-sized amber crystals grown in the rock's dry breath, cut free with a hot wire, packed in sawdust, carried down the ladder in someone's rucksack every few weeks and not spoken of again. The ducts she will not. Some of the crew go down them. They come back smelling of something that is not sulfur and they do not say, and the pit does not ask, and the props are re-tensioned, and that is what the crew are: forty people who have made an agreement with a mountain and are keeping their end.

---

Below the crew, on the walls themselves, the spiders.

You have to bring your lamp close. They are the size of a fingernail clipping, hunched, matte black on black glass, and the first sign of them is not the body but the eyes — two large forward-facing lenses that catch the sodium light and throw it back as tiny points of brass. Then you see one move, that sudden lateral flick that jumping spiders do, and then you see that the wall is alive with them, hundreds in the circle of your lamp, thousands on the tier, and they are all watching you back. Jumping spiders hunt by sight. They turn to follow movement. When a welding arc flares two levels up, the whole population of a wall pivots toward it in the same instant, a shiver of attention passing across the rock like wind across water, and then the arc goes out and they turn back to their business.

They have never known anything else. A jumping spider lives a year, perhaps two, and the pit has been open — nobody knows exactly; decades — long enough that these are the great-great-great-grandchildren of whatever first came up the mountain clinging to a moth. They have never seen sunlight except as the gray column that swings across the bubble at midday and never reaches their level. They have never seen a leaf. They eat springtails, which eat the crust, and moths, which the thermals throw up out of the living world three kilometers below and drop, stunned and frost-stiff, into the pit's warmth, where they revive on the steel for a few minutes and are taken. Every moth in the pit is a stranger and a gift. The spiders ride on the crew's jackets sometimes, from tier to tier, and the crew don't brush them off. They are considered lucky, or considered nothing, which in the pit amounts to the same thing.

Behind the ducting on the second tier there are pikas, and the pikas are wrong. They are small hay-gathering rabbits of the high meadows, round-eared, herbivores to the bone, and there has never been a blade of grass within three thousand vertical meters of where they live. They came up in a crate, probably — some shipment of hydraulic fittings from a valley depot — and found the warm gap behind the duct and stayed, and now they eat what falls: the biscuit crumbs, the paper off the packages, and, it turns out, the orange crust that grows nearest the vents, which nobody believed until someone saw them at it, and which turns their droppings the color of rust. You hear them more than see them, a thin chittering in the idle between engine cycles, and once in a while a face appears at the edge of the duct, very round-eyed, very much not where it ought to be, and withdraws. The crew leave them alone. There is a feeling, never articulated, that they are in the same situation.

---

The crust is the oldest thing that lives here, and the only one that isn't a visitor.

It was in the bubble before the ladder, before the first jack, probably within a season of the roof splitting and the vents beginning to breathe. It needs sulfur and warmth and nothing else — not light, not water, not a single carbon atom from the world of leaves — and it gets both from the mountain directly, and so it is the one inhabitant of the pit that is not, in some sense, stealing. Near the fumaroles it is a violent orange, thick as felt; it grades outward through yellow and then gray-white and then, on the cold high walls under the throat, to a sooty film you can only see when your lamp catches it edge-on. It is warm to the touch where it is thickest. If you press a gloved finger into it near a vent it gives slightly, like the inside of a cheek.

Everything above it hangs from it. The springtails graze it and the spiders take the springtails and the choughs take the spiders, and the pikas eat it outright, and even the crew — who eat nothing from the pit — could not be here without it, because the crust is the sign that the vents are breathing, and the vents are the warmth, and the warmth is the whole reason forty people can survive in a hole at the top of the sky. The crew read it the way they read the props. A vent that is dying shows first as a graying of its orange collar. A vent that is opening shows as a flush. When a fumarole on the fifth tier went cold three seasons ago the crust around it went the color of old bone within a month, and the crew moved the bunks.

---

On the lowest gantry, nearest the dark, there is the lammergeier.

You will not see it at first, because it does not move and because it is the color of the place — slate and rust and a pale dirty cream at the breast — and because your eye is drawn past it, down, to the black below the last catwalk where the ducts go and the lamps don't. Then it turns its head and you see the eye, which is red-rimmed and pale and has a look in it of very long patience, and you see the wing that hangs a little off true, unfolded at the wrist where it should fold, and you understand that this is the one that came in.

The others come at dusk. Every evening two or three of them ride the last thermals up to the summit and hang over the throat, enormous, three meters of wing, and look down into the pit with their heads cocked, and do not enter. They are bone-eaters. Somewhere in their long memory is the knowledge that warm enclosed places at altitude are where dead things collect, and the pit is warm and enclosed and smells of the crew's cut-offs, and every evening they consider it and every evening they leave. Except this one. Nobody saw it come. One morning it was on the lowest gantry with its wing wrong and the choughs in an uproar, and it has not left, and cannot, because the only way out is forty meters straight up a four-meter throat and it will never fly that again.

So the crew feed it. This is what the bones are for. Every few days someone climbs down to the lowest level with a sack and empties it onto the grating a few meters from the bird, and the bird waits until the person is gone and then walks — it walks, in the pit, with a heavy deliberate side-to-side gait like an old man on a boat — and takes a bone in its beak and considers it.

A lammergeier breaks bones by carrying them high and dropping them onto rock. It cannot carry anything high anymore. What it does instead, having considered the bone, is walk to the edge of the gantry where the last railing was never finished, and let go.

And the pit listens.

This is the thing nobody tells you about the lowest level and everybody knows. The bone falls into the dark below the last catwalk and there is a moment — the crew have counted it; some of them count under their breath — and then, far down, there is a sound. Sometimes a crack. Sometimes a flat slap like a hand on wet stone, which should not be possible in a mountain that has never known water. Sometimes there is no sound at all, and the crew count to twenty and then thirty and then look at each other and go back to work. The lammergeier stands at the edge and looks down after it, head cocked, as if it too is waiting to learn something, and then turns and walks back and waits for the next sack. It has not eaten a bone in the time anyone has been watching. It drops them. All of them. It has been dropping them for a year.

The crew have stopped calling this strange. They have a way of saying it: *she's sounding*. As though the bird were a plumb line. As though the dark had a floor and the only instrument in the pit that could find it was a broken vulture with a crate of scraps.

---

So: what lives here.

Not the mountain, though the mountain is the largest presence and everything else is a lodger in its held breath. Not the machines, which are tools and know nothing. What lives in the pit, when you have climbed all the way down and looked at all of it, is a single community with a single condition, and the condition is this: every one of them came for the warmth, and every one of them has stayed past the point where staying made sense, and every one of them has become an instrument for measuring the closing.

The choughs measure it by leaving the rail before the plate falls. The spiders measure it by turning, all at once, toward the arc. The crust measures it in the graying of an orange collar around a vent that is being squeezed shut a millimeter at a time three hundred meters down in the rock. The pikas measure it in whether the warm gap behind the duct is still warm. The crew measure it in the note of a brace, in the bleed cycle of a jack, in the fact that the bunks are on the third tier now and were on the fifth. The bird measures it by dropping a bone into the dark and listening for the floor, and the floor, some nights, does not answer.

None of them will be here when it finishes. The choughs least of all know this and the crew most of all, and it is hard, standing on the lowest gantry with the dust sifting down and the sodium light making everything the color of an old coin, to see much difference in how they carry it. The bird waits. The spiders watch. The tapping goes on overhead, *tik, tik, tik*, an inventory of the things still holding. Somebody two tiers up drops a wrench and the choughs go up in a black shout and come down again inside a minute, and settle on the warm steel, and preen, and wait for the next amber light.

And the throat, forty meters above all of them, tightens by its fraction, and the ladder flexes, and the mountain — which is the only thing here that is not waiting for anything — goes on.
