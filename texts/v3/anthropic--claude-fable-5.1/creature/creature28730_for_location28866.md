**What Lives in the Oven**

Begin outside, because the census must.

Nothing lives on the shelf. This is not a figure of speech. There is no lichen on the rhyolite, no mite in the ash, no seed blown up from below waiting for a rain that will not come. The wind has nothing in it but cold. If you kneel and look at the ground with your face close, you find the glassy surface pocked with tiny craters where gas escaped a very long time ago, and in the craters there is dust, and in the dust there is nothing. The plateau is one of the few places you will ever stand where the ground under you is exactly as dead as it looks.

So the border of the oven's population is the mouth of the crack, and it is a hard border. Warmth on one side and not on the other. Stand with one foot in and one foot out and you are standing on the edge of the world as far as anything alive is concerned.

---

**The film**

The first inhabitant is the one you will never see and will not, at first, recognize as an inhabitant at all.

It is on every surface of the room beneath everything else, and it is on the walls of the passage from about the second bend inward, and it is on the far passage going down as far as anyone has reached. It is on the underside of the grazers. It is, faintly, on the dome above the grooves, thinner there because the heat is less. If you could lift every other living thing out of the oven in one motion, what remained would be a room lined entirely in it, seamless, dark, the same in the corners as at the center.

It is not a colony of anything. That was the natural assumption and it is probably wrong. There are no edges to it, no borders between patches, no place where one growth meets another and the two texture differently. It behaves like a single tissue. A grazer rasps a hand's breadth of it down to bare rock and the bare rock is not bare for long, but what returns does not creep in from the sides — it comes up from beneath, out of the pores of the stone, as if the film on the surface were only the visible skin of a body that fills the rock behind it. No one has cored the wall deep enough to find where it stops. It may not stop. It may be that the oven is a hollow in a living thing rather than a living thing in a hollow.

It eats the breath. The warmth rising from below carries sulphur and iron and traces of gases the rock has been holding since it was fire, and the film takes these apart and builds itself out of what it finds. It is the only thing in the room that touches the water directly — the very little water there is, coming up as vapor with the rest, caught by the film before it can rise to the dome and be lost down the passage and out to the shelf. Everything else in the oven drinks the film. The film drinks the mountain.

It grows slowly. Not lichen-slow; slower. If you could mark a spot and come back in a human lifetime, you would find it a fingernail thicker, perhaps. But it does not die, or does not die in any way that makes sense as dying. What is grazed off is not killed, only moved — into the grazer, into whatever eats the grazer, into the dust, and back to the rock. The film's substance goes round the room and comes home. It is possible that not one atom of it has left the oven since the oven was warm.

It has a texture, in the thickest places, like the nap of very fine velvet. It has no color. It has a smell, under the sulphur, that is difficult to name and that you will not notice until you are back on the shelf and it is gone: something clean and faintly sweet, like warm paper.

**The grazers**

These are the room. When the description says the walls are covered, it is the grazers that cover them.

Take one up — they come up easily, they do not grip, they seem not to mind — and you hold something the shape and heft of a flattened river stone. Palm-sized, the large ones; the small ones no bigger than a thumbnail. The shell is one piece, low-domed, and its surface is rough in a particular way, granular and matte, so that a grazer set down on bare rock would be very hard to find by touch. The edge is beveled outward, thin, slightly flexible. Turn it over and there are legs, a great many of them, in two ranks around a central mouth — perhaps thirty, perhaps forty, jointed, pale, moving in slow waves even in your hand. The mouth is a rasp. Not jaws: a ring of hard ridges that scrapes.

There is no front. A grazer has no head, no eyes, no antennae, nothing at one end that is not at the other. It goes whichever way the heat is better, and it knows the heat with its whole underside at once, so that it turns not by deciding but by leaning. Put your hand flat into the wall and you feel them adjust to your palm's warmth: a shift toward you, a settling, a slow investigation by legs so fine the touch is like being brushed with hair.

They live in a stack. The bottom layer is on the film and eating. The layers above are on the layer below and not eating — waiting, digesting, holding still with their legs drawn in and their bevels overlapping their neighbors' like roof tiles, so that the surface of the stack is nearly as sealed as a single shell. What moves them up and down through the stack is hunger, and hunger is the tide. A grazer that has eaten grows heavy and slow and is climbed over; a grazer that has fasted grows restless and works its way down. The whole mass turns over like this, continuously, the bottom rising and the top sinking, and the direction the bottom layer grazes in is set by the heat, and the heat pulses, so the direction reverses with the pulse. This is what you feel under your hand for an hour: the room turning itself over.

They cannot molt. A creature that sheds its skin must be wet underneath, and there is no wetness to spare here. So they grow the way a limpet grows, by adding to the margin, ring on ring, and the oldest grazers are the widest and flattest and roughest, and their shells carry a record of every hot pulse and cool ebb they have lived through as a ridge or a valley in the growth. Some of the large ones on the dome — where the heat is gentlest and life is slowest — may be very old indeed. Older than the marks at the entrance. Older than whoever made them.

Each one is a sealed purse of water. It is the only water it will ever have. It takes a little from the film, so little that the film does not notice, and holds it, and gives none back until it dies. And when it dies — slowing, sinking through the stack, coming to rest against the film with its legs still — its neighbors drink it. Not with any appearance of appetite. They simply close in, edge to edge, and something passes through the seams. In a day the dead grazer is a shell with nothing in it. In a week the shell has gone brittle and begun to flake. In a month there is a roughness in the film where it was, and then not even that.

This is the closest thing the oven has to a rite.

**The lamp-bearers**

You will have watched them for an hour before you have any idea what they are.

They ride the top of the stack. Longer than the grazers and narrower, low, with a shell that is smooth rather than rough, and fewer legs, and — this is the difference that matters — a front. There is a head end. It is not much of a head, a blunt wedge with the mouth beneath it, but it is an end that goes first, and the whole body follows it, and the light is there: a single bead on the underside just behind the mouth, blue-white, no brighter than a star at the edge of sight.

They are hunters, and what they hunt is grazers, and they hunt by touch. A lamp-bearer moving over the stack is reading the shells beneath it with the whole length of its body, and what it is reading for is a flaw. The bevel of a young grazer's shell is thin. The newest growth ring on an old one is soft for a while. A shell that has been knocked has a crack in it that no one but a lamp-bearer would ever find. When one finds such a thing, it stops, and it holds, and then — this is what you were watching, the slow convergence of lights across the dome — the others come.

One cannot open a grazer. The shell is too much for one. It takes several, working the edge, and they do this without hurry and, apparently, without conflict, and when the shell gives they drink what is inside and leave the rest, and the lights go out together, which means, probably, that the light is how they gather. It is the only sense in the room that carries across distance. Warmth does not travel far in a place where everything is warm; pressure only reaches the next body over. But a pinprick of blue-white light on the far side of the dome — that is a message that arrives.

Except that nothing here has eyes. The lamp-bearers do not have eyes. This has been checked, or as near to checked as anyone has come, holding one to a candle on the shelf and finding no lens, no pit, no dark spot in the pale head-wedge where a lens might once have been. So either they feel the light in some way that is not seeing — and blue-white light is cold, it carries almost no warmth, so it is hard to say what they would feel — or the gathering is coordinated by something else and the light is a passenger. A chemistry that happens to shine. A leftover from a time when the oven was a different place, when there was something in it that could see, and the light was for that.

Or — and you will think of this only later, on the shelf, when you have stopped trying to be sensible — the light is for the one who cut the grooves in the dome. Not that it was made for them. But that in all the oven's long history they may have been the only creature it ever reached.

The lamp-bearers do not eat the dead. They eat only what they open. And they are in turn opened, when they are old, by their own kind, though this has not been seen, only inferred from the way the lights sometimes converge on a spot where no grazer is.

**The slow one**

There is at least one. There may be three. There is not likely to be more than a handful, because the room could not feed them.

You feel it, when you feel it, as a displacement — a shift in the warm mass around your feet as though the floor had bulged and settled. Nothing scatters. The grazers do not move away from it, exactly; they move over it, or it moves under them, so smoothly that the surface of the stack barely lifts. It is of the room in the way the tide is of the sea. If you get your hand down through the layers at the right moment you touch a shell that is unlike the others: not rough, not smooth-narrow like a lamp-bearer's, but broad and polished and very slightly warm on its own account, warmer than the room, as though there were more fire in it than in the stone.

It is about the size of a sleeping dog, or a curled child. It moves only at the height of the hot pulse, and then not far, and then it stops for a long time. What it eats is not certain. It may be the film directly, in a great slow sweep — the original grazer, from before the grazers were small — because behind where it has passed the film is sometimes worn to the stone in a broad band that no crowd of little rasps could have made so evenly. Or it may eat lamp-bearers. Or it may be past eating. There are creatures that reach a size and an age at which their needs become so slight that they seem to live on time itself, and this may be one of them, moving through the warm dark on the memory of meals.

It is the only thing in the room that has been felt to go down the far passage and come back. Not seen — felt, as the same broad displacement, going out of the room into the narrow way and then, a long time later, an hour or a day, returning. Whatever is down there does not stop it. Or is it.

Whoever piled the stones at the entrance would have known about it. A person who spent time in the oven, sitting on the cool slab by the door, would have felt it pass, and could not have failed to wonder how old it was, and whether it knew they were there.

**The dust**

The room has no floor of the dead. This has to be accounted for.

The neighbors drink a dead grazer dry, and the lamp-bearers leave the shells they open, and the film does not eat shell — it is too slow, and shell is not what it wants. So there must be something that does, and there is, and it is almost nothing.

Sift the lowest layer, down where the grazers meet the film, and your fingertips find a grit that is not stone. It moves. Each grain is a body, or the greater part of one — a speck with legs, pale, so small that a dozen could stand on a grazer's eye if a grazer had an eye. They live in the seams. Between the plates of every shell, in the joints of every leg, under the bevel of every grazer in the stack, there are some, and they are harmless there, or nearly; they take the film that grows in the cracks and the flakes of shell that are shed, and their hosts do not notice. But when a thing dies, they come out of everywhere at once. The grit thickens on the body until the body is grit. A shell that would be a shell for a thousand years in the dry air of the shelf is dust in a month in the oven, and the dust is alive, and the living dust goes back into the seams to wait.

They are most active in the cool phase, when the grazers are still and there is nothing to knock them loose. In the hot phase they cling. There is some evidence — the thinnest — that they are not a species of their own but a stage of something: that the lamp-bearers begin as dust, or the grazers do, or both, and that the pale specks in the joints of a shell are its own young riding it until they are large enough to be something else. Nothing has been raised from grit to adult under observation. Nothing has been observed for long enough.

But it would be fitting. It would mean the room has no separate undertaker, that the dead are taken apart by the children of the living, and that the specks of pale grit you brush off your palm on the shelf, into the dead wind, were the only things you carried out of the oven that had anywhere to go.

**The far passage**

In the cool phase, when the lights are out and the walls have gone nearly still, there is the click.

It comes up the far passage. It is dry and clear and single, not loud, about the pitch of a fingernail on a table. A long time after it, another. Then nothing for so long that you are sure it has stopped, and then another.

The room does nothing in response. That should be said. The grazers do not shift; the dust does not stir. If the lamp-bearers still had their lights on you might see whether the lights did anything, but the click comes only when the lights are out, or the lights go out only when the click is coming — and no one has stayed long enough through enough cool phases to say which.

Here is what it might be.

It might be the rock. Stone cooling and contracting speaks in clicks, and the far passage goes down toward the heat, and the boundary between hot rock and less hot rock must be somewhere along it, and might tick as the pulse ebbs. This is the sensible answer. It does not account for the spacing, which is not regular the way cooling is regular, but is regular the way a person's breathing is.

It might be another room. If the oven is one chamber in a mountain that breathes, there is no reason it should be the only one, and the far passage might open into a second hollow, hotter and darker, with its own film and its own stack and its own hunters, and the click might be a thing that lives there doing whatever it does — a shell against stone, a claw on a wall. It would be strange if the mountain had made only one oven.

It might be the slow one, or one of them, down there where it goes, knocking.

It might be someone.

There is a person in this account already — the one who piled the stones and scratched the entrance slab and cut the grooves in the dome and sat on the cool slab by the door. They are not there now. They took everything out that they brought in, or the dust took it. But no one has gone down the far passage and come back to say what is there, and someone once sat at the entrance with a sharp stone making one scratch and then, after a while, another, and there are dozens of scratches, and some of them are nearly worn away, which means they were made long before the last ones were.

If you were counting clicks you would count them like that. One, then a long wait, then one. And if you were waiting for someone who had gone down and not come back, you might count something, anything, to have a shape to the waiting. And the bowl in the floor, the small one glazed by what was put in it — that is about the size of a grazer, a large one, opened. There is water in a grazer. Not much. Enough, perhaps, to stay a little longer than you should.
