The first answer is the water, and it is the wrong answer, but it is where you have to begin, because it is the only thing on the plateau that visibly does anything.

It enters from above, somewhere in the cloud, where the upper flows must be taking rain you never see fall. It threads down through the basalt along the same fractures the cooling made, and it has been doing this long enough that it no longer explores. It knows the rock. It comes out at the flow-fronts because that is where the rock ends, and it goes into the channels because the channels are there, and it falls from notch to basin in the same thread it fell in yesterday. It is warm because the rock is still faintly warm, a hundred meters down, and it is clear because it has passed through nothing that could dirty it. If you asked what the plateau is for, the honest answer is that it is a way for this water to get from where it is to where it is going. Everything else is an arrangement made around that.

But the water does not live here. It passes. It is in the town for an hour and then it is on the shelf below and then it is gone into the white. Whatever lives here is what stays.

The moss stays. It is the one thing you can watch and believe is making a decision, though the decision takes years. It grows on the wall of the flow-front where the seepage is thickest, and it grows over the retaining courses of the terraces, and where it has been let alone long enough it has stopped following the rock and started proposing its own shape, rounding the sharp corners the basalt refused to round, swelling out over an edge, softening a lintel. If you pressed your hand into it your hand would go in to the wrist and come out warm. The tree ferns stay, and the sedge, and in their islands they are so lush that they seem to have been given everything, and they have been given exactly one thing, which is water, and they have made all of this from that. But you know what you mean by *lives there*. You mean someone.

So look again at the swept steps.

Sweeping is not something a place does to itself. Neither is the clarity of the channels. The water comes out of the rock carrying almost nothing, but *almost* is not *nothing*: a fine gray mineral dust comes with it, the ground-down interior of the basalt, and in the grooves of the channels that dust would settle, and in a season it would be silt, and in a few seasons the channel would be a bed of it and the water would be finding a new way. It is not. The grooves are bare stone. The basins under the notches, where the falling thread should be building a little delta, are clean to their floors. Something takes the silt out of the water. And the silt has to go somewhere, and when you understand where, the whole plateau turns over in your head.

It goes to the gardens.

That is what the fern islands are. The plateau gives soil nowhere; the seepage leaves a smear of mineral in a crack and that is the plateau's entire offering. But the town's channels collect it, a handful a day, a gray paste scraped from the groove with the edge of a flat stone, and the handful is carried out onto the bare flow and put down in a hollow where there is already some, and pressed in, and wetted from the nearest pool. The gardens are not where the ferns happened to take. They are where the silt was put. Their edges are sharp and their spacing is deliberate and they do not join because they have not been allowed to join; the town is not going to spread soil across a hundred meters of stone to connect two islands when it could deepen one. The extravagance of the ferns is the town's one harvest, and the crop is dirt.

Now you know there are people, and you have not seen one, and you begin to understand why.

You see a person because they move, or because they cast a shadow, or because they are a different color from what is behind them. Here nothing casts a shadow. The light comes from everywhere and the black rock gives back a low wet shine, and a person standing against it is a dark wet shape against a dark wet shape. The doors are sedge matting on fern poles. What the people wear, when they wear anything in air the temperature of their own skin, is sedge matting. A person sitting in a doorway and the door are the same material and the same color, both black with damp, and the interior behind them is the only true dark on the plateau, and they sit an arm's length back inside it. And they do not move. Not because you are here, or not only. They do not move because nothing here moves, and they have been here long enough to be of it.

They saw you. You were the only thing on the flow that walked. A dark shape crossing the shine, with wet on the backs of its hands, stopping at the pools, looking up at the wall. They watched you the way you watched the drip. They are watching now, and there is no malice in it and no fear; there is the attention of people whose whole life is noticing whether a thread of water has changed its note.

Let them come into focus.

They are wet. That is the first thing, and it is not a condition, it is a state of being, the way a fish is wet. Their skin has not been dry in their lives. The fingertips are permanently pale and ridged, the way yours go after an hour in a bath, and the nails are soft and kept short with a stone. But the palms are thick. The soles are thick. They work basalt with basalt, and the hands that do that are as hard as anything on the plateau that is not the rock itself. Their hair, where they have it, lies flat and dark and beaded, and they do not push it back, because there is no wind to have moved it. They are not tall. They eat fern pith and sedge root, both starchy, both bland, ground on the round stones you saw in the doorways and eaten as paste, and there is no fire, because there is nothing dry to burn and no cold to burn it against, and because the thatch and the fern-bundles under the eaves are being dried for building, not for burning, and drying anything here takes a year. So there is no smoke. There has never been smoke on this plateau. Their food is the temperature of the air and the water they drink is a little warmer than that, and the inside of their mouths is the one place they can feel a difference.

Their work is the channels, and the interval of their work is the interval of the silt. Each of them has a length of groove, from the notch above to the notch below, and a basin, and a portion of the wall where the seepage comes out. Each morning, if it is morning — the cloud goes a dimmer gray and then brightens and that is a day — each goes out onto the terrace with a flat blade of split basalt and draws it along the groove, and the gray film comes up on the edge, and is wiped into a cupped leaf of fern, and when the leaf is full it is carried out across the bare flow to the garden that belongs to that terrace, and put down. That is a morning. Then the steps are swept, because grit from the sweeping would go into the water. Then they sit. The tree ferns must be watched, the thatch must be watched, the retaining course must be looked at where the moss is pushing a block, and none of this needs doing today, or this year, but it will need doing once, and the person whose block it is will be there when it does. They are not idle. They are on the same schedule as the moss.

They hardly speak. It is not a rule. The cloud takes the voice; anything said past an arm's length arrives thinned to nothing, and after long enough the impulse fades. Within reach they talk low and briefly. Between terraces they use the water. A hand laid across the notch stops the thread, and on the terrace below the small clear note stops, and that is heard, because it is the one sound anyone here is listening for. A hand lifted and laid again is a pattern. It is not a language of much. It says *I am here* and *come up* and *the water has changed*, and it says them downhill only, because that is the way water goes, so the top terrace speaks and the bottom terrace listens, and anyone who wants to say something upward climbs the stair and says it in a doorway. They do not say much upward. There is not much to say.

There are perhaps forty of them. It is hard to be sure, because you never see two at once. They do not gather. The squares with the cisterns at their centers are for the cisterns; a person crosses one alone to draw water and crosses back. Each house holds one, or two, and each terrace is a row of these, and the rows are the town, and the town is like the gardens: the same water in every part of it, and every part an island. They are not unkind to one another. They are simply as unjoined as everything else here has settled for being.

You will want to know whether there are children, and whether they die, and you will not be able to tell. This is the same difficulty the plateau presented when you tried to see it change. There are small people among them, in some doorways, doing the same work with smaller blades, and there are people whose skin has the look of a stone that water has been on for a long time. But nothing here grows visibly, and you did not see anyone born, and you did not see anyone carried out to a garden and put down in the silt, though that is what would be done. The count holds. Whether it holds because each loss is met exactly by an arrival, the way the pools receive what they lose, or because there is neither loss nor arrival and these are the ones who came up and stayed, you would have to wait to find out, and then you would have to wait again, and the place has already shown you what waiting here is. You would be sitting in a doorway. You would be one of them.

They have no birds. This is a thing you carry up with you and they do not: they have never seen anything nest in the sedge, so the sedge is thatch and nothing else, and it has never occurred to them that a roof might be a place another creature would want. They have never brushed an insect from a pool. The water in the cisterns has never had anything in it to cloud it, and so they do not have the idea of water that is not clear, and if you told them what a stream is where you come from, thick and brown and full, they would think you were describing a channel that had been left alone, which is the nearest thing they have to a disaster. Their stories, such as they are, are about the coming up: the climb, the cloud, the first cut into the wall where the water came out. Nothing in the stories has wings. Nothing in them has ever followed.

When you leave — and you will, going back down the steps of black rock into the white, because you have somewhere the water is going too — one of them will come out. Not immediately. After the interval. A dark shape in the doorway will be a dark shape on the terrace, and it will go down to where you stood, and it will sweep. There will be grit from your boots, a few grains of something from below that the plateau does not make, and the grains will go into a fern leaf and be carried across the flow and pressed into a garden, and that will be the whole of your effect on the place: a little foreign soil, a fern that grows a millimeter it would not otherwise have grown. Then the shape will sit down again inside the door, an arm's length back in the dark.

And the drip you were listening to will go on, one bead letting go from the underside of a ledge, and there will be someone hearing it who has heard it every day of their life and would know, before you would, if it ever came a moment late.
