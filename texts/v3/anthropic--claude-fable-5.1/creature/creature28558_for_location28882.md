Start with the animals, because they are the ones the word will stick to.

The herons number somewhere around forty at any hour, gray with a rust wash at the throat, and they are the tallest living things in the ring that hold still. They fish the moats. They stand on the roofs. They have the enormous advantage of never having to know what they are standing on: a heron's foot goes down, finds something, and that is enough. The young learn the ring the way any young animal learns a shore, by falling in. You see a fledgling on a roof that has begun to roll and it does not step, it waits, the way it would wait on a bank, and the roof turns under it and it goes into the water with a slap and comes up furious and unhurt. By its second season it steps. By its third it has the adults' look, which is the look of something that has stopped noticing.

The small birds are more committed. There is a kind like a wren, brown and loud, and a kind like a swift, forked-tailed, which never touches water. Both nest in the walls, and their nests last an hour. This should have finished them long ago and instead it has become the entire shape of their lives. They have given up on persistence. They build shallow and fast, a cup of brass moss and shed fiber pressed into whatever pocket the wall has just offered, and lay in it, and when the wall begins to go they carry the eggs — in the beak, one at a time, across a hundred paces of open water to the next wall that has opened. The eggs are small and tough and warm from the fiber. A pair will make eleven moves in a day and lose nothing. The chatter you took for distress is not distress. They are the only creatures in the ring who know exactly what a wall is about to do, and they say so, continuously, to each other.

The frogs are more intimate with it than that. They do not live on the structures, they live in them, sealed into the fiber like stones in bread, the same color as the fiber, invisible while a wall stands. When it softens they come out with the softening, dozens at a time, and drop, and swim to the next opening, and press themselves in, and the fiber closes over them as it firms. Their skins have the same sheen as the sweat on the columns. They eat what lives in the fiber — there are things that live in the fiber, small, pale, quick — and in that sense they are cleaners, and the structures may tolerate them as large fish tolerate small ones. Or the structures may not know they are there. The knocking of their call never stops because there is never a moment when a wall is not opening somewhere.

The shelled things want more attention than you gave them. Hand-sized, low, with carapaces like wet bark and a pair of mouthparts that can close on a tuft of fiber the size of a fist. They cross the mud in straight lines. Follow one and it goes from a structure to another structure and sets the tuft down at the base and the base takes it up. Follow another and it goes to the rim, and sets the tuft down there, and presses with the flat of its shell, and leaves it. So: some of what the structures let go of is not reabsorbed with that milky blue flicker. Some of it is carried. Some of it goes to the rim and becomes the only permanent thing here, and it goes there in the mouths of animals. Whether the structures give it up on purpose, whether the shelled things are servants or scavengers or the ring's dull slow method of keeping accounts, is not something you can settle by watching. But the rim is a boot's height thick and ten paces wide and runs for more than a thousand paces around, and every fiber in it was set down by a mouth, and pressed.

The moss is the quiet resident. Dull brass, flat, slow, growing in the margins where nothing is built. It is the only plant in the ring not taking part. It may be a cousin. It may be what they were before they started, or what they will be after. It may be what the plain was covered in when the plain still had hills, and it has simply outlasted everything by never doing anything at all. It is very good at that.

And the pale thing in the walls. You see it once as a shadow through a translucent panel, long, boneless, moving with the slow loop of something that has never needed to go straight. You see it again in another structure, sixty paces off. You cannot tell whether it is many or one — whether there is a population of these, one to a house, or whether there is a single one of great length that passes through all thirteen structures at once, threaded through them below the level of the roofs, and what you see is the part that happens to be near a thin place in the wall. Hold that. Do not decide yet.

Now the structures, since they are what everything else here lives in.

There are thirteen, and there is the matter of the fourteenth, which will come. They are not alike. Stand long enough and each shows a temperament, the way a herd's animals do once you have stopped seeing the herd. One is always the tallest — it builds up before it builds out, and when the others bring it down with their adjustments it comes back tall. One reopens the same door, the same width, facing the same way, no matter how the ring around it shifts; the others adjust around that door as if around a stone in a stream. One is slow, and its slowness has the quality of a refusal — it takes three breaths to drop a wall the others drop in one, and when it finally answers, the answer is always a little late and a little off, and you cannot tell whether it is failing to keep up or declining to. One has stopped making rooms. Corridors only, at shoulder width, branching and rejoining, folding back into themselves in the space where you saw the stair make two turns. It is the one with the most impossible interiors and it is the one you would least like to enter.

They are old. The rim gives you the measure. They are older than any tree, older than the process that wore the country down, and they have been doing this — dropping, raising, answering — the whole time, without pause, at an expense that no body should be able to sustain. The sums do not come out because you are counting water and fiber and there is a third term. They are drawing something up out of the plain that is not water. The pumping is deeper than the pools. Perhaps the plain is this flat because of them. Perhaps there were once a thousand rings across this country, each one drawing up whatever it draws up, dropping its refusals onto its rim, and the hills went through them grain by grain and came out as silt and warm shallow water and a fibrous ring of what could not be taken back. Perhaps the land is finished because they finished it. That would make what you are looking at not the last holdout against the plain but its author — the mouth that ate the country, still chewing.

Or not. The rim does not tell you which. It only tells you how long.

What they make, though, is not in question. They make rooms. Corridors at shoulder width. Shelves at the height of a seat. Doorways you would have to duck through. Beds — that shape drawn up out of the floor, longer than wide, slightly hollowed, once you have seen it you see it in every structure, always half-formed, always reabsorbed before it is finished. They are not building shelter. They are building fit. Every wall that goes up has the proportions of an occupant in it, the way a shoe has a foot in it, and every wall that comes down is a proposal withdrawn. The argument across the water — one drops a side, the other raises a matching side within moments, no, like this — is an argument about the shape of a person. Thirteen bodies who each remember someone slightly differently and have not, in an age, agreed on the height of the shelf.

So there is the second answer to who lives here, and it is an outline. Something the size of you, which sat, and slept, and walked corridors with its shoulders nearly brushing, and is not present. The rooms are held open for it for as long as they can bear to hold anything, then let go, then tried again a hundred paces off. What lives in this place, in the strictest sense in which a place can be said to hold a living thing, is a fit that has outlasted whatever fitted it — a negative, carried in thirteen bodies, corrected and recorrected, never permitted to set.

Except.

Go back to the pale thing in the walls. Consider that the household need not have left. Consider that it may have done what the herons did and the frogs did and the small birds did — stayed, and adapted, and lived in the rhythm of the churn rather than against it. Consider what a body becomes over the span the rim describes if it lives inside walls that never hold still: it loses whatever will not bend. Shoulders. The need to sit. The need to lie down in a shape longer than wide. It learns to move through the fiber the way the frogs do but never leaves, and it grows long, because there is nowhere in a rolling wall for anything to be short, and it becomes what you glimpsed, boneless and pale, looping through a translucent panel. That may be one of them. That may be all of them, joined. That may be the last one, threaded through all thirteen houses at once so that no house has to be without.

And the houses go on building shelves at the height of a seat.

That is the version that begins to feel like the place. Not a household absent and a house forgetting, but a household present and a house unable to see it — building, over and over, rooms for a body that it fed and sheltered and changed, until the body no longer fit anything the house knows how to make. The argument across the water would then be an argument neither party can win, because the house is right about the shelf and the thing in the wall is right that it cannot sit on it, and both of them have been right for longer than the plain has been flat. Perhaps the pale thing does not know it is being built for. Perhaps it knows and cannot say. Perhaps it has been saying, through the fiber, from inside, for the entire span of the rim, and what you are watching is not deafness but the terrible patience of two things trying to become recognizable to each other again by trial, at the speed of weather.

Or the frogs are the household. Or the herons. Or there was never anyone and the fit is a shape the structures dreamed. You are allowed to leave it there.

The fourteenth, then, because it is what the count keeps catching on.

It is on the far side of the ring, a little apart, and when you first counted it was standing and when you counted again it was not. What is there now is a mat — a wide low raft of dark fiber lying on the water, unbuilt entirely, not a wall or a column left, pooled out flat like something that has finally been allowed to lie down. Herons stand on it. Frogs have come out of it and not gone back in. A shelled thing is at its edge with a tuft in its mouth, and it is not carrying the tuft toward the mat. It is carrying it away. Toward the rim.

You can see what is being decided. One of them has stopped arguing. One of them has let its rooms go and its fit go and its portion of the pale thing go, if it had one, and is lying on the water in the posture of the plain, ready to be carried out by the mouths of animals and pressed into the ring of everything else that gave up, and become permanent, and be quiet.

And the pumping does not let it. Watch the pools around the mat. They are dropping. Across the water, the tall one has gone still, the stubborn door has closed, the slow one has stopped mid-motion with a wall half-raised — the whole ring has gone quiet in a way that you had not known it could, the creaking fallen off to almost nothing, the small birds silent for the first time, and under the silence the suck of water, deeper than you have heard it, all of it directed at one place. The mat thickens. It is being fed. Fiber is arriving at its edge in a dozen mouths and being pressed not away but in. The blue flicker runs along its underside like a fuse. Something in the middle of it begins, very slowly, against what you now understand is its own wish, to rise.

That is who lives here. Thirteen who will not stop, and one who tried to, and an outline held between them that no one can fit, and an audience that long ago stopped looking down. The mat comes up into a hummock, and the hummock into a column, and a heron that was standing on it steps, one foot and then the other, onto whatever has come up under it, and does not look.
