Ask what lives here and the honest first answer is: nothing you would call a resident. The plateau does not keep things. It coats them. Anything that stops moving on this stone for more than a season is, by the season after, part of the stone, and so the population of the place sorts itself, without anyone deciding, into two kinds — those that live by never being still, and those that live by agreeing to be built in.

Start with the second kind, because they were here first, and because they are the ones who actually made the city.

---

The bands of color around every pool — the orange, the yellow, the rust and olive that you took for staining — are not stains. They are mats. Cyanobacteria, mostly, in sheets a few cells thick, with filamentous algae threaded through them and, underneath, a layer of bacteria that never see light and do not need it. They are the oldest living things on the plateau by an unimaginable margin and they are the only ones that could be said to be *at home*.

They are also the masons. Water alone does not make a rim. Water alone makes a puddle with a smear of chalk at the edge. What makes the lip — the rounded balustrade wall, the scalloped drapery, the crisp curved edge of every terrace — is the mat. The film grows fastest where the water is thinnest and most agitated, which is the edge; the lime falls out of the water fastest where the film is, because the film changes the chemistry in its own tiny neighborhood and pulls the mineral down onto itself; the edge therefore rises; the pool behind it deepens; the film grows along the new edge. The wall is the mat's own accumulated dead. Every rim in the city is a living skin a fraction of a millimeter deep over a body of its own ancestors, and the whole boulevard-and-plaza architecture that looks planned from the high ground was in fact drawn, edge by edge, by something that cannot see and does not know there is a plateau.

Each color is a different community, and each community likes a different depth, temperature, light, flow. So the bands are districts. The orange lives in the splash where the water is thinnest and coldest and most exposed. The dark green lives just under the surface in slow water. The rust lives in the channels, in the current, and is the one that lines the streets. Where a spring changes and a pool warms or cools by a degree, the districts shift over a few years, the way neighborhoods do, and the wall they build changes color at that height, and you can read the history of the water in the stripes of the stone as you would read strata, except that these strata were laid down by their own inhabitants.

They are not growing up out of the stone. They are growing up *through* it, at the exact rate it forms, keeping their heads above their own monument by a hair a season. They have been doing this for so long that the question of whether they are alive on the stone or the stone is alive with them stops having a good answer.

---

Above them, the moss, which you have already met. What you may not have understood about the moss is that it has made the same bargain and is losing it more slowly. A cushion of it is green and soft for perhaps a decade. Then the lime finds the base of the stems and the cushion begins to stiffen from the inside, and for a century or so it is a living shell over a stone core, and the shell gets thinner, and one year it is a moss-shaped stone with a faint green bloom, and the year after it is a stone. But by then it has sent spores downwind to the next wet crest, and there is a new cushion, and so the species survives by a relay of monuments, each one a few centuries long. The cushion plant on the fallen towers does the same, only slower, because it starts with soil. You can find whole slopes of what look like carved rosettes, perfect, each one a plant that finished.

The snails in the tunnels, likewise. Each one is a grain of rice grazing pale moss for a year or two and then a grain of rice that has stopped, and the floor of the tunnel is, if you look, thick with them, a gravel of finished snails cemented into the filigree. The living ones walk over the shells of the dead ones and are not troubled, because a snail does not know what a stone is.

---

The first kind — the movers — are fewer, and they are all, if you look at them long enough, a little frantic.

The flies never land for long. They breed in the mats, in the warm film at the edge of the pools, and the larvae are the only things here that eat the masons. The adults live for days. They rise and settle and rise. If one is injured and stays down it is, by the following week, a small dark inclusion in a white crust.

The spiders have solved it differently: they build fast and abandon. A web across a scallop lasts one night of fog before it is too heavy with drops to hold, and by the second night the drops have begun to set, and the spider has moved on and spun elsewhere, and the web it left is becoming a fossil of a web. In the older districts you can find a wall where every scallop holds a small white lace, hundreds of them, each one a night's work by an animal that lived a year. The spider is the most prolific architect on the plateau after the mats, and never intends any of it.

And then the bird.

The bird is the exemption. Understand that: it is the one creature here to which the water does not stick. Its feathers are oiled and dense and packed so that it can walk under the surface of a pool in a coat of air, and when it comes up the water falls off it whole, and the lime goes with the water. The stone takes its droppings, its nest, its dead — but never, as long as it moves, the bird itself. It bobs. It is always bobbing. It walks the rims, it drops into the pools, it walks the floors of them upside down under the lime-heavy water and eats the larvae of the flies that eat the mats that build the walls, and it comes out dry and is gone into the fog, and in the whole vast slow city it is the only thing that is *fast the right way*, which is not the fly's desperate speed but something more like discipline.

It lives alone, and you never see two, because a pair could not feed in one district; the flies are too thin. But it must meet another somewhere, because there are chicks in the tower rooms in early summer — you can hear them, a thin hiss from inside the stone. So somewhere out in the fog, once a year, two of them find each other on a rim, and part, and the plateau does not record the meeting because nothing about it stood still long enough.

The falcon is not native. It cannot be. There is one, and there is only ever one, and it does not breed here; the territory would have to be the whole plateau and there is not enough prey in it for two adults and a nest. What happens, almost certainly, is that a young bird from the country below the escarpment comes up on the afternoon wind, finds the towers, finds the bird, and stays — because a falcon that has found a perch above an infinite hunting ground does not leave it — and lives up here for as long as a falcon lives and then dies, and for some years there is no falcon, and then another comes. The plateau receives falcons the way it receives rain. It does not make them.

---

In the deep districts, in the tunnels, there is a third population that belongs to neither kind, because it has never had to choose. In the ankle-deep water so clear you cannot see it, if you crouch and put your face near the surface and wait until your own ripples die, you begin to see the movement. Small pale things, jointed, translucent, the size of a fingernail paring. They swim in short sideways bursts and settle on the moss. They are the color of the water because they have never had a reason to be any other color. Their eyes are there but do not work. They eat the pale moss and the film on the walls and each other, and they have been in the tunnels since the roofs closed, which may be longer than anything else here has been anywhere.

They do not know about the fog. They have never been rained on. The lime does not take them because they are too light to sit still in the water — the current in the tunnels is nearly nothing, but nearly nothing is enough to keep something that weighs nothing drifting. When the straws overhead let go their drops all at once and the sound goes through the tunnel like thrown sand, the pale things do not react. That is the whole of their weather.

---

Winter tells you what the plateau actually is. The air, which is never warm, goes below freezing for months, and the pools skin over — thin, because the water beneath is always arriving from below at a temperature a few degrees above the air — and the dripping stops, and the fog stops, and the city stands still under a sky that hurts to look at. Everything that lives contracts toward the towers. The springs there are warmest, the water comes up fastest, the pools at their bases stay open when everything else is glass. The mats there stay in color; everywhere else they go grey and wait. The flies breed only there. The bird works those pools and no others. The snow, when it comes, does not stay on the towers.

So for four months the entire living population of a district is gathered in a ring a few meters wide around a fluted white column, in the steam, like people around a fire — and the columns are the only things making heat in a country of stone. In spring the ice goes and the rings widen and the city is inhabited again, and the ring's edge, where the ice met the warm water all winter, has left a step in the stone that will still be readable in a thousand years.

---

Now: people. No one lives here. That is true and it should be said plainly. There is nothing to eat; the water is too hard to drink for long; the cold is patient. The plateau is unpeopled.

But it is not unvisited, and the water treats a visitor the way it treats a spider's web. Someone's footprints in soft rim sludge fill with water in an afternoon and are white by the next year and are footprints in stone, a line of them going off across a flat and stopping. A cairn stacked to mark a way is, in three hundred years, a tower — a squat one, oddly regular, that travelers from another century point to as evidence of ancient builders. A walking stick leaned against a wall while its owner rested is a column now, with a knob at the top where the hand was. A body — and there have been bodies; not everyone who goes up comes down — is a long low mound on a terrace, a little taller than the wall around it, smooth, with nothing to say what it was. The plateau is full of human marks and every one of them has been made indistinguishable from the city, and this is why the accounts do not join: each traveler sees the last traveler's traces as architecture, describes them, and is right.

There is one more kind, and it is not quite a resident and not quite a visitor. Call them the returners. People who have been up once and come down and cannot stop coming back. You meet them at the treeline sometimes, going up as you are coming down, and they are thin and quiet and have a way of standing with their weight slightly off one foot, as if ready to move on. They stay up for a season, or two, walking, never camping in the same place twice — they have understood the rule — and they come down and eat and sleep in a bed and cannot bear it and go back up. They are the plateau's version of the falcon: something from below that found the place and could not leave it, and that the place did not ask for and does not need and will, eventually, take. They do not build anything. They do not leave marks on purpose. They know what marks become.

Ask one of them what lives up there and they will look at you as though you had asked what the sea is full of. Then they will tell you about the bird.

---

So the answer is a list, and the list is short, and every item on it is either fleeing the stone or being folded into it: the masons in their colored bands, drawing the city with their own bodies; the moss and the rosettes and the snails, consenting; the flies and spiders, running; the pale blind swimmers in the closed streets, exempt by weightlessness; the falcon, received; the wanderers, returning; and the bird — walking the rim in the fog, bobbing, dropping under, coming up dry — the one thing on the whole enormous wet pale working surface that the water has never once been able to keep.

Nothing here has a name for the place. Nothing here knows it is a city. The only creature that has ever stood at the edge and understood what it was looking at is the kind that comes up from the trees and goes back down again, and that kind does not live here, and never will, and keeps climbing back to look.
