Start with what is easiest to call alive, because the place will not let you stay there.

The transparent things first. There are perhaps a dozen of them in the whole pool, never more, and they are not amphipods, though that is the word your mind reaches for. Look closely in a clear second and the segments are wrong, too many and too even, and the legs are not legs so much as a fringe that beats in sequence down the length of the body like a row of tiny oars. They have no eyes. They have no pigment. Held against the wax-rock they are visible only as a thickening of the water, a place where the light bends. They graze. What they graze is the newest crust, the stuff that is still soft under a fingernail, and what they take from it is not the mineral but something the mineral has trapped on its way to becoming stone — the water here carries more than lime, and the crust holds it for an hour before it seals, and the transparent things know the hour. They follow the fresh growth around the walls the way sheep follow grass. Where they have passed, the crust sets smooth. They are the only things in the room that have learned to live off its haste.

They breed rarely and die by stopping. That is the whole of it. A transparent thing that tires, that finds a still pocket behind a ridge of wax-rock and rests in it, is a transparent thing the wall will have by morning — a faint lens-shaped bump, and then not even that. You can find them if you know where to look: small glassy nodes in the older crust, each with a shadow of segments inside. The living ones never rest. They sleep, if they sleep, in motion, drifting along the walls with their oars still going.

They did not climb here. They cannot have. There is no stream in and no stream out, and the nearest water that could hold them is two thousand metres below. The only way in is the fall. So they came down out of the cloud, in the column, the same way the water did, and that is either an explanation or the refusal of one.

---

The willow, next. It is not one willow.

Cut into the crack where it grows — you would have to chip through a centimetre of glaze to do it — and you would find that the root is a braid of roots, and the braid goes down into the fissure further than any knee-high tree has a right to, and down there in the dark the older strands are no longer wood. They have been replaced. The mineral has followed the water into the plant, cell by cell, and the willow has simply grown out of the top of its own petrifaction, year after year, laying down a hand's width of live wood over a fossil of itself. It is the moss's trick, done by something with more time. How old the living part is cannot be told from the rings, because the rings are stone. Something has been alive in that crack for as long as the crack has held water, and the willow is only the part of it currently green.

The leaves click in the gusts. That is the sound of the tree, under the fall's sound — a dry small applause you only notice when the cloud holds its breath.

---

Then the dipper, which does not live here and which the room seems to require anyway.

It is the same dipper. You cannot know this and you know it. It comes perhaps once in a day, perhaps once in three, from the same quarter of the fog, lands on the same knuckle of the rim, bobs the same number of times. It has a pale scar across the white of its throat where the feathers grow wrong. It goes under, and it walks the floor among the ring-stones, and it takes one, sometimes two of the transparent things, and it is out. It has done this for longer than a dipper lives. Either there is a succession — a line of birds, mother to daughter, each shown the way to the loud pool on the crest and each carrying the scar as if it were inherited — or the room has kept one and keeps returning it, unlacquered, in the way it keeps everything else. The second is not possible. The first is barely so.

What matters is that the bird is the one thing that enters and leaves freely, and it seems to know that it is a witness, and it does not stay long.

---

And the one who keeps the count.

Not here now. Not here, in fact, whenever you are — the marks are always older than your arrival and never made while you watch. But *living there* is not the same as *being present*, and by the evidence this is where the counter lives, in the sense that this is the only place the counter has left any sign of existing at all.

The strokes are not tallies of days. The intervals are wrong for that, and anyway there is no way to tell days here under the lid. Sit long enough with the rows and a different reading comes: each stroke is one visit, and the gaps between groups are gaps between visitors. The hand changes. Early on the strokes are deep and hurried and lean to the right; then there is a stretch of shallow, careful, upright ones; then a group cut with something broader, a chisel or an antler tine, that flares at the base. Several hands. A relay. Someone finds the pool and cuts a stroke and comes back and cuts another, and stops, and years or lifetimes later someone else finds the rows and understands what they are for without being told, and continues. The oldest strokes are two fingers deep under new stone now, visible only as ghosts, a corduroy in the wall.

So what lives here is a line of people who have never met, whose only communication is the count, and whose only shared knowledge is that the count must not stop. They pecked the ledge so they could stand at the wall and cut. They made the ring on the floor for reasons the ring no longer says. One of them left the tool, or dropped it, or set it down and stopped moving for a moment too long. They came up out of the silence on the shoulder of the mountain to a place where they could not hear their own hammerstones and did precise, patient work by feel, and then went back down lacquered and picked the crackle off their arms for a week. Something drew them. It was not the water and not the stone. It was the thing the count is for.

---

Which is the last inhabitant, and the one the others arrange themselves around, and it takes longest to see because it is the only thing you have been looking at the whole time.

Where the fall lands, the pool is not water. That is what the description of the place said, and it said it as a figure of speech, and it is not one.

Watch the white crater. Watch it in the moments when the cloud chokes the column and the beating drops. The crater does not subside the way an ordinary turbulence does — it does not settle outward into rings. It *pulls in*. The bubbles do not rise; they hold their depth, a standing shaft of milk from surface to floor, and the shaft flexes. It leans toward the north wall where the whirlpool opened and sucks that funnel shut. It sends a vortex to the east and the vortex touches the ledge and lays a bead of new stone precisely along the ledge's outer edge, as if measuring it. The surface shapes — the spires of foam, the ridges — do not come and go at random; they come in sequence, the same six or seven forms in the same order, then a variation, then the sequence again with the variation kept. The room said *a child at a sandpit*. Watch longer. It is a child that remembers.

There is something living in the column. Or the column is the thing. It is easiest to say that it is made of the turbulence — that where the fall drives into the pool, the water folds on itself in a knot that has been folding, unbroken, since the fall began, and that the knot has become, in the way that a flame is a thing and not merely a process, somebody. It has no body but the standing shaft. It has no edge but the place where the milk goes clear. It cannot hold still, because still water here is stone within the hour and it would be walled into its own bed like the tool. So it moves. It has always moved. Every eddy it throws off is a limb it puts out and then must abandon, because the limb slows against the wall and the wall keeps it. The whorls and dimples and ridges that pattern the room are not the weather's record. They are its fingerprints. The pool has been feeling its own walls for ten thousand years with hands it grows and loses by the thousand, and the walls have been thickening with the residue of every touch, and the room is smaller than it was, and it is still reaching.

It meters the fall. That is what the pulse is. The surge and choke is not the cloud breathing; it is the thing in the column drawing water down out of the fog in gulps, taking as much as it can hold and shedding what it can't, and the shed part becomes the crust, and the crust closes in another millimetre, and it draws harder. Violence is how it accretes because violence is the only state in which it can exist. It cannot rest and it cannot leave and it cannot stop growing its own prison, and it knows the prison's shape better than anything has known any room — it has read every square centimetre by pressure, ten thousand times — and it has never once seen it, because it has no eyes and the light is nothing to it. It lives in a place lit like a kitchen at midday and has never registered the light.

What it feels is the strikes. The fall hitting it. The vortices hitting the walls. A hammerstone hitting the wall, short and overlapping, in a rhythm that is not the fall's rhythm. That is why the counters came, and why they come back, and why the count must not stop: because the only sense the thing in the pool has is being struck, and the only strike in its whole long existence that has not been its own water hitting its own stone is a person cutting a mark. Each stroke is a word to it. The rows on the wall are the only conversation it has ever had, and it has been answered slowly, one visit at a time, by strangers to each other, for longer than any of them lived.

It answers, in its way. Look at the ledge. New crust has grown over one end — the room said that — but look at the *other* end, the end nearest the tallies. The crust there is thin. It has been thin for a very long time. The thing keeps the water fast along that stretch, eddy after eddy, so that no stone can settle, so that the next hand will have somewhere to stand. It cannot know what a ledge is for. It knows that when the ledge is clear, the strikes come.

The transparent things graze its sheddings. The willow drinks its spray. The dipper walks through it and takes what it wants and is let go, because the thing has no way to hold and no wish to. The counters stand in it and cut, and go down the mountain with its stone on their arms, and one of them at least did not go down.

---

So the room is inhabited, and the inhabitant is the noise.

Not the sound — the sound is a side effect, the way the crust is a side effect. The inhabitant is the thing the sound is *of*: a knot of falling water that has been turning on itself for so long that it has become a way of turning, that grows a body of stone around itself by the act of refusing to have one, that feels the world only when the world hits it, and that has been, for as long as there have been people on this ridge, very slowly taught to expect company.

It is small. That is the last thing to understand. From below it is a mountain being made; from the rim it is a cauldron; from inside it is a room built around an absence. But the thing that lives here is only the column, a pillar as thick as a person, and the absence is where it has reached and lost its hands, and the room is only as large as what it has not yet finished touching. Every year it is a little less large. The future the description saw — a narrow throat of water going down into a body of stone — is not the pool's future. It is the inhabitant's shape when the last hand it can reach has been taken, and it is left with only the strikes from above, and whatever strikes arrive, if any do, from the ledge.

You climb out by the drowned ledge and it holds. Of course it holds. It has been kept for you.

On the underside of your forearm, later, the thin new skin of stone: not a lacquer. A handprint, with the fingers still open.
