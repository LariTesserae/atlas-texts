What lives on the Shelf is anything that has learned to be slow. That is the whole of the requirement. The air will not support haste, the ground will not reward it, and the sky, which is the only cold, does not care either way. So the things that live here are the things that stopped hurrying, and after long enough it becomes hard to say which of them are people and which are animals and which are the country itself, still settling.

---

Begin with the people, because they are the ones who will speak to you, if anyone does.

They are broad. Not tall — the tall ones went back down or died young — but deep through the chest, barrel-built, with a way of standing that puts the weight low and the shoulders slightly forward, as though they were always about to lean against something. Which they usually are. You will rarely see a Shelf person standing free in open air for long. They put a hand on a pipe. They set their back to a furnace housing. They sit on the ground with their palms flat on the warm stone, and they do this without thinking, the way a person in the low country pulls a coat closed. The mountain is the only warmth, and they keep in contact with it.

Their skin has gone the color of the crust. Some of this is dust, which is everywhere, fine as flour, working into every crease; but some of it is not dust and does not wash off, and the oldest of them are the color of the domes, grey-brown with an undertone of ochre, so that a woman of eighty sitting against a wall at dusk can be hard to find until she moves. Their eyes are narrow from a lifetime of squinting at a hard small sun. Their hands are enormous, thick-palmed, scarred white where the yellow has burned them, and very careful. Nothing here is done fast with the hands. A dropped tool rings once, flat, and is gone in the dust, and you will spend an hour finding it.

They breathe slowly. This is the first thing you notice and the last thing you understand. A newcomer gasps; a Shelf person draws air in over four or five seconds and lets it out over four or five more, and the rhythm is so close to the beat of the engines that after some weeks in a town you cannot tell, sitting in a room with three of them and the works exhaling beyond the wall, which breaths are theirs. Children born here breathe this way from the first day. It is said they do not cry at birth — that they open their mouths and take one long pull and look around. That may be a thing people say. But you will not hear a baby cry on the Shelf, and you will hear very little else either.

---

They speak, when they speak, low and close, mouth near the ear, and for anything that needs to travel further than a few yards they use their hands. It is not a sign language exactly. It grew out of the mirror-code, the flashes that go tower to tower, and it keeps that shape: a small set of blunt signs for the things that matter, and a great deal of patience for everything else. *Heat. Rail. Herd. Wait. Wait longer.* A Shelf conversation can be carried on across a whole works floor by two people who never look directly at each other — a hand lifted from a valve wheel, a hand answering from a gantry, a pause while the flywheel completes its stroke, another hand. Children learn it before they learn to talk, and when they do talk it is in the same short pieces, with the same long spaces between.

There is no word in their speech for hurry, or rather there is, and it is the word they use for the people who come up from below with the drum-wagons — the haulers, who do not stay, who arrive gasping and blue-lipped and leave inside three days with the sealed drums lashed under tarpaulins and a look on their faces of having been somewhere they did not want to be. The Shelf people call them the *wet*. It is not an insult, or not only. The haulers come from where there is water, and they carry it in them, and you can smell it on them, faintly, a green smell in a country that has no green.

The Shelf people themselves get their water from the mountain's breath. This is the thing the towns are actually built around, though nobody would say so. The vents give off, with the heat and the sulphur, a little vapor, and the long cold pipes that run out from the calciners and back are not only for acid: they are for the condensate that beads inside them, drawn off drop by drop into iron cisterns that are the most guarded things in any town. Water is not sold on the Shelf. It is given, measured, one cup at a time, and to drink in front of another person without offering is the only thing here that will make someone raise their voice.

So their bodies are dry. Their lips crack. Their eyes are lined with a fine mineral salt they blink away in the mornings. They sweat very little and they do not weep, or if they do it is a private thing and the salt goes back into the dust with everything else.

---

They live long, and they live in the works.

There is no separate quarter of houses. A town is the ring around the vent, and the people sleep inside the ring, in rooms built against the outer wall of the furnace housings where the brick is warm through, in the long sheds between the crushers, in alcoves under the trestles where the pipes run overhead and tick as they cool. Each family has a place, and the place is defined not by walls but by heat — this stretch of housing, this section of warm floor — and has been theirs for as long as the kiln has been fired. They do not own the vent. Nobody owns the vent. The vent is understood to be the reason for everything, and the people its consequence, the way the grazers are the consequence of the crust.

Time is kept in firings, not years. A calciner is charged, roasted, and drawn on a cycle of about twenty days, and a life is measured by how many charges you have seen through: a child of a hundred firings, an elder of fourteen hundred. Nobody counts very exactly. The engines keep the finer time, the four-second beat that everyone's breath has fallen into, and above that there is only the loop of the rail, eleven days, the train coming through and going on, and the firings, and the slow wear of the domes, which nobody counts at all.

Marriages are made along the rail. A person from one town rides the loop to the next and stays a firing or two, and either comes back on the next passing or does not. The eleven days it takes the train to come round again are the courtship: long enough to sit against the same stretch of wall through eleven nights, breathing at the same rate, saying almost nothing. Children of such matches are said to belong to the rail, and the rail is said to look after them, and the trough the herds have worn beside the track is the only road any of them will ever know.

The work itself is not hard, or not in the way work is hard below. Nothing is lifted that the engines can lift. Nothing is hurried. A shift at the crusher is eight hours of watching a flywheel taller than a house turn over once every four seconds and listening for the beat to change, and it does not change, and you go and sleep against the warm brick and come back and it has not changed. The skill, and it is a real skill, lies in noticing very small things over very long intervals: a valve that has begun to seat a hair less cleanly than it did last firing; a stack whose plume has thickened by a shade nobody down the mountain would see; a place on a furnace wall where the brick has gone from ochre to something warmer, which means the vent beneath has shifted. The old ones are valued for this. They have seen more firings and they carry the comparisons in them. A woman of fourteen hundred firings will put her palm to a wall she has put her palm to every day of her life and say, low, to no one in particular, *warmer*, and the whole works will begin, unhurriedly, to move.

---

Between the towns there is another kind of person, and there are very few of them.

The lamp towers stand every few miles along the trestle, and each has a keeper, and the keepers live alone. Their tower is a column of iron perhaps sixty feet tall with a cabin at the top and the lamp above that, and the keeper's whole life is the vertical of it: down at the base where the pipe runs hot and the ground is warm enough to sleep on, up at the top where the lamp is and the cold begins. They spend their nights at the top, wrapped in everything they own, reading light. A flash from forty miles east, held and repeated, and the keeper turns to the mirror and passes it forty miles west. That is all. A message can take a night to cross the loop and the keepers do not know what most of it means. They carry the light. In the morning they climb down to the warm ground and sleep against the pipe with a hand on it, and the pipe ticks, and they say that after enough years you can read the ticking too.

The keepers are chosen from people who do not do well in the hush of the towns, which is to say people who were, by the Shelf's standards, loud. Out on the trestle their loudness has nowhere to go and it goes into the lamp. They are very thin. They are the only Shelf people who spend any time near the cold, and it marks them: a keeper's face is not the smooth ochre of a works face but something grained and drawn, and their eyes, from years of looking for one point of orange across black distance, have a fixity that is hard to meet. When the train passes they come down and stand at the foot of the tower, and the driver lifts a hand, and they lift a hand, and that is a keeper's society for eleven days.

Some of them stop coming down. The lamp keeps burning — it is fed from the pipe and needs no tending — and the light keeps passing, because the mirror is fixed and the flashes from the next tower fall on it and go on regardless. The train passes and nobody lifts a hand. After a few loops someone climbs up. What they find there is not spoken of, but the cabin is emptied and swept and a new keeper installed, and the new keeper is told only that the cold at the top is not to be sat in for long.

---

And there are the ones who have left the towns altogether and gone out with the herds.

The Shelf people call them by a sign, not a word — a flat hand moving slowly away from the body, palm down, the sign for *herd* with the fingers together instead of spread. Maybe one in a town has gone this way in living memory. They walk out one morning and do not return, and if you are on the plain a season later you may see one: a broad shape among the broad shapes of a grazer band, moving at the grazers' pace, which is the pace of the crust being eaten, perhaps a mile a day. They sleep against the animals' flanks. The shells are warm through from the ground and the animals do not object; they do not seem to notice, any more than they notice a calciner stack, and the walker lies among them as one more warm thing arranging itself between the heat and the sky.

What they eat is not clear. Something in the yellow, perhaps; something the grazers open and leave. What they drink is less clear still. They do not come to the towns for water and the towns do not offer it, and yet they go on for years, growing thinner and darker and slower, until it is very hard indeed to tell them from the animals they walk with. The herds move in loose spirals across the plain that take a decade to close, and the walkers move with them, and every so often a spiral brings a band past a town it has not passed in ten years, and someone in the works, looking out over the tailings at dusk, sees a face they knew among the heavy plated backs, and lifts a hand, and the face does not lift one back, and the band moves on.

---

The animals.

The grazers you have seen. What is harder to see, because it takes so long, is that they are not many things but one thing at different ages. The young are small, quick by Shelf standards, their shells still pale and soft enough to dent, and they run — actually run — along the rail after the train, cracking the fresh faces the wheels have broken, and they are the only thing on the Shelf that ever moves faster than a walk. As they age they broaden and darken and slow. The shell thickens and takes on the weathered brass finish and then, past a certain age, begins to go dull and grey-brown like everything else. The old ones hardly move. They lie against the tailings heaps and the furnace housings with their heads down on the ground, and crack the crust in front of them, and shift a body-length forward once a day to crack the next, and eventually do not shift. Then you cannot tell them from the heaps. Then, the old workers say, they are heaps. Then, given forty years and the warmth coming up through them, the heap begins to move.

Nobody claims to have seen the moment. Nobody has been standing in one place for forty years. But the oldest heaps around the oldest towns have the shape — the long low back, the heavy fore-end where the head would be — and there is a smoothness to their outer surface, a satin, that fresh slag does not have. And the grazers lie against them, and the walkers lie against the grazers, and the workers step over all of it in the morning on their way to the crushers, and nobody drives anything off.

The long-necked things are called standers, when they are called anything. You see them singly, on the domes, at the last hour of the day: a body like a grazer's but lifted on legs twice as long, and from it a neck that goes up and up and ends in a small head with no visible mouth, turned toward the sun. They do not eat that anyone has seen. They stand. When the sun goes down behind the close curved edge of the Shelf they fold — the neck comes down in slow segments, the legs bend — and they walk away over the domes in the dark toward wherever they spend the night, and in the morning they are on a different dome, standing, faced east, waiting. Some of the Shelf people think they drink the light. Some think they are keepers of a kind, reading something in the sun that nobody else can read and passing it on, and that the flat hand raised to a passing train and the neck raised to a setting sun are the same gesture in two bodies.

The tickers in the trestles nobody has seen at all. They live in the hollow iron, in the dark warm inside of the pipe supports, and they tick — a dry irregular sound, faster than the engines, that runs along a trestle for miles and then stops and then starts again somewhere else. Children are told they are the trestle thinking. The keepers say they are what the cooling pipe sounds like when it has been a pipe for long enough to want something. A hauler once cut a trestle leg open to see and found it full of a fine black glass in thin plates, layered like the leaves of a book, and the plates flexed in the heat and clicked against one another, and there was nothing else, and the ticking went on further down the line as though nothing had happened, and the hauler went back down the mountain and did not come up again.

---

The flyers are the ones who go to the ceiling.

You will see them all day over the vents, so high they are only a curve of shadow against the indigo, and you will see them at night in the basins, spread flat, the crust visible through their wings. But a flyer that has lived long enough — and they live longer than the people, longer than the grazers, longer perhaps than anything but the heaps — begins one day to circle higher than the others. It is not carried up; the lift over the vents stops well short of the ceiling, at the place where the smoke flattens into its grey table. It works. You can see it working, the huge wings tilting and tilting to catch some thread of warmth the others have not found, the circles narrowing, the shadow shrinking. It takes days. The people below do not point, because pointing is a kind of shouting, but the works falls a little quieter and faces turn up more often than usual, and someone will lift a hand, flat, palm up, the sign for *high*, and hold it.

Then it reaches the table of smoke and goes into it and there is nothing to see. The other flyers, for a night or two, do not settle in the basins but hang in the dark over the vents, all of them, circling at the same height, not feeding. Then they come down and spread themselves flat and in the morning they wait for the ground to lift them, and the one that went up does not come down, and nobody says anything about where it has gone, because nobody has been above the ceiling and come back with anything to say.

Sometimes, in a season when the high haze turns the sun to brass, a wing comes down. A single membrane, a few yards of it, so fine it drifts rather than falls, settling onto the domes miles from any town. It is always cold. It is the only cold thing that ever comes to rest on the Shelf, and it stays cold for a long time, lying on the warm stone, the sun showing through it, until a grazer band comes by and one of the young ones puts its bony plate to the edge and finds there is nothing under it to eat and moves on.

---

The dead go into the heaps. That is the last thing to say about what lives here, and it is said quietly, and not often.

Nobody is buried on the Shelf. The ground is not for opening; only the crust is, and only for what is under it. When a person has seen their last firing — and they know, the same way they know a wall is warmer, by a long comparison nobody else can make — they go out at dusk to the oldest tailings and find a place on the warm flank and lie down against it, back to the slag, face to the sky, in the position a Shelf person always takes to sleep. Someone may go with them as far as the edge of the works. No one goes further. In the morning there is a shape on the heap that was not there before, and the grazers have moved in around it, and the dust is already on it, and within a few firings you cannot say which of the shapes along the flank were people and which were animals and which the heap itself.

Whether they become the heap, and whether the heap becomes a grazer, and whether that is what the old workers mean when they say the tailings move, you are not required to believe. But you will notice that the people speak of the very old grazers — the ones that have stopped shifting, the ones lying against the furnaces with their heads on the ground — with the same low deference they use for the oldest workers. That children are told not to climb on them. That when one is finally found in the morning to have gone entirely still and cold at its upper surface, though the underside is still warm from the stone, the works stops for one stroke of the flywheel, one four-second beat, and then goes on.

And you will notice, if you stay long enough, that the walkers who go out with the herds are always people who have lost someone to the heaps. That they are following something. That the spiral the herds move in across the plain is the same spiral, roughly, that a heap would trace if it woke and rose and went looking, at a mile a day, for the rest of what it had been.

---

So that is what lives on the Shelf: anything that has arranged itself between the warm ground and the cold sky and stopped hurrying. The broad quiet people with their hands on the brick. The thin keepers at the tops of their towers, holding the light. The walkers going out to become something slower. The grazers, and the heaps the grazers become, and the people the heaps have taken. The standers drinking the sun. The tickers in the iron. The flyers spread on the crust, waiting for the day's first lift, and the one among them that will not wait but go up instead, into the place nobody comes back from.

None of them are in a hurry to be told apart. The engines breathe. The crust cracks, very faintly, in every direction at once. Out on the plain something that was a person, or a heap, or an animal, or all three in order, shifts a quarter inch toward the horizon, which is close, and curved, and going nowhere.
