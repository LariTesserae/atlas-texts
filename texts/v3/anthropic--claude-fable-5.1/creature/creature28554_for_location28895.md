Start on the shadow side, since that is where the water is.

The first residents are on the north faces, and they are not hiding; they are simply the color of where they are. The black lichen is a crust. It has no edge you can find with a fingernail, and it does not come off, and if you wet it it goes from matte to glossy and back again in the time it takes the stone beneath it to dry, which is to say it does exactly what the wall does, only faster. This is the thing to understand about it. It is not growing on the wall. It is doing what the wall does — taking water out of the fog and holding it against itself — at a scale you can watch. The wall takes a whole afternoon to darken. The lichen darkens as you breathe on it.

There are three kinds, and they keep to their faces. The black one owns the north. The rust-orange one takes the seams and the drip-lines, wherever water runs rather than sits, and it grows in fine radiating rings from a center you cannot find, so that a channel-lip looks lightly scorched. The grey-green one is the oldest, and the strangest, because it is not always on the face it should be on. On some south-facing stones — warm, dry, blond — there is a pale patch of it on the sunward side, in a place it could never have grown, dormant or dead, the color of chalk. That patch grew when the stone lay elsewhere, on the mountain, with that face turned into the weather. Then hands turned the stone over and rubbed it into a course, and the lichen went where the stone went, and has spent the whole life of the town facing the wrong way. You can read the walls like this if you want to. Every stone remembers which way it used to face. The lichen is the town's only record of the mountain before the town, and it keeps that record by having stopped.

---

Inside, the moss.

Each room has one, in the corner where the ceiling slab meets two walls and the condensation gathers to a point and lets go. The moss is under the point. It is the size of a coin because that is the size of the wet. Where the drip is heavier the coin would be larger; it is not, so the drip is not. The moss is not a plant that happens to be in the room. It is the room's measurement of itself — how much water this corner receives, how much life that water will carry, expressed as a disc of green about the size of a thumbnail, and expressed once, and left.

Put your finger on it and it is cold and springs back. It has no ambition toward the next stone. There is nothing between it and the next stone that it could live on.

---

In the joints, the lizards, and they are almost the joints.

There are perhaps a dozen in the whole town; you will never see two together and will never be sure the one you see is not the one you saw. They are grey with a dark line down the back, and the dark line is exactly the width of a mortarless seam, so that a lizard lying in a seam is the seam, with the grey of its flanks disappearing into the grey of the stones on either side. They come out for the hour. There is one hour, on the south faces, in the early afternoon, when the stone is warm enough to be worth lying on, and in that hour they are on the sills and the lintels and the parapet-caps, flattened, shoulders spread, taking the heat in through their bellies the way the walls take the water in through their faces. They are the only things in the town that use the warmth. Everything else lives off the cold.

When the cloud comes up the west slope they are already gone. You do not see them go. That is the property the town has given them: they move only in the gaps of your attention, so that a lizard is always either here or there and never between. They eat what comes up in the fog — small soft things, midges, whatever the wet air carries over the shoulder of the ridge — and they drink from the stone, pressing the side of the jaw to a slick face and taking it off as a film. They have no need of the cistern. They were never told where the water was. They know it is under things.

---

The chough is the one who leaves.

It does not live in the town. It lives below, in the cloud, on some ledge of the western cliff you will never see, and the town is its parapet — the flat top of its world, a place to stand and look at nothing for a while before dropping back into weather. It comes up at the same hour and stands in the same place and it is the loudest thing that ever happens here, and it does not make its noise here. It calls from below. It saves its voice for the cloud, where there is room for it. Up here it walks, red-legged, unhurried, along a wall-cap that someone rubbed flat with another stone before there were choughs in that particular family, and it treats the wall as a rock, which is what it is, and then it steps off.

Some years it must be a different bird. The town would not know. The parapet is worn very slightly paler in one place, the width of two red feet.

---

Then the ones that lie down.

The cushion plant on the high dry terrace is a resident by virtue of being nearly stone. It has made itself a dome hard enough to walk on and it lives at the center of the dome, where it is damp, and it has pushed everything dead to the outside as armor, and its flowers are so small and so brief that the question of whether it is flowering is not a question the plant would understand. It holds them out. Whether they are open is between them and the fog.

The juniper — three, or one — has taken the other route and lies flat. It has agreed to be horizontal. Its bark hangs off it in strips and the strips do not fall; they hang. The wind that goes over the top of the town goes over the top of the juniper too, and the juniper has arranged itself so that nothing about it catches. Every part of it that ever reached up has been folded down and kept. It does not look like a tree that is dying. It looks like a tree that has finished deciding.

---

So far the town is inhabited the way any high stone place is inhabited: by what can live at the size the water allows, and in the color of where it is, and without any motion that leaves a trace.

What is not a resident is the water. Notice that. The water arrives and the water leaves. It comes up the slope as cloud at its hour, and it touches the walls, and the walls take it, and it runs where the grooves take it, and it fills the cistern to the line, and the excess goes over the lip of the lowest terrace and down into the scree and is gone. It is the only thing that moves through. Everything that lives here lives by standing very still in the path of the one thing that moves, and taking a little as it passes — the lichen off the face, the moss under the drip, the lizard off the sill, the cistern out of the channel. The town is a device for taking water out of passing air. What lives in the town lives inside the device, at the points where the device is wet.

That leaves the marks.

---

The wedge of slate in the door. Look at what it does: it holds the door open a hand's width, and a hand's width of fog gets into the room each afternoon and goes to the corner and lets go on the moss. Shut the door and the corner would dry in a season and the moss would go brown and then grey and then nothing. So the wedge is not there for the person who set it. The person who set it does not need a door open to get in. The wedge is there for the coin of green in the corner. It is a very small act of keeping a very small thing at the size it has always been.

The flat stone across the channel, at the third turning, making a pool. The pool is where the trickle stops for a moment before it goes on. In it, the juniper scale turns and does not travel. Someone made a place in the town where the one moving thing holds still. Not to drink — the cistern is below, and clear, and always at the line. To look at, then. To see the water stop. In a town that is finished, where nothing is becoming, the water was the one thing that kept going, and someone laid a stone across it so that for the length of a pool it would be like everything else.

The pebbles on the sill. River pebbles, round, from two days down, sorted by size. This is the one thing in the town that comes from elsewhere. Everything else was already here — the stone was the mountain, the water was the cloud, the lichen was on the stone. The pebbles were carried. Someone went down through the scree and the cloud into the valley where the water moves fast enough to wear things smooth, and picked up stones that had been shaped by motion, and brought them up to a place where motion has stopped, and laid them out. And sorted them by size, which is not a sort that tells you when they came. If there is a count of the years on that windowsill, it has been arranged so that it cannot be read as a count. It has been arranged the way the town arranges everything: by fit.

---

By now something has emerged, and it is not the lichen and not the chough, though it is like them.

It is a person. One. Which here is the same as several, since two of them would not speak either. Not old, because the town does not run in that direction, and not young; the age of someone who has been at a fixed size for a long time, the way the moss has. Cloth soft with damp, the color the stone is on the north side. Hands with the particular finish of hands that have rubbed stone against stone — not calloused, since there is nothing left to build, but smooth in the palm, worn to fit the tools that were already worn to fit the previous hands. Feet that know which slab on which stair breathes in a west wind, and step around it, out of a kind of courtesy.

They move at evening, when the skin of water on the walls begins to run, and they move through the town the way the water does: down the channels of the stairs, under the lintels, into the courtyards you cannot see into, and they touch what needs touching, which is almost nothing. A wedge that has slipped. A slab that has drifted a finger's width and needs to be pressed back into its overlap. The cistern lid, lifted off and set down so the water can be seen to be at the line, and then not replaced, because there is no dust here to keep out and nothing that would fall in. That is the work. It takes the time it takes and the time is the time between two drips.

They do not speak because speaking would be a loose thing, and in the whole town there is nothing loose to rattle. They do not keep the place in the sense of preventing its decline, because it does not decline. They do not survive here in the sense of holding out against something; the fog is not against them. They are what the town does with its leftover time. The builders had time and nothing else and put it into the joints, and when the joints were done the time kept arriving, one afternoon of cloud after another, and it had to go somewhere, and it goes into a person who sorts round stones on a sill and lays a flat stone across a trickle to watch a leaf turn.

Every so often — the interval is not one you would notice — they go down. Two days through cloud to where the water runs loud and cuts things. They stand in it. They pick up something the river has been working on for longer than the town has been standing, and they bring it back, and it does not go anywhere. It goes on the sill.

---

Go up to the top terrace again and sit.

If you stayed, and you could, you would meet them the way you meet everything here: by not seeing the moment. You would be on the main stair in the evening light, the even grey light of a room with high windows, and the stair is a body and a half wide, and you would become aware that the extra half was occupied. Someone in a doorway, stepped back to let you by, damp cloth, the face turned a little toward the wall the way the lizard turns toward the seam — not hiding, only being the color of where they are. You would pass. You would not hear them. Behind you the drip would land in its channel, and a long time later another, and by the time you thought to look back the doorway would be a doorway.

That is who lives here. Not a ghost, because nothing was left undone for a ghost to attend to. Not the last of anyone, because the town does not count. Something the town made, after it had made everything else, out of the only material it had left over — the slow arriving afternoons — and fitted, by hand and eye, into the half a body's width between the finished stones and the running water, where it sits, and holds, and has not moved.
