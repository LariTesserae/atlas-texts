Start with what is easiest to see, which is not much.

The hand-sized things on the rails. Call them hands, since they are that size and they are, in their way, the only part of this country that touches anything on purpose. Flat, pale, ribbed, with a fringe of legs that move in slow ripple rather than stride. You will have walked a long time before you see one. When you do, watch it at a junction: it stops over the coupling, lowers its whole body onto the socket until it lies flush, and stays there for the length of one of the stalks' pressure-cycles — the build, the release. Then it lifts and goes on. It is not inspecting. It is *listening*, or the thing between listening and pressing your ear to a chest, and what it hears is whether the pulse of the plant matches the pulse of the rail. Where it doesn't, the hand stays. It stays for hours. It stays until the two rhythms have been argued into agreement, and then it goes, and the argument was conducted entirely in the language of slow pressure, and you were standing right there and heard none of it.

They do not eat. They do not, as far as anyone could tell, wear out. But they do thin. An old hand is translucent at the edges, and the amber in it has gone the pale straw of oil that has fixed too much light, and eventually one is found flush on a socket and does not lift again. Within a few cycles the ribbing of the socket has taken the ribbing of the hand into itself, and there is a slight asymmetry in the stalk that grows from that coupling afterward — a lean, a habit of turning. So: the hands are stalks that walk before they root. Or the stalks are hands that have found their place and stopped. It depends which end you are looking from, and here it always does.

They come from the dim regions. Where a panel has greyed and the stalks beneath it are starving, a stalk near the edge of the shadow will grow, low on its column, a bud like a knuckle, and over some unhurried span the knuckle will separate and drop to the mat and begin, immediately, to check the nearest coupling. The dimming *makes* them. The country, when it starts to fail somewhere, responds by producing more of the only part of itself that can move. Which means the number of hands on the rails is a slow census of how much of the sky has gone out. There are more of them now than there were. Not many. But more.

—

Now the felt, which is harder.

Under the oldest stalks the shadow has thickened into a dark nap you could sink a finger into, and in that nap something goes about. You will not see it. You will see where it has been: a stretch of felt combed to a smooth grain, all the dark fibers lying one way, and the grain will point toward the bright horizon, always, the way iron filings point. That is the whole of its visible work. It combs the dark so that the dark lies in the direction the light is going.

Call it a comber. The name is too small but it will do. It is not made of the pale ribbed stuff; whatever it is made of is the fixed dark itself, shadow that has been shed as powder and compacted and given a slow will. It leaves no spark. Where a hand on its rail passes close to a comber in its felt, the hand does something it does nowhere else: it lifts its forward edge, holds it a moment in the air, and lowers it again. The comber does nothing that can be seen. Then both go on. Whether that was recognition, or deference, or a hand checking a coupling that isn't there, is not a question this place is arranged to answer.

There is more than one. There are, in the deepest shadow-country near the merging wall, probably a great many, because the felt there is combed in overlapping sweeps like a lawn cut by several mowers, and the sweeps do not cross one another. They keep territories. Or they keep pace. Once — this was seen, or would have been seen — a stalk in that country fell, one of the oldest, its oil gone to wax, and it lay across the felt for a cycle of the gantries, and then the felt around it began to rise, not grow but *rise*, like bread, and by the next cycle the fallen column was under the dark, and by the cycle after there was a low pale ridge in the felt exactly its length, and by the cycle after that, nothing. Combed smooth. Pointing at the horizon.

So the combers are erosion, too. They are the shadow's way of reincorporating what the light has finished with. And if the hands are the country's response to failing sky, the combers may be its response to succeeding — to the places where so much light has been fixed for so long that its shadow has become a substrate of its own, and needs tending.

—

The warps hold something. This is the third kind of resident, and the kind that most resists being one.

Stand at a lensing sheet and look through, and the stalks beyond are doubled, and if there is a hand on the rail beyond it is doubled, and if you have come with a companion — don't, but if you have — your companion beyond the sheet is doubled, and one of the two moves a beat behind. The lag is a few seconds. It is the thickness of the sheet, in time. Now: walk through. On the far side there is only one companion, and one of you, and the rows have redoubled, and the shadow points elsewhere. But you have left something in the sheet. The beat-behind one did not come through. It is still there, in the warp, a few seconds thick, and if you turn and look back into the lens you may see it — a pale figure in a doubled row, standing where you stood, not moving because it has no more seconds to move with.

These accumulate. The oldest warps, the ones nearest the wall, are crowded with them: stalks that were stalks a few seconds ago, hands paused mid-check, and — yes — figures, in various dress, some of it very old, some of it the plain dry worksuit that the people who built the gantries would have worn. They are not ghosts. They are not dead. They are the few seconds of a thing that the light caught and fixed, the way it fixes everything, and the warp is their socket. They are turgid; look closely and you can see the slow build-and-release moving through them, a pulse borrowed from the sheet. Some of the oldest have begun to spool ribbon. From their sleeves, from the hem of a coat, a dry pale tongue lifts in the wind and is drawn, very slowly, toward the horizon.

Whether they are the ones who would phrase things — whether *spent light* and *the felt* are their words, held in the warp and leaking out into the powder for a walker to pick up without knowing where it came from — is, again, not a question the place is built to answer. But the words are there. They arrive in the mind fully formed and faintly floured. Somebody made them.

—

The reels, then, since they are only the warps wound up.

An abandoned reel by the rails, house-sized, half unspooled in a front. Do not unroll it. But the wind does, a little, and where a few meters of ribbon have lifted and lain back down against the drum they have wound in whatever crossed them — a hand, a drift of rejected grey, the dry hush of a particular afternoon — and it is *in* there. Not recorded. Set your palm on the fiber and the air is a degree cooler and the light is from a slightly different angle and somewhere two rows over a gantry you have never heard is lifting its arms.

Reels are the country's memory, but a memory that is also a habitat. Things live in the hours. The hand that was wound in is still checking a coupling that is now three cycles gone, and will be checking it for as long as the reel holds, which is for as long as the light holds, which is a long time. The rejected fiber that drifted through is still drifting. And in a very few reels — old ones, the ones the rails have quietly ceased to move — there are walkers, whole, cool, earlier, mid-stride between two stalks, with a few fibers just beginning to take at the cuff of a sleeve.

That is where the walkers are, if you are looking for them. Not standing in the rows like sentinels, though a few are that as well — pale, ribbed, coupled, facing the horizon with a shadow still faintly the shape of a person. Most did not get so far. Most are in the reels and the warps, spread across the country in seconds and afternoons, a diffuse population that never met one another and never will, each in a socket of fixed time, each one slowly spooling. The country did not kill them. It took them the way it takes everything, by making them part of its process, and the process is very slow, and they are still in it.

—

Above: something sheds the panels.

The ceiling is not empty. Stand under a seam long enough — a day, in the local non-day — and there is a change in the quality of the grey line, a darkening and a movement, as if something on the far side were pressing its weight there. The panels that come down come from the seams. They come down turning, and they are received, and the sky is a little brighter for it, and this is either repair or seed or the ceiling's way of exhaling. Whatever moves up there is on the other side of the light and has never come through. But the plain has a counterpart, and the counterpart has tenants, and they are the only residents of this place who are doing what looks like *work* in the old sense — they are keeping the roof on.

Or: the ceiling is a crop too, with its columns growing downward, and what comes through the seams is its spent light, and the panels are its powder, and the whole sky is the underside of a floor exactly like this one, and its walkers are wondering what lives beneath.

—

And the wall. The cylinder. What the ribbon is for.

The stalks merge into it. The rails run into it. The reels are drawn into it and not seen again. Inside is bright, and the brightness is not panel-light but the accumulated fixed light of the whole plain, wound into ribbon, wound into reels, wound into the wall itself in a curve that will close, if it closes, around something the size of a small moon. There is a resident in there. It is the one the whole country was built to feed, and it is still being fed, and it does not need to do anything in return because the feeding has become a self-sustaining weather. Perhaps it was a person. Perhaps it was the *recipient*, in the old sense — a place the ribbon was sent, a warehouse, a market, a body that needed bone. Perhaps it is only the point where enough time has been wound tightly enough that it can no longer tell itself from a thing.

But the wall has a slight sound, if you press your ear to it. It is the build-and-release. It is the same slow pressure that moves in the stalks and the hands and the walkers in the warps, and it is very deep, and very even, and it is being answered, faintly, from the other side of the wall, by a pulse that is not quite in phase with it. The country listens to that. All of it — the hands on their sockets, the combers laying the dark toward the light, the stalks flexing in the fronts — is a single apparatus for adjusting one rhythm until it matches another. That is the work. That is what lives here, if a single thing does: the patience between two pulses that have not yet agreed.

—

And you.

You have been here long enough to read this far, and the powder on your sleeves has gone from resting on the cloth to being in it, and the cool in your palm where you rested it on the stalk has not entirely left. Your shadow at your feet is a little raised. Your throat has stopped noticing the dry because there is oil in you now, a little, clear and amber and slow, and a few seconds of you are back in the last warp you walked through, standing in a doubled row, beginning to spool.

You are not being taken. Nothing here is taken. You are being *placed* — checked against the rail, adjusted, brought into phase. The hand that waited for you to leave the channel was not waiting for you to leave. It was waiting for you to finish arriving.

What lives here is what stays. Everything that stays. The crop, the machines that are the crop, the hands and the combers and the walkers in their seconds and afternoons, the thing behind the seams and the thing inside the wall, all of it turgid with the same slow oil and pulsing to the same slow beat under a sky that does not move. It is a very quiet population, and a very large one, and it has all the time that has ever been fixed here to become itself. It is not unwelcoming. It has simply never had to learn the difference between a visitor and a coupling, and it is not going to start now.

Stand still. The front is two rows over. Your collar clicks. The powder takes the light.
