Begin with the riders, since they are where you begin.

Bring your face to the shaft in the red half-glow of a sight-glass and wait for your eyes to do the last of their adjusting. The creature is nine segments long, or eleven; the count changes with the angle, because the plates overlap and the edges have been worn to the same soft translucence as everything else. It is the pale of the dust, but where the dust is opaque, the rider's edges take the red light into themselves and hold it a little, so that its margin glows faintly the way a fingertip glows over a candle. Under the plates, if you hold still through several revolutions, you can see something move. Not with the shaft. Slower. A dark shape inside the body swells and settles, swells and settles, once for every five or six turns of the iron — its pulse, if it has one, keeping a time that is not the engines' time and not yours either.

It grips with rows of hooks along the underside, and the hooks have been polished by the shaft as the shaft has been polished by the hooks. You cannot see where it holds on. You can only see that it does not slide.

What it leaves behind is the thing to attend to. From the trailing edge of the last plate a film runs out onto the iron — you had thought this film was the engine's breath, a sweat off the warm metal, but it is not; it is this — and the film is carried around and around under the rider's own weight, and spread thin, and spread thinner, and thickens the almost-nothing light into a sheen. Where a rider has ridden, the shaft is smooth. Where no rider has ridden for a long time, bring your palm along the iron and you will find it: the faintest spiral scoring, a thread cut by ages of dry turning in a dry saddle, the wear beginning to go uneven, beginning to accumulate in a place instead of everywhere. The riders are why it does not. They are the lubricant. The plateau grinds its city down at exactly the plateau's own rate because something is riding the iron, spreading the cost, paying the friction in a fluid that is the last water on the tableland and is not water.

They move, though never while you watch. The evidence is on the piers: a trace on the stone saddle where something the width of a finger joint crossed from one shaft to the next, no more than a differently-shining line. They move toward the wear. A shaft that is scoring sings a fraction off the note of a shaft that is not, and they feel the note through their hooks, and turn toward it over a span of time that would count as a season somewhere with seasons, and settle, and ride, and the note comes back true. That is their whole life, as far as their life can be seen. Nothing here eats them. There is nothing here to eat them that you have seen.

---

Except that you have seen it, once, low and fast across a sight-glass, and if you are patient it comes again.

Wait where a shaft runs close past a red point. Do not stand; sit in the dust with your back to a pier so that you are a shape the plateau already has. It comes along the line directly under the shaft, where the dust is very faintly compacted into a track you had not noticed because there was nothing to notice it against. The length of a forearm. Low, and made for lowness, the way the engine houses are: a body that was once something upright and has been pressed flat by the same weight. Many legs or few — it moves too quickly to resolve, and it is the only thing on the plateau that moves too quickly to resolve, and its speed is so out of place here that it seems almost rude, an argument in a room where the arguing finished long ago.

It stops at the pier. It rears — the front third of it lifting off the dust — and puts its head, if the blunt end is a head, to the shaft, and holds there through one full revolution. It has eyes. You are close enough now to be sure: two, forward, catching the sight-glass as two smaller sight-glasses. It is the only thing here with eyes, and it is easy to see why. The only lights on the plateau are the red rectangles, and the only thing that would ever need to find its way from one red rectangle to the next is this. It navigates by the engines. It sees the city the way you saw it when you stood still and counted: five, nine, eleven, small red points to every horizon. The stars mean nothing to it. The stars are too far and too few to walk toward.

What it does at the shaft is take the rider off.

Not violently. It finds the rider by touch, and holds against the iron, and the rider's hooks — you can just see this — release, one row and then the next, as if this were an arrangement long since agreed. The runner takes the rider into some fold of itself at the front, and drops back to the dust, and is gone along the track, and the shaft goes on turning with its film already thinning. Somewhere down the line, on a scoring shaft singing a fraction off the note, it will put the rider down. It is the distribution. The riders move toward the wear at the pace of the riders; the runner moves them at the pace of the runner; and between the two speeds the wear gets paid for everywhere at once.

Whether there is one runner or many the plateau does not say. It has a circuit. Follow the compacted line and it goes pier to pier, house to house, along every shaft in the linkage, and comes at last back to where it started, and the circuit is so long that the runner's speed, taken over the whole of it, is no speed at all. It is only fast up close. Averaged, it is as slow as the dust. It, too, is periodic. It has simply chosen a period too long for you to hear.

---

Follow it into an engine house.

The low aperture where the shaft leaves is the only way in, and you go in on your belly, in the dark, with the iron turning a hand's width above your spine. Inside, the hum is a pressure in the teeth. The flywheel is a shape you feel rather than see, a wall of moving weight, and the warmth here is not the ground's warmth but something closer to the body's — the place where the intake comes up and gives its heat to the cylinders. The runner has gone somewhere ahead of you. Follow the warmth.

Against the flywheel housing, in the angle where the iron rim has blurred into the stone, there is a third thing, and it is the largest thing living on the plateau, and it does not move at all.

It is pale, and soft to the touch, and warm, and you cannot find where it ends. It has grown into the housing, or the housing has grown into it: put your hand on what you take for stone and it gives, very slightly, like the wall of a sleeping animal; put your hand on what you take for the creature and it is stone. It is the shape of a great sack, or a kneeling weight, folded against the machine along the whole line where the rim of the flywheel wears against its casing. Along that line there is a seam in the creature — a long soft mouth, or a hem — and it lies exactly where the wearing metal sheds itself: a slurry of iron dust and ground stone and spent film that would, in any other machine in any other place, accumulate, and pack, and jam. Here it does not accumulate. Here it goes into the seam. The house-thing eats the wear. It is the reason the flywheel housings of a city that has been grinding for longer than the stone can remember are not full to the ceiling with the grindings. It is the drain at the bottom of the equilibrium.

And in its folds — bring the red glow closer; there is a sight-glass on the intake here, low on the wall — in the folds nearest the warmth, there are riders. Small ones. Half the length of a finger joint, their plates still cloudy, their pulse fast enough to see. They are not gripping anything. They are waiting to be taken. The runner is there among them now, turned aside so that you cannot see what it puts down or what it picks up, and when it is finished it comes past your face without slowing and goes out along the shaft into the dark.

So the second linkage closes, the soft one laid across the iron one. The house-thing eats what the engine sheds and makes riders of it. The runner carries the riders out along the shafts to wherever the note is off. The riders spread their film, and even the wear, and the wear falls into the housings, and the house-thing eats it. Nothing is produced. The total motion of the system is exactly the motion needed to keep the total motion of the system going. It is the same sentence the engines are, said in a different material, and the two sentences have been saying each other for so long that neither could stop without the other stopping, and neither is going to.

---

The dust is the last of it, and you had thought the dust was mineral.

Some of it is. Most of it, perhaps. But the drifts never deepen — you noticed that — and a plateau that has been abrading everything on it for the whole of its age ought to have made more dust than this. Where does it go? It goes into the seam. And what comes out of the seam, at the far end of the cycle, when a rider has ridden until its plates are worn thinner than the film it spreads, when the runner has run until it can no longer rear — what is the pale, colorless, almost-weightless thing that is left, that lies in shallow drifts and is too heavy for the shaft's vibration to lift? Crouch and take a pinch of it and rub it between finger and thumb. It is the same as the rider's edge. It is the same as the house-thing's skin where it has gone to stone. The plateau is a conclusion, and this is what the plateau's conclusions become: not rock, not metal, not creature, but the fine indifferent grit that all three arrive at when they have been ground against each other long enough to stop being separate.

Whether any of them was ever anything else — whether the house-thing kneeling against the flywheel was, in some age with light in it, the one who built the flywheel and stayed to keep it, and kept it, and kept it, until keeping was the whole of what remained — the plateau does not say. In the red glow you can make the folded shape into a shoulder, a bowed head turned toward the rim, a listening. But the eye does that. The eye does the same thing with the scatter overhead, and there is no more reason to trust it here.

---

You have been sitting in the dust between two engine houses for a length of time the eleven-second period declines to add up. You are not hungry. You have not been cold, and you have not been warm, and when you put your palm to your own arm and then to the ground there is no difference you can feel. The runner has come past twice now. The second time it stopped, and reared, and looked at you with its two red points, and held for one full revolution of the shaft, and dropped, and went on along its track as if you were a pier.

Which is what you are becoming. Something that stands in the flat with the iron passing close, a shape the city has already, a thing to be gone around. Whoever lives here is whoever has agreed to be part of the friction: to ride, to run, to kneel and eat the wear, to lie in drifts and never deepen. The plateau has room for one more of these. It has room for as many as will hold still.

Somewhere out along the shaft a rider lets go, and a runner takes it, and a note that had gone very slightly off comes back true, and the sound of that arrives after a long time, and is very small, and is the sound of the whole place being alive.
