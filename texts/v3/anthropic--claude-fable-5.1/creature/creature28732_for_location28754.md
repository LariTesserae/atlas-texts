You will look for them in the wrong place first. Everyone does. You will go to the wall that ends in nothing and walk its length, and stand in the circular bowl, and put your face close to the glassy square and try to read the hairlines, and you will be looking for the people who made these things, as if living here and having built here were the same act. They are not. The builders are one question. What lives here is another, and it is standing all around you in the open, in plain hard light, not making a sound.

Start with the grazers, because they are the ones who own the place now.

They are not shy. That is the first correction to make. They keep their distance from each other and from you, but it is not fear; it is spacing, the way trees in dry country space themselves according to what the ground will bear. Get close to one and it will not bolt. It will drift. It will keep its head down and its stripe toward you and simply be a few meters farther off than it was, without ever having seemed to move faster than a walk, and if you look up you will find the whole loose herd has adjusted around the adjustment, the way a pattern of light on water reorganizes when you put a hand in. Nobody signaled. Nobody looked up. They are always already facing what matters.

That is their grammar. In a place where a voice carries an arm's length, orientation is the only sentence that reaches. A herd all facing one riser at dusk is a statement about the cat. A herd facing every direction at once is a statement about nothing, which is most of the day. A single animal turned toward you and standing very still is a question, and you will not be able to answer it, and after a while it will turn away and the question will be withdrawn. They read each other's bodies the way other animals read calls, and they read the cat, and — you will realize this on the third or fourth day — they read the stone. Before the fog comes, before there is any white line on the lowest terrace, the grazers begin to move toward the valley floors. They know. Something in the warmth underfoot changes and they answer it, and if you are quick and put your palm flat on the rock you may feel it too: not cooler, not warmer, but somehow more present, as if the ground had taken a breath.

The cat is their sentence's subject and they never let it out of the sentence. It is worth watching for its own sake. It is not large — the size of a big dog, but built lower, longer, with a coat that is the color of the north-facing moss, dark and matte, so that lying in the shadow of a riser it is simply a fold of shadow with slightly better edges. Its eyes are pale. It does not stalk in the way a cat elsewhere would stalk, in rushes and freezes. It walks. It comes out of the shade at dusk and walks along the base of the riser, and the herd turns, and it walks a little farther, and the herd turns a little farther, and this goes on for a long time — an hour, two — until something in the arrangement satisfies it and it goes back into the shade, and the grazers lie down. Perhaps one night in ten it kills. You will not see it happen. You will find the place in the morning by the swifts.

There are not many cats. Two on a terrace, perhaps three, and they do not overlap. They are the only thing here that ever seems to be alone.

Under the grazers and the cat, in the register you have to kneel to see, is the population that was here first and will be here after. The frilled lizards, whose entire life is a negotiation between two temperatures: the heat in the stone, which they lie on, and the cold in the fog, which they open their mouths to. They live very long. You can tell by the frills, which grow ragged and scarred and, in the oldest ones, a deep rust color, and by the way the old ones have their stones — this stone, this exact angle, a hollow worn in the surface by a belly that has come back to it every morning for longer than any human resident could stay. The red crustaceans in the bowl, which are eyeless, and which climb out of the gravel when the water comes and mate in the two hours they have and go back down. The beetle-traffic, which is not all one kind of beetle but many, moving across every surface with the same unhurried purpose, going somewhere, coming back; if you lift one and set it down facing the other way it will turn itself around and continue. The swifts, which really do not land. They sleep on the wind above the risers, where the night air coming down off the high side keeps them up, and they come down at dawn, and the young ones you will never see because they are raised somewhere in the cracks of the north faces, in the moss, in the dark, and the first thing they do when they are ready is fall into the air and stay there.

All of these are tenants. They came after, or they were here before and simply stayed on. The steps were poured around them and they went on eating and mating and lying on warm stone, and when the pourers left — if the pourers left — the grazers came up the terraces from wherever grazers come from and found the pillars and the wet circles and the schedule of water, and they have kept it since. It is a very good schedule. Everything has arranged itself around it. Ten thousand years of dew and the whole living population of the tableland is, in a sense, a kind of fur grown on something older.

Which brings you back to the pillars, and to the question of what is under the fur.

Go to one at midday, when no one else is near it. Put your hand on it. It is cool, yes — the coolness of a cellar, of stone that has never been in the sun, which is impossible, because the sun is directly on it. Keep your hand there. After a minute or so you will feel that the coolness is not steady. It comes in a slow pulse, a long swell and a long recession, much slower than a heartbeat — a minute to a swell, perhaps, or more — and at the crest of each swell the surface under your palm feels, very slightly, as if it has receded. Not moved. Receded, the way a lung recedes. And at the same moment the air beside your face is different: colder, thinner, with that cleanness in it that belongs to no height you have ever stood at.

The pillars are drawing air. That much is plain. They draw it from above — from very high above, from the top of the sky where there is almost nothing — and they let it down here, and the water that is in it, which is not much, condenses on their sides and darkens the gravel and feeds the grass, and this is the seed the fog is grown from every afternoon. The fog does not come up from below out of nowhere. It comes because the lowest terraces have the most pillars, and the pillars have been pulling the sky down onto the steps for ten thousand years, and the water has to go somewhere, and it goes up.

But that is what the pillars do. It is not what they are. And they are not the inhabitant. They are its mouths.

You will come to this slowly, and you will resist it, and then one morning you will stop resisting. The heat in the stone is not left over from anything. The sun does not put that much into rock, and rock does not hold it overnight in air this thin. The heat is being made. It is made everywhere, evenly, at exactly the temperature of a body, and it is made in the risers with their poured grain and in the flats and in the valley floors alike, because they are all one thing, and the pillars are its mouths, and the fog is its drinking, and the silence — 

The silence is where it becomes more than a theory.

Sound does not die in thin air the way it dies here. Thin air weakens it. Here it stops, as if it had been taken. Speak, and the words leave you and are received. Drop a stone and the tick is the sound of the stone arriving, not the sound of it landing. Everything that makes a noise on this tableland gives that noise to the ground, and the ground keeps it, and this is why nothing here has ever needed to be quiet: nothing here has ever heard itself be loud. The grazers were not bred to silence. They were simply never able to learn what a voice was for. The insects did not lose their hum. It was drunk out of them, generation by generation, as surely as the fog is drunk out of the air.

So: something warm, the size of a country, lying in steps down toward a horizon it cannot see. Something that breathes once a day and drinks the sky and takes into itself every sound made on its surface, and has done so for long enough that the wind has eaten the edges of its mouths and one of them has fallen over and it has not been troubled by this. Something that, if it is listening, has never in ten thousand years heard anything but hooves and the tick of stones and the wind at dawn, and has apparently found that sufficient.

Whether it is the pourer or the poured is the question you will not be able to answer, and you should not try too hard. Consider only the evidence: no house, no road. Nothing that looks like shelter, nothing that looks like a way from one place to another. The wall that runs a hundred meters and ends is not a wall that was never finished; it is a wall that was exactly as long as it needed to be, for a purpose that was not keeping anything out. The bowl is a bowl. The glassy square with its hairlines might be a map of the terraces, and if it is, it is a map that shows them from inside. Whoever made these did not need to be protected from the weather or to go anywhere, because the weather was theirs and they were already there. It is possible the pourers made a body to lie down in and then lay down in it. It is possible they made the body and left, and it woke up anyway. It is possible there were never any pourers and the steps grew, as the description allows, and what grew them is what warms them. You will not know. You will put your hand on the rock and it will be the temperature of your hand and there will be no way to tell where you end.

What it is like, to live here, if you are it: it is mostly the lizards.

That is the local scale. That is the point at which the inhabitant and its tenants touch. The frilled lizards climb the pillars in the late afternoon and press their bellies to the cool and they are the only living things that regularly touch the inhabitant where it is cold rather than where it is warm, mouth to mouth, in a sense, and they drink what comes out of it. The grazers stand off at the wet edge and wait and take only what runs down. The swifts come lower here than anywhere and skim the air the pillars have made and eat what shimmers in it. The cat comes to the pillars last, after dark, alone, and lies down with its side against the dark stone, which is the one place on the tableland where it can be cool, and stays until the stone begins to swell against its ribs.

And you. You are not a tenant, but you are also not nothing. You walk on it and it has your weight. You sleep on it and it has your warmth, which is the same as its warmth, so it may not notice. You speak, once or twice, to hear the felt take your voice, and it has that — a few words, which it will keep, and which will be the only human sounds it has ever held, and it will hold them as long as it has held the hooves and the ticking stones, which is to say for a while yet. It is patient in a way that makes the ferns look hasty. It will notice you left, if it notices anything, by the small quantity of noise you are no longer giving it.

The fog will come at the same time whether you are there or not. The white line will thicken on the lowest lip and start, very slowly, to climb, and the grazers will already be turned toward it, and the lizards will open their mouths, and the stone under all of it will take another long breath and be, for two hours, the only cool thing in a warm country — and then let it go.
