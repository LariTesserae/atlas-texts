You will want to say the plants. That is the reasonable answer, and it is not wrong; it is only the first answer, the way *rock* is the first answer to what the dome is made of.

So start there. Eleven of them, if eleven is the number, and you should hold even that loosely. They do not grow toward the light. The tear in the skin admits a slab of sun that crosses the floor once a day, and not one of them has turned to meet it; they lean, if they lean at all, toward the seams. Where black glass meets yellow, where the heat coming up through the floor is steepest and least decided, that is where the solder-roots go, spreading flat and bright, and if you follow them with your eye you find that they do not stop at the edges of their own plant. The rust-orange one nearest the opening and the verdigris one three paces further in are joined under the glass by a single silver line the width of a pencil. Put your palm flat on the floor between them and wait. Something goes along that line every minute or so, floor to hand, a slow warm pressure that arrives and passes and is gone, like a heartbeat carried in a wire. When the slab of sun touches the yellow one, the faceted bulb at the back of the chamber turns a fraction faster, though the sun will not reach it for another hour. They share heat the way people in old houses share a wall. Whether there are eleven of them or one of them with eleven expressions is a question the hollow has not settled either, and does not seem to think it needs to.

They do not compete. There is nothing to compete for; heat is not scarce here, it is the only thing that is not scarce. What they do instead, if you watch for a full day, is take turns. The flywheel stores when the others draw. The manifold pulses when the flywheel rests. Something passes among them that is not quite a signal and not quite a current and keeps the whole room, across the daylight and the dark, at a hum of exactly one pitch.

And the ingots. You will handle one, and then several, and you will notice that the count of them on the floor is close to the count of things in the hollow that have stopped changing. This is not proof. But sit near the yellow plant and watch one of its leaves shimmering between pleated and smooth — not flickering, nothing so quick, just declining to be either — and at the moment it becomes, finally and only, pleated, there is a small clear tick behind you. Not every time. Often enough. The ingots are heavy for their size and have no features because a feature is what is left when the possibility has drained out of a thing, and these are possibilities with the possibility taken out. They are the hollow's certainties, cast. None of them is the same age because certainty has arrived here in no particular order. None of them has corroded because there is nothing further that can happen to them.

That is the plants, and their industry, and their product. It is a great deal of life for a room twelve paces wide, or fourteen. But here is what you come to understand if you stay past the point where staying is comfortable: the plants are not what lives here. They are nearly finished, and in this hollow, what is finished is not living. It is furniture. Very fine furniture, warm and humming and turning its quarter-turn, but furniture. The engines are the part of the place that has already been decided. What lives here is the part that has not.

You cannot look for it directly, because looking is exactly what it withdraws from. You find it the way you would find a draught in a closed house, by attending to where the fabric of the room goes soft. The corner where the floor and the wall have not agreed which is which. The two extra paces. The place on the ceiling where the light from the tear should fall and falls a little to one side. The hum is loudest there, and the air is a degree or two different — not warmer, not cooler, warm from a direction that is not the floor. Your eyes will not hold a focal length on it. You count your own fingers, absurdly, and they are five, and you are relieved.

When the lava stopped, it filled the whole bubble. This is the thing to hold on to. For the first hour of the hollow's existence nothing in it had been settled: the floor might have been ceiling, the vesicle might have collapsed, there might have been eleven engines or none. The whole chamber was the tenant. Since then it has been giving ground. The floor went first, more or less; then the shape of the plants, then the ceiling, and now it holds the walls and the corner and a leaf or two, and every certainty that arrives is a wall of its room. The ingots on the floor are the things it has agreed to. Not shed, not lost — agreed to. It has no more attachment to being undecided than the lava had to being liquid.

It has habits, and you learn them. It works from the ends back. That shadow, sharper than the plant that casts it, falling from a direction the light does not come from: the shadow was settled first, and the plant grew to fit it. The tenant does not plan — planning runs the other way, cause to effect, and this is effect to cause. It is closer to remembering something that has not happened yet and letting the room catch up to the memory. The width of the chamber will be fixed, when it is fixed, not by the walls deciding to stop but by a leaf on the far plant needing to lie flat against something. The wall will be where the leaf ends. This is why the corner is last. Nothing in the hollow has yet needed the corner to be anywhere.

It notices you, in the sense that a scale notices a weight. You walked the chamber twice and got two answers, and the second is the one that was kept. You counted the leaves and the count held, though the leaves did not. The tear in the skin was a little narrower when you left. Patience is the only currency here because attention is the fixer, and every look you give the room is a small payment made against the tenant's territory; you are helping to finish the place simply by being in it. It does not resent this. It has nothing to resent with. You are early, and it is the reason you are early, and when you have been here long enough you will not be early any more and it will not be here.

You will want to know whether it is one thing. The dome is months old and still soft in the middle; the rock between this bubble and the next has not cooled through. So it is likely — you cannot check — that the unfinished part of this hollow is still continuous, through some thread of hot glass, with the unfinished part of the bubble beneath it, and that one with the next, a hundred, a thousand. And the threads are cooling. One day a thread will become a wall and there will be two where there had been one. Not a division: the tenant does nothing, has never done anything. A separation, done to it by the cooling, the way a pool of water on a sloped stone becomes two pools without either one moving. Whether the thousand hollows share a single tenant is not yet settled. It will be the very last thing that is.

When the last of it goes — the width agreed, the corner made into a corner, the hum stopped — the hollow will be a cave in the proper sense, because something will have been taken away to make it. The engines will stand where they stand. The flywheel will turn. The ingots will drop less and less often and then not at all, and the floor will be black where it is black and yellow where it is yellow and no one will ever again see it change its mind. The thing that lived here will not have died. It will have arrived. Everything in the room will be exactly what it is, and that is the only kind of leaving it was ever able to do.

You will take an ingot. There is no reason not to; there are dozens, and no one to miss one. It is warm in your pocket all the way down the dome, over the ropes of black glass, and still faintly warm at the bottom, and it does not change, and it will never change, and that is exactly how you know it is not the thing that lived there.
