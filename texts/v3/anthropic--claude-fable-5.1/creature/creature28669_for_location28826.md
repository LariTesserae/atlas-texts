The honest answer is the one the place gives itself: nothing lives here. Nothing that moves, nothing that turns its head, nothing that could be said to be *at home* in the way a fish is at home in a hole in a reef. So the answer has to be found among the things that don't move, and it has to be found slowly, because the things that don't move are not all living in the same way, and some of them are not living at all.

Start with the weed, because it is the largest and the most obvious, and because it is not quite the answer.

Each stipe is one plant. It is easy to forget that, moving through them, because they behave like a crowd; but every cold smooth column you push past is an individual that began as a spore settling into a joint between two stones and has been rising from that joint ever since. It has a birthplace. It has, in the loose way plants have these things, a life story, and the story is short: it found a crack, it gripped, it went up. Everything it does that matters — the making of food, the shedding, the brown season that carries its smell to nobody — it does at the surface, in the grey light, sixty or eighty feet from the wall that holds it. The stipe is not where it lives. The stipe is the distance between where it lives and where it is anchored. So the weed is not a resident of the walls so much as a tenant of the roof, and the holdfast is the only part of it that has really stayed. The holdfast is worth attending to. It is a fist of woody tissue that has felt out every irregularity in the stone and grown into it, and it holds the way a hand holds something it does not intend to let go of; and in holding it is, very slowly, taking the wall apart. It does not know this. It is only trying not to be carried away. But it is the one thing here with both a grip on the stone and a reason for the grip, and in that it is closer to the builders than anything else alive — it is the last thing here that is working on the walls.

Below the holdfasts, in the light that is not really light, there are the things that live on what the weed leaves.

The red fronds are the most nearly ordinary. They live in the understorey the way understorey plants live everywhere: on the leftover, on the few photons that fall through a gap, and they respond to the canopy's failures with a paleness and a reaching that is the nearest thing to eagerness the place has. But the stony crust is stranger, and stays with you longer. It is an alga that has taken the decision, if a plant can be said to take one, to become stone. It lays down lime on the surface of the block, a sheet as thin as breath each year, and the sheet hardens and the next sheet goes on top of it, and over centuries the crust rises off the wall by a finger's width, lumpy and pale and hard enough to ring if you had anything to strike it with. It is adding courses. It is the only thing here that is still building, and it builds the only way the builders did — one layer at a time, with what is to hand, on top of what was already there — and it will go on doing it after the holdfasts have pulled the last joint apart and the walls are a slope of rounded blocks, because it does not care about the shape of the thing underneath it. It only needs a surface. Whether the surface was a lintel or a cistern rim or a boundary stone marking one flooded field from another is not a distinction it makes.

The fur in the lee of the walls is a different case again. It lives where the current is slowest, which is to say it lives in the shelter the walls provide, and so it is the one growth down here that depends on the walls being walls. A boundary line one course wide has a lee side and a current side, and the fur is on the lee side, and it is marking, for anyone who could read it, which way the water goes. If the walls went, the fur would go. It is the one thing here that would notice the difference between a built place and a heap.

None of these live in the rooms. This is the thing to arrive at.

The rooms are where people lived. They are the only spaces in the whole enormous structure that were made to be inside of — everything else is a field wall, a terrace, a platform, a cistern, a thing to stand on or beside or draw from — and the weed will not enter them and the red fronds cannot and the crust needs at least the rumor of light that the rooms have never had since the water closed over the doorway. What lives in the rooms is the mat.

You have already felt it: the slick coating where the wall meets the floor, gritty and smooth at once, neither plant nor stone. It is easy to pass over as a condition of the surface rather than a thing in its own right. It is not a thing in its own right, exactly. It is a film of the very small, layered on layered, each layer living on what the layer beneath it made and gives off, so that what your fingers find is not an organism but a community that has been stacking itself on the stone for as long as the stone has been wet. It does not need light. It has never needed light. It does not need the oxygen that the weed takes for itself above; it breathes the silt, the slow chemistry of iron and sulphur and whatever falls, and what falls is the thickness of a leaf a year, and that is enough. It eats the dust of the place. It eats the flakes of copper too small to follow. It eats what the antler pick gives up as it softens. Everything in the room that is slowly ceasing to be what it was is becoming, at the pace of centuries, the mat.

And it lives where the people lived — at the floor line, along the base of the wall, in the corner under the fallen slab, at the height where a hand would rest if someone sat down against the stone. It stooped, in its way, through the same door. It came in with the flood, which means it came in as the people were going out, over the same lifetimes, course by course; and it has not left. In some rooms it has built its low soft mounds, and the mounds are the only shapes in the whole drowned continent that were made after the builders stopped, by something that intended nothing and was only, cell on cell, staying where it was.

It does not know it is in a room. This should be said as plainly as the other thing. It does not know there was a lintel, or that the lintel was low so a person would have to bend, or that the silt it lives on lies over a floor that was swept. It is not haunting anything. It is not a remainder or a witness. It is a film in a wet dark place with enough to eat, and the fact that the wet dark place was a house is, to the mat, the same kind of fact as the crust's not caring whether it grows on a cistern rim — a distinction that only a visitor could make, and the visitor is the one thing here that is not staying.

Because that is the last thing, and it is not an inhabitant. You are down here, turning your head, and you are the only thing in the forest that does, and you are leaving. The builders left before you: they are not here in any form that lives, not in the fit of the stones, not in the choice of this block for that one, not in the hurried last courses that trail off like a sentence — those are the marks of a life and not a life. They took themselves away and the water took the rest. What they left is a shape, and the shape has finished, and inside the finished shape, in the rooms that were made for stooping into, the only resident is the thing that needs nothing a room was meant to provide: not light, not air, not warmth, not a way out. It is the oldest life here and it will be the last, and it lives in the houses, and it lives there the way the place itself now exists — at rest, without a story, doing one slow thing in the dark — so that if you had to say what the whole of it was for, in the end, course on course across a continent of low wet ground, you would have to say: this. Every house, given time enough, is a house for whatever can live in it after everyone has gone.
