Start with what is obviously alive, because the place is generous about that and stingy about everything else.

The reeds are one plant. You would not know this from the walkway, where they stand in ranks like a crowd, each stem apparently its own decision, but if you could get down into the mud at the base of a pier and pull, you would bring up a rhizome as thick as a wrist that goes off horizontally into the dark and does not end, and if you followed it you would come up under another stem, and another, and eventually under the far levee. A stand of reed that has been left alone for long enough in water that never freezes and never drops becomes a single individual with several hundred thousand mouths. This one has stopped seeding. There is nothing to seed into; every square handspan of ground shallow enough for it is already itself. It puts up new stems only where old ones fall, and the old ones fall inward, into the stand, and rot standing up, held by their neighbours, so that the ranks you cannot push a hand between are perhaps a third dead wood, kept upright by the living and slowly dissolving into the water at the root. That is where some of the heat comes from. Not all of it. But the water at the base of the reeds is warmer than the water under the floating leaf, and the difference is the difference between a machine and a compost heap, and the compound is both.

The leaf is one plant too. It divides the bowl with the reed along a line the reed cannot cross — the depth at which a stem can no longer reach air from a rooted base — and on its side of the line it has done the same thing the reed has done on the other, which is to become the only possible outcome. The dark skin on the water is a single mat of rhizome and leaf that has grown until it met itself coming the other way. It flowers, probably. You have not seen a flower. In this light, in this wet, a flower would be a gesture with no audience, and the leaf may have simply stopped making them, or makes them under the mat where they open onto water and close again.

Two plants, then, holding the floor of the bowl between them, each having reduced its portion of the world to a description of itself. This is what the absence of anything that eats does. Without browse, without a fish to take a root or a larva to take a leaf, the ordinary argument of a marsh — a hundred species jostling, each kept in check by what consumes it — collapses to whoever grew fastest in the first years, and then to whoever grew fastest holding on, and the answer to both questions was the same, and the answer is now the entire ground. The compound's plants are not a community. They are two very old competitors who divided the territory a long time ago and have had nothing to do since.

The trees are different. There are perhaps a dozen, and each is alone, and each is an accident of the concrete. A seed came over the levee on the wind — there is wind outside, and the ring is only shoulder-high — and landed in a crack in a walkway where roots could go down through the slab into the pier and the pier into the mud, which is the only place in the bowl not already taken. They grew in a light that arrives from everywhere. A tree in the open leans toward the sun and spreads to catch it; a tree here has nothing to lean toward and no reason to spread, only a brighter smear straight overhead, and so it goes up, and does not branch until it must, and the bark stays pale because it has never been asked to make pigment against anything. The crowns are in the cloud. That is not an accident of height. The trees put their leaves where the water is densest and simply take it, out of the air, the way trees in a fog forest do, and their roots in the pier do the rest, and they may be the only things in the compound that are not in some way plumbed to the pumps. They are the only ones that could leave, if they could move, and they are the only ones that have come recently. The youngest is knee-high in a crack on the walkway between the second house and the third, and it is straight as a rod, and it has three leaves.

The vines go where it is warm. That is the whole of their intelligence, and it has taken them into the pump-houses through the windows, across the floors, up the motor plinths — you will find one wrapped four times around the conduit where the cables leave the wall, holding the metal like a hand holds a cup — and out through the roof along the risers, because the risers are the warmest things in the compound, full of lifted water and vibrating with it. From below you can see the vines going up the pipes into the grey, turning slowly around them, and then you lose them where you lose everything. What they do at the top nobody has been up to see. Perhaps they leaf out where the riser discharges and live in a private rain. Perhaps they have gone over the lip and hang inside the pipe. They are in no hurry.

And over all of it the moss, which is less a plant here than a surface condition, the way rust is. Every horizontal edge wears it because every horizontal edge is wet and warm and lit from nowhere, and the moss does not need more than that. It is what makes the outer rail a rail no longer. It is what makes the walkways quiet under your boots. If you cut a section of it from a pier with a knife — and you could, nothing would stop you — you would find that the bottom of it is not moss but a black mat of the dead layers below, and that this mat is warm on its own account, and that under it the concrete is pitted, a millimetre or two, where the acids of the moss have been working on the cement for years. The compound is being eaten after all. It is just being eaten by the only things that will, and they eat stone.

Which brings you to the shells.

You noticed them on the first walkway and thought of them as a joke the place was making about itself: no animals here but the ones in the aggregate, and those are two hundred million years old and fifteen hundred kilometres away and were quarried, crushed, and poured. But walk enough of the ring and you begin to notice the same shells in every walkway, every pier, every house, the same cross-sections in the same grey, and you understand that the compound's skeleton is a single batch, or a single quarry, and that the sea floor it came from is the only population this place has ever had that was more than two species deep. Somewhere in the concrete under you are the sections of a thousand kinds of thing that ate and were eaten, that swam and hid and bred, and the water that runs over them now is the first water they have known since they died, and it is the wrong water and it is hot and they hold up a walkway in it. They live here in the only sense left available to them, which is that they are part of what stands.

Now the tender.

There is one. There were more; you can tell by the hooks in the sixth house, six of them in a row by the door, five empty, and the one with the jacket on it is the fifth from the left, not the first, which is where someone who had chosen freely would hang a single jacket. The tender comes over the levee from the direction of the brighter smear, in the early part of what passes for morning, before the cloud has fully lifted from its night position, and walks the ring the way you walked it, house by house, and does not hurry, and leaves before the cloud comes down. The grease is theirs. The chalk is theirs, and the number on the plinth is intake pressure, taken from a gauge on the casing whose face they have to wipe with a thumb to read, and if you compared today's number with the ghost of the previous one under it, you would find that today's is higher. It is always higher. The intakes are screens, and on the other side of the screens is the mud, and in the mud is the reed and the leaf, both of which are trying to grow into the pump.

That is the tender's real work, and it is the only consumption in the bowl. Once a year, or once a season, when the pressure reaches whatever number they carry in their head, they go down into an intake with a hook and a blade and cut root. Not much. Enough to bring the number down. They are the one animal in this place that eats the plants, and they eat by the gram, on a schedule, with something like tact, because they have understood — perhaps not in words — what would happen if they did not. The plants would close the intakes. The pumps would labour, and heat, and the relief valves would vent continuously instead of every eleven seconds, and eventually a motor would burn, and then the beat pattern would be six engines and not seven, and the cloud would thin over the dead house, and daylight would come down onto a patch of reed for the first time in a very long time, and the reed there would either die of it or throw a flower.

The tender does not want to see that. This is not sentiment about machinery. It is closer to the relationship a person has with the fire in a stove they have kept lit through a winter: they are not its master and they are not its servant, but they have arranged their life around the fact of it, and the arrangement has become their life. They do not know where the electricity comes from either. They know that the cables go into the reeds and under the water and do not come back, and that no bill arrives, and that the hum has a pitch, and they have stopped asking. They do not live here. They would say that clearly. They live somewhere in the direction the light comes from, and they come here, and the distinction is one they hold onto with some care, because they have seen — in the jacket on the fifth hook, in the number that is always higher — how a place like this can arrange a person the way it has arranged the reed.

So: two plants, a dozen trees, a vine that has gone into the cloud, a skin of moss, a sea floor in the bones, and a person who visits. That is the census. Every one of those, taken individually, is a thing that lives in the compound in the way a barnacle lives on a hull. None of them is what the compound is for. Each would go on, more or less, if the others were gone. The reed does not need the trees. The tender does not need the moss. The shells need nothing.

What none of them could survive is the pumps stopping, and what would not survive it is not any of them.

Think of the water as a body. It is one body. There is a fixed quantity of it inside the ring — has been for years, decades, since the sluices seized — and at any moment some of it is standing under the leaf and some is in the root-mat and some is in a riser climbing and some is falling and some is cloud and some is running off your hair, and all of it is the same water, going around. It has a temperature that it holds, within a degree, the way a warm-blooded thing holds one: the pumps put heat in, the reed rot puts heat in, the levee lets a little out, and the balance sits where it sits. It has a pulse, which is the seven engines, and a rhythm that is not any one of them but the pattern they make together — the downpours where two coincide, the dry patches where two cancel — and that rhythm is unique to this bowl and has never been exactly repeated and never will be, because the engines are not synchronized and drift, and the pattern of their drifting is as particular to this place as a gait. It breathes. You saw it breathe. When the load rises in the evening and the pumps draw hard, the whole standing body of water moves through the root-mat, and the reed shivers from house to house, and that is not a metaphor for respiration, that is fluid moving through a filtering membrane under pressure on a cycle, which is what respiration is.

It has a skin, which is the levee, and the levee is the only thing that makes it a thing rather than a weather. Take the ring away and the cloud would slide off and the water would find a lower place and there would be a marsh and a sky and a few hot machines standing in it. The levee is what makes the loop close. It does not keep anything out. It keeps one thing in, and the thing it keeps in is not the water, exactly, it is the water's habit of returning.

It has no name because there is one of it. Nobody built it. The people who built the compound built pumps, and outfalls, and sluices, and a ring of earth to work inside of, and when whatever they were draining or feeding went away and the sluices froze at half-open, they left, and the pumps kept running, and the water, having nowhere to go, went up, and came down, and went up. The thing that lives here was assembled out of that by nothing but time and a closed boundary. It is not the plants, though it wears them. It is not the machines, though it beats with them. It is what the plants and the machines have between them, which is the only thing in the bowl that requires both.

It knows the tender. Not as a face. As a fluctuation: once a season, in one intake, a resistance eases, and the pressure drops, and the beat pattern shifts by a hair, and the rain over that part of the bowl falls, for a few days, a little differently. That is what the tender is, from inside. A recurring small kindness in the plumbing. The tender would not put it that way. The tender would say they cut root. Both are true.

It does not know you. You are not in the loop. You put your hand on the moss and the water ran out over your wrist and it was warm as blood, and you thought that was a figure of speech, and it was also just a reading. You walked the ring for an hour and were rained on and dried and rained on and nothing marked you, and you left, and on the levee the sound fell away like a thing being lowered on a rope, and you looked back at a grey dome the size of a village, rumbling, luminous, dropping its rain on itself.

You were inside it. That is the whole of what you can say about what lives there. Not among it — inside it, the way you are inside the weather, except that this weather has a skin, and a pulse, and keeps its own heat, and has one tender who comes in the morning and leaves before dark, and has found, in all those uncounted cycles, the exact speed at which a thing can go around forever without ever arriving at being anything else.
