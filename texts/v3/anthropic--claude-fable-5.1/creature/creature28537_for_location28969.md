Begin with one of them, because you have to begin somewhere, and because the fabric on the ceiling is made of individuals even if it does not behave like it.

Take a single bat down from the vault. It weighs about what a letter weighs. Its body, stripped of the wings, is the size of a thumb, and it is mostly muscle and heart: the heart is enormous for the animal, and at rest in the roost it runs at a few hundred beats a minute, and in flight it will go past a thousand. The fur is short and dense and brown in a way that has never been seen in this room, because nothing in this room has ever been seen. The face is not pretty. The upper lip is wrinkled into folds, the ears are broad and pressed forward like cupped hands, and the tail — this is the detail that names the animal — runs out past the edge of the membrane between its legs, a bare, whiplike thing, the free tail of a free-tailed bat. The wings are long and narrow, built for speed and distance rather than for turning tightly in cluttered air. This is not a bat that flits around trees. This is a bat that climbs a kilometer above the desert on the evening thermals, into air the swifts have left, and hunts moths there at forty, fifty, sixty kilometers an hour, and comes home with its stomach a quarter of its body weight.

Hold it — you could not, it would bite, and you would deserve it — and you would feel a heat off it beyond what its size suggests, and a smell. The smell is musk and ammonia and something like warm bread, and it is the individual's share of the atmosphere in the room. It is talking the whole time. Some of what it says you would hear as a thin, irritated chittering. Most of it you would not hear at all.

It is a female, probably. In the season when the room is hottest, the ceiling is almost entirely female. The males are elsewhere, in smaller, cooler roosts along the mountain's flank, and it is the mothers who need this heat, because a bat pup is born naked and blind and cannot hold its own temperature, and a room that sits at forty degrees in the dark is an incubator. The pups are not carried. They are deposited in a part of the ceiling that becomes, for a few weeks, a creche: a tighter fabric within the fabric, pink and hairless, packed at a density that has been measured in other rooms like this one at several thousand to the square meter. They hang there by their feet and their milk teeth, which are hooked for exactly that purpose, and they wait. The mother goes out at dusk and hunts and returns, and then she does something that ought to be impossible: she finds her own pup, in that mass, in that noise, in that dark. She lands near where she left it and calls, and it calls back, and out of the roar she picks the one voice that is hers, and confirms it with her nose, and feeds it, and only it. She will not, as a rule, nurse another's young. In a room built on the abolition of the individual, this is the one thing that stays private.

Put her back. She is not one animal anyway.

On her, in the fur along her spine and behind her ears, there are flies. They have no wings, or have wings so reduced they are useless; they are small, flattened, spidery things with strong clawed feet, and they spend their whole lives on bats, walking through the fur the way a person walks through tall grass. They drink blood. When they need to pupate, they leave the bat for the roost wall, glue themselves to the glass among ten thousand others, and wait for the air to be full of hosts again. There are bugs, too, relatives of the bedbug, that live in the cracks of the stone and come out to feed on whatever hangs closest. And there are mites: in the wing membranes, in the nostrils, in the ears, mites that are found on no other animal and nowhere else, mites whose entire universe is the inside of the ear of a free-tailed bat in a hot cave. Every bat on the ceiling is a small country with its own population. The room contains a colony; the colony contains a colony; the tenant of the tenant of the tenant has never once encountered stone.

Now go down.

The floor's dominant citizen is a beetle a few millimeters long, dark and oval and unremarkable, one of the dermestids — the family that museums keep in boxes to strip skeletons and that householders find in the cracks under carpets. Here, it is not a pest. Here, it is the economy. The adults are the part you would notice, a granular scrim over everything, but the real work is done by the larvae, which are bristly, banded, tapering things like animated brushes, and which eat with a steady, mindless competence anything that was recently alive. A bat's nightly output lands and is met before it has finished settling. A dead bat lands and is unmade — first the soft tissue, then the membranes, then the tendons, then the fur — in the time it takes the surface to wake and sleep. The bones last a little longer. Eventually even those are worked over by things that eat bone, and disappear into the deposit, and become floor.

The beetles are not alone on the floor either, and the ratio of what accompanies them tells you what sort of place this is. The cockroaches are the pale kind, cave-adapted, thin-skinned, fast, with long antennae that do the seeing. They are scavengers and opportunists and they are everywhere. The mites are not individuals at all; they are a phase of the substrate, a dust that moves. Where the beetles are thickest the mites are thickest, because many of them are not eating guano but eating what eats guano, or riding on it. The pseudoscorpions do the same thing more deliberately: they are the size of a grain of rice, they carry venom in their pincers, and they will clamp onto the leg of a passing beetle and let it carry them to fresher ground, and then step off and hunt the mites there. The centipedes are bigger and fewer and are the closest thing this floor has to an apex. They move through the beetles the way a shark moves through a shoal, and the shoal does not part for them, and they do not need it to.

And below all of this, in the deposit itself, the floor gets stranger.

The top few centimeters are fresh and crawling. Below that the material is older, drier, compacted, and the visible animals thin out and the invisible ones take over. The guano is threaded with fungal filaments — not the soft white mats of a wet cave, but tough, sparse, heat-tolerant strands that pull nitrogen and phosphorus out of the deposit and build themselves from it. There are bacteria here that eat ammonia. Ammonia, which is poison to nearly everything, which would blind you and burn the lining of your lungs, is to them what sunlight is to a leaf: they oxidize it and live on the energy, and their waste feeds a second set of bacteria, and that set feeds a third, and so the reek that fills the room is, from a sufficiently small point of view, a table set for dinner. Down near the bottom of the deposit, where it meets the original glass, the material is old enough that the organic part has been eaten away and what is left is mineral — phosphates, salts, the faint crystalline beginnings of a rock that will one day be a rock. There are things living there too, but they are the kind that live in rock: slow, few, indifferent to the roar above them.

Near the fissures, on the pale crusts the description calls the room's only flowers, there is a separate small nation that wants nothing from the bats at all. It lives on the sulfur breath of the mountain. It sits where the warm gas meets the ammonia haze and the two chemistries argue, and it takes its living from the argument. It is the one population in the room that would survive if the ceiling emptied tomorrow, and it does not know the ceiling is there.

Go, briefly, to the throat.

Nothing lives in the throat itself; it is too exposed, too dry, too much a corridor. But things live at it, and they are the room's outermost inhabitants, the ones that participate in the place without ever entering it. In the last hour before sunset a snake may come and coil on the warm rock just outside the slump, and wait. It knows what is coming. So do the hawks that begin, at the same hour, to gather in the air above the flank, and the owl that takes a perch on the ridge, and a fox or a skunk that noses around the pale stain of dust for whatever fell short. When the exodus begins — a trickle, then a rope, then a river of bats pouring out of the hole and spiraling up in a dark column that thickens and leans downslope toward the desert — the predators take their percentage from the edges, the same way the centipedes do on the floor. A hawk stoops through the column and comes out with one. The snake strikes at whatever passes within reach. It is not much, against tens of thousands. It is enough to live on. A ring of animals on the outside of the mountain is fed by a hole they will never go into, and have arranged their whole evenings around.

And now step back, because there is a larger inhabitant here than any of these, and it is easy to miss it by looking at the parts.

Nothing on the ceiling is old. A free-tailed bat, lucky, lives fifteen years; most live far fewer. Nothing on the floor lasts a season. The flies and mites and bacteria turn over in weeks or hours. Come back in a century, the description says, and the room would sound exactly as it does now — and it would, and not one body making the sound would be one that is making it tonight. Every animal in this room will be replaced, and the room will not notice. The thing that persists is not a body. It is the arrangement: the ceiling that is warm because it is full and full because it is warm; the floor that is fed by the ceiling and cleans up after it; the ammonia that is poison and food; the dusk exodus that empties the room and the dawn return that fills it; the mothers who find their pups. It has a temperature, and defends it. It has a metabolism, measured in the moths of a hundred square kilometers of desert. It has a shape it maintains against the constant departure of everything that composes it. It behaves, in every way that matters, like something alive, and it has been alive for as long as the rock has been cool enough to hold it — which is to say, longer than any creature that has ever been part of it, and not nearly as long as it will be.

Under it, the mountain. Whether the mountain lives is a question of definitions. It is warm; it breathes through the fissures; it is growing, slowly, in the dark below; it made this room without meaning to and will, someday, close it — with a new flow, or a collapse, or simply by letting the floor rise until it meets the roof. The colony does not know this. The colony does not know anything. It hangs in the heat and roars and rains and eats itself and is, for now, the tenant, in a room where the landlord is only warm, and thinking about it.
