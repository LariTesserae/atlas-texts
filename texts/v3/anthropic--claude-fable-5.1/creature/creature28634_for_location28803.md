You would say the salamanders, if you were asked. They are the largest thing, and there are nine of them, and they are lying in the warmest place, and a pile of animals has the look of ownership about it. Or you would say the snake, because it is watching, and watching is what a resident does. But none of them live here. They came up from the ditch for the heat, and they will go back down to the ditch to eat and to breed, and if you came in winter you might find the pile doubled and if you came in a dry August you might find it gone. They occupy the place the way the sick occupy a ward. It is full of them and it is not theirs.

Go smaller and it is the same. The midges are here because the pools are here; the spiders are here because the midges are; the harvestmen and the woodlice and the thin-shelled snails are here because there is damp and warmth and a thousand small crevices in a material that nothing else has yet claimed. All of them have a somewhere-else. All of them are, in the strict sense, on a visit that has lasted since the slab slid.

What lives here is the mat.

It is hard to look at directly, because there is nothing in it for the eye to fasten on. Around each pool a ring, a hand's breadth wide, of something slick. In the centre of the ring, where the water is, it is rust-coloured and soft and lies under the surface like silt that has decided to hold together. Further out, where the drips do not reach but the damp does, it thins and goes grey-green, then olive, then a black that has purple in it when the light from the roof shifts. At its outer edge it is only a sheen, a change in the way the cinder reflects. Put your finger on it and it is warm and slightly tacky and comes away with a smell of struck match and old pennies. Put your finger on it a second time and the mat has already closed over the print.

It has no shape of its own. It has the shape of wherever the iron water goes, and the iron water goes wherever the heap's slow settling tells it to, so the mat is a map of the drips, and the drips are a map of the fractures in the glass overhead, and the fractures are a map of how a slab of slag the size of a garage door cooled unevenly one afternoon last year. The mat is the last term in that chain. It is what all the accidents above it come to.

It is the only thing in the cavity that was not somewhere before it was here. The salamanders were in the ditch; the spiders were in the grass along the fence; the snake was under the pallets by the furnace hall. But the mat has no ancestor you could point to. Its beginnings came in as a dust of cells on the rain, or on the feet of the first midge, or were already lying dormant in the slag itself, and they met the water coming down through the heap with its load of dissolved iron and its taste of sulphur, and they found they could live on that. Not on light. Not on anything that had ever been alive. On the iron, taking it from one state to another and keeping the small change. That is the whole of its diet. It eats the heap.

And it is building. The crusts around the pools, white and orange, that would be taller if you came back in a month—those are partly the water's doing, evaporation leaving its salts, but they are partly the mat's. Where it has lived and died and been overgrown, the iron it has handled is left behind as a rind, and the rind is cemented, and the next generation grows on top of the rind. Cut through one of the crusts and it is layered like a tree stump, and each layer is a season of drips. The mat is making rock out of itself. It is the only thing in here doing so on purpose, if a film can be said to have a purpose, and the rock it makes is the floor the salamanders are lying on.

Because it is on everything. That is what takes the longest to see. It is not confined to the rings around the pools. It has crept out along the fracture lines where moisture travels, so that the walls have a faint slickness in their creases. It is on the thermocouple wire, a rust-coloured sleeve where the insulation is split. It is on the survey stake, a shadow on the plastic below the waterline of the damp. It is in the weave of the glove. It has grown up the outsides of the snails' shells, so that what you took for thinness from a poor diet is partly a coat of something else's living. It is on the undersides of the toads, where they sit against the warm cinder and never move: a grey bloom on the belly skin that the toads do nothing about. It is on the salamanders. Lift one—they do not resist—and the pale of its belly is stippled with rust where it has lain against the floor, and the floor is stippled with the pale of the salamander, and there is no clean line between the animal and the place.

This is not parasitism. The mat does not take anything from them. It is simply that everything in here is in contact with everything else, and the mat is the medium of contact, and so everything in here is wearing it.

Which is where one begins to wonder about the truce. Not that the mat causes it—there is no mechanism to point to, no sedative in the slime, nothing so tidy. But the truce and the mat are the same fact described at two scales. The mat is a surface on which nothing is separate. The truce is a room in which nothing is separate. A strike needs a gap—a space to draw back into, a space to cross—and here the gaps are all filled, and the filling is warm, and it is slightly sticky, and it has grown a little way onto everyone. A snake in a fissure with a film of the floor on its chin. Toads with the floor on their bellies. Salamanders lying on the floor and the floor lying on them. What eats what, when the substrate is the thing that connects them and the connection is alive?

It breathes, in its way. Not visibly, not on any scale you could watch, but the pools are its lungs. When the conveyor runs and the pulse of heat comes down through the floor and the drips quicken, the mat quickens with them; more water, more iron, and the rust-coloured centre of each ring darkens by the smallest degree as it feeds. When the heap is quiet for days and the drips slacken, the outer edge of the mat dries to a crust and dies back and the ring shrinks toward the pool. It expands and contracts with the heap's own exhalation, a metre-wide organism lying on the floor of a hollow inside a mound of cooling glass, keeping the heap's time.

And that is the measure of its life. It is not long. The heap is cooling from within—that is the whole reason there is warmth here, and the warmth is a debt being paid down a degree at a time. In two years, or five, the core will have given up enough heat that the underside of the slab no longer runs with condensation, and the drips will slow to nothing, and the pools will dry to rust-coloured pans with white rims, and the mat will contract to a ring, and then to a stain, and then to a rind of iron on the cinder that a geologist might recognise and nobody else. The salamanders will have gone down to the ditch long before that, when the floor stopped being warmer than the mud. The snake will have followed the toads. The spiders will stay longest, because the cavity will still be dry and out of the wind, but there will be no midges, and one by one the tents in the hollows of the slag will empty.

The mat will be here through all of that, growing smaller. It cannot leave. It has no somewhere-else. It came into being out of this exact water and this exact heat, and it lives precisely as long as the heap breathes, and when the heap stops breathing it will stop too, and leave behind the only durable thing that anything in here has made: a floor of layered rust and salt, a few centimetres thick, with the prints of salamander bellies in it, and the shadow of a wire, and the shape of a glove.

That is who lives here. Not the nine bodies in the warm place, though they are what you see. The film they are lying on. The one tenant that did not come in from outside, that eats the house it lives in, that is turning itself into the floor. Everything else is a guest of it, and does not know.
