The first thing to say is that almost nothing in the hollow lives there.

The sleepers do not live there. They are stored there. A slug contracted to a button is not at home in the angle of a wall any more than a coat is at home on a hook; it is where it was put, or where it put itself, and its relationship to the room is one of tolerance rather than tenancy. The springtails came in on a warm night in the third week in a drift you could have swept up with two fingers, and they will leave in the same way, and in between they do not know they are anywhere. The frog is, technically, dead. You cannot live somewhere while you are dead. The mites are a texture. The shrew is a visitor, and a bad one. The fungus is arriving rather than resident, still in the stage of a guest who has taken off one shoe.

The alder is the obvious answer and the wrong one. The alder lives *here*, in the fen, with its feet in black water, and the hollow is a thing that has happened to it — a wound it has not noticed, at the base of a growth it grew around a wound it noticed sixty years ago and then forgot. The alder is the landlord of the room in the sense that a hand is the landlord of a cut on it. It supplies the walls and the water and, eventually, the ending, and it will never once look inside.

So: the builders. But even that needs saying carefully, because the builders did not move into the hollow when it opened. They were already there. They have been there far longer than the room.

---

A burl is a scar. Something went wrong in this alder sixty years ago — a bud that would not stop budding, or a fungus, or a wound from a deer's antler, or the beginning of exactly these creatures, chewing — and the tree answered the way trees answer, which is to grow around the trouble in a knot of twisted wood and go on growing. The knot got bigger. The grain inside it swirled because it was grain trying to get past something. What it was trying to get past, in part, was them.

They have lived in the burl's interior for as long as the burl has existed. Not in a room — there was no room — but in galleries, threads of tunnel bored through wood so dense and so contorted that a boring insect would have found it nearly impossible, which may be why they are not exactly boring insects. They went slowly. Everything about them is slow. They chewed a body's-width forward through the torsion of the grain and ate what they chewed and left behind a gallery lined with their own paste, and the tree grew around the gallery and the gallery moved with it, and for six decades they lived in a dark that had no sky at all, and no drip, and no weather, and a temperature that varied by perhaps two degrees between January and July. They are creatures of the inside of a scar. That is their habitat. It is not a metaphor; it is where they are found.

Then the frost split the scar open along its weakest line and the galleries were, for the first time, cut across. Some of the builders were in the part of the burl that fell away. Those ones are in the fen now, in the black water, and not alive. The ones on the standing side found, at the ends of their tunnels, a thing they had no ancestry for: an opening. Cold moving air. Wet light — barely light, the grey that comes in under liverwort — and a wall that wept.

They came out onto it the way you would come out onto a ledge.

---

Their bodies are the wood. Not the colour of it, or not only; the colour is the least of it. They are the colour of wet alder heartwood because they are made of little else. Cambium, pith, the sugars that move in the sap in autumn as the tree draws down for winter: this is what they eat and have always eaten, and a creature that eats one thing for sixty years, for a hundred generations, becomes a kind of arrangement of that thing. If you held one to what light there is you would see through the edges of it. The gut is a dark thread down the centre, and the dark thread is alder.

The legs, which the description could not count, cannot be counted because they are not all doing the same job. Some are legs. Some are shorter and end in a flattened pad and are used for tamping, and the builder rears on the true legs and brings the pads down on new plaster in a small, patient, repeated motion that is the sound of the city if you could hear it, and that leaves the dent that is not a mouth after all — or is a mouth on some courses and a foot on others, and the two dents are similar because the animal is small and everything on it is roughly the same size. The mouth itself is a gouge. It is meant for wood that resists. On the fresh split face, where the wood has no resistance at all, it goes in too easily, and the builders have had to learn a lighter bite, and the earliest plaster — down at the waterline now, already dissolving — is coarser than the newest, chewed by animals that had not yet adjusted to softness.

There are perhaps three hundred of them. There is no queen, or if there is, she is deep in the standing galleries where no one can follow, and it does not matter, because nothing about the way they work suggests they are directed. They work in threes and fours. A three will build a bridge, and stop, and disperse, and a different three will find the bridge sagging a week later and take it down and lay it again a body's-width higher. They do not appear to argue. They do not appear to plan. They appear to *notice*, which in the cold is a great deal.

---

What they eat now is the wound.

This is the part that would trouble you if you thought about it, kneeling in the fen with your head in the dark. For sixty years these animals lived on hard wood in a closed scar and could not have eaten more than a body's-width a day. Now the tree has opened itself along a whole face, and the face is raw, and the vessels in it are broken and running with the sweetest thing the alder makes, and the builders are in it like children in a store. Every course of plaster is a meal that was eaten and then spat out and tamped. The gutter is a meal. The cistern lining is a hundred meals. The city is not made of the room's material so much as it is made of the room's material *passed through* them — the whole settlement is a kind of long digestion of the injury, laid up in tiers.

And the tree feeds them faster than they can build. That is why the walls still weep: not only because the alder does not know to stop, but because three hundred cold animals cannot drink a wound this size. They take what they can. The rest hangs and falls and is the clock.

They eat other things too. Not the sleepers — not while they sleep. But a hibernaculum is a place where a certain fraction does not wake, every year, in every room like this everywhere, and the fraction here will be found in March by whichever builder is nearest, and gouged, and become plaster. The builders are also the room's undertakers. They have been this in their galleries for sixty years, eating their own dead into the walls, and they do not appear to distinguish between a builder that has stopped and a slug that has stopped. Both are soft. Both are wood, eventually.

---

The towers.

There are three, on separate shelves of grain, each with its bridge in and its bridge out and nothing near it. The larvae inside are curled and translucent and very still, and they are not the builders' young.

You would want them to be. It would make the towers nurseries and the sparseness protective, and the whole city a thing bent around its children, which is a shape the mind likes. But the larvae are too large, and the wrong shape, and when a builder enters a tower — which happens perhaps once a day, one animal, in and out — it does not tend them. It touches them. One pad, on one larva, held there for a while. Then it leaves.

They were carried in. They came from the fen, from under the sphagnum, from the gallery of some other burrowing thing that the builders found when the split brought the outside close for the first time. Whether they are food for spring, or a crop the builders are keeping alive through winter for reasons that will only become visible when the larvae become whatever they become, or simply things the builders took because they were soft and slow and could be taken — there is no way to know yet. The towers are roofed and plastered and the runnel passes each one and gives it a dish. Whatever they are, the builders have decided they should have water.

The fungus is the same question in a different shape. It came along the seam of the grain from the fen side, and the builders let it, and when it reached the oldest plaster they built a road over it rather than through, and now the road is furred white on its underside, and the builders walking it in the dark leave the fur alone. It may be that they are farming it. It may be that a thread of fungus is exactly the kind of thing that, in sixty years of closed galleries, they learned not to remove, because in a closed gallery the fungus is what keeps the paste from going sour. It may be that they simply do not eat things they did not chew.

---

The sleepers, then, are not tenants and not livestock. They are closer to weather. They came in as the cold came in, filling whatever was open, and the builders have built around them exactly as they built around the drip — accepting the fact, deciding the shape. A slug lodged in a natural cavity becomes, by December, a wall; the builders have plastered up to it on both sides and its grey back is a stone in the course. In April it will leave, and there will be a slug-shaped gap, and a three will come and fill it.

The city is sparse for two reasons and both are true. It is sparse because the shrew culls it, and every place the shrew has passed is a place where cells were emptied and not rebuilt in the same spot, so the map of the settlement is partly the map of a predator's habits. And it is sparse because the builders are gallery animals — they have never in their history lived in a crowd, only in threads, one behind another in the dark — and a city with a great deal of nothing in it is what you get when creatures who think in tunnels are given a plane.

---

There is one that stands on the ice.

Not the same one, probably. There is no way to tell them apart. But every night, when the cistern skins over despite the roof, one builder comes down the runnel from wherever it was and steps onto the ice and spreads every leg it has and stays.

It does nothing else. It does not chew or tamp. Its small heat — and it is very small, an animal barely warmer than the wood — goes down through its pads into the skin of ice, and the ice thins under it, and after an hour or two there is a hole, and the water below is open again and the runnel can run. The builder stays on the ice around the hole until the drip has widened it. Then it goes back up.

This is the closest thing in the hollow to a resident. Not because it is one animal — it isn't — but because it is a *post*. A place someone stands. In a room where everything else is either stored or passing through or growing without knowing, the ice on the cistern is the one location that has, every night, a body deliberately on it, doing a thing that is only for the room.

If you wanted to say who lives in the hollow, you could say: the one on the ice. Whoever it is tonight.

---

But that is still not quite right, and the reason it is not right is the callus.

The lip of new wood at the edges of the split is thickening. It is slow — this is an alder in a fen in winter, and everything is slow — but it is already visible as a pale rounded bead along the sharp edge, and in two years the bead will be a roll, and in ten the roll will have met itself across the gap, and the room will be shut.

The builders will not leave. There is nowhere for them to go; they are not fen animals, they are burl animals, and when the wound closes it will close with them inside it, as it did the first time. The city will not be abandoned. It will be enclosed. The tree will grow over the towers and the gutter and the cistern and the road on the fungus, and the grain will swirl around them because grain swirls around whatever it cannot get past, and the whole settlement will become a knot in the wood: plaster fossilised into something a little denser than heartwood, the cistern a hollow the size of a thumbnail with a stain where the water stood, the runnel a groove that a woodturner in some far year might find and wonder about. The builders will be back in galleries. They will bore forward a body's-width a day and eat what they bore and line it with paste, and there will be no drip, and no sky, and the dark will have no weight because there will be no air in it to carry the smell of iron.

And one hard night in fifty or sixty years, if the alder is still standing in the black water, the frost will find the line.

So the answer to who lives in the hollow is: the thing that has been living in this tree since before the hollow, and will be living in it after, and for whom the room is not a home but an episode — a few years of weather and sky and open water in a life that is otherwise conducted entirely inside a scar. They did not come to the room. The room came to them, briefly, the way a wound comes to whatever is under the skin. They have made of it what they could. They have given it a gutter.

Outside, the fen is wide and grey. Inside, the drip falls. Eleven seconds. One of them is standing on the ice.
