Start with the wrong answer, because it is the one the room offers first.

The swallows live here. There are more of them than anything else, they have built the only thing in the room that looks built, and they come and go through the water as though the water were a door they had made. But watch one for a day and you see it is not true. A swallow is in the room for the time it takes to enter a nest, and the rest of its life is outside, in the bright, over the river, over the canopy, a mile up in the evening insects. It sleeps here. It hatches here. It does not live here any more than a hand lives in a glove. And the colony — the mass with many mouths, the thing that builds forward while the stone closes behind — the colony lives here in a way, is the right size for the place and moves at nearly the right speed, but a colony is not a creature. It has no eye. It cannot be startled. It does not know the snake has come.

Go down, then. The wasp is a tenant on a lease of weeks. The gecko is here as long as the wall is warm and will be on another wall next season. The frogs at the seep line have the stillness of residents and none of the tenure; they were spawned in the pool outside and will spawn there, and the room is a cool place to sit through the afternoon. The snails are closer. The snails are here for their whole lives, and their whole lives are written on the stone in white, and the shell in the basin is one of them, being kept. But a snail's life is a season. It does not know the ceiling.

The moss knows the ceiling. The moss is the first thing in the room that is doing what the room is doing — living at one end and stoning at the other with no line between — and if living somewhere means being made of the place, the moss lives here more than anything with legs. But moss is not a who. It has no direction except toward the light. It does not choose the room; it grows wherever the seep comes through and would grow there if the room were a road.

So look again at the empty floor. The rims, the basins, the pale dry stone between. Most of it is empty; that was the first thing. Now look at what is not empty and has not moved.

In the deepest basin — the one the fig root goes into, under the low ceiling, where the crab has its place — there is a shape on the bottom that is not the bottom. It is dark where the lime is pale. It is flat, roughly the size of a dinner plate or a little larger, and its edge is not quite the edge of a stone because it is too regular, and not quite the edge of a leaf because it is too thick. Around that edge, where the water stands and does not stir, there is a rim of white. Not the white of the floor. A thinner white, patchy, like frost that has begun on a window. It has been there long enough for the water to start on it.

Stand long enough and a part of the shape lifts. A neck, the colour of the shadow it comes out of, goes up to the surface of the basin without hurry. There is the smallest disturbance where it breaks the water, a nostril's worth. Then it goes down again. The whole thing took the time it takes a string in the veil to break and rejoin, and if you blinked you saw only that the water had trembled, which it does anyway.

A turtle. A river turtle, low-shelled, the kind that lies on the bottom and waits. It came in when it was the size of a coin, up from the river in a wet season, into the pool where the fish hang, and from the pool up the shallow spill into the basins, when the lip was lower and the veil was a hand's-breadth nearer the floor and the room was a smaller room. That is not a guess. Nothing else could have brought it. It could not have flown in and it did not walk over the bluff. It came with the water, the way everything in the room came — the leaves, the red mud, the lime itself — and unlike the rest of them it has not been laid down yet. It is the one thing that arrived and is still deciding.

It eats what the room provides. Snails, taken whole off the wet stone with a crack you would not hear over the water arriving. The things that fall through the crack in the ceiling beside the fig root — figs, when the tree fruits, dropping into the basin one at a time and floating for a moment before the shadow comes up under them. What the swallows drop. What the swallows lose: a nestling that leaves the mouth too early, a bird that the snake did not finish. The crab comes out sideways and takes the surface and the turtle takes the crab, some years, and the crab's place is empty for a while and then another crab has it. The turtle and the snake have passed each other along the base of the wall many times and have never done anything about it. They are not competitors. One of them is here for the colony and the other is here for the room.

It moves. It does move. When the rain comes and the basins fill and overflow into each other, the deepest basin is the last to change, but it does change, and the turtle rises with the water and sets itself down on a rim that was dry an hour before, and sits in the new skin of lime that the rain is laying, and when the water goes down it goes down with it. When the fig root began to whiten it was already there, and it has watched the root become a thing with a crust, and it will watch the root become a column and the column meet the floor and the basin become two basins. It shifts from one to the other. It is not sealed in. It could go out through the veil the way it came, down the spill into the pool, into the river, and be a turtle among turtles. It has not. Whether that is a choice is the wrong question to ask of an animal that breathes eleven times an hour. It is where it is.

And here is the thing that makes it the resident and not another tenant. Its life is long — long enough, far longer than a bird's, longer than the snake's, a span that in this country runs to a good many decades. And the ceiling extends by less than a fingernail in a year. Those two numbers meet. They are the only two numbers in the room that do. Over the length of one turtle, the ceiling will move out over the basin by the width of a hand. The turtle will not see that, because it does not look up. But it is the one creature here whose being-alive is the right length to contain the change. The swallows live in a room that does not move. The stone lives in a room that is nothing but movement. The turtle lives in a room that closes by a hand's width in a lifetime, and it is the only one for whom that is a fact and not a figure.

It carries the room on its back. That is not a manner of speaking. The white on the margin of its shell is the same lime that is sealing the old nests and casing the fallen leaves, and it is there because the turtle lies still in the water for longer than the water needs. Where its shell is under the surface and unmoving, the mineral finds it. Where it rubs on the rim, it comes off. So the shell is a map of how much it moves, and the map says: not much. Around the edge, and in the low places between the scutes, the room is beginning. A turtle sloughs its shell in flakes, over years, and the white goes with the flakes, and comes back. It is a slower version of what the moss does. Alive at the middle. Stone at the edge. The line moving.

It will die in the basin, probably. Nothing comes into this room to take a thing that size, and it has no reason to leave, and the years it has are the years the room has. And when it stops, it will lie where it stopped, on the bottom, in the warm clear water, and the water will do what the water does. It will go white the way the snail shell is going white. The rim of the basin will build up around it and the floor will build up under it and in time there will be a shape in the pale stone, a low dome, and the ceiling will have come down to meet the floor, and the room will be a solid thing with the shape of a turtle in it, and above that the shape of a hundred sealed mouths, and above that the fig.

So the answer, finally, is two answers, which are one.

The stone lives here. It is the only thing that will be here in a thousand years and it is alive in the sense that matters to a place like this: it eats, it grows, it takes in what falls and makes it part of itself, it has a direction and is going in it. The water is its blood and the veil is its breathing, and the whole room is the inside of its body, slowly filling in. Everything else is either food or a guest.

And the turtle lives here, inside the stone's patience, at the one speed slow enough to be a companion to it and fast enough to still have eyes. It is the stone's guest that has decided to stay for dinner and is being, very gradually, eaten, and does not mind, and lifts its head eleven times an hour to take a breath of the green air, and puts it down again.

Nothing else in the room is doing both things at once — living, and being laid down. The moss is, but the moss cannot be looked at by anyone but you. The turtle looks back. If you stand very still for a very long time, still enough to hear the drip into the nearest basin, still enough to see the ceiling has moved, the turtle will already have seen you. It will have seen you the way it sees the snake: as a thing passing through a room that is not passing through anything.
