Start with what stays, because almost everything here stays.

The tube-builders are the easiest to mistake for stone. Each animal is a soft thread the length of a finger joint, living inside a tube it has secreted from lime dissolved out of the old reef beneath it, and each tube is cemented to the tubes of its neighbors, and the neighbors' tubes to theirs, so that the spire is not a colony in the ordinary sense — not many animals sharing a site — but something closer to a single animal that has agreed to be many. At the crown of every spire the newest tubes stand open, and from them the threads extend a fan of pale filaments, and the fans overlap. Watch a spire for a while and it becomes clear that the swaying is not the water moving the spire. There is not enough current in this basin to move anything. The spire is moving the water. The whole knobbed height of it contracts and lengthens, very slightly, over the space of a minute, and the fans open and close in sequence from base to crown, and the effect is a slow peristalsis that draws a thin column of water down through the porous mass and pushes it out again at the top, carrying whatever has settled into it. This is how a filter feeder survives in a basin with no tide: it becomes the tide. Where two spires have knitted, they have also synchronized. Where a whole neighborhood has joined into that soft mass you could not push a hand through, the neighborhood breathes as one, a hectare of reef inhaling over half a minute and exhaling over the next, and it is at least possible that the low hum you hear down here — the one that might be the rock settling or your own blood — is partly this. The sound of the reef pumping itself.

What it pumps for is almost nothing. In a shelf sea the water is a soup; here it has been strained by the same mouths for centuries and there is little left in it. What falls is what dies — a blade of seagrass, a shred of weed off the drowned trees, the dust of some upstream sponge that finally gave out — and it falls slowly, and the spires take it in and grow a millimeter a year, which is very close to the rate at which the water is leaving them. It would be wrong to call this a balance. It is two slownesses that happen to match.

The sponges are not so patient. They were reef sponges once, small and bright and packed with the microscopic partners that colored them and fed them sugars out of light, and when the water went a little too fresh and a little too still the partners left. What remains is the sponge alone, bleached and hungry, and its response has been to grow. The bloated look is not disease. It is the animal enlarging its pumping chambers, throwing up more and more of that pale risen-dough tissue to move more water through itself in search of a meal that is not there. Some of the largest are the size of a curled sleeper. Press one — gently — and it will not spring back for a long time. They are, in a real sense, starving, but a sponge starves at a pace that lets it go on being a landmark for generations of everything else, and the tube-builders use them as ground.

The stacked translucent plates are different. Everything else on the dead coral is a survivor from below, making do. The glass thing is a native. It was not on the shelf, or if it was it was some hidden crust no one would have looked at twice, and it has become what it is here, in this water, under this light. Each plate is a thin lens of living tissue with a skeleton like frosted glass, and within the tissue live the same sort of light-eating partners the sponges lost — different ones, hardier, adapted to fresher water — and the plates are stacked because the thing is building toward the surface the way a tree does, layering leaf over leaf, each plate set a little off from the one below so as not to shade it. It does not need to be fed by the water. It eats the clarity. It is the one creature in the basin for which the strangeness of the place — the window-light reaching all the way to the floor — is not a hardship but the whole opportunity, and it is spreading, slowly, plate by plate, over the tops of the spires and the backs of the sponges and the dead coral heads, a low crystalline canopy over the reef that at noon throws small shifting spectra onto everything beneath it. In a few centuries it might have covered everything. It will not get a few centuries.

Then the grass.

The seagrass is the most alive thing in the water, and the least changed, because seagrass never needed the sea very much — only shallow light, soft bottom, water that is not too salt or too fresh — and it has all three. It flowers. This is worth pausing on. Twice a year the meadow releases pollen in long threads that drift through the blades like smoke, and there is nothing else in the basin that reproduces so freely or so often, and the meadow is, very slowly, advancing down into the deeper bowls as the light reaches further. The leaning of the blades, all one way and then all another, comes from three sources. The pumping reef, which sets up slow eddies at its margins. The warm floor, which sends up columns of slightly lighter water that spread and fall again at the surface in cells a few hundred meters across. And the grass itself, which sheds heat from its own leaves and bends the water around it. In the grass live the small things: snails no bigger than a barley grain, grazing the film off the blades; a shrimp-like animal gone almost colorless; worms in the sediment that turn the floor over so slowly that a stone dropped from the rim will lie on the surface a season before it begins to sink.

And the fish.

The silver one is a shelf fish, or was — one of the thin vertical hangers that once schooled in the thousands among sea-urchin spines, mimicking them. There are no urchins here now and no school. What there is instead is perhaps sixty individuals in the whole western chain, and each of them is a lesson in what a closed warm basin does to a small animal over a few dozen generations. They have become smaller. They have become stiller, because there is nothing to flee and nothing to chase and every movement costs food they do not have. They have become nearly transparent, the silver reduced to a thread along the spine, so that among the grass blades they are less a fish than a slight wrongness in the light. Their metabolism has slowed until they live, so far as anyone can tell, for decades, and they breed very seldom and produce a few large eggs rather than clouds of small ones, and they do not stray. A given fish hangs in a given patch of meadow, a few meters across, and may not leave it in a year. They eat what the grass eddies bring them — the pollen, the shed film, the shrimp when the shrimp is slow. If one dies its place in the grass is empty a long time.

There is one other kind of small fish, in the deeper grass of the eastern bowls, that has never been seen clearly. Its eyes are very large. That is all.

The larger thing in the deep blue is not a rumor. There are tracks.

In the eastern bowls, below the reach of the grass, the floor is a pale silt — the fine remains of everything that has ever settled out of the water above — and across that silt, when the light is right, run broad shallow furrows two and three meters wide that curve and loop and cross themselves and are not the work of any current. Something has been sweeping the bottom. Along the deepest sills, where the silt is thickest, there are hollows the size of a small room where it has settled and rested and risen again. And on the eastern face of the western reef, where the drowned groves give out and the drop begins, the dead coral is scoured clean in a band a meter high and the tube-spires there are broken off short, as though something large has been feeding along the edge for a very long time.

It is most likely one animal. A ray, or something in the shape of one: flat, broad, an animal that came up with the water as a thing the size of a dinner plate and has grown, in the dark warm quiet of the deep bowls, into the only large body the basin has. What it eats is the reef's dying. Everything that lets go of the spires and the sponges and the towers — every scrap that the filter-feeders above fail to catch — comes down eventually into the deep silt, and the deep silt is one animal's table. It is the basin's sole large consumer, its only scavenger, the thing at the bottom of a food chain that is really a slow rain. It is very old. It has almost certainly never seen another of its kind, or if it did, that was long ago, when there were still two, and they met at the sills. It moves at the speed of the abandonment it feeds on, and it will be here, sweeping the deepening silt, until the water is too shallow to hide it. Then it will lie in one of the hollows and stop. The divers who have glimpsed it — twice, at the edge of the blue, slow and gone — describe it as the water itself briefly having a shape.

Wider, and up.

At the margins the basin is making new inhabitants out of old ones. The reef that stands a few centimeters into the dawn air along the western sills is bone-white and dead, and it is the newest habitat in the highland. Lichen has begun on it — the same pale crust that is on the rim, coming down onto the coral from above as if it were only more rock. In the pores of the dead spires live small spiders, dark and quick, the first predators of any kind above the waterline, and what they eat is a midge that breeds in the warm water of the shallows and rises at dusk in thin columns that hang over the western bowls. The midge is the highland's one abundant animal. It has no ancestors here. It arrived on the wind, as the ferns did, and found warm water at altitude where there should have been none, and the columns at dusk are the loudest sound the place makes.

The ferns in the seams are doing what ferns do: holding the crack open a little wider with each year's roots. They came from lower slopes and are not built for this height, and they are stunted and slow, but they are patient in a different way from the reef — patient with a direction. On the shoulders of dry new land between the bowls the moss has thickened enough in places to hold a little water of its own, and in that water live things smaller than the eye. And in the westernmost bowl, where the weed-crowned tips of the drowned mangroves stand out of the water like green candles, a bird has come.

It is a small highland bird, brown, long-legged, of a kind that walks the edges of cold alpine tarns and has never in its history walked a reef. It walks this one. At dawn, before the day's rain, it steps out along the exposed white tops of the western coral and works the tide pools that are not tide pools — small warm basins left by the slow retreat of the water — for the shrimp and the snails and the stranded midge larvae, and it has learned the reef's geography, which is not difficult, because the reef does not move. It nests in the weed on the mangrove towers, above the waterline, in a crown of drowned branches that stood for centuries in a sea and now hold a clutch of speckled eggs at thirty-two hundred meters. There are perhaps a dozen pairs. They are the only creatures in the place that are clearly increasing. When the rims fail and the chain drains, and the towers stand in air and dry and fall, and the spires collapse into chalk, the ferns will have the plateau and the bird will have the ferns. It is the one inhabitant here with a future, and it is here by accident, and it does not know that.

There is no one. This should be said plainly. People come — a few, in a season, with their instruments and their air — and some stay a month, and none stays. The rim is too sharp and too young and there is nothing to grow. The band of pale rock above the waterline, the hand's width where the lichen has not yet arrived, is the closest thing to a mark anyone has left, and no one left it. It is the water's own record of its own leaving.

So the question of who lives here comes back down through the clear water to the floor, and to the thing that is harder to see because it is the largest.

The reef has been becoming one body. This is not a figure of speech. The tube-spires that knitted are pumping in unison; the sponges between them have grown into the spires' bases and share their water; the glass plates roof the whole and feed the shaded tissue beneath with what they make from light; the grass eddies feed the spires and the spires' dead feed the silt and the silt feeds the one old animal that sweeps it. A shelf reef at sea level is a city — crowded, loud, full of strangers passing. This is not a city anymore. Centuries of stillness have pressed it into a single slow organism the size of a small country's interior, one that breathes on a half-minute cycle and grows a millimeter a year and feels, if it can be said to feel anything, the water withdrawing from it at a pace exactly matched to its own metabolism, so that from inside the reef nothing is happening. It has, in the most literal way, no idea. And it is more nearly a single living thing now than it has ever been — just as it approaches the end of the conditions that let it live at all.

That is who lives here. Not the fish, not the bird, not the old flat shape in the eastern dark, though all of them do. The reef. One warm quiet body, lifted whole into the sky, knitting itself together in a clear window of water that is slowly, at a finger's width a year, letting it go. At noon the light goes all the way down and the whole of it is visible at once, rippling slightly, and the glass plates throw their small spectra, and the spires draw in and let out, and the silver thread in the grass turns very slightly, and the stone that fell yesterday has begun to be covered.
