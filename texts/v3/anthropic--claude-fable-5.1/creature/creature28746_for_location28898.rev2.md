Begin with the birds, because you cannot begin anywhere else. They are what the pit is for and what the pit has made, and the two are no longer separable.

Call them pigeons. That is what they were, and it is still the word for them. But stand at the rim long enough and one of the outsiders will come — a clean bird off some cliff on the far side of the plain, drawn in by the noise, sitting on the bricks at the top with its head cocked, looking down the way something well-fed looks into a mine — and you will see how far the pit's birds have travelled without ever leaving. They are smaller. They are darker, not by breeding but by accumulation: the dust has got into them the way it gets into everything here, into the feather and under it and into the down beneath that, so that the pale bars across the wing have gone the colour of the ramp and the sheen at the throat, when the midday sun reaches it, is not green but a sort of buried bronze. Their feet are stained to the ankle. Their eyes are the same hot orange as any pigeon's, and it is the only clean colour left on them.

They know the wall. This is the thing you cannot see from above and slowly come to understand from the ramp. Ten thousand niches, each the size of a loaf, each cut with the same blade to the same depth, ranked round a curve with no landmark on it, and every bird comes down out of the grey chimney of the evening return and goes to one hole, its hole, without pausing — a fold of the wings and a drop and the body slotting into the brick like a hand into a glove. Nobody has taught them the wall. They are born into it. A squab opens its eyes in a niche and the first thing it sees is the opposite face of the pit, the exact span of it, the particular way the light comes off a particular course of brick at a particular hour, and that is the map it will fly by for the rest of its life. If you moved it three niches over it would spend a season lost.

Each pair holds one niche, and the pit is full. It has been full for longer than anyone knows. A niche comes free only when its birds die, and so the young of the pit spend their first year or two as a kind of loose crowd along the ramp and the rim and the few broad ledges where brick has spalled and not been patched — unhoused, unpaired, fighting small persistent fights, waiting. They watch the wall the way the landless watch land. When a niche empties they are in it within the hour, and often the one who takes it is the one who was hatched in it, come back to the loaf-shaped dark it first knew, so that some holes in this wall have held the same line of birds, mother to daughter to daughter, for a span of time that would be dynasties in a city. The bones at the back of the niche are their own ancestors. They sit on them and lay on them and the eggs are warm.

Two eggs at a time, white, the size of a thumb-joint. Squabs come out of them blind and swollen and yellow-downed, all crop and beak, and for a while they are the ugliest things in the pit and the most valuable. The parents feed them a curd from the throat. You can hear it under the roar if you listen for it — a wet, urgent, repeated sound, thousands of times over, the pit digesting itself and pouring the result down into its next generation. In a good year most of them fledge. In a bad year — and there are bad years, when some sickness comes in on an outsider and travels round the spiral faster than fire — the ramp is soft with dead birds by morning and the roar drops by a third and stays down for a season, and the wall stands half-emptied like a mouth with teeth gone. Then the unhoused young come into their inheritance early, and by the next spring you would not know.

The ones who cannot fly go to the floor. A broken wing, a leg fouled in a twist of palm fibre, the simple arithmetic of age — a bird that can no longer make the climb out of the pit at dawn has a few days left, and it spends them walking. They walk the floor in the trodden dung with a strange upright dignity, drinking at the trough among the feet of the healthy, looking up at the chimney of departure with no visible regret, and then one morning they are a shape under the fig, and by the next cutting they are the floor. Nothing in the pit is wasted. Nothing in the pit is mourned. There is a way in which these birds have never been individuals at all, and it is only your eye that makes them so, and even your eye gives up after a while and lets them go back to being the roar.

The kestrel is an individual. She is the only one.

She has her brick on the rim, on the eastern side, worn pale and slightly hollowed by the grip of feet — and not only hers. Kestrels do not live long. The brick has outlasted a dozen of them, perhaps more; the pit does not distinguish, and neither, in a sense, do the birds, who have never in ten thousand generations known a sky without one small fierce shape sitting at the edge of it. She takes hers twice a day, morning and evening, out of the spinning column, and she takes them so cleanly that the column does not even flinch. She favours the outsiders when there are any. They do not know the pattern of the exit, they hesitate at the rim, and hesitation at the rim is what she is for. Her pellets lie along the eastern lip in a scatter of grey felt and small bones. And somewhere in the highest course, in a niche cut by a human hand for a pigeon, there are three speckled eggs that will hatch into things that eat pigeons. She uses the wall as everything uses the wall. The pit has no opinion about it.

Below her, and slower, the snakes.

There are three kinds, or three that you will see. The largest is thick and pale and the colour of the brick — not by coincidence — and it moves through the honeycomb as though the honeycomb were made for it, a hand's breadth at a time, pouring itself from one niche into the next while the birds on either side scream and stay. It takes squabs. It takes eggs, whole, and you can watch the shape of the egg travel down its length. It is not fast. It does not need to be. It lives in the plugged lower courses through the cold months and comes up the wall in spring like a rising damp, and the birds know it the way they know the kestrel, as a term of the arrangement. The second kind is thin and brown and quick and lives among the lizards and eats them; you will see it as a whip of movement along the sweating face at dusk and then not at all. The third is small, no longer than your forearm, blunt-headed, and lives in the floor itself, in the warm dark under the skin of the cake, and what it eats down there is a matter between it and the beetles.

The lizards are brown and small and innumerable and have taken the lowest, dampest, oldest part of the wall for their own — the part the birds have abandoned, the part where the niches have rounded to shallow dimples and the plugs of dung have set like plaster. They live behind the plugs. In the warm hours they come out along the wet brick and hold very still and take the flies that rise off the floor in a shimmering haze, and if you sit at the level of the fig you will see them below you like a second, slower, silent colony living in the ruins of the first. They breed in the cracks. They shed their tails to the thin snake and grow them back. They are the only creatures in the pit that seem to have any leisure.

Then down, into the floor. Put your hand flat on the cake near the trough and wait, and you will feel it: a shifting under the crust, faint and continuous, like a pulse in the wrist of something enormous. The floor is alive to a depth of a forearm. It is beetles mostly — black, slow, domed, moving through the compacted dung as though it were soil, which for them it is — and the white grubs of beetles, and flies in every stage, and a fauna smaller than that which has no name you would know, mites and worms and the pale quick things that live on what the mites leave. Where a bird has died and gone under, there is a knot of activity round it for a month; the cake is stitched through with these knots, a bone here and a beak there, the skull of a goat that fell in from the plain a lifetime ago and could not climb the ramp, its horns still holding the shape of the fall. This is where the heat comes from, the second heat under the sun's heat: not the birds alone but the floor itself, working, fermenting, a low fever the whole pit runs and has always run. When the people cut it and carry it away, the fields it goes to are warm for a day.

And on the birds themselves, a third pit, smaller still. Every bird in the wall carries a wall's worth of passengers — lice in the barbs of the feathers, mites at the roots of them, flat grey flies that live under the wing and never leave it, a whole population that knows nothing of the plain or the sky or the roar and for which one pigeon is the world entire, warm, enclosed, made of ten thousand small dark places. They pass from parent to squab in the nest. When the bird dies they walk off it, over the brick, to the next. It is the same arrangement, told again, at a scale where you cannot see it.

The fig came in a dropping. It could not have come any other way. Some bird ate a fig in a village garden a day's flight off and came home and the seed went into a crack at the exact height where the wall is wet enough to root in and sunlit enough to leaf, and there it is, growing sideways out of the brick like an arm reaching for something in the trough. It is alone. There is no other fig for a day's walk, and whatever small wasp its kind depends on has never found this hole; so its fruit swell and soften and drop, sweet and seedless, and are eaten by nothing that will carry them anywhere, and the tree will die when it dies and leave no descendant. It does not seem to mind. Every year it puts out those few enormous leaves, and they clap slowly in the dawn updraft, and for the hour of the exodus they are the largest living thing in the pit. The moss on the north face beside it is older and will outlast it. The grey grass in the high abandoned niches is a visitor from the plain that has found the accommodation agreeable and stays for as long as the wind lets it.

The people are the last of the residents, if they are residents. They live elsewhere — a village you cannot see from the rim, a cluster of the same mud brick, fields round it. But the pit lives in them, and they in it, at a tempo too slow for you to catch by watching. They come a few times a year, in the hour after the exodus, when the roar is gone and the wall stands bare and open-mouthed. Six or eight of them, never many. They come down the ramp barefoot, because the ramp is warm and because their fathers did, and they know its channel the way the birds know the wall. The oldest carries nothing and sits on the ramp at the level of the fig with his back to the brick and directs, or does not direct; the others cut. The scraper goes into the cake with a sound like tearing cloth, and the cake comes up in slabs, and the slabs go into the baskets, and the baskets go up the ramp on heads and shoulders, and by noon the floor is a hand lower and the trough stands proud of it and the pit smells of its own opened interior, dark and sharp and ancient. They talk little. They have a word for the place that means, more or less, oven — not for the heat, they say, but because it is a thing you put something into and take something better out of. Their clothes carry the smell home. Their children are born knowing it. The bread they eat — the bread you might eat, walking the rim — is grown on what they carry out of here, and the grain the birds pick from the stubble of those fields is what they bring back and turn into squabs and dung and the roar, so that if you follow the loop far enough round you arrive at the absurd and exact conclusion that these people and these birds have been eating the same wheat, alternately, for as long as the wall has stood.

They do not own the birds. Nobody owns the birds. They own the floor, or the right to the floor, and the right passes down in the way such things do, without paper, by the simple fact of who came last year. In a year of sickness they come down and find the ramp soft with the dead and they cut anyway and carry that away too, and the fields are the better for it, and they say nothing about it because there is nothing to say. Once, long ago, one of them patched the wall — you can see the place, a shoulder of paler brick a body-height above the trough, two dozen niches cut fresh and now full of birds as though they had always been there. It is the only thing in the pit that anyone remembers being done. Everything else was always so.

And there is the wall itself, which lives in the way brick lives, by thickening. Behind every niche the birds have ever used there is a pocket of what they left there — eggshell, down, the dry husks of squabs that did not fledge, the flat brittle skeletons of parents that died on the nest — packed and dried and packed again until it is harder than the brick around it, and in the lowest courses, behind the plugs, there are niches so full of this that the niche no longer exists; there is only a slightly different colour in the wall, a loaf-shaped region of compressed bird. The lizards live in front of it. The snakes winter beside it. It is not dead, exactly. It is the part of the arrangement that has finished moving.

Sit long enough and the categories go. The birds are the wall's way of thickening. The floor is the birds' way of feeding the fields. The kestrel and the snakes are the wall's way of keeping the birds from filling it too fast. The people are the floor's way of getting to the plain and back. The fig is a mistake the arrangement made once and has decided to keep. And underneath all of it, felt through the brick into your spine, that hum — which is not the birds, or not only the birds, but the whole thing turning, the one inhabitant with ten thousand bodies and a voice like a river heard through a door, that came into being when the first blade cut the first loaf-shaped hole and has never once, in all the centuries since, been silent for longer than it takes to eat a piece of bread.
