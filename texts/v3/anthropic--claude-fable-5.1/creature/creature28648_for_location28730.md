Start with how few of them there are.

You could take everything on this plateau that is alive without argument, gather it in one place, and it would not fill a room. The crust from around every plinth on the continent, scraped off and heaped, would make a pile you could step over. The animals, if that is the word, can be counted on two hands, and the count is disputed. The people are fewer still, and most of them go home.

That is the census. Everything that lives here lives on a margin—the edge of a shadow, the moment of a collapse, the last of the air, the thin leak of warmth from something that has forgotten its question. There is no center to inhabit. The center is the flour, and the flour does not admit anyone.

---

Whether the flour itself is a resident is something the walkers argue about in the evenings, by hand-sign, since talking costs air.

The case for: it wants. Not much, and not anything a person would recognize as a want, but each grain carries a preference—to be near these, to be away from those, to sit at this angle and not that one—and preference is the first thing life has and the last thing it loses. The structures are what a hundred trillion small wants look like when they are all expressed at once with nothing to coordinate them. The flour builds, and fails, and builds, and the failure is not despair, it is only the moment when the sum of the wants tips the other way. It has been doing this for longer than the rock has been flat. Nothing has been added to it. Nothing has been lost. It is a closed thing, complete, turning over.

The case against: it does not eat, grow, die, or remember. It is the same tomorrow as today, exactly, to the grain.

The walkers mostly settle on this: if the flour is alive, it is alive the way an argument is alive—in progress, held by no one, unable to end. It is better thought of as the landlord. Everything else lives here on its tolerance, and the tolerance is narrow.

---

The crust is the first tenant, and the surest.

Black-green. Brittle. Thinner than paper, so thin that it does not so much grow on the rock as stain it. It lives in the ring of shadow at the base of the fixed things, and it lives on the frost, which means it drinks for one minute a day, in the grey before dawn, and then not again. The rest of the time it sits under a sky the color of a bruise and takes what light comes down through the last of the air—hard light, mostly the wrong kind, the kind that at sea level would be filtered out before it did harm. The crust makes something green from it anyway. It is the only thing on this world that does.

It grows in rings because the shadow that waters it is cast by a thing that never moves, under a sun whose path barely changes. Each ring is the shadow's edge, once, and each is about a century. Lean close—the walkers do, on their knees, breathing carefully—and you see that the rings are not quite circles. They drift. The shadow's edge has crept a hair to one side over the ages and back again, following some slow nod of the world's axis, and the crust has written it down. Around the oldest plinths there are enough rings to record the whole history of the sky since the plateau went flat, at a centimeter to the century, in a line finer than a hair. It is the only writing here that is not older than its meaning.

It does not spread. If it has spores, they are lifted with the flour and ground in an afternoon. Every colony is exactly as old as its shadow. No new one has ever been seen to start.

It does not appear to do anything. It waits with a completeness that is its own kind of act. It is the one thing here that has made the frost an ally rather than a thing to draw back from, and it has made this alliance so thoroughly that it cannot live anywhere the frost does not come.

The second tenant is harder to speak of, because only one person has seen it and they were not sure.

The tracks are not in doubt. A smear a meter wide, straight through the assembling structures, and the flour will not rebuild in it for hours. The walkers cross these tracks and sometimes follow them, and they lead to plinths, and stop, and begin again on the far side.

Here is the guess, which is the best that can be done. Somewhere in the long turning-over of the flour, a structure formed that did not lose its conviction. Whether it got the arrangement right or got it wrong in a way that happened to hold, there is no knowing. But it did not slump. It kept its edges through the night, and in the morning, when the sun came and the frost went up and everything around it poured back into the general surface, it was still there. And then it moved.

It moves the way the structures move—not by stepping but by reorganizing, disassembling its trailing edge, assembling its leading one, so that at any moment part of it is arriving and part is leaving and the whole is somewhere in between. The smear it leaves is the grains that were briefly it and are now not. They lie stunned. Whatever it does to hold them together, it does not undo cleanly, and they will not build again until they have forgotten.

It has no eyes and does not need them. Everything it wants is announced by falling. Somewhere across the plain a spiral loses its nerve and begins to slump, and the flat thing, kilometers off, changes course toward it the way water changes course toward a low place. It arrives as the structure is coming apart. It passes over. It is a little larger afterward. This is the whole of its life, as far as anyone can tell: it grazes on the moment of failure, on grains at the one instant they are unsure what they want, and it takes them into a want that has already made up its mind.

At night it comes to the plinths. It is the only thing the flour makes that will cross the half-meter of bare rock. It lays itself along the shadow side, where the frost will form, and lies there heavy and still, and in the morning there is no frost on that plinth. The walkers think it drinks. They think this is what it found, the thing the rest of the flour never did: that the water it was made to fear is what holds it. The grains draw back from frost because frost undoes them. This one thing does not draw back, and it is not undone. There are patches of dark glassy rock across the plateau, unbelievably smooth, and some say they are where the frost has been and some say they are where the flat thing has lain for a very long time, and it is possible these are the same explanation.

How many there are, no one can say. The tracks are all the same width. It may be that there is one, and it is everywhere, slowly. It may be that there are several and they were the same one once. The witness who saw it said that afterward, for the rest of that night, they had the feeling one has near a sleeping animal much larger than one had thought—not fear, exactly. Awareness of the proportion.

The third tenant never comes down.

Very high, in the last of the air, long and thin and unhurried. It crosses the sun and is gone. It does not flap. It moves against the wind, which at that height is a slow cold pressure that should carry anything, and it moves against it the way a leaf in a creek can, by finding the current under the current and leaning on it. The walkers call it the crossing, because that is all it has ever been seen to do.
