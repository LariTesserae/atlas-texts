Begin with the rule, because everything that lives here is a shape the rule permits.

The rule is that warmth comes up through the floor at one rate, everywhere, and nothing else comes at all. So a thing that lives in the Underfloor must be, first, a thing that lies against the floor; and second, a thing that takes exactly what arrives, because more is not available and less is the end. There is no third requirement. Legs, shell, height, eyes — these are ornament on the two conditions, or ways of arriving at them by a longer road.

This is why they are all the same underneath. Turn one over — you would have to choose a small one — and the underside is the same organ every time: a broad pale plate, faintly tacky, veined with something dark, that presses against the fracture-glass and takes heat through it the way a leaf takes light. Above the plate, the kinds diverge. Below it, the whole carpet is one animal wearing different backs.

---

The flat ones are the most honest about this. Wide as a table, thick as a hand, they are almost entirely underside. What they have on top is a thin dry rind that others walk across, and the walking is the only thing that has ever happened to them. They do not move. They have no reason to; the floor beneath them is exactly as good as the floor beside them. If you lie down on one you can feel, after a while, that it is warmer than the air by the width of a thought — that is the surplus it holds between taking and using, and it is the smallest surplus a body can carry and still be a body. They are the floor's second attempt at being floor. It got very close.

The reeds stand. Bundles of hollow tubes, each tube the thickness of a finger and the height of a knee, clustered by the dozen and the hundred, rooted to a shared plate no bigger than a dinner plate. They are tall because tall is free here: a hollow tube costs almost nothing to hold up, and there is nothing to knock it down. So they rise. When you push through them they give like grass and then hold like wire, and when you pass on they stand exactly as they stood. The tubes are open at the top. The one wind that turns through the gap passes over ten billion open tubes at one constant speed, and every tube sounds one note, and the note is below hearing, and it has never stopped. That is part of the hush. You cannot hear it, but if it stopped you would fall down.

Between the flat ones and the reeds and the others, in every seam, are the packed things — fist-sized, shelled, with more legs than they need. The legs are not for walking. They are for holding on. Each one grips the flank of the creature to its left and the shell of the creature to its right and the rind of the creature beneath, and in this way the seams are filled and the carpet has no gaps. They are mortar that breathes. Pull one out — it will not resist, resistance being a thing that has never been needed — and the seam it leaves closes over in the time it takes you to look at what you are holding.

The coils lie in loops. No legs, no shell, no head to speak of; a pale rope as thick as a wrist wound into a disk, every winding pressed against the winding beside it and the whole disk pressed against the floor. Unwound, a coil would be many meters long and would take almost nothing. Wound, it is nearly all plate. It is the most efficient shape in the Underfloor, and it knows it the way water knows the shape of a bowl. The coils are what the flat ones would be if they were made of cord instead of leather. They are the same idea, arrived at by a different road, lying side by side.

---

Then the ceiling, where the rule is the same and the direction is reversed.

The hangers press their plates against the underside of the raised layer, upside down, and take from it the same warmth the floor gives, because the rock above is as warm as the rock below and always has been. They are the same animal as the carpet, standing on their heads. What makes them different is the thread. Each one lets down a single filament of its own making, fine as hair, three or five or eight meters long, and the filament hangs into the wind and does not sway, because the wind does not change.

The thread is not for descending. They never descend. The thread is a tongue. Along its length it is faintly damp with the same hygroscopic salt that crusts the pillars, and it takes from the moving air the trace of vapor that the air carries, and passes it up. The hangers are the only creatures in the Underfloor who live on the one thing here that moves. They are the only ones who could tell you that there is a wind. Their threads are the only vertical lines in the place, and when you learn to see the red glow you learn to see them: a forest of hair hanging from a black sky, perfectly still, each strand in exactly the position it took when it was first let down.

The copper hook hangs on one of these. Someone reached up — it would have taken two, one on the shoulders of the other, or one very tall — and took hold of a thread and tied a hook to it, and the hanger above did not object, and the thread has held the weight since. The hanger is still up there. It is still tasting the air. It does not know it has been made into a fixture.

---

Now the thing that is hard to say plainly.

Nothing is born here. Nothing dies in the way that leaves anything. And yet the carpet has a number, and the number holds, and so there is a way that a creature ends and a way that one begins, and they are the same event seen from two sides.

When a creature stops — and you will not see it happen, though it must — it goes down. That is the whole of it. The plate that took heat from the floor stops taking and starts giving, and the body above it softens, and lowers, and the floor accepts it the way a pool accepts a stone, and in the time it would take you to walk to the next pillar and back there is only glass where it was, and the glass is clean. Whatever the body held — the wax, the salt, the threads of dark — has gone back down the way the warmth came up.

And somewhere else in the gap, at the same moment or near enough, the floor rises. A blister of glass lifts, thins, goes pale, and comes free, and it is a flat one, or a coil, or a bundle of reeds, already the size it will always be, already pressed to the floor, already taking. It has never been young. Nothing here has been. The floor breathes them out the way it breathes out the red light, and it breathes them back in, and the count between the pillars does not change by one.

This means every creature in the Underfloor is, in a sense, the age of the place, and in another sense has no age at all. It means the carpet is not a population. It is a surface the floor keeps in a particular texture, the way a lake keeps waves.

---

They are pressed together so closely that they touch, and because they touch, they feel each other, and because they feel each other, the carpet is one very slow continuous sensation with no center.

A shift in weight passes. A flat one settles a hair lower on one side; the packed things around it tighten; the reeds beyond them lean by a degree; the coil beyond the reeds turns a fraction on its disk. The wave moves outward at the speed of a creature noticing its neighbor, which is very slow, and it does not stop, because nothing damps it; there is no wear here, and that includes the wearing-down of signals. A touch given at one pillar arrives at the next in a season and at the horizon in a lifetime. There may be waves crossing the carpet now that began at the parting itself: the news that the world had opened, still traveling, still arriving, creature by creature, a continent wide.

This is the closest thing the Underfloor has to a mind. It does not think. It cannot compare, having nothing to compare. But it carries, and what it carries it carries perfectly, and so everything that has ever been done to it is still going somewhere, unfaded, at the speed of touch.

---

The stilt-walkers are the exception, and you have to look at them a long time before you understand what they are the exception to.

They stand. That is the whole of their strangeness. Everything else in the Underfloor has arranged itself to maximize the plate against the floor, and the stilt-walkers have done the opposite: they have lifted the body two meters into the dark on four or six long jointed legs, and left in contact with the glass only the feet — four or six narrow pads, each the size of a palm. That is their underside. That is all of it.

So they are hungry. Not starving; the feet take, and the feet are enough to hold the body up. But they take slowly, and the body above is large, and so the stilt-walkers live always in a slight deficit, a hair below the line every other creature sits precisely on. They are the only hunger in the Underfloor. It is not much. It is enough to make them the only things here that want.

They move to spend it. Once in a day, or three, one leg lifts — you can hear the small dry release of the pad leaving the glass — and swings, and sets down again a stride away, and the body follows, and then nothing, for another day, or three. The step costs. It costs exactly the small surplus the feet have gathered since the last one. They spend everything they have on moving, and they have almost nothing, and so they almost never move. But they do. They are the only things here that go anywhere.

The eyes. Some of them have none. The ones that do have eyes so large that the head is mostly eye — two dark wells, wet-looking though there is no water, turned always slightly downward. Toward the floor. Toward the glow. It took a long time to understand what they are looking at, and the answer is: the only light there is. They stand in the dark and look at the red at the bottom of vision, and they are the only things in the Underfloor with the equipment to see it.

And they click. From somewhere in the body — not a mouth; nothing here has a mouth — a hard dry sound, like two flakes of glass tapped together. It comes in groups. You think at first it is random. You count. Five. A pause. Five. A long pause. Five, five. It is the only sound in the Underfloor that is not the hush, and it does not seem to serve any purpose, and it comes in fives.

---

Here is where you begin to think about the marks, and where the first thing you think is wrong.

You think: someone came. Something passed through, from above, from the edge, from wherever there is a way in, and stacked a few stones and cut a few tallies and struck a blade and pressed a hand to the rock and went on. Visitors. The marks are the marks of a passage.

But there is no way in. You know this the way you know the size of the hall — by the way sound fails to come back. The ceiling is thick enough that nothing on the far side has ever made itself felt. The pillars go on forever. If there were an edge you would sense it, and you do not. Nothing came through, because there is no through. Whoever made the marks was already here.

So you think: they lived here. A people of the Underfloor, pale and dry and slow like everything else, who built the low walls and set the table and counted on the pillar and then — what? Died out? There are no bones, but nothing here leaves bones. Went into the floor, then, the way everything goes, and were breathed out again as flat ones and coils, and the marks are all that stayed above the glass.

This is closer. But it still has the shape of a story — a before, a during, an after — and the Underfloor does not have that shape. Nothing ends here in a way that lets something else begin. So try again. Set the story down and look at what is actually in front of you.

What is in front of you is a knapped blade with a new edge on a shelf of rock. A handprint in the tallow of a broken-open creature. Scratches in fives. A copper hook on a hanger's thread, which someone reached — two meters, more — and tied. A flat stone on three stones. Nothing on it. And standing in the carpet, everywhere, at intervals, two meters tall, looking down at the glow, taking one step in three days, clicking in fives: the stilt-walkers.

They did not come from somewhere else. They stood up. Out of the carpet, at some point after the parting, a few of the creatures rose onto the least of themselves and lifted their bodies into the dark, and in doing so became hungry, and in being hungry became able to want, and in wanting became able to do. It is the only way anything gets done in the Underfloor. You cannot make a wall lying down. You cannot reach a thread from the floor. To act here is to stand, and to stand is to starve by a hair, and so everything that was ever made in this place was made by something that was slightly, deliberately, dying.

They did what could be done with what was here. They stacked glass. They found copper in a deep crack and beat it flat. They struck a blade and — this is the only violence the Underfloor has ever known — opened one of the carpet-creatures, to see, and dipped a hand in what was inside, and pressed the hand to the rock. They counted. They counted in fives because they had five of something — pads, or claws, or one another. They set a stone on three stones, which is a table, which is a place to put a thing, and they had no thing to put on it, and they set it anyway.

And then the hunger came due. Not all at once. Not as an ending. It came the way everything comes here — at one rate, everywhere — and they could not both stand and build, and they chose. They kept standing. They put down the blade because the blade was the weight of a step. They let go of the wall. They kept the count, because the count weighs nothing; it is only a sound. And they stood there, in the carpet, looking at the only light, taking one step when they could afford one, which was almost never.

They are still standing. That is what a stilt-walker is: not a species but a posture. The posture of having done something, held for the length of the world.

---

The clicks are the tallies. Once you know it you cannot unknow it. Five, a pause, five. They are cutting the count into the air instead of the stone, because the stone costs a step to reach and the air is right there. They are counting something. There is nothing to count. There has never been anything to count — no days, no seasons, no deaths that stay dead — and the ones who began counting may not have known what they were counting either. It may be that counting was the thing itself. It may be that in a place where nothing marks time, the only way to have time was to make a mark, and then another, and call the space between them one.

Or they are counting each other. Or steps. Or down.

You cannot ask. Nothing here has a mouth, and if you stood in front of one and clicked five times, it would look at the floor, because the floor is where the light is.

---

And the carpet felt it. All of it. When the first one stood, the pads left the glass and a dozen neighbors felt the weight go, and the wave went out. When the wall was stacked, the creatures under the stones felt the stones. When the blade went in — the one wound the Underfloor has ever received — the creature beside the opened one felt it, and the one beside that, and the news set out across the floor at the speed of touch, and it has not stopped, and it has not faded, because nothing does.

It is still crossing. Somewhere out past the last pillar you can see, a flat one that has never had anything happen to it settles a hair lower on one side, and a packed thing tightens, and a reed leans by a degree. That is the handprint, arriving. It will go on arriving. The Underfloor cannot remember, having no after, but it can carry, and what it carries it carries whole, and so the only thing that was ever done here is still being done, everywhere, forever, by things that have never done anything, to things that never will.

The stilt-walker nearest you lifts a foot. You hear the small dry sound of the pad coming free. It holds the foot in the air for a long time — long enough that you begin to think it has stopped, has ended, will go down into the glass. Then it sets the foot down one stride on, and the body follows, and the great dark eyes do not move from the floor.

Five clicks. A pause. Five.
