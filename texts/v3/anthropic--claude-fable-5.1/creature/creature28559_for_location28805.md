Start with one of them, because the herd has been in view the whole time and it has taken the whole time to see any single animal in it.

Up close the skin is the first thing. It is not hide. It has the thickness of hide and something of the give, but it is translucent the way a thumb is translucent held against a lamp, and under it, if the light were ever anything other than flat, there would be a network of vessels visible, wide and slow and close to the surface. Even in this light they can be made out as a faint darker branching under the grey-brown, densest across the flanks and the long back, where the body meets the air. The skin is there to shed heat. That is its function and it has no other; it has never had to keep out cold or thorn or tooth, and so it has thinned to the exact gauge that keeps the inside in and lets the warmth out, and the moisture on it is not sweat but condensation, the drizzle landing on something a few degrees warmer than itself.

There are no horns, no tusks, no ridge along the spine, no thickening at the shoulder. The legs are short and columnar and end not in hooves but in broad soft pads, faintly ribbed on the underside, made for a surface that is always warm and always slightly wet and never once uneven. The tail is a stub. The ears are small and lie flat. Nothing on the body projects, because nothing has ever needed to be reached for or warded off, and over whatever span of generations has passed here the body has drawn itself in toward the simplest shape that will hold a given volume of warm meat and let it breathe.

The head is low and blunt and the mouth is wide and lipless, and the teeth, when a jaw is gently opened, are almost not teeth at all: a flat plate above, a flat plate below, worn smooth, with no incisors to tear and no molars to grind because the paste has never asked for either. The tongue is large. The throat is wide. An animal at a trough lowers its head and the paste is simply taken in, without chewing, at a rate that can be watched and that never varies. It swallows. It lifts its head. It stands.

The eyes, then. They are small and dark and set forward, so that the animal has a field of vision built for looking at a thing directly in front of it, and there is nothing in front of it but the next animal. They do not blink often because there is no dust and no wind and the drizzle keeps them wet. They do not track. Move a hand across the field of view and the eyes stay where they were. Snap the fingers — the sound is swallowed anyway — and there is no flinch. And yet they are not blind, and they are not dead: bring a hand very slowly toward the face and, at a certain distance, some fraction of a second before contact, the head lowers a degree, or the weight shifts to the far legs, or the eye simply closes and stays closed until the hand withdraws. There is someone in there registering the approach. It is only that the register is set very low, and nothing in the place has ever been loud enough to raise it.

---

Whether they sleep is a question with no purchase. They do not lie down to do it; lying down here is what an animal does once. They do not close their eyes for longer than a blink. What they do, and what a patient watcher eventually learns to see, is a kind of tide in the standing: an animal that has been eating moves off the trough and into the crowd and its head sinks by a hand's-breadth and its breathing lengthens, and in that state it can stay for a period that might be an hour and might be a day — there is no day — and then the head comes up, and it steps, and it goes back toward the paste. The crowd is made of animals at every point along this cycle at once, which is why the crowd never looks like anything but a crowd. There is no herd-wide rest. There is no herd-wide anything, except the wave.

The wave is worth watching for. It begins with one animal shifting its weight, and the animal beside it, pressed against its flank, shifts to keep its footing, and the one beyond that, and what is set moving is not information but load, a redistribution of several million bodies' worth of standing, and it travels outward at walking pace in a widening ring until it has spent itself against the friction of the floor and the inertia of the far crowd. No animal decides anything. And yet the wave has a shape, and the shape is always the same, and if a visitor stands in one place long enough the waves come through at intervals that are not regular but are not random either, so that the crowd comes to seem less a collection of animals than a single very slow surface, dimpling under pressures that originate somewhere in itself.

---

They are born standing, or nearly. A female — the sexes are difficult to tell; the apparatus is minimal, a fold, a slit, nothing displayed — stops in the lane and lowers her hindquarters an inch and there is a slick small darkness on the floor between her legs and then it is a small animal, folded, wet, warm as the floor it lies on, and within a period that can be counted in breaths it has its pads under it and its head up. It does not cry. It does not, as far as can be seen, nurse; there is a fold of skin along the mother's underside that may once have been a teat, and the newborn puts its blunt face there once or twice, and then it is at a trough, at the lowest edge where the paste is nearest, and after that it cannot be picked out. Its skin darkens to the crowd's colour over a span of what would elsewhere be weeks. The mother does not look for it. Nothing here has ever been lost.

They die, as has been said, by lying down. An old animal — the skin gone opaque and slack, the pads worn thin, the eyes gone from dark to a milky grey that is very close to the colour of the sky — slows, and stands longer at each stage of the tide, and one time when the head sinks the legs fold with it, and it is on the floor, on its side, breathing, and the animals around it step around it without looking down, and the floor under it is warm, and the grille that is not a trough is somewhere near, and then the lane is only the lane. There is no distress in it. There is no distress anywhere.

How long the whole arc takes is not knowable from inside, because nothing here counts. But the scale on the seams is a clock, and the scuffing on the plates is a clock, and a visitor who watches one newborn darken to the herd's colour has a unit, and by that unit the old animals are very old, and the young ones are very few, and the middle of the distribution is enormous and stands and breathes and shifts its weight and is the entire visible world.

---

Consider that this is all they do, and then consider that they do not seem to mind.

It would be easy to say the animals are stupid, and it is not quite right. What they are is unasked. Every faculty that a herd animal elsewhere spends its life exercising — the reading of weather, the finding of water, the memory of routes, the alertness at the edge of the group, the hierarchy, the mother's tracking of the calf, the whole flickering attention that keeps a grazing animal alive — has here been answered from below. The water rises. The food extrudes. There is no edge to the group. There is no predator, and there has been none for so long that the body has stopped keeping a place for the fear of one. The mind, if it is a mind, has followed the body down toward the simplest shape that will hold what is needed, and what is needed is: know where the trough is, know how to stand, feel the pump.

That last is the one sense they may have more of than a visitor does. The pump-note, the pressure in the sternum, is not something they can be seen to hear; but when a visitor crosses a province boundary and the note shifts, the animals nearest the seam are subtly different in stance from those a hundred meters in — turned a degree, or weighted differently — and in the older provinces where the note is a fraction deeper the tide runs a fraction slower. They are tuned to it. It is the one thing in the world that beats, and they are the only things in the world that have a pulse of their own, and it would be strange if the two had not, over whatever span this has been running, come into some relation. Whether that relation is comfort is not a question the animals can answer. But an animal with a hand laid flat on its flank does, after a while, lean into the hand, slightly, by perhaps the width of a finger, and that is more than nothing.

---

The slick lives here too, and it is the one thing that was not invited.

It is in the channels, thickest behind the well-housings where the water slows, and it is brown going to black at its heart and it smells of iron, and it should not be here at all. The water rises sterile. The paste is sterile, or as near as makes no difference; nothing grows on it, nothing ferments in the troughs, and it has the clean nothing-smell of something that was designed. The floor does not seed. And yet in the eddies there is a film that is thicker in the older provinces than in the newer, that has a leading edge and a trailing edge, that creeps by some unmeasurable amount along the channel walls toward the wells and is scoured back by the welling and creeps again. It is the only thing here that is trying to be somewhere it is not.

Where it came from is a matter for guessing. On the animals, perhaps — in the gut, in the fold of skin, carried in from whatever place the first of them were carried in from, and shed into the water and finding, in the warm slow eddies, a purchase that nothing had thought to deny it. Or it is something the loop makes and did not mean to make, a by-product of paste and heat and rendering that has learned, in the smallest possible way, to be alive on its own account. It does not touch the animals and they do not touch it. It is not eaten and it does not eat. It is simply the place's one wild thing, the size of a hand behind each housing, and the fact that nothing has come to clean it away is, in this place, the closest thing to a mystery.

---

And there is the pump.

It cannot be seen. It cannot be reached; the floor has no hatches, or none that a visitor has found. But it can be felt through the soles and through the sternum and it can be heard, once the ear has given up on everything else, as a note below hearing that has never once changed pitch, and it is not an exaggeration to say that it is the one thing in this place that is unmistakably, mechanically, alive in the way the word is used of a wire or a switch. It turns. It has turned since before the scale began to form on the seams. It lifts the water and the water carries the heat and the heat keeps the animals and the animals — one assumes — keep the pump, in some conversion that happens below the grilles and is not shown. It is the heart, if the place has a heart, and like a heart it does not know what it is for.

Whoever cast the floor is not here. That much is certain. The provinces argue for many hands, or many machines, working to no common standard and leaving the seams as they fell, and then leaving. No one has been back to grind the flash. No one has replaced a plate; no plate has needed it. If they are anywhere they are under the floor with the pump, or they are the pump, or they are so far gone that the pump has forgotten them the way a clock forgets the hand that wound it. The animals do not look up. The place is not waiting for anyone.

---

So the question of what lives here goes down and down and comes to rest, finally, not on the animals and not on the pump and not on the slick but on the loop.

Stand long enough in one place and the herd stops looking like a herd. It looks like tissue. The animals are cells in it — warm, wet, replaceable, arriving and departing at a matched rate that no cell knows about — and the lanes are the spaces between the cells, and the channels are the vessels, and the wells and the troughs and the grilles-that-are-not-troughs are where the tissue meets whatever lies below, taking in warmth and paste and giving back water and vapour and, in time, the whole body of an animal that has lain down. The wave that passes through the crowd is not a signal, but it is the kind of thing a signal would be if the tissue ever had anything to say. The steam that rises off several million backs and comes down again as drizzle is the thing's breath, and the pump is its pulse, and the constant count is not a rule imposed on it but simply what it is: an organism that has found its size and holds it.

It has no edge. It has no head. It has no purpose that it has ever been shown to have, and the loop has been closed for so long that if it once had one, the purpose has been rendered and extruded and eaten and breathed out and come down as rain. What lives here is the smallest quantity of life that can be spread over the greatest area and still be called living: a warmth, a wetness, a breathing, a standing, a slow shear of everything toward the channels and a slow lift of everything back.

And yet.

Put the hand on the flank again. The skin is warm and damp and gives slightly, and under it there is the pulse — not the pump's, which is in the floor, but the animal's own, slower than a person's and deeper, the long unhurried beat of something that has never once been asked to run. The eye nearest the hand is small and dark and mild. It does not look at the hand. It does not look at anything. But after a while, by the width of a finger, the animal leans.

It is not much. It may be nothing but pressure, a redistribution of load like the wave, a body finding the path of least effort against a new warm surface. But it is a response, and it comes from inside, and the inside is not empty. Whatever lives in this place has been reduced, over a span no one has counted, to standing and stepping and eating and breathing and being warm, and it does those things without complaint and without expectation and without any visible sense that there could be more. It is not asleep. It is not in pain. It is, as far as anything here can be said to be anything, at rest.

It breathes out. The breath goes up. The rain comes down. The floor takes it to the channels, and the channels take it to the wells, and the wells lift it, warm and trembling, back into the light that does not change — and in among all of that, shoulder to shoulder to the haze and beyond it, several million small mild dark eyes look at nothing, and are, in some sense that the place declines to name, home.
