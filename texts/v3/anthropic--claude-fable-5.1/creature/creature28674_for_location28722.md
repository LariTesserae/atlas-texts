They were named already, in passing, the way you'd name the furniture. Now the tenants.

The first thing to understand is that nothing in here lives as an individual. The tree has a floor, a seam where floor meets wall, a seep, a door, a crevice at the height of a door, a band near the chimney, a corner. Each of these is a position, and each position has been filled for so long that the occupant and the place have stopped being separable. You would not say the woodlice live in the seam. The seam is what the woodlice are doing. When one dies it is not replaced; there was never a one. They are a grain on the edge of the duff, a pale scurf that pulls back a half-inch when your shadow crosses it and closes again behind you, and they are older than the char in the sense that matters, which is that they were here in the first hollow and they will be here in the last and no census of them has ever meant anything.

The ants are more like a body. One colony, rooted somewhere up in the heartwood where the ruled lines disappear, a queen who might be twenty years old and a lineage that goes back, in this tree, to a queen who landed on the still-warm char and walked in. They are not eating the wood. Carpenter ants don't. They are excavating it, gallery by gallery, in the resin-hardened heartwood the rot could not finish, and so they are the last agent of the process that made the room—the only thing in here still, very slowly, making it larger. The tree feels nothing. The tree stopped feeling anything at this depth around the time the last fire cooled. The ants are doing the work of a fire, at the speed of a glacier, and when they have made a gallery they fill it with themselves.

The slug is the janitor. It rasps the film of algae and the flakes of loosened char off the wet flute and leaves behind it a line cleaner than the one it found, and it has a territory the size of a bath towel and never leaves it and does not need to. Its gloss and the seep's gloss are the same gloss; from ten feet away you can't tell which is which until one of them moves.

The three spiders at the door are three separate animals and not three generations, though it comes to the same thing. The outermost web catches most of what comes in. The two behind it live on the leavings, and when the outermost spider dies one of them moves forward and a new one settles at the back, and so the arrangement is permanent while nothing in it is. They sit with their feet on the radial threads and they read. What they read is the roar, which is constant, and so what they are actually reading is the deviation—a moth is not a moth to them, it is a wrongness in a note that has never varied. They are the most attentive things in the tree and the only things that use the sound.

Under its plate of char, the ensatina has moved perhaps eight feet in its life, which is going on sixteen years. It is the room's senior citizen by body, if not by line. It breathes through its skin, which means it breathes the damp of the wall, which means it is nearer to being part of the tree than anything else in here—a small tan organ of the heartwood, orange-legged, with a pulse. It has never seen the slot. It has never been where the light is enough to see by. It eats what the seam produces and it has never once been dry and it will die under that plate or the one next to it, and something the same shape and the same color will be under the plate the year after, and you would not be able to tell the difference and neither would the tree.

The wren is a succession. Pacific wrens live four or five years and the crevice has been a wren's crevice for longer than that by a factor you'd have to guess at. Each one finds it as if for the first time, packs it with moss and shreds of bark on top of the compacted felt the last one left, and comes in through the slot at the top and drops sixty feet of chimney in a stitch of little falls and lands and shouts. The song is the only thing in here that is bigger than what makes it. It is also the only thing in here that is trying to be heard. The rest of the tenants keep still and let the roar cover them; the wren stands in the roar and argues, for two seconds, and loses, and does it again in an hour.

The bats are the commuters. Thirty or forty, tucked in the greasy seam near the chimney by day, and at dusk they pour out the slot and go across the highway to the mill yard, where the sodium lights bring the moths up off the river flats in clouds. The mill did not mean to do this. The mill has never once considered the tree. But it is feeding the bats, every night, three shifts, and the bats bring it back: the brown band on the char is eighty years of mill moths rendered down to guano, the only thing in here that smells of anything, a stripe of nitrogen the ants have noticed and the ensatina, indirectly, is living on. Of everything in the tree, the bats are the only residents whose lives the roar has improved.

And then the woodrat, which is where the room starts to have a mind.

Dusky-footed woodrat. One at a time. The house against the far wall—the lean of sticks and cone scales and shed bark, chest-high and as wide as a car—is occupied by a single animal, and has been occupied by a single animal, and will be, and the animals last three or four years each and the house has been building for longer than the mill has been running. Which means twenty occupants, thirty, each one born into it or inheriting it from a mother who moved on or didn't, each one certain the house was hers. She adds. That is the whole of the relationship. She goes out through the rent at night, under the culvert-limb, through the huckleberry, and comes back with something in her mouth—a stick, a lichen frond, a bay leaf, a sword-fern stem, a cone—and she puts it on the house. Inside there are rooms. A nest chamber lined with bark shredded to the texture of hair. A pantry of cone scales and stored lichen going slowly to black. A latrine, apart. Around the nest, in a ring, bay leaves, fresh ones, replaced when they wilt, because the volatile oil kills fleas, and no woodrat has ever been told this and every woodrat does it.

She is the only thing in here with taste. She is the only thing that has ever considered an object and decided about it. The ants don't choose. The slug doesn't choose. The woodrat has stood in the wet outside the rent with her nose over a bottle cap and turned it down, and stood over a redwood cone and picked it up, and every stick in that house is a judgment she or her mother or her mother's mother made in the dark.

If you were to take the house apart—you wouldn't—it would come away in layers. At the bottom, a felt of cone scales so old and so compressed it has the grain of leather. Above that, decades of bark, sticks gone soft and then hard again, the black remains of lichen. And somewhere in the upper third, small and pale and wrong, a chip: a flake of redwood two inches long with one edge machine-square, a piece of the chipper's output that blew across the highway or lay in the road and was found by a woodrat on a night's forage and judged, and approved, and carried home in the mouth and set on the house. It is the only thing in the tree the mill made. It is a piece of a tree—another one, a cousin, felled and debarked and run through the drum and reduced to this—and it is held, now, in the house of the one animal in the hollow that keeps things.

The woodrat does not hear the roar as roar. Neither did her mother. Go back far enough in the house and you reach a woodrat who noticed it start—who went out one night and the world had a new floor under it—but that woodrat is thirty layers down and her alarm is compost. For the animals living here now the sound is older than anything. The tree is just where you live. The roar was always there.

So: what lives here.

Not a population. Not a community, in the sense that word wants. Something more like a standing wave—a shape the small lives make by passing through it, each of them briefer than its position, each position older than the tree's attention. Together they are what the tree has instead of a metabolism at this depth. It stopped moving water through this wood six centuries ago, and in the space where its living used to be, other things have arranged themselves into a kind of borrowed physiology: the ants for excavation, the slug for cleaning, the spiders for the door, the bats for bringing nitrogen home from across the road, the ensatina for keeping the damp company, and the woodrat—only the woodrat—for memory. The tree does not know any of this. The tree has one job left, which is to stand, and it has been standing so long that standing has become a kind of ignorance.

And the roar is a tenant too. That is the last thing to understand about it. It is eighty years old, which makes it the youngest resident here after this year's wrens, and it has already been given a position—it is what the spiders read, what the bats follow out, what the wren argues with—and it has already been absorbed the way the fires were, and the fog is, and the ants. It will stop. Mills stop. One night the chipper will go quiet and the kilns will cool and the highway will be audible for the first time in three woodrat lifetimes, tires on wet asphalt, a sound no one in the tree has ever heard as a sound. The spiders will feel the threads go strange. The woodrat will freeze at the rent with a stick in her mouth. And then she will go in, and put the stick on the house, and the silence will begin to be what the roar was, and in thirty layers it will be older than anything.
