It is not easy to say what lives here, because the word wants a tenant and there is none. But if you stay past the first darkening, and sit in one of the upper rooms with your back to the cold inner glow of a wall, and stop expecting a door to be used, the question changes shape. Nothing lives *in* the town. Several things live *as* it.

---

Begin with the builders, since they are the ones you can hold.

Picked up, one weighs almost nothing and is slightly cool, and its flat underside pulls at your skin the way a wet stone pulls at a dry one. It does not struggle. It does not seem to register that it has been lifted; the organ at its front end, a blunt slightly darker nub no bigger than a grain of rice, goes on testing the air in front of it for a wall that is no longer there, turning a little to the left, a little to the right, the same small arc each time, patient as a compass needle. When you set it back down on the sill it resumes exactly, as though the interruption had not been an event but a pause in the material.

There is nothing inside it that you would call an inside. Held to the glowing wall it is uniformly translucent, without dark parts, and the only structure you can make out is a faint lengthwise graining, the same graining as the plates of the floor. That is the first clue to what it eats. If you follow one down a wall and across the street it will, at some point, stop on the bare floor and go quite still for a long time, and when it moves on the plates under it are gone, not scraped up but absorbed, and there is a small shallow depression in the floor with a smooth bottom. It carries this back. What it lays down along a joint or a rising edge is floor, rearranged: the same white, but taken up, dissolved in whatever the builder has instead of blood, and put down again denser and aligned. The town is not built on the basin. It is the basin, standing up.

They do not hurry and they do not rest, and they have no faces to be indifferent with, but they are indifferent in a way that goes further than any face could. Two of them, meeting on a lintel, will stop, test one another briefly with the nub — the same testing gesture they use on a corner — and then one will turn ninety degrees and go around. They treat each other as architecture. They are not wrong to.

The right angle is not something they know. You can watch for as long as you like and never see a builder measure, or sight along a wall, or refer to anything. It is more as if the angle were a property of the material at the moment of setting, the way a salt cannot help its cube, and the builder simply provides the conditions and gets out of the way. A ridge laid across a curve will, as it hardens, pull itself straight. The fringe of frost-spires at the top of a growing wall is the material choosing its edge over and over in small increments and being corrected, each spire a little wrong and knitting into something less wrong. That is why no builder needs to know the plan. There is no plan. There is only a substance that wants to be a line, and small blind things that carry it to where lines are missing.

Where they come from you cannot see. There are no small ones. There are no dead ones lying about, no husks. But in the oldest walls around the plaza, if you put your eye close to the translucence and let the glow come through, you can find, deep in the laminae, small denser nodules the size of a thumb joint, faintly grained, oriented along the joints they must once have been finishing. They did not die. They stopped in a corner and set, and the wall came up over them, and now they are grout. This is perhaps what a builder is for: to be the part of the wall that moved, for a while, before it became the part that did not. And perhaps that is where new ones come from as well, the wet skirt at the base of a rising wall thickening in one place, pinching off, a lip becoming a thing that can leave. You never see it happen. You see only that the wall is a hand higher and there is a builder on the sill that was not there before.

---

The eater is harder, because it does not want to be seen and the town helps it.

You will hear it described in your own head as *low and long*, and that is what your eyes will give you at a hundred paces: a movement close to the ground, greyer than the walls, crossing a gap and not emerging. But if you stop moving for long enough — an hour, in one of the plaza scallops, sitting on the warm spheres — you will see it nearer, and it is less an animal than a condition of the wall. It comes along the base of a building without any part of it leaving the surface; it is pressed to the stone the whole length of itself, the way the builders are pressed to their joints, and it is not grey but the exact white of the wall behind it, only matte where the wall is faintly glossy, so that what you see is not the eater but an absence of glow, a long soft unlit place that pours.

It has no front. It changes direction without turning. Where it touches the wall the wall gives, not with any violence, and behind it the sharp lines go round, and the arris of a sill becomes a bolster, and the corner of a house becomes a shoulder. It does not appear to consume anything; the scallops it leaves are bounded by clean glassy surfaces and the floor below is scattered with the little spheres, and if you gather the spheres you will find, roughly, the volume of the missing wall. It has taken nothing away. It has taken the angle out of the material and left the material behind, rounded, dull, unable to be built with — the builders never touch the spheres, will walk over a drift of them to reach a plate — and warm, as though the angle had been holding some cold in and the eater had let it go.

It is the warmth that tells you it is alive in a way the builders are not, or in a different way. The builders are cool and could be stone. The eater has heat in it. When it has passed along a wall you can put your hand where it was and feel, in the rounded stone, a faint residual glow that lasts a minute or two, and once, at the far end of the plaza, you see it stop in a scallop it has just made, and settle, and go entirely still, and the glassy inner surface around it fogs very slightly as if with breath. It sleeps in its own work. It sleeps in the only rounded rooms in the town.

You look for a mouth and do not find one. You look for eyes and are almost relieved not to. You never see more than one at a time and are never sure it is the same one, and the town gives you no way to know. There may be several. There may be one that is everywhere the walls are old. What is certain is that it prefers the oldest, densest, most translucent stone — it goes to the plaza again and again and leaves the raw new walls at the edges alone — and that it never once comes down below the curb. It does not eat floor. Whatever it wants, it wants only from what has stood up.

The builders do not flee it, and it does not take them. You watch a builder on a sill go on laying its ridge while the eater passes not a hand's breadth below, softening the base of the same wall, and neither acknowledges the other, and the ridge sets straight and the base goes round and the two conditions of the wall coexist for a while, a foot apart, until one or the other spreads. It is not a war. Nothing in the town has the temperament for a war. It is two things each doing the one thing it does, on the same material, at the same speed, forever.

---

Then the fans.

They are the only colour in the basin, if grey-green is a colour, and the only thing that looks like it was made by something that had seen the world above the rim. Flat, branching, a hand across at most, crisp enough that you expect them to crack and they do not. Their root, when you pry one up — it comes free without resistance and without damage — is not a root but a small shallow disc, smooth on the underside, and beneath it the stone is unmarked. They are not attached. They are set down. You put one back and it stays.

They grow only in angles: the inside corner where curb meets street, the meeting of two walls, the deepest reach of a doorway, and the glassy inner surfaces of the eater's scallops, where the curve is tightest. Not on flat walls. Not on open floor. Not in new scallops, either — only in ones that have begun to be refilled, where the builders have started to lay straight ridges across the round, so that the fan sits exactly in the seam between what the eater made and what the builders are unmaking. It is as if they grow in the disagreement. Where the town is simply itself, one thing or the other, there are no fans. Where it is arguing, they come.

They do not eat light, you are sure of that. Whether they eat sound you cannot decide. The silence of the town is the material's doing, all of it drinks noise, but it does seem to you, late, when the glow is up and the haze has gone slate, that the small ticks you half-hear — the crystal sounds, the settling — come more often from doorways where the fans are thick. It may be nothing. It may be that the fans grow where the ticking is because the ticking is the sound of a seam knitting, and that is where they grow anyway. You cannot tell cause from company here. Nothing will help you.

The builders route around them, unhurried, resuming the line on the far side as though the fan were a lintel. The eater leaves them untouched in its scallops, a grey-green frond standing crisp in a warm glass bowl. Whatever the two of them are doing to the town, the fans are not part of it and are not against it. They are the only thing in the basin that is simply present. It occurs to you, sitting with your back against the glow, that they might be the only thing here that would recognise you as another such.

---

There is a fourth thing, and you will not be able to see it, and you will become more certain of it the longer you stay.

The floor. The plates. Every one of them lying flat, every one sharp at the edges, none worn, though there is no wind below the rim to wear them and no water and no feet but yours. When you kneel and look along the surface at a low angle in the last of the light you can see that they are not scattered but laid — each one slightly overlapping the next in one direction only, a grain, the way scales lie on a fish, the way shingles lie on a roof. The grain runs toward the town. Everywhere in the basin, as far as your hundred paces will show you, the plates point in. And the builders, when they come down off the walls to take up floor, take it from the near side and leave the far side, so that the depressions they make are always on the side of the plate that points away, as if they were eating in the direction the floor was already going.

So it is possible, sitting there in the cold, to think of it this way. The basin is one thing. Its floor is the thing's skin, laid in scales all pointing toward a centre. At the centre the skin has stood up into walls, because that is what the material does when it is carried in and set down: it makes lines and corners and rooms. The builders are its hands. The eater is its other hand, or its tongue, or the part of it that cannot bear a corner for long, and the warmth in the eater is the only place the thing's own heat comes to the surface. The fans are not it. The fans are something else that found a footing in its seams. And the town — the streets at right angles, the doorways just taller than a person, the stairs cut to a person's stride — is not a habitation but a shape the thing takes, the way frost takes a fern's shape without having any interest in ferns.

You do not have to believe this. Nothing in the basin will confirm it. But it is the only account in which the town's patience makes sense: the patience is not the town's. It is the ground's. The ground has all the time there is, and the town is what it does with a little of it, over and over, raised and rounded and raised.

---

And the doors. You come back to the doors.

Because whatever built them had never seen a person, and yet they are the height of one, and the sills are at the height a person leans on, and the treads of the stairs are a stride apart, and the upper rooms are the size a person sleeps in with a window at the height a person looks out of, and there is not a single dimension in the town that does not fit you. You spend the last hour testing this and it holds. You lie down in one of the upper rooms and the room is exactly long enough. You stand in a doorway and your head clears the lintel by the width of your hand, which is the height of the curb, which is the width of a builder's ridge, which is the thickness of a fan.

It could be coincidence. It could be that the material has one natural module and a person happens to be built to it. It could be that the shape of a person is one of the shapes a line-making substance will fall into, given enough corners, in the same way that a certain kind of crystal will, given enough time, make something that looks like a tree. The town has never had an occupant and cannot know that occupants exist. It only has the form of waiting for one. It waits with its whole substance, in the dark, glowing very faintly from within, while two kinds of small blind creature raise it and round it, and something grey-green stands in its seams and drinks whatever there is to drink.

You are the first thing that has ever fit it. For the hours you are below the rim, you are what lives here. Then you leave by the straight line where the street stops, and the plates point in behind you, and the room you lay in is, by the time you reach the lip of the basin, a hand higher and perfectly bare and the exact size of no one.
