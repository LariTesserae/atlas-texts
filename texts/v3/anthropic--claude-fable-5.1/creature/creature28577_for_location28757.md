The easy answer is forty people, and it isn't wrong, but it stops too soon. Start with them anyway.

They come up in pairs, roped, from a road-end two days below where the last truck can still turn around, and they carry what the pit cannot make: water in sealed drums, fat, sugar, dried meat, grease in tins, gaskets, gauges, the short cylinders of welding gas. A person hauling a full load up the last five hundred meters to the throat does not think. They put a boot on the rock and then the other boot, and the summit air takes the thinking out of them and leaves the boots. By the time they reach the ladder they are something simpler than they were at the truck. Then they go down the rebar into the warm, and their lungs fill, and they come back to themselves tier by tier, and by the second catwalk they are a person again — with a name, a position, a hammock in one of the galleries — and the pit has them.

There are two kinds.

Those who rotate: three weeks in, ten days below to bleed the altitude out of their blood, then back up. They are most of the forty, and they are always half new. They have the look of people who are visiting even after a year of it. They talk more than they should and pay for it in breath. They still flinch when a plate lets go. They still, in the first days of each rotation, put a hand on the rail when the grating flexes, as though the rail were attached to something that wasn't also moving.

And those who stopped going down. Eight or nine at any time, though the number isn't fixed, because sometimes a rotator simply doesn't take the ladder when their turn comes, and no one says anything, and some months later it's understood. These are barrel-chested, slow. Their blood has gone thick and dark and their lips have a permanent dusk to them. Their voices have dropped into the floor of the chest and they use them rarely — one word where a rotator would spend ten. They have stopped being interested in rain. That is how they put it, when they put it at all: not that they can't go down, though after long enough the descent has its own dangers, but that down there the ground holds still and the sky does things and it seems, after the pit, like a place where nothing is happening.

These are the ones who listen to the steel. There is no training for it and no one claims to teach it. A long-stayer will take off a glove — the only time you'll see one bare a hand — and lay the palm flat on a prop that reads true on every gauge, and stand there while the pumps thud up through the grating, and then say a number. The number is a level and a position. Someone goes and pulls that prop, and when it comes out the thread is a hair from stripping, or the foot has crept off its bearing plate by the width of a fingernail, and no gauge in the pit would have caught it for another day. The rotators think of this as a skill. The long-stayers, if pressed, would say it's closer to hearing a familiar voice go wrong in the next room. They know the whole cage. They have slept inside its noise for years. When a piece of it lies, they know.

They keep the log. It's a ledger — actual paper, in a steel box on the fourth tier — written in grease pencil because ink dries in the pen at this altitude before it reaches the page. Every prop has a number and every number has a history: when it was driven, what it read, when it bowed, when it was pulled, what came out of the hole. The ledger goes back further than any living crew member and further than anyone can quite account for; the early hands in it are unknown, the early numbering scheme has been abandoned twice, but no one has ever started a fresh book. It's the pit's memory. The rock does not permit any other. There's no weathering here, no stain, no watermark, nothing that records what happened yesterday except steel and paper and the bodies of the people who were there.

They sleep in the galleries on the outer wall, in hammocks, because nothing is plumb and a cot walks toward the center in the night. So they sleep leaning inward, toward the work, and they dream — the ones who mention dreams — of falling not down but *in*, drawn toward a middle that is also somehow closing. They eat fat and sugar and meat and salt. They drink from measured cups. Water is the one thing the pit makes none of and the one thing everything in it needs, and the drums on the fifth tier are counted every shift by whoever holds the log.

Here is a thing about the crew that takes time to see: they are the only wet thing in the pit. The rock has never known rain and never will. The dust has never been wet in its existence. Nothing drips, nothing fogs, snow sublimes on the way down. And into this come forty bodies that are mostly water — sweating under jackets, breathing out moisture the air swallows before it can show, bleeding when a glove tears on a knife-edge fracture. Blood on that gunmetal glass dries in seconds and leaves a brown that won't come off. Those marks — on rails, on wall, on the ladder rungs — are the only weathering the mountain has ever received. The dust goes into them and in them it is wet at last. They cough it. The rotators cough gray. The long-stayers spit black, and there's a darkness at the back of their throats that no doctor at the road-end has a name for. When a long-stayer dies, and they do, the body does not go down the ladder. There's a dead fumarole on the lowest tier, a throat in the wall that stopped breathing decades ago, and they put the body in it, and over the following months the rock does what it does everywhere else and closes. The mountain, which has never had a drop of anything, takes a person. This is not spoken of as grief. It's spoken of, when it's spoken of, as the mountain getting the only water it will ever get.

But even this stops too soon, because the crew turn over and the pit does not. The people are more like weather than like residents — a front of them moving through, replaced by another front. What stays is the work, and the shape the work has worn. There's a hammer on the fourth tier whose handle has been ground by palms into a form that fits no one living but fits the job; whoever holds the fourth-tier brace position picks it up and their hand learns the previous hands. There are foot-polished patches on the grating where someone has stood to watch a particular gauge every shift for longer than the pit has kept a proper count. The positions outlive their holders. The pit is inhabited by a set of stances — crouched here, braced there, hand on that rail, eyes on that lamp — and it fills the stances with whoever comes up the ladder.

The lamps have a grammar, and the grammar is older than the crew too. One amber, steady: a bleed in progress on that level, stand clear of the vent. Two whites: water being moved. Red-red-green: clear the tier, something's going. Green alone means nothing, which is why there's so much of it. The rotators learn the grammar in their first weeks and speak it clumsily. The long-stayers speak it without looking.

The choughs speak it better than either.

This is where the easy answer really starts to come apart. A chough can live twenty years. The colony in the pit has been breeding here for generations — there are birds on the gantries whose grandmothers hatched in nests lined with stolen washers, birds who have never roosted on anything that wasn't warm steel and never will. They are the pit's actual citizens. They do not rotate. They treat the crew the way the crew treat the mountain: as a condition, an environment, something that shifts and must be read but is not, itself, company. They know that a steady amber means insects in about four minutes and they gather on the rail above the vent before the bleed begins. They know that red-red-green means a plate is coming down and they lift off before the crew have finished looking up. They know which of the forty carries food in an outside pocket. They walk the catwalks at the crew's feet with the gait of landlords.

And they have an arrangement with the wall that neither party knows about. The crust that grows on the rock — the orange-to-gray-to-black film that eats sulfur and warmth and asks for nothing else — grows thicker under the roosts. Chough droppings are, apart from the bodies of the crew, the only water that has ever fallen on this stone, and where they fall the film blooms. Under the film the springtails graze, and the spiders hunt the springtails, and the choughs eat the spiders, and so the birds are feeding the thing that feeds them, in a loop that closes on the warm pipe where they sleep pressed in rows with their red feet tucked up out of the draft.

Behind the second-level ducting the pikas live, and they are the only mammal here besides the crew. No one knows how they came. They may have been carried up — a rotator's pack, a crate of gaskets — or they may have found a thermal crack from some lower slope and followed the warmth the way everything here followed the warmth. They live on spilled food and beetles from the kiln ash and, it is assumed, on the film itself, since there's little else. They have never seen a plant. Whatever a pika is built to want, these have never encountered it and never will, and they chitter behind the ducts when the engines idle in a voice that would, three thousand meters down, mean *grass*.

Then there's the one lammergeier.

It came in through the throat perhaps six years ago, or eight — the log doesn't record birds — and hit a cable run on the way down and went through two tiers of gantry and landed on the lowest catwalk with its left wing folded the wrong way, and did not die. The crew don't know why they fed it. The first cut-off of dried meat was probably a joke. But a lammergeier is a bone-eater, and the pit is a place almost without bones, and the bird learned very fast that the figures in dust masks were where the food came from and that the pumps' thudding meant nothing and that a welding arc was to be looked away from. It walks the lowest gantry with a hop and a drag. It has been up to the fourth tier twice, by stairs, in a way no one saw. It has a name among the rotators and no name among the long-stayers, who don't seem to feel it needs one. It is the only creature in the pit that has seen the outside — seen it from above, from a height that makes even this summit look like ground — and is still here, and whether it stayed because it couldn't leave or stopped wanting to is a question that doesn't seem to trouble it. Toward the end of each afternoon it turns to face the column of gray daylight and watches it swing across the void and go out.

The long-stayers, when they say anything about it, say it's one of them.

Underneath all of this is the film, and the film is the first inhabitant and will be the last. It arrived on dust — everything here arrived on dust or on wings or on the ladder — and it found sulfur and warmth and began to eat, and it has been eating since before the first prop was driven. It's on everything. On the wall, on the steel, on the cable jackets, on the insides of the dust masks hanging in the galleries. It is very likely the black at the back of the long-stayers' throats.
