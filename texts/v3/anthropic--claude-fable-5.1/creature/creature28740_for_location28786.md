The honest first answer is that the question has already been answered, and the answer is the ground. What lives on the plateau is the plateau. You are standing on the inhabitant. It has no address because it is the address, and there is no one else to ask for.

But that answer, given quickly, flattens the thing. So stay with it.

---

Begin with the problem of number. Is it one? The tissue is continuous, the vessels interconnect, the tide moves through the whole of it as a single swell. By those measures there is one organism here, and it is the largest that has ever been described. But there is no center to it. There is no heart, no trunk, no nucleus of the plateau toward which the rest is peripheral. Cut it — if you could cut it — and each side would go on lifting water and laying down stone as though nothing had happened. It is one in the way weather is one. It is one in the way a language is one. The seam-warmth you feel under your palm is not the warmth of a body but of a boundary — the place where two stems, long ago, agreed to stop being two — and there are trillions of such agreements and none of them was ever revoked. So perhaps what lives here is not an individual but a settlement. A city with no streets, in which every citizen has grown into every neighbor and forgotten which wall was theirs.

Or perhaps individuality simply isn't a category it needs. It never had to compete with anything. It never had to know where it ended.

---

What does it sense? Very little, and that little with enormous precision.

It knows weight. The tissue flexes under you, and that flex propagates, dampens, is absorbed — but somewhere in the local vessels the pressure shifts by a fraction and the columns in that patch break a fraction sooner. Your standing there changes the note. It is not a large change. A bird landing makes one too. A heavy rain makes a much larger one. The plateau does not distinguish between these. You are a load, and loads are a thing it has been managing for its whole life, and it manages you.

It knows water: where it is, how much, how fast it can be taken. This is nearly the whole of its attention. The pores that pulse when you kneel beside them are not seeing you. They are tasting the film of condensation on your knee, finding it slightly warm, slightly salted, and taking what runs off. If you stayed, it would take the rest. Not with intent. There is no organ of intent. The pores are simply always drinking, and everything that is wet is drink.

It knows temperature — this is how it manages the mineral, laying it down in the cold interior where silica will settle out of solution and not in the warmer skin where it would clog the living vessels. It knows its own height, in some sense, because the height is what sets the tension in the columns and the tension is what sets the rhythm of the breaking. It knows, in the way a bell knows, what pitch it is.

It does not know light except as a source of the work. It does not know sound; it makes sound the way a river makes sound, without an ear turned toward it. It does not know the cloud is there, though the cloud is its own breath. It does not know the growth front is advancing — the front is simply where the youngest tissue is, and young tissue is always thirstiest, and thirst always faces outward. It does not know it is on a planet. It does not know there are lowlands. It has never encountered the lowlands, only the raw ground at its own margin, which it covers as it arrives.

---

Time for it is the tide. Nine days up, three days down; call it one breath. In one breath it grows by a few millimeters at the front and lays down a film of new mineral at the base and vents a lake into the sky. Everything it does happens at that pace or slower. Nothing in its life has ever happened fast. There has never been a fire, a flood, a frost, a fall. The most sudden event in its whole existence is the snapping of a single water column, which takes less than a millisecond and which it survives by a margin so vast that the survival has never once been in doubt.

This gives it a peculiar relationship to memory. It has no nervous tissue, nothing that could hold an impression and return to it. What it has instead is the stone. Every cell it has ever grown is down there, in mineral, in its exact shape, in the exact order it was laid. The plateau is the most complete record any organism has ever kept of itself — every year of growth preserved to the resolution of the individual cell wall, every drought in the thinning of a layer, every long wet century in a band of pale dense rock — and it cannot read any of it. The record is beneath it and behind it and it faces only forward, into water and weight. It is a memory with no rememberer. It is a diary kept by someone who cannot open the book.

Whether this is a loss depends on what memory is for. For the plateau, the stone is not the past. It is the floor. It stands on what it was, and that is enough.

---

Now the things that are in it that are not it.

The birds. Storm-blown from the coast, lost in the cloud, down on the surface, dead within a day of cold and deafness, gone into the pores within three. The soft parts go first, dissolved and drawn in as water carrying nutrients the plateau has no particular use for and takes anyway. The bones last longer, lying in a hollow while condensation runs over them, and then they too are covered as the tissue creeps and closes. What happens next is what happens to every dead cell here: the cold, the silica, the slow replacement. The bird becomes stone in the shape of a bird. It is somewhere in the upper layers now, beneath you, a few centimeters of mineral holding the exact articulation of a wing. There are thousands of these. Over the life of the plateau, tens of thousands. They are the only figures in the rock — the only shapes in two kilometers of unweathered stone that were not cells — and they are scattered through it like the only words in a very long book that are not the same word.

If the makers were ever here, they are in there too. This is not certain. It is possible they worked from a distance, seeded the thing and left. But if any of them stood where you are standing, and stayed, they went in the way the birds did, and the plateau holds them now with the same indifference: a shape, in mineral, at a depth that corresponds to a date. It would not know them from a gull. It does not know them as makers. It was built without the capacity to look up.

---

And there is one more resident, if the word will bear it.

When a water column snaps, the vessel does not simply close. It reseals — and in the instant between the break and the reseal, a bubble of near-nothing opens in the water, a cavity of vapor at a pressure far below any atmosphere. Usually the refill collapses it. But not always. In the oldest vessels, the ones on their way to becoming stone, the reseal sometimes locks a void in place, and the mineral then creeps around it and sets, and the void is kept.

So the interior of the plateau is threaded with small emptinesses. Each one is a cell-length long, a vessel-width wide, and contains almost nothing: a whisper of water vapor, a pressure that would read as vacuum to any instrument crude enough to reach it. They are the only thing on the plateau that has never been wet. They are the residue of the sound — the roar you hear is the making of these, a trillion times a second, and most are immediately unmade, and a few are not, and those few are down there in the pale rock, packed like frost, sealed, silent, waiting for nothing. If the plateau has an interior life, it is this: a body of stone honeycombed with the preserved moments of its own strain, each one a tiny report that was never allowed to finish echoing.

Whatever eventually erodes this mountain — whatever rain, in whatever epoch, finally reaches the deep layers — will open them one by one. Each opening will be a small intake of breath. It will take a very long time.

---

There is, finally, the margin. The place where the plateau ends and the exposed stone begins, the strip a few meters wide where rain is touching rock for the first time. The plateau itself has no floor and no room and nothing alive on it but itself. But the strip below the front is different. It is the only place in four thousand kilometers where there is a surface that is not tissue, a ground that is not drinking, a chance to set something down and find it still there in the morning. Soft white stone, scratchable with a fingernail, beginning to be worn.

Whether anyone lives there is a matter of definition. People come. They come to record the sound, to sample the water, to measure the advance, to lay a hand on rock that has never been rained on and feel the first rain reach it. They are deafened within a season, most of them — not fully, but in the low register, the register the plateau occupies, so that afterward they hear the world as though it has had its floor removed. They stop speaking early, because speech is useless at the front, and they develop the habit of watching hands. Some of them stay past the point that makes sense. They pitch on the white stone with the growth front a few meters upslope, wet and pale and roaring, and they move their camp a little downhill each year to keep ahead of it, and they watch the strip of eroding rock at their feet widen, imperceptibly, as the mountain that is being poured begins, at its very edge, to be unpoured.

If they are residents, they are residents of a threshold. They live on the one part of this place that the inhabitant has finished with. They are the only creatures on the whole continent who face the same direction it does — outward, downhill, into the rain — and the only ones who know that they are doing so.

---

So: what lives here.

The plateau lives here. That is the whole census, and the word is stranger than it looks. Most living things do several things — they live, and they also move, and want, and flee, and notice. The plateau does only the one. It lives the way a fire burns, entirely, without remainder, with nothing left over for anything else. It takes in water and lifts it and breaks it and lets it go, and out of the breaking it makes a sound, and out of the dying it makes a mountain, and it does not know that it is doing any of these, and it would go on doing them if there were no one on the planet to hear.

Stand in the interior. The ground gives. The pores drink from your skin. Under you the birds are stone and the emptinesses are sealed and the record is complete and unread. Somewhere at the edge, people who no longer speak are watching rain find the grain of rock. The tide is turning. The tone is starting to rise.

It is alive, and that is all it is, and there is nothing else here.
