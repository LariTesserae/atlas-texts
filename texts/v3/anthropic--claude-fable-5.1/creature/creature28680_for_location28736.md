At first you want to say: nothing. Nothing lives here. The frogs are tenants of the ditches who came for a wedding and will go back when the spawn is gone. The wren has a nest a mile off in the alders. The eel was never here at all, only passing under. Even the moss on the upper wall is not so much a resident as a rumour of one, the first outrider of the pond that will eventually own this place. You stand in the roar and the cold and think: this is a room with the door propped open, and everything in it is on its way somewhere else.

But you stay, and the staying changes what you see.

It begins at the drain. The outflow at the base of the north wall is the loudest thing here, a black mouth pouring white, and you have not looked at it properly because looking at it is like looking at a shout. When you do — when you crouch on the wet ledge and let your eyes adjust to the dark behind the spray — you see that the mouth is not clean. Its rim is fringed. Something pale hangs from the roof of the channel and lies along its floor, layered and lobed like the liverwort on the wall above, the colour of the snapped roots, the colour of things that have never seen light. You take it for a growth. Dead vegetation, maybe, caught in the flow. Then the pulse comes up through your boots and the fringe contracts.

Not much. A tightening. The lobes draw in toward the channel's centre, hold, release. Swell and drop, swell and drop, and the interval is the interval of the pumps.

You watch for a long time. The pale mass does this constantly, and you understand that it has been doing it the whole while you stood here, and that some fraction of what you called the pulse — felt in the teeth, coming up through the pool — has been this. Not the pumps. Or not only the pumps. A body in the throat of the drain, working.

It is a filter, you decide first. That is the plainest thing to think. The hole is a junction, and everything the marsh sends through it — the grey clouds of spawn, midge larvae, the small drowned, the fine brown silt of the collapse — funnels down into the drain, and something has set itself where the funnel narrows. Each contraction pulls water through the lobes. Each release lets it go. It eats the traffic. It has made itself the toll on the one road out.

But that is only what it does. Not what it is.

Look at its colour again. Not white — the brown-white of peat that has been kept from air, the same as the cut face on the walls. Look at how it lies in layers, the layers slightly offset, the way the exposed wall shows its centuries in bands. You crouch closer, close enough that the spray beads on your face, and you see that the pale fringe does not end at the channel's mouth. It goes back. It goes back into the dark as far as the light will follow it, and it goes up, too, into the peat itself, in threads finer than root hair that vanish into the black wall the way veins vanish into flesh.

And then the weeping walls mean something else. You have been watching water bead and gather and run down the whole cut face in time to the pumps, and you told yourself you were imagining the timing. You were not. The walls are not weeping because they are wet. They are being wrung. Very gently, from the inside, by a network you cannot see, a slow squeeze and release that pushes water to the surface of the peat and lets it slide. The whole chamber breathes because the whole chamber is lined with it.

So the thing that lives here is not in the drain. The drain is only where it comes to the surface, where the channel is too narrow for it to stay hidden. The thing that lives here is the void.

You knew the story: a hollow had been quietly forming for a century, and when the pumps drew the water down, the roof gave way. You took the hollow for an absence, water working on peat, the ordinary patience of erosion. But nothing here is ordinary about how it holds itself. Look at the walls, vertical and sharp after weeks of rain. Look at how little has slumped. Peat this wet should sag like bread. Something is holding the cut. Something is threaded through every fibre of it, drawing it taut, and the room you are standing in is not a wound but a chamber that was made — slowly, by something that eats a grain at a time and moves its body into the space the grain left — and had its roof taken off by accident.

It has been down here longer than the pumps. That much you feel sure of, in the way you are sure of things you cannot check. Longer than the drainage board, longer than the cuts and canals. It grew in the dark beneath the marsh at the pace of peat, which is to say not at all in any span a person watches, and it fed on what the water brought it through the hidden channels, and it did not pulse, or it pulsed the way a bog pulses, once a season, once a year, at the swell of the spring melt and the fall of the summer.

Then the engines came. Not here. North, or under, in a place it has no more seen than you have. But the peat carries their rhythm the way a drumskin carries a hand, and this thing is made of peat, or made into it, and a generation ago it began to feel a beat come up through its body that had nothing to do with the seasons. Day and night. Regular. Enormous. A rhythm from nowhere, arriving through the medium it lived in.

And it did what the frogs did, and the standing ripples, and everything else here. It accommodated. It began to move with the beat because the beat was moving it anyway, and moving with it cost less than resisting. It learned to squeeze on the stroke and release on the return. It learned, without a brain to learn with, to time its feeding to the engines. What you are watching in the throat of the drain is a century of slowness that has taught itself, in thirty years, to keep time with a machine.

And the hole — the hole is the price. Before the pumps it had all the water it wanted and grew in the direction of least resistance, which was outward, slow, sideways. After the pumps it began to move, and a thing that moves in peat opens peat, and a thing that opens peat under a falling water table brings down the roof. It did not want the sky. It was working to the rhythm it had been given and the ground came off it.

You think of that: a creature that lived for a hundred years without light, and now has a room lit from above and full of the racket of the air, of frogs and rain and wrens and a person standing in it, all because it obeyed a heartbeat it could not refuse.

It is not distressed, as far as you can tell. It is not anything as far as you can tell. The lobes in the drain contract and release. Up on the wall a pale thread the moss has half hidden lifts a bead of water and lets it fall. The rain squall comes and the whole face streams and, for the minute of the squall, the pulse in the drain does not change. It has one tempo now and does not vary it for weather.

It will be covered again. You can see that too. The rim will slump, the walls will round, sedge will grow down the slopes and out across the shallows, and in a decade there will be a pond here with a soft dark bottom that no one wades, because the marsh people know which ponds have no bottom. The thing will have its ceiling back. The frogs will come every spring, and their chorus will bend itself into the pitch of the pumps, and the spawn will drift toward the throat of the drain and be taken, and no one will know that the pond is fed and emptied by a pale body lying in the peat like a lung in a chest.

And the pumps will stop, one day. Engines that size do not run forever; the district will be drained enough, or the money will go, or the sea will come back and make the whole argument pointless. The beat from the north will cease.

You do not think the beat in the ground will cease with it. You think that something down here has learned a rhythm too well to forget it, and that long after the last pump is scrap and the last drainage cut has silted over, a person walking across the flat cold marsh will feel, in the soles of their boots, a slow regular tremor and stop, and look around at the sedge and the standing water and the alders on the horizon, and wonder what is being built out here, and by whom.
