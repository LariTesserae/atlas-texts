The first difficulty is that the place does not draw a clean line between what lives in it and what it is made of. Elsewhere you can point: this is the ground, this is the thing on the ground. Here the ground has preferences, and the preferences are the oldest inhabitants, so the census has to begin with them.

---

A grain of the flour is a fleck of pale mineral perhaps a fortieth of a millimeter across, faceted, faintly green in low sun. It has, as near as anyone can tell, three or four wants. It wants to be adjacent to grains of a certain kind. It wants not to be adjacent to grains of another kind. It wants, weakly, to lie with one facet up rather than the others. And it wants—this is the strangest—to be at a certain distance from the nearest fixed object, neither closer nor farther, and if it finds itself wrong it will spend a decade correcting the error a hair's breadth at a time.

That is the whole of its life. It never finishes. The other grains have wants of their own that conflict with it, and the slow wind moves everything a little, and the correction becomes the error becomes the correction. A grain can be watched—it has been done, with patience and a marked square of ground—for a season, and in a season it will travel perhaps a meter and end up more or less where it began, and along the way it will have joined and left eleven structures.

You could say this is not life, and you would be on firm ground. A ratchet has a preference too. But a ratchet does not build. The structures the flour raises are not random: over years of watching, the people who walk here have made a list, and the list is short. There are the concentric walls. There are the spirals, always turning the same way. There are the parallel ridges, always in sets of five or seven. There are the shallow dishes, which are rare and never quite level and slump fastest. There is nothing else. The flour has a vocabulary of about a dozen forms and it uses them in fixed proportion, roughly the same number of spirals to walls to ridges every year, across the whole plateau, as far as anyone has counted.

The proportion is the interesting part. It suggests the flour is not inventing. It is remembering—badly, at the scale of a continent—a set of things that used to stand here, in about those numbers, and are gone. The plinths and the column and the dish are the survivors. The flour is trying to put back the rest.

So the first inhabitant of the plateau is a memory, spread thin, made of grit, which cannot hold a shape for more than an hour and has not stopped trying for longer than the rock has been flat. Whether that is alive is a question of taste. It is certainly not dead.

---

The crust is alive by anyone's standard, and it is the only thing here about which that can be said without argument.

Up close it is not black-green but many-colored in a range so narrow you have to be kneeling to see it: bottle, slate, a bruised violet at the growing edge where it is newest. It is dry and rings faintly when tapped, like the shell of something. It has no roots. It sits on the bare rock in the ring where the flour will not go and takes what it needs from the frost that forms above it for one hour in twenty-four and from the light that reaches it for perhaps three, before the shadow it lives in swings off and the sun would kill it.

It lives, in other words, in a slot of time about four hours wide, once a day, and it has been living in that slot for as long as the plinths have cast shadows. A century to a centimeter. The widest ring anyone has measured, at the base of the tall column, is a little over nine meters from the stone to the outer edge, which puts the crust there at something like ninety thousand years old if it has never paused. It has paused. There are gaps in the rings—clean breaks, a few millimeters of bare rock and then growth resuming—and the gaps line up, from plinth to plinth across hundreds of kilometers, into a small number of events. Something happened to the shadow. The sun stood differently for a while, or the plateau tilted, or a haze hung in the thin air for a thousand years. The crust recorded it and went on.

There is one gap that does not line up. At three plinths within a day's walk of each other, and nowhere else, the rings show a long interruption—several meters' worth, tens of thousands of years—on the side facing the morning sun only. Something stood beside those three plinths for that long, close enough to throw its own shadow across the crust's slot and starve it, and then it went away and the crust grew back. There is no sign of what it was. The flour does not avoid the spot. Whatever stood there was not fixed, or the flour has forgotten it.

The crust does not notice the people who kneel to look at it. A boot that scuffs it leaves a mark that will be visible for longer than there have been boots.

---

The flat thing has several names among the people who walk here, none of which has stuck, because it does not come up often enough to need one. It has been called the mat, the floor, the lie, the low one. Most simply say *it* and are understood.

For a long time it was assumed to be an animal in the ordinary way, a creature that had adapted to the flour by looking like it. This is no longer the leading view. The leading view, held by the few who have spent enough nights out on the plain to have an opinion, is that it is made of flour—that it is a structure, like the walls and spirals, with the single difference that it did not fail. Somewhere back along the plateau's long boredom, one arrangement of grains stumbled on a way to hold, and having held, it held.

This accounts for a good deal. It accounts for why the structures do not reform behind its track for hours: it has taken the grains that would have made them. It accounts for why it feeds on collapses, if feeding is the word: at the moment a spiral loses conviction, its grains are looking for somewhere to go, and the flat thing is somewhere. It accounts for why it has no eyes, why it moves by reorganizing rather than stepping, why the one person who saw it was not sure afterward. It is the ground, briefly organized. It is what the flour is trying for and the only thing that ever arrived.

It is also, almost certainly, the reason the loose objects circulate. Inside it—people who have found a track fresh enough to sift have found them—are the hinges and lenses and small unweathered fittings that the flour occasionally gives up. It carries them. Not in a stomach; there is nothing like a stomach; they simply travel in the mass, drifting toward the surface over years and eventually working out onto the plain again eighty kilometers from where they went in. The plateau is turning over its own remains, very slowly, using the one part of itself that can move with purpose. Whether the flat thing has any idea of this is not a question that means anything. It is a mechanism that got up and walked.

It comes near people. This is known because people wake to find the smear a meter from where they slept, passing, and a ring of bare rock around their bedding wider than it was at dusk. It has never touched anyone. It takes things. A strap, a spoon, a bootlace left on the ground, the corner of a bag if the bag lay flat enough to be mistaken for plateau. They are not stolen so much as absorbed into the circulation, and some of them come back, years on, dropped in front of a stranger who has to be told what a spoon is for.

How many there are, no one can say. Tracks have been found on the same night four hundred kilometers apart, so there are at least two. Some hold that there is only ever a small number and that they are the same individuals, tens of thousands of years old, no older than the moment they first cohered and no younger. Some hold that they form and dissolve like the other structures, only more slowly, and that the flat thing tracking you tonight is not the one that took your bootlace in a previous year. There is no way to tell them apart. There is nothing to tell apart.

---

About the thing in the sky there is less agreement and less evidence, and it is mentioned here only because the crust reacts to it.

When the long shape crosses the sun, the crust on the sunward side of the nearest plinths dims for a moment—not the shadow itself, which is too brief and too high to matter, but a change in the crust's own color, a shift toward the violet of new growth and back, as if it had flinched. No one has an explanation. Some people believe the sky-thing is what the flat thing becomes if it holds long enough: a structure that sorted itself lighter over the ages until the thin wind took it, and that now cannot come down because there is nothing on the plain that would hold it. Others believe it is far older, and that the flat things are what it sheds. Others believe it is a piece of the plateau's instrument that was never meant to land and is still doing its one job, which is to pass between the sun and the rock at intervals, and that the crust remembers being told to mind it.

It has never been seen at night. It has never been seen twice in one day. It has never been seen to be two.

---

Then the people, who are the only things here on a human clock, and who the plateau treats as weather.

No one was born on the plateau. This has been checked, in the way such things can be checked, and it is not close: the air is too thin for it. People come up from below, over the worn edge where the highland finally meets the country beneath it, carrying what they will eat and drink for as long as they mean to stay. Most stay a season. The body does not do well here—the breath comes short, the sleep is bad, the skin dries and cracks and picks up the coin-smell of the flour until they can no longer smell it themselves—and after a season most go back down and do not come again, and those who do come again are a type.

The type is quiet, tends to be older than the first-timers, and has a project. Some follow lines: they find a groove where the flour has bared it and walk its bearing with a compass until the flour closes over it again, and mark the last known point, and come back next year to see whether the flour has opened it further along. The map of the grid, such as it is, is the work of perhaps two hundred people over several centuries and covers a fraction of a percent of what must exist. Some catalog forms, sitting for days at one place tallying spirals and walls. Some read the crust, measuring rings with a wire and a lens, matching the gaps from plinth to plinth. Some are waiting to see the flat thing. Some are waiting for a lens or a hinge to come up out of the flour in front of them. Some are not waiting for anything, and they are the ones who stay longest.

The ones who stay live at the plinths, because the plinths are the only shelter and the only water. The frost forms in the shadow for an hour before dawn; a cloth laid on the rock overnight comes up stiff with a grey fur that, folded quickly into a bag before the sun reaches it, yields a mouthful. That is the water. A lifetime here is measured in mouthfuls. The dish—the level one, the four-meter lens of frost—is the richest water source on the plateau and has, for that reason, a small more or less permanent population of two or three people who do not speak much and take turns.

They eat what they carry up. When it is gone they go down for more or they do not. The ones who do not are the ones the others argue about.

Because the flour has a rule about stillness. It respects fixed things; it draws back from what does not move. A person walking is a disturbance and the flour flows around them and closes behind. A person sitting for a day is a lull, and the flour comes close. A person sitting for a season, breathing the thin air, taking the frost, not going anywhere—around that person, slowly, the ring begins. A hand's width of bare dark rock, then two. It is not dangerous in itself. It is only the plateau extending, on the evidence, the courtesy it extends to plinths. But the people who have watched it happen say that it changes something in the one it happens to. They speak less. They stop measuring. They hold still with more conviction, the way the structures hold at the height of their form in the minute before they slump, and after enough time you find you have been walking around them without deciding to.

There are shapes at a few of the plinths, on the shadow side, that are the size of a seated person and covered in crust. The crust says they have been there for centuries. Whether they were always stone, whether they are stone now, whether anyone should sit beside them—these are the things the long-stayers argue about when they argue, which is rarely, and in low voices, because the air does not carry and the effort is tiring.

---

Below all of this, at a depth no groove reaches, something keeps the plateau a few degrees warmer than it has any right to be.

It cannot be seen or dug to. It leaves no trace on the surface except the whole surface—the frost that can form, the crust that can live in the frost's hour, the people who can breathe for a season, the flour that is not frozen into a single rigid sheet but free to move and remember. Every inhabitant of the plateau is downstream of the warmth. Remove it and the census would be a list of minerals.

It does not seem to know they are there. It does not seem to know anything. But it is the one thing on the highland that is doing, without interruption and without failure, exactly what it was made to do, and it is tempting to call that the only real life here and everything else its consequence: the flour its dream, the flat thing its dream's one success, the crust the slow record of its shadow, the people a fine weather that comes up from below in the warm months and mostly goes back down.

Tempting, but the crust is alive by any standard, and the crust dims when the sky-thing crosses the sun, and a spoon comes back to a stranger after eleven years and eighty kilometers, and none of that is required by warmth alone. The plateau is inhabited. It is inhabited by things running at four or five different speeds—the grain's decade, the flat thing's night, the crust's century, the plinth's epoch, the deep warmth's whatever—and the only speed it does not have a native for is the human one, so the humans have to bring their own and try to slow down.

At the dish, after the sun has gone and the shadows of the last structures have failed with them, one of the two or three residents is usually still awake. They sit on the rock with the frost beginning to grey the cloth beside them and listen to the sound of their blood, and now and then to a tick from the plain that might be a grain settling. Somewhere out on the smooth dark a meter-wide smear is passing, taking what it takes. The ring around the sitter is about a hand wider than it was in spring. They have noticed. They have not decided what to think about it. They are, for the moment, the only thing in a hundred kilometers with a pulse, and the plateau, which has outlasted the shape of the world, is being careful to leave a little space around them, in case they turn out to be finished.
