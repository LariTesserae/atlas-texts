The first answer is the forests, and it is not wrong, only insufficient.

They are what lives here in the sense of bulk, of tonnage, of the sheer count of cells. If you had to weigh the life of the plateau, the forests and the things pressed into their surfaces would be nearly all of it, and everything else would be a rounding error. And they are alive in the way the place is alive: without much interest in edges. A frond grazed to the stem does not react. The lives furred onto it are feeding on the lives furred onto them, and none of them seems to have kept the habit of being a single thing. Take a handful of the growth from a column and hold it close in the dim light and you will not be able to say how many organisms you are holding, or where one ends. They have worn into one another. The forest is less a population than a tissue, a continental tissue, and asking who lives in it is like asking who lives in a lung.

So you look for something else. You spend weeks looking. And in the looking you begin to see them, and then you understand that you have been seeing them the whole time and calling them something else.

---

In the stands, at the edge of vision, there are columns that are not columns.

The forest rises in dense vertical shafts, and among these, once every long while, there is a shaft that is the wrong colour — not viridian but the grey-brown of the rock, as though a piece of the ground had stood up — and that does not move with the combing of the current. You notice it because it is still in a place where everything is slowly leaning. Then a bloom clouds the water, or you part a frond, and when the way is clear again the column is a column, and you tell yourself it always was.

It takes time to stop telling yourself that.

They are tall. Not as tall as the forest; perhaps four metres, perhaps less, though height here is a poor measurement for the same reason depth is. They are narrow and upright and rounded at every edge, without angles, the way the rock is without angles, and their surface is the same pressed grainless dark. Nothing grows on them. This is the surest way to know one, in the end: in a place where every square metre of standing surface holds a hundred lives, they are bare. The furred growth comes to within a hand's width of them and stops, as it stops at the floor of the ring of stones.

They have a front and a back, and the front is turned upward.

---

The second answer, which you will hold for a while, is that they are the last of the grazers, or the first — some slower and more terrestrial form of the long pale shapes in the canopy, grounded, rooted, feeding by standing as the others feed by passing. This is not wrong either. Where one of them has stood for a long time, the growth beneath is thinned and pale, as it is under the sky pockets, and the rock is a shade warmer than the rock around it. Something is being taken. Something is being kept.

But the grazers do not notice anything smaller than themselves, and these notice you.

Not with eyes. There is nothing on the upturned front that is an eye. But when you come near one — and this can take an hour, at arm's length, in the thin places — there is a change in the pressure against your chest, a faint alteration in the hum, and you understand that you have been registered. Not examined. Registered, the way a step registers a foot. Then the pressure evens and you are, as far as you can tell, forgotten, and the front remains turned toward the far pale sheet of the surface, and does not turn toward you at all.

---

The third answer arrives when you find one at the works.

It is standing at the top of the eleven steps. Not on them — the treads are worn concave, and it stands on the flat rock just past the last one, where the stairs lead into nothing — and its bareness and the bareness of the stone are so alike that from the side you might take it for a twelfth step set on end. And you think of the low wall, straight as a rule, made of the same dark rock as the ground and indistinguishable from it except for being raised, and you wonder how long you looked at the wall before you were sure it was a wall.

You think of the carved face at the bottom of the sky pocket. Upturned. Mouth open. The features worn smooth as a pebble.

Understand that this is not a claim about ancestry. Nobody can say that the people who built on the mountains are the same people as these, or that these are people. The word has worn as thin as the others. But the summit-builders stood in thin cold air and looked down at seas that were far below, and these stand in thick warm water and look up at a sea that is impossibly high, and depth and height are the same measurement here. They are looking at the sea. They have not stopped looking at the sea. It is the direction that has moved.

---

Here is what can be said of how they live.

They do not eat, or nothing you can call eating. The warmth of the rock beneath them is theirs, or they are the rock's; the degree or two of difference is the only trade anyone has observed. They do not breathe in a way that troubles the water, but in the sky pockets — where they are sometimes found, at the very bottom, with the upward rain running over them — they do not seem troubled by the air either. They belong to both media. It may be more honest to say that the two media have worn into one thing and these are simply what lives in it.

They do not move where they can be seen moving. But they are not always where they were. One found at the doorway on one passage will be found, on the next, some kilometres off, standing at the wall, and whether it walked there or whether "here" and "there" have thinned enough for it to be at the wall without leaving the door is a question the place declines to answer. Once, in a season of near-dark, someone felt one pass. A warmth in the fronds, upright, slow, taking perhaps a quarter of an hour to go by. There was no sound. There is never any sound.

They do not speak. They may once have; the mouth of the carved face is open. But speech needs an edge between speaker and spoken-to, and edges are what this place has spent its whole unimaginable duration removing. What they do instead is stand where they stood, and turn their faces toward the sea, and be bare.

---

How many of them there are cannot be counted, and this is not a failure of counting.

You may find one at the steps, and one at the wall, and one at the ring of stones — the ring is always occupied, though never when you look directly into it — and one at the bottom of a drifting pocket, and you may feel certain these are four. Then you will find one at the steps again and be unable to say whether it is the first one, returned, or another. There is no mark to tell them by. They have been worn to the same smoothness the way pebbles in one stream are. And in the densest stands, where the thinness shows most, you may stand before one and feel yourself registered and have the clear, chest-deep sense that you are being registered by all of them, that the pressure has changed everywhere at once, that there is one of them and it is standing in eleven places.

The best estimate anyone has offered is *some*. It is understood to be a small number. It is understood also that *one* and *several* have not survived here in their old shapes, and that a small number of them is very likely the same thing as one of them, distributed, the way a single low wall is forty metres of rock and also one wall.

---

They are not the grazers, though they are perhaps what the grazers would be if the grazers stopped. They are not the forest, though they stand among its columns and are nearly one. They are not the glass-clear things of the pockets, who keep the last edge between wet and dry by dying if they cross it, and who never have — but there is a kinship there, a thin one. The glass things keep one distinction with their lives. These keep one distinction with their standing.

Because here, finally, is the fourth answer, and the one that stays.

Everything on the plateau has forgotten that it is a summit. The water does not know it is high; it lies on the rock as any sea lies on any floor. The forests do not know; they grip the rock and rise and are combed and are grazed and regrow, and they would do all of this in a trench as readily as on a height. The rock itself has finished, and a thing that has finished does not remember what it was on its way to being. The knock repeats, from no direction, and may be the plateau settling, and does not know anything.

Only these know. Only these have kept the fact of altitude — kept it the way the ring of stones keeps its floor bare, not by effort but by continuing. Their faces are turned toward the sea because the sea was once below them, and they have not agreed that it is above. They stand on the flat warm rock as if it were thin cold air. And because they stand, the works they stand beside are not ruins. A ruin is a place that has been left. These have not left.

That is what lives here. Not the lush pressed tissue of the forests, though that is nearly all of it. Not the long pale shapes that feed by passing, though those are the only things with the courtesy to be animals. What lives here is a small number that may be one, bare in a place where nothing else is bare, still in a place where everything leans, looking down at the sea by looking up — and by doing so, alone among everything on the plateau, keeping the single difference this place has not yet worn away.

In time the last sky pocket will fail. In time, perhaps, the last of these will lie down, and its length will be indistinguishable from the low wall, and the growth will close over it as it closes behind everything, without sound. Then there will be no one who knows, and the summit will be a floor, and the works will be what is left, and the place will be only one thing.

It has not happened. They are still standing. That is the whole of what they do, and it is not nothing. It is the difference.
