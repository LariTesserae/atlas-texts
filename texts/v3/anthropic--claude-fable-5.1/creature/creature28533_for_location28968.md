**Who lives in the Poured City**

Begin with the feet, because the city does.

The people here are barefoot indoors and shod outdoors, which is the reverse of most places, and the reason is heat. The floors of the sleeping-galleries are the oldest glass in any building — laid when the district was last raised, and cooled through — and they hold the warmth of the mountain the way a hearthstone does, evenly, without spite. You can lie on them. Children do. But the streets are newer and unevenly cured, and a pour that looks black and finished may be soft a hand's depth down and hot enough to take the print of a heel and keep it. So the shoes are thick, and soled with layered slag-felt that is replaced every few months, and everyone walks the same way: a short, flat, testing step, the weight held back until the front foot has reported. Outsiders call it the caster's shuffle. The casters call it walking.

Their feet are broad and the soles are pale and callused and faintly glossy, like the surface of a well-used table. Their hands are the same. They do not have the burn-scars you would expect, because they do not touch the material — no one here touches the material, ever, at any stage; it is drawn, walked, tipped, and left — but they have a particular kind of dryness in the skin, a crazing at the knuckles, that comes from a life spent between hot ground and freezing air with no moisture anywhere except what drips. They oil their hands from small iron pots and the oil comes up on the rail with the salt.

They are not tall. Generations at this height have done what generations at height do: barrelled the chest, thickened the blood, slowed the stride. Their lips have a bluish cast that they do not notice in each other and that alarms the rail-crews. They can hold a breath for a long time and they do, often, without meaning to, the way other people hum.

---

Every one of them belongs to the schedule, and this should be understood before anything else about how they live.

The schedule is not a document, or not only one. It is a spiral cut into the inside wall of the Throat-house, one turn for each district, one notch for each courtyard, and a pin that is moved along it by hand as the pours are completed. It is also a body of knowledge held by perhaps sixty people at any time, the schedulers, who spend their lives learning it and their last years teaching it and who are the closest thing the city has to a priesthood, though they would not use the word and would be uneasy if you did. What the schedulers know is where the glass is going to go. Everything else — where you live, when you move, what your children will stand on — follows from that.

So a person here can tell you, without looking anything up, roughly when their street will be filled. Not the year, usually, but the span. "This is a twelve-year court." "We're behind the Kilnwards by two turns." They speak of it the way people elsewhere speak of the seasons, as a fact of the calendar rather than a threat. A courtyard that is nine years from its fill is a young courtyard, and the families in it put down the heavier things — the cast-glass cisterns, the iron beds. A courtyard that is two years out is old, and the beds have been dismantled and carried up a storey to the district being reopened, and the people sleep on felt, and the mood is one that outsiders read as mourning and that the inhabitants would call, if they called it anything, packing.

Nobody owns a house. A house is a stage the glass has not reached yet. What people own is a right to a position in the rotation — a place in the order in which the displaced are rehoused above — and this is inherited, traded, disputed, and occasionally stolen, and it is the only property in the city that means anything. A family with a good position moves up early into a district with cured floors and working cisterns. A family with a bad one moves up late into a place where the new walls are still ticking and the drip-lines haven't settled and the water tastes worse than usual for a year. Nobody starves for a bad position. But people have killed over one, quietly, in the low-voiced way things are done here, and the schedulers know which families have done it and account for that too.

---

The casters are the ones who make the pours, and they are fewer than you would think — a few hundred in a city of some tens of thousands — because the pour is slow and does not need many hands, only patient ones.

A caster's work is mostly watching. A ladle-walk takes a day, and the caster walks beside it, on the gantry, at the speed of the gantry, which is the speed of the steam, which is the speed of the mountain. They watch the ladle's skin for the color-changes that mean the material is cooling too fast or too slow, and they signal back along the gantry to the valve-hands with the gesture-language, and the valve-hands adjust the heat under the ladle by a hair. There is nothing else to do for most of the day. They do not talk. Talking uses air. They watch the ladle, and the shadow of the spoke on the wall, and the sky in its slot overhead going from dark blue to darker, and they think whatever a person thinks who has walked beside the same heat for thirty years.

When the ladle reaches the pour-site — a courtyard emptied of everything but its walls, its doorways bricked to the height of the last fill — the caster does not tip it. A scheduler tips it, or rather a scheduler gives the sign and a valve-hand throws the counterweight, and the caster stands at the courtyard's edge and watches the material go in. It does not splash. It is too heavy and too slow. It comes out of the lip of the ladle the color of the inside of a peach and it goes into the courtyard the way honey goes into a cup, folding on itself, and it finds its own level and lies there, and the light comes off it and up the walls and onto the faces of everyone watching, and this is the one moment in the day when the city is bright.

Casters' faces are distinctive. Decades of standing at the edge of pours have cooked them to a particular deep, even, leathered brown from the chin to the hairline, sharp-edged where the felt hood was, and the eyes in that brown are startling — pale, most of them, because the ones with pale eyes see the heat-colors better and have been chosen for the work for generations. They squint even in the dark. They have the calm of people who have watched the same thing happen ten thousand times and have never once been able to stop it.

---

Then the engineers, who are the readers.

The engineers live in the maintenance shafts — not literally, but they might as well; they go down in the morning and come up at the bell and they smell of cold glass and lamp-oil and they blink in what passes for daylight. Their work is the old fills: the bands. They keep the shafts open through the accumulating glass the way a miner keeps a drift open, casing them, extending them downward as the city rises, and they walk them with lamps and they look at the walls.

What they see is time. Each pour leaves a band, and a band tells you the year the Throat ran high or low, the year a bad seam came up, the year the schedulers rushed and the glass cooled with bubbles in it. They read the bands to know how the mountain is doing. It is the only way to know. There is no other record; there was never a time before the mountain to have kept one in. So the engineers are also the historians, and the history they keep is written in a language of thickness, color, inclusion, and fracture, and it goes back as far down as the shafts go, which is farther every year, and stops at a depth where the glass is so old and so black that the lamps cannot find a band in it and it might as well be the original stone.

The engineers do not believe the stories about the lambs. This has already been said. What has not been said is that the engineers have a story of their own, which they do not tell the schedulers, and it is this: that at the bottom of the deepest shaft, in the bandless black, there is a level where the glass changes texture — becomes, very slightly, less like glass and more like something that was never liquid. They have not been able to say what. They have cored it and the cores look like the glass above. But the ones who have put a hand on it say it is cold, and nothing else in the city is cold that is not in the air.

---

The cistern-keepers are women, mostly, and old, mostly, and they are the reason anyone can drink.

A cistern is a basin of cast glass set under a heavy drip-line, and it will fill in a day if the line is good and in a week if it is not, and the water in it will boil off in an afternoon if the glass under it is too new. So the cistern-keeper's skill is placement. She knows the drip-lines of her district the way a farmer elsewhere knows which fields flood, and she moves the cisterns by hand on slag-felt sledges — an hour's work for a meter, done in the dark before the drip starts — and she knows when a line is dying because the iron above it has been re-tied, and she knows when a new one is coming because a roof-ridge has cooled, and she is never wrong, and when she is wrong a courtyard goes thirsty and does not forget it.

The keepers meet at nightfall on the parapets to watch the cloud-tops and to judge tomorrow's drip from the color of the sky. They speak more than anyone else in the city, in low voices, and it is from the keepers that the children get whatever stories there are. It was the keepers who named the lambs.

---

The children are the same as children, and different.

They lie on their bellies on the clear streets of the Kilnwards and look down. This has been said. What they see, they draw — on the frosted grey of the newest fills at the city's edge, with the point of an iron nail, before the surface cures. Doorways. Hinges. The letters of shop-signs in an alphabet that is theirs but with forms their grandparents' grandparents used. And figures in the doorways, always, whether or not the adults agree the figures are there. The drawings last a few months and then the fill blackens and they are gone, and the children draw them again farther out.

They are taught the gesture-language before they are taught to speak aloud, because speaking aloud is a thing you do indoors, with people you trust, in the good air near the warm floor. Outdoors you sign. A child of six can tell you across a courtyard, without a sound, that the pool by the third pier is boiling, that the drip on the eastern line has stopped, that a lamb has moved, that a stranger is on the gantry. The signs for heat are made low, near the body. The signs for cold are made high. The sign for the Throat is a hand held still.

They are not afraid of the glass. They are afraid of the sky, a little — the dark hard blue that the old people say is darker — and they are afraid of the rail, because it goes down into the cloud and nothing that goes down into the cloud comes back the same. The rail-crews, frost-shaken, hollow-eyed, gasping in the summit air, are the city's ghosts as far as the children are concerned. The people under the glass are only neighbors who left late.

---

Now the ones under the glass.

The schedule is strict and everyone knows it, and every courtyard is walked before a pour by a scheduler and two casters with lamps, calling — not shouting; calling — and looking in every room, and no one has been left in a fill in the memory of anyone living. This is true. It is also true that the memory of anyone living is not long, at this height, and that the clear pours in the Kilnwards are older than that, and that there are three figures, at least, that nearly everyone agrees on.

One stands in the doorway of what was a bakery, or a place where grain was handled; the sign is legible. One is seated at the top of a stair. One is small.

The engineers have gone down to them, in the shafts, and come as close as the shafts allow — a hand's width of black glass, no more — and put instruments to the wall and found nothing they would call a body. Slag inclusions, they say. The shape a bubble takes when a pour cools around a beam. They are probably right. The keepers say the three are the ones the city stands on, in the way a house stands on its footings, and that if you filled them in with opaque glass — poured a black pour over the clear streets, which has been proposed — the city would come down. This is nonsense. The proposal has not been carried out.

There is a fourth figure that only some of the children see, and it moves.

---

The lambs.

Here is what is known. They are the size of a large dog, pale, warm, eyeless, slow. They press themselves to the seams between pours and the seams under them go matte and etched, so they eat, or they do something to the glass that resembles eating. There are perhaps two hundred of them, and there have been perhaps two hundred of them for as long as anyone has counted, and no one has seen one born and no one has seen one die, and no one has seen two of them together in a way that looked like anything but two lambs lying near each other on a warm seam.

Here is what is done. When a fill approaches them, they are moved. Two casters lift a lamb between them on a felt sling — it is heavy, heavier than it looks, dense as the glass it lies on — and carry it at the caster's pace to the eastern shoulder, or to whichever cool old fill the schedulers have marked as lamb-ground this turn, and set it down on a seam, and it settles against the seam as if it had always been there, and the casters go back for the next. It takes days. The pour waits.

Here is what is thought, by the keepers, and told to the children, and not repeated where the engineers can hear.

The mountain is not a mountain. It is a thing the lambs are making. The heat comes up because they need it to; the Throat swells because they are drawing on it; the city pours because the lambs cannot pour and need it done for them, and so the city was called up here, long ago, before the bands, out of whatever green country is under the cloud — called the way the birds are called, on the heat, circling higher — and set to work, and has been working since, and does not know it. The pale translucency at a lamb's edge, the keepers say, is because a lamb is not entirely here. Most of it is in the glass. What lies on the seam is only the part that eats.

The engineers say this is nonsense.

The engineers move the lambs first. They move them very carefully. When a lamb is set down on new ground, the engineer who supervised the move will stand beside it for a while, longer than the work requires, with one hand near it — not on it — the way a person stands by a sleeping animal to see that it goes on breathing. Then they go back down the shaft.

---

The birds have been described. Add only this: the children have a sign for them, and the sign is the same as the sign for the rail-crews, and the same as the sign for someone who has been moved to a district they did not want. It is a hand rising and turning over.

---

And there is one more inhabitant, which is the mountain, and whether it lives depends on what you mean.

It is warm. It swells. It has a Throat and the Throat has a rate, and the rate has not varied in living memory, and it has walls that are read like scripture and a bottom that no one has reached, and it holds in it every street the city ever laid and every roof and every hinge and three figures at least, and it is fed — glass into glass, the same material forever — and it grows, a hand's width a year, toward a sky that goes on darkening, and it has never once done anything sudden.

The people do not say it is alive. They do not have a sign for that. They have a sign for the Throat, which is a hand held still, and they make it low, near the body, with the signs for heat, and the children learn it before they learn to speak, and when they make it their faces are the faces of people indoors, near the warm floor, with those they trust.

That is who lives here. Casters with cooked faces and pale eyes. Schedulers who know where the glass is going. Engineers with lamps, reading downward. Keepers on the parapets at dusk, moving basins a meter at a time. Children on their bellies looking into the past and drawing it on the future with nails. Two hundred lambs, or the parts of them that eat. Three figures, or four. Birds that do not land. And under all of it, warm, patient, without a finished state, the thing they stand on, which has come this far.
