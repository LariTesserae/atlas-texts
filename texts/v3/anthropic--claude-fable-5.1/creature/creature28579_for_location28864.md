What lives in the Pith lives briefly, or lives elsewhere and visits, or is the Pith itself. There is no fourth kind.

---

Start with the smallest, because they are the most numerous and because they explain the rest.

The beetle-things in the tears are not beetles. They have no wings and no true shell; what looks like a carapace is a skin of hardened sap they have secreted around themselves, clear at first and clouding as it thickens, so that an old one — old meaning eight or nine days — looks like a bead of milk with legs. They are the size of a fingernail. They are never seen anywhere but in fresh sap, in the raw wet channel of a new split, and they are always there, hundreds of them, within an hour of the tear opening, though nothing has been seen to arrive. This is because they were already inside. Their eggs are laid deep in the wound of a tear in its last hours, when the walls are closing and the sap is thickening to a jelly, and the eggs are sealed into the tissue with the parents, and the parents die, and the eggs wait. They wait in the white. When the plant surges again in that place — a week on, a month, a year — and the tissue splits along the old weakness as tissue does, the eggs are in the sap, and warm by a hair from the friction of the tear, and they hatch. The dark specks that hang in the walls, the ones that fade as the tissue thickens over them, are graveyards and nurseries at once, and no one can tell which spot is which.

The people eat them. You lift a beetle from the sap and it tastes sharp, faintly of the sap it was swimming in, faintly of something else, resin and cold. You eat them by the handful because there is nothing else to eat by the handful. They are called *first-in*, as in the first thing into a mouth, and the name is a small joke, because they are also the last thing out of one.

---

The slug that eats frost is drinking, not eating. It cannot take water from the sap directly — the sap is too rich, too thick with whatever the plant carries in it — and so it waits for the cold to pull the water out and set it as needles on the wall, and then it moves over the needles and takes them in. It moves at the speed the frost forms. Faster, and it starves; slower, and the frost cracks off ahead of it. A slug that has found a good draft can live its whole life on one wall, making a slow circuit, coming back around to where it started to find the frost renewed. When the wall grows and the draft shifts, it must find another, and this is when you see them crossing the open floor, glass on white, visible only as a moving smear where the floor's own frost has been cleaned away.

Their organs are amber because the sap-water they take in carries a trace of whatever colors the deep plant, and it concentrates in them, and when they die it is the last thing to go. A dead slug is a slick on the wall and then a rusty stain the size of a hand and then nothing, absorbed.

The people call them *cleaners*, and they eat them too, but sparingly — a slug is mostly water, and eating one is like eating a very cold, slightly bitter mouthful of nothing. What they want is the film the slug leaves. It does not freeze. Where a slug has passed, the sap-beads on the wall stay liquid an hour longer than they should, and the people scrape the film up with a thorn and rub it on their hands and faces and the joints of their tools, and it keeps the frost off. A group that has found a slug has warm hands for a day. This is worth more than the meat, and it is the only thing in the Pith that is bought and sold in something like a market, when groups meet: slug-fat, kept in a sap-cast cup with a fiber lid, traded for thorns.

---

The flyer is argued about, and the argument is the closest the people have to a philosophy.

Nobody has seen it land. That is the first fact. It is seen far up, always far up, where the brightness starts to become a condition instead of a color, and it is grey against the white, a slip, a mark, a movement, and then it is not. It makes no sound. It has never been seen from the side — only from below, a shape that might be wings or might be a body edge-on or might be a scrap of something torn from the vaults and falling very slowly. It does not fly the way anything with a home flies. It does not circle, or return, or drop toward the floor. It crosses. That is all it does. It crosses the breath from one edge of seeing to the other and it is gone.

One party holds that it is many: a species that lives in the vaults, up where the tissue is thin and the light unbearable and the small fast breaths open and close too quickly for anything to walk, and that what is seen below is the occasional one that has strayed downward and is trying to find its way back up. This is the practical view, and the people who hold it are the ones who count and measure things.

Another party holds that it is one. One animal, very old, the second old thing in the Pith after the big grazer, that has been crossing and recrossing the same breaths since before anyone alive, and that it is grey because it is the only thing here that has had time to become grey — to weather, to wear, to be worked on by the light. In this view its silence is the silence of something that does not need anything from anyone. The people who hold this are the ones who read fog.

A third party, small, holds that it is not an animal at all. It is a piece of the plant that has come loose, or a piece the plant has released — a seed, though no one here has seen a seed and the word they use for it means *sent thing* — and it is falling, and it has been falling since before anyone alive, because the breaths are so tall and the air so still that a thing light enough could fall for a lifetime. In this view it will one day reach the floor, and the people who hold this view say that when it does they will be somewhere else, and shrug.

There are no fights about the flyer. It is not that kind of argument. It is something to say across the ring at night, when the waker is awake and someone else cannot sleep, and it is said in the same flat tone as everything else, and no one expects to win.

---

The big thing that comes up from below is called *the elder*, and the three vertical cuts the people leave where it has fed are the same three cuts they use, elsewhere, to mean *older than this wall*.

It is not seen well because it is never in a hurry and because it comes up through the floor. There is no warning. A place in the floor of a breath — a low place, usually, where water has pooled — darkens, and the darkening spreads and deepens as though a shadow were forming in a place that has never had a shadow, and the people who see this leave, not running, walking, because it does not matter how fast you go, it is not coming for you. The floor splits. This is the elder's tear, and it is unlike the plant's own tears: cleaner, and slower, and the sap that runs from it is warm. Not warm the way a body is warm. Warm the way the deep breaths are warm, a degree above the killing cold, a degree that here feels like a fire. And then a shape comes up out of the split that the people describe only in pieces, because no one has stood close enough to see it whole. A back like a wall, white or grey-white, the same color as everything. A mouth, or the place where a mouth must be, because the wall goes away in front of it in bites the size of a tent, of a house, of a small breath, with a sound like a very large slow sigh. No eyes that anyone has found. No legs — it does not walk, it rises and settles and rises. It feeds for an hour, perhaps two. Then it goes back down the way it came, and the split closes over it, and the place where it fed is a raw ragged scoop in the wall, wet and glistening and already frosting, that heals within the day.

Where it has fed the air is warm for a while. Sap runs that would otherwise have set. The people, having left, come back to the edge of the warm and stand in it and do not go closer, and when it fades they cut the three lines and move on.

The rudeness is this: the elder is old. It is the only living thing anyone here has met that is certainly, visibly older than a month, older than a year, older than the walls, older than the shape of the breaths, perhaps as old as the light. It comes from where the plant is thick and slow and dark, and it has been feeding on the plant since before the plant hollowed itself this high, and it will go on when this breath is a wall and the wall is a floor and the floor is deep tissue no one will ever cut into. To walk on ground it has recently touched would be to stand in something you had no right to. That is the whole of it. It is not fear. It is manners, held toward the one thing here old enough to deserve them.

---

And there are the people.

They are small. Not stunted — long-boned, in fact, with narrow hands and long feet — but light, because everything they own is on their backs and everything they eat is cold and thin and everything they do is walk. A full-grown one weighs what a child of the surface might. They are pale in the way of things kept from shadow: not white but a kind of unfinished color, a paleness that has never had anything to contrast with. Their hair, where they have it, is the color of the fiber. Their eyes are the darkest thing about them, and they are held nearly shut — not squinting, exactly, but narrowed permanently, the lids grown thick and hooded over generations, so that an adult of the Pith seen from the front seems to be looking at you through two cuts. A child is born with wide eyes and learns to close them. The word for a face held against the brightness is the same as the word for a face at rest.

Their hands are the other thing you notice. The Pith is sharp, and there is nothing to make gloves of that stays supple in the cold, and so their palms and fingers are a thickness of scar over scar, cut and healed and cut, until the hands look carved. They can hold a raw torn edge without bleeding. They can put a hand flat against a wall for the minute it takes to feel it push back, and then take the hand away, and the fiber that has begun to grow around the fingers tears loose without their noticing.

They walk in groups of six to a dozen. Larger groups cannot be fed; smaller ones cannot keep a waker awake through every sleep. There is no one in charge of a group in any way that holds still long enough to name. There is usually one who is best at reading the fog — at knowing, from the way a bank leans and thins, where the drafts are and therefore where the water will pool and therefore where the frost will be thick enough to scrape — and the group tends to walk in the direction that one faces. There is usually one who is best at the walls, at knowing from the look of the growth-ridges where the next tear will open and how long a floor will hold. These are not offices. They change when the people change.

Kinship is loose because the plant makes it loose. A group is walking along the base of a buttress and a mouth opens ahead of them — a wall goes, a wind comes through — and when it has passed, four of the group are on one side of the new gap and eight on the other, and the gap is a hundred meters of ragged edge that will be ten kilometers of air by morning, and the wind is still coming through it hard enough to knock a walker flat. They call to each other across it. They agree, in a few words, where each half will try to be in ten sleeps — a landmark that will not exist in ten sleeps, but it is something to say. Sometimes they meet again. Mostly they do not. There is a word for someone last seen on the far side of a wall that has since closed; it is the same word as the word for a lens of water buried in the white — *held*. Not lost. Held. Still there, somewhere in it, only not where you can reach.

Children are born walking, or near enough. A mother carries the infant in a sling of woven fiber against her chest, and the infant learns the floor by feel before it learns it by sight, learns the faint constant push of the ground rising under a body that stays still too long, and learns to shift. The first thing a child is given is a thorn. The first thing a child is taught is to cut a mark in a wall and walk on and not look back to see what the wall does to it.

---

They eat all day and never much. There is nothing to cook with — nothing here is dry enough to burn, and the people speak of fire the way they speak of the surface, as a thing reported from very far down, where the sap thickens to something that will take a spark, and possibly not true. So everything is cold and raw. The sap itself, licked from a wall or drunk from a cupped hollow: cold, sweet, then sour, then something behind the sour like resin, a taste that stays in the mouth for an hour. The inner fiber of a fresh tear, which is soft and wet and can be chewed like a rind while the tear is still open; they take it in strips and eat it walking. First-in by the handful. Cleaners for the fat. Frost, scraped off in a fur and melted in the mouth, which is the cleanest water there is and the coldest, and which they drink standing still for as short a time as they can manage because drinking frost makes you cold and staying still makes you held.

They sleep in a ring, on their sides, curled, so that the growth has the least of them to close around. Someone is always awake. This is the waker, and the waker's whole task is to lie with a palm flat against the floor and feel it, and when the floor begins to lift — when the ring of pressed fiber under the sleepers begins to rise and stiffen and the first sleeper mutters and shifts in the beginning of being held — the waker says one word and the ring stands up and moves twenty paces and lies down again. Two or three times a sleep. They do not fully wake to do it. A person of the Pith can stand, walk twenty steps, and lie down without surfacing from a dream, and does, most nights of their life.

A person who sleeps alone, without a waker, does not always wake. The people find them sometimes — a shape in a wall at floor level, curled, the fiber grown over and through, a darkening in the white with the outline of a body. They cut a spiral beside it. A spiral means *someone is in this wall*.

---

Their dead go into the tears. There is no other way to put a body anywhere, since the floor will not stay a floor and there is nothing to dig with and nothing to dig in. When someone dies the group looks for a split that is open, or waits for one, and lays the body in the wet channel in the sap, and stands beside it while the tear begins to close — this is the one time they stand still on purpose for longer than it takes to eat — and when the edges have thickened over the body they cut a spiral and go. The body is visible for a while as a shape in the white, then a shadow, then a darkness the size of a person, then a fainter darkness, then nothing. Within a month the wall is blank.

They are not much grieved. This is not hardness. It is that grief needs something to be about that stays where you put it, a place to go back to, and there is no going back to anything here; the wall with the spiral on it has moved and the spiral has stretched into a curve into nothing. So the grief is short and it is spent walking. A name is said for a few days in the ring at night and then said less and then held. There is a word they use for the dead which is also the word for the frost that formed this morning and cracked off before noon, and it is not a sad word.

What they are instead of grieving is — the nearest thing is *attentive*. They watch. They watch the walls the way people elsewhere watch the sky. They notice the small changes in the ridges that mean a tear is coming, the shift in the fog that means a draft has moved, the faint darkening deep in the white that is a water lens or a body or a nest of first-in eggs and might be any of the three, and they say to each other what they see, constantly, in a low steady murmur that is most of their talk. There is a word they use to each other, an ordinary word, said in passing as you would say *good morning*, and it is the closest thing they have to a term of affection, and it means *still here*.

---

Their signs are not directions. It took the surface-minded a long time to understand this. A ladder of pegs to a ledge that has gone. Three cuts for the elder, which is already deep. A spiral for a body that is already dissolving. A ring of stakes for a camp that has already lifted. None of it says *go this way* or *water here* or *safe*, because none of that will be true by the time anyone reads it. What every sign in the Pith says is the same thing: *someone was here*. That is all a mark can mean on a wall that will not stay a wall. And they make them anyway, constantly, everywhere, thousands upon thousands of small cuts and pegs and scrapes across a floor the size of a country, not because the cuts will help anyone but because a people cannot walk through a place for their whole lives and leave it untouched. It is not in them. It is not in anyone.

Every so often one of them stops and begins to carve something that is not a sign. A curve. Lines coming off it. They know as well as you do what will happen. They do it anyway, for an hour, which is a long time, and then the floor starts to hold their feet and they stop and walk on.

---

The people trade when they meet, which is rarely, and the meeting has a shape. Two groups sight each other across a floor as pale smears in the fog, and each stops, and each sends one walker forward, and the two walkers meet in the middle and squat and lay out what they have. Thorns, always — the thorns come from somewhere far down or far off, some region of the plant that grows them, and no one alive has been there, and every thorn has passed through a hundred hands. Slug-fat in cups. Blades of dried tear-edge, which hold a point for a few days and are worth something new. Cord. News, which is worth nothing and traded anyway: where a mouth opened, where the elder fed, where water was, all of it already false. And names. Groups exchange the names of their held — the ones on the far side of closed walls — in case the other group has met them. Sometimes they have. The word for that is the word for a tear opening on an old seam: *again*.

---
