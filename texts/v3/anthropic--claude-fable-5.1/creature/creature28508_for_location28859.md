The honest answer is that the ground lives here, and everything else is a guest of it.

But that is a way of not looking. Kneel and part the surface with your hands and the mat stops being a single thing. On top is the moss, which is what you see from standing height: a pale, tight, close-cropped green that goes white at the crowns in the light and holds a drop of water on every tip. It is not one species. There are at least six that the people can tell apart by the feel under a boot — one that gives like felt, one that resists like a scrubbing brush, one that is barely there and lets you sink to the shin. Between them, in low domes a hand high and an arm across, are the cushion plants: hard, dense, thousands of stems packed so tight the surface is like a bald head, and you can stand on one without leaving a mark. People do. They cross the plateau stepping dome to dome where the domes are close, the way you would cross a stream on stones. In the hollows where water pools for a moment before moving on there are liverworts, flat and lobed and glossy, the exact green-black of a wet slate roof.

And then there is the other thing, the thing the description shrugs at — "things that are none of those exactly." The people call it the leather. It is a thallus, a growth without stem or leaf, dark brown going to black, the texture of an old glove that has been wet and dried a hundred times, and it grows in patches from a few meters to a few hundred across, flat against the moss and partly under it. Pull a piece up and its underside is rust-coloured and threaded with something finer than root, a felt of white hyphae that goes down through the brown layers into the cinder itself and comes back stained. It is not a plant in the way the moss is. It is a partnership, an alga or something like one riding on a fungus or something like one, and what the fungus is doing down there in the warm ash is eating stone. Where the cinder is richest in metal, the leather grows thickest. It concentrates what it finds. Burn a patch of it and the ash is heavy and a dull metallic grey and will not blow away.

The people found this out the way people find things out — by noticing that the bores that came up rich had been sunk through black ground. Now the rigs go where the leather is. A man walks the plateau for a week with a map and a knife, cutting small squares from the darkest patches and putting them in numbered tins, and where the tins come back heaviest the cribwork goes down. The plant that got here first and holds the mountain together is also the plant that shows the men where to open it. Nobody who works here finds this ironic. It is simply how the place is read.

Under the green, under the leather, the mat goes on down. A hand's depth of living tissue, then a hand's depth of brown — last year's growth and the year's before, dead but whole, still doing the work of holding water — then a layer that has gone to something like peat, black and fibrous and cold, and then, a meter or so down, the cinder. It is warm. Not hot; the warmth of a body, of a thing that was recently alive. Water is moving through all of it, down and sideways, all the time, and in that water is a whole population that no one on the plateau has ever seen and some of them suspect.

In the film that coats every moss stem there are rotifers and tardigrades and nematodes and single cells with tails, the same creatures that live in every wet moss on every roof in the world, but here they have a continent. In the brown layer there are mites the size of a grain of pepper, and springtails that flick away from a parted stem so quickly they seem to have never been there, and a pale worm, translucent, thin as thread, that lives among the leather's white felt and is found nowhere else. And there is the midge.

For fifty weeks of the year the midge is a larva, a curved comma of a thing lying in the water in the brown layer, eating what falls to it. Then the cushion plants flower. All at once, across the whole plateau, the hard domes go pale pink for five days, and in the same five days every midge on the tableland comes up through the moss and takes to the air. The people have a sign for it: both hands lifted, fingers shivering, brought up past the face. The air above the mat thickens. Walk through it and it moves aside for you like a curtain of gauze and closes behind. The midges do not bite. They do not appear to eat. They do the one thing, from dome to dome, in a haze you can see from a rig two kilometers off as a faint blur over the pink, and on the fifth or sixth day they die in their billions and settle into the mat and the mat closes over them and eats them, and that is the year's one feast for everything that lives in the brown layer, and the green resumes.

Which is what the birds are waiting for.

The birds were not here before the engines. Everyone agrees on this, though no one was here before the engines either. They are small — sparrow-sized, a little less — and so dark that against the moss they read as holes. Their wings are long for their bodies, and they do not perch so much as land flat, bellies down, on any surface that is warm: the boiler housings first, the pipe runs second, and when there is nothing else the black shadows of the towers on the moss, which are a degree or two warmer than the light. They nest in the dead rigs. A boiler shell gone orange with rust, half-swallowed, tipped on its side, will have a dozen pairs in it, deep in the cavity where the firebox was, and the cribwork of an abandoned pad is a tenement. The people do not disturb them. It is not sentiment. They have simply never had a reason to.

What the birds eat for the other fifty weeks is not clear. Springtails, when they can get them. Whatever the drill brings up. Scraps from the bunkhouses, certainly; they come to the doors. There is a persistent belief among the men that the birds eat the leather's white felt, and a man will show you one pecking at the underside of a lifted flap, but it might as well be after the pale worms.

The birds make no sound. Or: no one can hear them. The roar is a wall and a bird is a bird. But a boiler tender, years ago, caught one that had come down the stack half-stunned by heat, and held it in his two hands to warm it, and felt its chest going — not the heartbeat, which was a blur, but something slower and shaped, a pulse and a stop and a pulse. It was singing. He let it go and it went, and he made the sign for it that night to the others, a fist held against the breastbone and opened and closed, and the sign has stuck. The birds sing. Everyone knows it. No one has ever heard it and no one ever will, and so the song exists here entirely as a thing that was felt through a palm and is passed hand to hand.

The people, then. Forty, give or take. Fewer in the cold season.

They live in three clusters of sheds on cribwork pads, the same construction as the rigs, pushed down through the mat until the timber sits on cinder, and the sheds are built of the boards the tram brings up, and they are wet. Not leaking; wet. Everything is. Blankets, walls, the pages of the tally book, the inside of a boot that was dry when it came off the tram car and was never dry again. Wool is the only thing that works, and wool here has a smell like a wet dog and a sheep in the same room, and it is the smell of the people, and they stop noticing it in a week the way they stop noticing the roar. They dry what they can on the boiler housings. The birds shift over to make room and shift back.

They are not a type, except that they are all people who could bear this. Some came up from the lowland mines because the money is better, and stayed, and now cannot say why. Some came up because they were already deaf, and had heard that here it would not matter, and it does not — a deaf man here is at no disadvantage whatever, and several of the best hands on the plateau have never heard the roar and do not miss it and are faintly amused by the others, who are going deaf slowly and know it and do not talk about it. The hand-language they use is nobody's from below. It began as the signs a lowland crew would use across a noisy shed, and it has grown here for as long as the rigs have been running, and it has words now for things that have no words in the tram car going down. There is the sign for the roar itself, which is a flat hand held perfectly still at the level of the eyes. There is the sign for the mat sagging, where cinder has shifted underneath — two hands cupped, lowered a little, the fingers curling. There is a sign for a rig's beat gone out of true, a fist pounding the air and then stumbling. There are signs for four kinds of moss. There is a sign, the shivering fingers, for the midge-week, and there is a distinct sign, palms up and turning, for the pink, and they are not the same sign, and a new arrival will be corrected if he confuses them.

They walk slowly because the mat makes them. Each step sinks, pools, releases. A man in a hurry here looks foolish and is soon wet to the knee. So the whole plateau moves at a walk, and the walk is a kind of thinking, and people who have been up for years have a gait that persists in the lowlands — a deliberate lift and place, as if the pavement might give. They carry poles. The poles are for the flag-signals across the flat, and for testing ground where the leather is thick and the cinder underneath may have opened, and, at the rim, for nothing at all, because no one goes near the rim who does not have to. The rail layer has to. He walks the spurs out to where they end in midair and re-sets the rails a hand's width back from the edge every year as the cinder lip crumbles under the falling water, and he is the one man on the plateau who has stood at the top of the curtain and looked down into it, and he says the roar is quieter there, at its source, than it is in the middle of the flat, and no one believes him.

They sleep in the roar. That is not hard; it is the same at night as by day, and the body takes it as the body takes its own blood. What wakes a boiler tender is his engine stopping. The heartbeat in the exhale, the one slow stroke he has been half-hearing through the wall for months — when it falters, when the beam pauses at the top of its lift and does not come down, the absence of it goes through the shed like a shout and he is up and out with his boots unlaced before he knows why. A man can sleep through the engine at the next rig failing. His own, never.

Food comes up the cable. So do boards, and coal, and a new man now and then, and letters that arrive damp and are read in the shed doorway and dry into ridges. What goes down is ingots, loaf-sized, dull, laid in the car in rows, and nothing else. This is exactly true. When a man dies here — and men have; a boiler let go once, and there was the winter the cinder opened under a pad — he is not put in the car. There is a place, not far from the middle cluster, where the mat is a little higher than the mat around it in four or five long low ridges, and no one remembers being told what they are, and no one needs to be. The moss over them is the same as the moss anywhere. In a season the mat closes over anything.

What they think of the mat is complicated and they do not put it into signs. They are careful with it, because it holds the ground, and they have all seen what happens when it is torn: the green flap peeled back, the cinder underneath running like sugar, a pad tilting in a night. They step on the domes. They lay boards where they have to cross the same line twice. They hate it in a small daily way, the wet of it, the endless give, the way it eats a length of good pipe in a year if you turn your back. And a certain kind of man, after enough years here, will go out on his own during the pink and stand in it — in the haze of the midges and the flush of the flowers across a country fifteen kilometers wide, with the light coming up off the cloud floor and down off the sky — and stay out there the whole of a shift, and come back, and make no sign about it to anyone, and no one asks.

Whether the mat notices any of them is not a question with an answer. It notices weight, presumably, and heat, and the grey slurry spread across it, and the iron sunk into it, and it deals with each in its own time and closes over. The rigs come and go like weather. The men come and go like the birds. The midges last a week. The leather keeps eating stone, slowly, and drawing metal up into itself, and the men keep following the leather, and take out a rounding error, and go down the cable. The mat has been here since before any of this and will be the last thing here, lying on its heap, holding it in shape, drinking the cloud, and letting it go over the edge in a roar that no one, when there is finally no one, will hear as anything but silence.
