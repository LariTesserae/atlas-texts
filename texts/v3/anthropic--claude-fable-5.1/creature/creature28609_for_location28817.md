Begin with what the first account already named, because it was not wrong, only early.

The fern lives here. It is the oldest resident with a face you can see. It has been in its seam for longer than the shrub has been leaning, and it knows its drop the way a person knows the step that creaks: every eleven seconds the water lands a finger's width from its rootstock, and the splash wets the fronds, and the fronds are built for exactly that much wetting and no more. Move it a hand to the left and it would die of thirst. A hand to the right and it would drown. It has not been moved. It puts out one new frond in a year, sometimes two, and lets one old frond go brown and curl, so that it is always the same size and almost never the same fern.

The moss lives here, in its spilled-drink shape, and the liverworts pressed to the ledges, and the shrub with its twisted trunk. They are the ones the eye finds. But they are tenants of the surface. They live on the hollow the way lichen lives on the plateau above — a skin on a thing, not the thing.

To find what lives *in* the hollow you have to look at the water, and you have to look for a long time, and you have to stop expecting anything of a size that matters.

---

Kneel at the basin. The water is clear to the floor. The chisel marks are there under their brown skin, rounded, and the thread of overflow leaves by the notch, and the drop from the overhang comes in — *plip* — and the rings spread and flatten and are gone before the next one.

Nothing. Then, at the edge of nothing, motion that is not the water's.

They are the length of a grain of rice, and not that wide. They are white the way a fingernail is white, or the underside of a leaf that has never seen sun — not a color but the absence of one, the pallor of something that stopped bothering with pigment a very long time ago. They have no eyes. There is nothing where eyes would go, not a scar, not a dimple. They move by a flicker along their sides too fast to resolve, and they move rarely: a short glide, a pause, a turn, a pause. One rests against the mineral skin of the basin floor and is nearly invisible there, white on brown-white. Another hangs in the middle of the water doing nothing at all, and does it for as long as you are willing to watch.

There are perhaps a dozen in the basin. It is hard to be sure, because they do not gather, and the ones you are not looking at are the color of the water.

They eat the film. On every wet stone in the hollow there is a layer thinner than paper of things smaller than they are — bacteria, the mineral-fed slime that makes the brown skin, the dust of the fern's own decay carried in by the drop. The white things graze it. Their whole economy is the width of a fingernail. In the basin they have a few square hands of pasture and it is, for them, sufficient; they have never wanted more, because wanting more would require knowing there was more, and they have no organ for knowing that.

They are cold-blooded but they have never been cold. The water they live in is the temperature of the stone and the stone is the temperature of a body at rest.

---

Here is what changes the picture: the basin is not their home. It is their window.

They did not climb down the ledge. They did not come across the plateau. Nothing crosses the plateau. They came the way the water came — through the rock. Behind the sweating walls, in the pores and seams of the limestone, in a labyrinth that runs under the whole dry country above, there is water moving at the pace of a season, and they are in it. They have always been in it. Somewhere back in the dark their ancestors had eyes and used them, and then the water they lived in went underground, and the eyes went with the light, and the color went, and the hurry went. What was left is what you see in the bowl: a thing pared down to the shape of its conditions, as the fern is pared to its seam.

The seep carries them out. Not many, not often — a few every year, maybe, borne through the hairline cracks in the wet band and delivered onto the ledges in the film, where most of them dry in an hour. The ones that reach the basin live. The basin is the one place in the hollow where their world comes to the surface and holds still long enough to be inhabited. They live in it the way a fish lives in the pool a wave left behind: not visiting, not colonizing, simply continuous with the ocean that put them there.

And the hiss, the faint continuous sound you could not place — it is not blood in your ears. Or it is not only that. It is the whole plateau's stored rain moving through a mile of stone, and in that stone, unnumbered, the white things graze and pause and turn in the dark, and the basin is the single lit square of a house whose other rooms you will never see.

---

Now sit back on the rubble and let the small things be small, and think about what the people knew.

They came for the water. That much the first account had right. But the plateau is scattered with places where water could be had with less trouble than three steps cut in a wall and a bowl chiseled from a floor. Whoever made this room made it here, at this seep, and made the bowl not merely to hold the water but to hold it *still* — to make a place where the seep could be looked into. The notch keeps the surface from stirring. The depth is a viewing depth. The rim is worn at the height of a resting hand, or a resting forehead.

The niche behind the stone ring is empty, and the emptiness is not because something was taken. Nothing that mattered was ever in it. It is squared and hollowed and set into the wet band, so that the seep runs over its lip and down its face, and the mineral beard beneath it is the accumulation of that running. It is a place cut for the water to show itself. An honoring of the wall for what the wall does.

They sat in the ring. They watched the bowl. They saw what you see — the white grains that move against the current with no eyes to steer by, coming out of the rock as if the rock had a life inside it and this was where it surfaced, mildly, without alarm. You do not need a name for that to build a stone curve around it and sit down. You do not need a god. You need only to have crossed the dry country and found, at the bottom of a hole, a place that was warm and did not want anything from you and kept, in a bowl, a few living pieces of the dark that fed it.

They did not build a house because they were not the ones who lived here. They understood that. They shaped the room slightly so that the resident could be seen, and they came, and drank, and looked, and went back up. And then at some point they did not come back, and that has made no difference to anything below the rim.

---

What lives in the hollow, then, is this: an animal the size of a rice grain, blind and colorless, that has never been in a hurry; and behind it, the thing it belongs to — a body of slow water inside the plateau, breathing at the rate of the seep, warm as a flank, of which the hollow is the one opened place and the basin the one clear eye.

It does not know you are there. The drop rate does not change when you lean over. The white things do not startle; they cannot see you, and the shadow you cast is no shadow, in this light. If you put your finger in the water they will move around it as they would move around a fallen stone, and when you take it out the water will close and be exactly as it was.

It has arrived. That was the right word for the place and it is the right word for its resident. Everything on the plateau is waiting — for rain, for wind, for the crossing to be over. This lives on the other side of waiting. It drank the rain years before it fell on you. It is drinking now, somewhere back in the stone, the storm that has not yet formed. The bowl is full. The notch runs. A white grain glides, and stops, and hangs in the warm water like a held breath that no one is holding.

If you drink, you drink them. Not many — one, perhaps, or none. You will not feel it. It will taste of stone, as it always has. And that is the closest the hollow comes to letting you in.
