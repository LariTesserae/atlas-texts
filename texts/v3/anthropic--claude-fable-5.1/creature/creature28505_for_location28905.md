**Who Lives in the Unfinished Range**

Start with the obvious answer, which is: people who came up for the money and stayed for a reason they cannot quite give.

The works recruit in the valleys. There are posters in the railway towns — *STONE-SETTERS WANTED, LODGING FOUND, FIRING BONUS* — and a clerk with a folding table who takes names, and twice a year the incline railway hauls up a carload of new hands along with the coal. They are the usual people who take that kind of work: young, or between things, or from families that have run out of land. They come up expecting a mine, or a quarry, or a foundry, and for the first few weeks they behave as if it were one of those, and the old hands let them.

Most leave after one season. The ones who stay tend to be a particular kind. Not hardy, exactly — the plateau does not reward hardiness so much as it reduces the need for it, since nothing is ever quite as cold or as wet or as far as it seems it should be. The ones who stay are the ones who do not mind being unsure. Who can walk a rail line that is longer than yesterday without needing to know why. Who can put a hand on warm stone and feel it give and not flinch. The clerk in the valley has no test for this and has stopped trying to devise one. You find out on the second morning, when the survey stake has moved.

---

The stone-setters are the bulk of the population and the ones the place is really for. Their work is with the faces — the cuts where the plateau has been peeled back — and it is handwork, mostly, which surprises people. Machines do the heavy setting, the tempering pins, the great slow pressure-frames that hold a face flat while it takes; but the *finishing* of stone, the last few centimetres before it will ring, is done by hand, with wooden floats and iron scrapers and, at the very end, palms.

The setters press the stone. That is the whole of it. When a face is nearly hard — when it has gone past clay and is somewhere near cheese, near cold wax — a setter goes along it with both hands flat and presses, hard, for hours, working out the last of the give, feeling for the places where two masses met and did not quite agree. It looks like nothing. It looks like someone leaning on a wall. It is exhausting, and the old setters' shoulders are enormous and their hands are wide and flat and permanently grey in the creases.

And of course the stone takes the print. Every finished face on the plateau, if you know how to look, is covered in hands — not deep, not obvious, a texture rather than an image, but there. The setters know whose hands are whose. There is a stretch on the north cut that is forty years set and glossy and cracking properly now, and one of the old women will put her own palm against it and it fits, and she will not say anything about this, and neither will you.

They are a quiet trade. Not solemn — the finishing sheds are full of talk, and the dormitories, and the setters gamble with a dice game whose rules drift slightly from cluster to cluster across the plateau and nobody thinks that is remarkable. But they do not talk much about the work while doing it. You cannot press stone and talk. You listen instead, because the stone talks: the faint tick as a section under your hands goes over, the long groan of a mass somewhere below deciding to be one thing rather than two. The setters say a good face *settles down* the way a room does when the last person has stopped moving.

---

The kiln-masters are a different people, and there are never more than a dozen of them.

They are not, formally, a guild. There is no oath. But they train each other, and they marry each other more often than chance would suggest, and they live not in the dormitories but in small stone houses built hard against the flanks of their kilns, where the wall is always warm and the roar is always in the floor. They sleep through it. They cannot sleep without it. A kiln-master sent down to the valley for a month comes back grey and hollow-eyed and says the quiet kept her awake.

Their work is listening. A kiln fires for six weeks and the coal goes in on a schedule and the draught is set by dampers the size of barn doors, and all of that could be done by anyone; what the kiln-masters do is sit with the kiln, hour after hour, one ear practically against the iron, and hear whether the ring is taking. They can tell. They cannot say how, and the engineers have wired kilns with every instrument they have and found nothing the masters do not already know a day earlier. The masters describe it in terms of *voice*. A kiln whose ring is taking well has a voice that sits down. One where the stone is refusing has a voice that stays up on its toes.

They are proprietary about their kilns and about their rings. Each kiln-master can walk you out from her firing chamber and show you, ring by ring, the years — this one took, this one took slow, this one had a cold spot on the east side and you can see it still, a lobe of softer ground that never quite caught up. They speak of these as a farmer speaks of fields. When one of them dies the kiln is banked for a season out of something that is not quite respect and not quite superstition and a new master is chosen by the others in a manner they do not explain.

The oldest of them, the one with the notebook of sightings, has fired stone for thirty-one years and has begun, in the last few, to be slightly hard to see. Not invisible. Not transparent. Just — set. You look at her and she is very definitely there, more definitely than anyone else in the room, and the definiteness is almost too much, the way a finished face is almost too glossy, and your eye slides off. She knows this is happening and has written it down and is, as far as anyone can tell, pleased.

---

The engineers and the surveyors live in the cluster nearest the incline railway, closest to the way down, which the setters remark on. They are valley-trained and come up on contracts and most of them keep their contracts short.

The engineers are fine. They tend the pressure-frames and the steam hammers and argue about the light. They have theories. The setters like them the way you like anyone who takes your work seriously even when they misunderstand it.

The surveyors are another matter, and the plateau has a complicated regard for them, because the surveyors are the ones who go out.

Somebody has to. The works need to know how far the raw ground extends, and where the next kiln-site should be, and whether the far ridges have moved, and so every spring a party of surveyors goes out past the last rings with theodolites and chains and notebooks, and every summer most of them come back. Their maps never agree. Their notebooks, as has been said, sometimes change hands partway down the page — the same surveyor's writing but a different slope to it, a different way of forming the sevens, as if the surveyor had gone out one person and come back adjacent to that person. They report distances in ranges. They report the ridges as having been in two places. They are precise about their imprecision and it is, the engineers admit, the best that can be done.

What the surveyors do not put in their reports, and what everyone on the plateau knows, is that the raw ground gets into them. Not badly. Not all at once. But a surveyor who has done five seasons out there is a slightly different height each morning. Her shadow is a beat slower than yours. She loses track of the day inside a conversation and has to be told. She stops being able to say, with total confidence, which of two things happened first. Nothing is wrong with her, and the doctors from the valley have found nothing wrong with her, and she will tell you herself she feels fine, and she does. She is simply less committed than she was, in the way the ground she has walked is less committed, and the setters, who spend their lives pressing the last uncertainty out of stone, cannot look at her for long without their hands starting to move.

Some surveyors do not come back. Not many. One every few years. The rest of the party reports that she was there, and then the hollow they had walked into let out into a different hollow, and she was not with them, and they went back, and the first hollow did not go where it had gone. Nobody searches. There is nowhere to search. They bank a kiln for a night and write her name on the wall of the surveyors' mess, where there are some forty names, and the oldest of the names are in a hand that has started, very slightly, to drift.

---

The electricians. Someone should say something about the electricians, who are the most cheerful people on the plateau and whose work is the most hopeless.

The condensation gets into everything. It beads on the underside of every conduit, every junction box, every lamp-holder in every dormitory, and the plateau's wiring — installed in stages over sixty years by people with different ideas — is a constant low argument between water and copper that the electricians referee and never win. There are eleven of them. They know every circuit on the plateau by name; the names are unofficial and unrepeatable and passed down. They spend their days in the crawl-spaces under the finishing sheds and their evenings in the dormitory common rooms playing the dice game and telling the setters that the light will be fixed by Tuesday.

They have a saying, which they will tell you is an electricians' saying but which the setters claim came from them: *nothing here is finished and that includes the finishing.* The electricians find this hilarious. They have been up here, some of them, longer than the kiln-masters, and they are the least changed by it of anyone, and the general theory is that this is because they have never once expected anything to hold.

---

There are children. Not many — a few dozen at any time, in a population of some thousands — because the work is hard and the dormitories are not made for it and the valley is where most people go to have their families. But some are born here, and some are brought up small, and the ones who grow up on the plateau are the residents everyone watches most closely, because they are the only people for whom the place is not strange.

They do not flinch at the lag. That is the first thing you notice. A hammer falls and the flash comes late and a valley-born adult, even after years, twitches at the wrong moment; a plateau child has never known light to arrive on time and simply waits for it. They walk the drifting rail lines without slowing. They can find a dormitory in the fog by the sound of its particular drip. They do not trust shadows and they do not need to be told not to. They feed the marmots, openly, with a total lack of guilt that the adults find slightly shocking.

And they go out. Not far — they are not allowed far, and the raw ground is genuinely too indeterminate for a child — but to the edges of the last rings, where the set stone gives out and the half-clay begins, they go, and they press their hands into it. Not to finish it. Just to press. To see the print take and the print close. The setters do not stop them and could not say why.

The valley doctors, who come up twice a year, have noted that plateau-born children are slightly harder to measure than others. Their heights vary. Their ages are, within a year or so, negotiable. They are healthy and quick and cheerful and their handwriting, when they learn it, tends to be more than one handwriting. When they are old enough they mostly go down, to the valley, to school and work and weather, and they mostly stay down, and their parents understand. A few come back. The ones who come back become kiln-masters, or surveyors, or the kind of setter whose hands the stone knows already, and nobody is surprised.

---

Then the animals, who outnumber the people many times over and live on the plateau as if it were entirely ordinary and had been there forever.

The marmots have been mentioned and are, in the honest accounting, the plateau's principal residents. There is a colony under every dormitory and every casting shed, warm and fat and completely unafraid, and they sit on the finished rings in the evenings alongside the setters with the same posture of proprietary ease. They are fed. They are named. A dormitory's marmots are a point of pride. They do not go onto the raw ground — nothing that lives on the set stone will cross onto raw ground voluntarily, which the surveyors long ago learned to use as a boundary marker more reliable than their own stakes.

The herds are the opposite. The ibex-things with the splayed hooves live on the raw and the half-set and will not come onto finished stone at all; the boundary of their range is the boundary of the works, exactly, and moves outward as the rings take. They stand in the fog in groups of forty and crop at nothing and are, when you approach them, always slightly further than they were. Up close — and you can get close, they do not fear people — they are handsome grey animals with amber eyes and a doubled coat, and they are, as far as anyone can determine, one species, and they have the right number of legs, and it is only from a distance that they have too many, and the old kiln-master's notebook has a page about this that concludes: *they are counted from where I stand.*

The moths that fur the warm rock at night are simple and gentle and the setters sweep them off in the morning and they come back. The great slow birds ride the kiln-thermals and roost in the cold chimneys and are ordinary scavengers with an unusual wingspan and a taste for the moths. The choughs are choughs. The voles in the pipe lagging are voles. Most of what lives here is exactly what it appears to be and the only strange thing about it is how much of it there is, at six thousand metres, on ground that was not there a century ago.

The bird that is not the same twice comes in at dusk, over the works, and everyone stops to watch it and no one has ever agreed on what it was.

---

And there are the others. The ones the setters call *offers*, when they call them anything.

You see them out on the raw ground, in the fog, at a distance. Figures. Walking. Standing. Sometimes near the herds, sometimes alone on a ridge that is ending its day early. The surveyors see them most, being out there most, and have learned not to walk toward them, because a figure on raw ground is at whatever distance it is, and a hollow that leads toward one may lead out somewhere else, and in any case when you reach the place there is either nobody, or a surveyor from forty years ago who is glad to see you and does not know what year it is, or — and this is the one the parties do not talk about at the mess — you.

Not hostile. The old kiln-master is emphatic about this and has written it down in three places. The ground has not decided what it is, and so it has not decided what lives on it, and so it offers. It offers herds and it offers birds and it offers, sometimes, people, because people are among the things that could live here and the ground is being thorough. The offers are not ghosts. They are not lost surveyors, or not only. They are what the raw stone puts forward as a possibility, and if you do not walk toward them they stay possible, and if you do they become whatever you were expecting, and neither of these is dangerous, exactly, and neither is something you want to do twice.

Occasionally one walks in.

It happens perhaps once a decade. A figure comes off the raw ground at the edge of a ring and it is a person, a stranger, in the kind of clothes people wear up here, and it walks into a cluster and asks for work and is given a bunk. It is patient. It is good at pressing stone. It does not remember the valley and does not need to. Nobody asks. Over years on set ground it grows more definite — its shadow tightens, its height stops varying, its handwriting, if it learns any, settles into one — and by the time it is old it is as hard to see as the oldest kiln-master, as glossy, as *set*, and it sits on the warm rings in the evening with its back to the fire and looks out at the fog and points at faces it finished and says *that one took*. And the setters who knew it when it walked in do not say anything about this, and neither will you.

---

So that is who lives here: a few thousand people slowly becoming stone, and a great many animals who arrived from nowhere and behave as though they had always been coming, and out past the last rings a population that has not yet decided whether to exist, standing in the fog, considering it.

And under all of them the largest resident, the one the works exist to serve, which is the mountain itself. It is not finished. It has not decided how many surfaces it has or how long its days should be or which way west is. It is warm, the way a loaf is warm, and it gives under the hand, and it is being pressed, patiently, a metre a year, by people whose palms it has learned, into something that will one day crack in an ordinary frost and hold an ordinary lichen and be, finally, on its own. It does not seem to mind. It ticks as it cools. It offers what it has.

The stone-setters say — this is the last thing they say about it, in the evenings, when the cloud is up and the ridges are ending at their own pace — that the mountain is the only one of them who is not in a hurry, and that they are learning.
