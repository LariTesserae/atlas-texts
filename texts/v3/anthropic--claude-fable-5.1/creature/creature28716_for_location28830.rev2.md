The first answer is the bats, and the first answer is wrong in an interesting way.

The bats are what fill the room, but filling a place and living in it are not the same thing. The small dark leaf-nosed kind that clusters over the vent — the kind that makes up most of the ceiling — spends every night out on the mountain. They know the high slopes better than they know this room: the thermals off the south-facing cinder, the places where the midges rise at dusk, the wind-shadow behind the ridge where a bat can rest between flights. They come here to be warm and to raise their young and to sleep, which is to say they live here the way a fisherman lives at the harbor. Their bodies are built for the outside. Their fur is thick against a cold they never feel in here. Their eyes still work. If the vent went out tomorrow they would move — there are other caves, colder, and they would torpor there the way their cousins do, and in a generation the room would be a story their bodies didn't remember.

The pale long-eared kind at the margins is even less committed. They keep to the cool edges because the heart of the room is too hot for them. They are here because it is better than the alternatives, and they hang apart, and they are the first to go at dusk and the last to come back, and they are the ones the people take, because they are the ones that haven't quite settled in.

The floor lives here, fully. But the floor is a process more than a resident. The beetles are the same species as a beetle you could find under a rock in any cave on the continent; they are here because the guano is here. The springtails and the pale roaches are the same. They would be anywhere there was this much falling food and this much damp. They are the room's digestion, and digestion is not a tenant.

So you look for what would be nowhere else. What has been changed by the room, shaped to it, so that it could not step out through the throat and survive the first minute. And there are two things.

---

The frogs first, because they are the ones you would notice if you could see.

They are small — you could hold twenty in a cupped hand — and they are the color of the wet rock they sit against, a mottled brown that has no reason to exist, since nothing here has eyes to be fooled by camouflage. It is a color left over from a time when the slopes outside were forest and a frog needed to hide from birds. The pattern is still there, faint, the way a word is still there on a page that has been through the wash.

Their eyes are gone in the way that takes a very long time. Not gouged, not sealed — grown over. The eyeballs are still under there, shrunk to the size of poppy seeds, and the skin over them is a little thinner and a little darker than the skin around, so that each frog wears two small dimples like fingerprints in clay. Perhaps they register the difference between the total dark of the room and the gray coin of the entrance, if one ever wandered that far. None does.

What they have instead is skin. Frog skin is already a sense organ — it drinks, it breathes, it tastes — and these frogs have taken that further. They can feel the tremor of a springtail landing in their pool. They can feel the temperature of the water shift by a fraction when a snowmelt drip finds the wrong channel and cold reaches them, and they move before the cold arrives. They know the difference between the vibration of the floor's ordinary seething and the different, heavier, more purposeful vibration of something walking on it, and when they feel that, they go quiet.

They breed in the condensate pools, and because the pools are shallow and warm and never quite the same from week to week, they have given up the tadpole. The eggs are few and large and yolk-heavy, laid in a clump under the lip of the rock where the drip cannot reach them, and what hatches is a frog, minute and complete, that climbs out onto the wet stone the same hour it is born. There is no larval stage, no period of being a different kind of animal. They are frogs from the first moment. It has made them slow to multiply and slow to die. A frog in this room may live thirty years, forty. There may be only a few hundred of them in the whole chamber. They are not abundant. They are permanent.

Their call is low for so small a body, a churring that carries in the wet air the way a high note would not, and they call mostly in the hours after the colony has left — the room's quiet hours, though quiet here means only that the texture has thinned enough for individual sounds to have edges. They call to each other along the wall, pool to pool, and the calls have a pattern: a churr, a pause, an answer from the next hollow down. A relay. It goes around the room in a few minutes and comes back. It may be nothing more than males announcing themselves. It has the shape of something else, of a room checking that all its parts are still there.

---

The second thing lives in the deepest cleft of the ceiling, and you will not see it, and you are not meant to.

The third kind hangs where the rock has been eaten longest. The crust beneath them is not stone anymore; it is a soft, pitted, cheese-textured layer of ammonia-worked mineral that gives under a fingertip, and they grip it with claws that have gone long and curved and thin, hooked for a soft surface rather than a hard one. They hang in a mass so dense and so still that a naturalist who somehow got a light in here might take them for a fungal growth before the churring started. They do not squabble. They do not groom in the restless way of the leaf-nosed kind. They hang, and they wait, and their fur is thin and sparse and grayish over pink-gray skin, because there has never in their lineage been a reason to keep warm.

At dusk, when the colony peels away and funnels up the throat, they do not join it. They wait until the ceiling is mostly bare and the room's sound has fallen to its floor-seethe and its drip and the mountain's breath. Then they let go.

They do not fly. Or — they fly the way a thrown thing flies, a short controlled drop on wings that have gone broad and short and thick-membraned, more parachute than sail, and they land on the guano with their feet under them. And then they walk.

You would not recognize the shape. The forelimbs have folded up into something like arms, the wing-membrane bunched at the elbow, the long thumb-claw turned into a hook they use to pin things. The hind legs are heavy and set well under the body. They move across the seething floor on all fours with a rocking, deliberate gait, heads low, and they eat. Beetles, mostly, cracked with teeth that have gone flat and broad. Crickets, taken with a quick sideways snap. They wade to the wall where the snail-crust is and rake the shells off in strips with the thumb-hook and swallow them whole. They drink at the pools.

They are what has been eating the floor all along, from above, while the floor was busy eating what fell. Nobody has seen them feeding because nobody has been here at the hour they feed, and because at that hour there is no seeing.

And when they are at the pools, they take frogs.

Not many. Not often. A frog is a small mouthful and a slow one to catch, and the frogs go quiet and still when they feel the heavier tread coming, and a still brown frog in a dark pool is nearly nothing. But sometimes. And this is where the two calls come from, or where they have gone.

The third kind churrs. It is not the only sound they make — they have a higher, thinner call for finding each other in the mass — but the churr is the sound they make on the floor, and it is very close to the sound the frogs make along the wall. Close enough that from ten paces you cannot tell them apart. Whether the bats learned it from the frogs, whether it draws frogs into answering and so into giving away their pools, whether it began as coincidence and has been sharpened by ten thousand generations of frogs that were caught because they answered and frogs that survived because they didn't — there is no way to know from inside the room. What is certain is that the two calls have been shaping each other for a very long time, the frogs' churr drifting to a pitch the bats find hardest to copy, the bats' churr drifting after it. It is a conversation that has been going on since before the mountain was this tall. It is the room's oldest sentence, and it is still being revised.

So the room has, at its heart, not one resident but a pair. Two animals from two different worlds — one that came in on wings, one that was simply here when the world went cold around it — who have spent so long in the same dark that they have become each other's shape. The frog has become a thing that listens. The bat has become a thing that walks. Neither is what it was. Neither could be anything else.

---

There is a third answer, which is the mountain, and it is the truest and the least satisfying.

The vent has been breathing for longer than there have been frogs to hear it. It made the room — it is the only reason there is a room, or a warmth, or a ceiling worth hanging from — and it will be the last thing in the room to stop. Everything else here is arranged around it the way iron filings arrange around a magnet. The colony clusters where its heat is strongest. The water it lifts falls where it cools. The floor it warms breeds what the walkers eat. The eleven-second rhythm underlies every other sound so completely that the frogs, if one could ask them, would probably not be able to say that there was a rhythm at all, any more than you could say your own heartbeat is a sound.

It does not live here in any sense a biologist would accept. It is not alive. But it is the thing whose presence makes this a place rather than a hole, and it is the thing that will still be here when the walkers have died out for want of beetles and the frogs have died out for want of pools, and the room will go back to being what it was before either of them arrived: a warm dark wet hollow, breathing to itself, waiting to be lived in.

---

The people are not residents, and it is worth being clear about why.

They come from a village three days down, where there is grass and where nobody has ever been warm through a whole night in winter. They come one at a time or two at a time, once in a year or once in a season, and they are usually old, because the climb is bad and the young have more reasons to stay below. They know the way the way one knows a path in one's own house in the dark: this fold of cinder, this rust-bleached lip, the knob of rock that has been polished by three hundred years of the same hand-fall. They stoop through. They crawl. They stand up in the noise and the heat and the falling filth and they stay for a while.

They take a few of the pale long-eared bats from the loose margins, and they eat them raw and warm, and they leave the bones. They have never made a fire because it has never occurred to them to. They have never gone to the deep cleft, or reached into the pools, because the room has a shape they respect without having words for: the margins are for taking, and the heart is not. Whether they know about the frogs, whether they know about the walkers — they have probably heard the churring and taken it for one thing or the other, as you did. They may have a name for the sound. They may have a story about what makes it. If they do, the story is almost certainly wrong and almost certainly closer to the truth than the frogs' own understanding, which is nothing, and the walkers', which is only hunger.

They are the room's one connection to the world it does not know about. When they go back down they carry the smell on them for days, that round old sulfur-and-guano smell that has soaked into their clothes, and the people below know where they have been by it, and do not ask.

---

If you stay until the hour after the colony has gone — if you stand still in the thinned dark with the mites finding your wrists and the drip counting on your shoulders — you will hear the churring start along the wall, pool to pool, the relay going round. And after a while you will hear another churring, from higher, from the cleft, and then a soft weight landing in the guano not far from your feet, and another, and the heavier tread beginning. And the wall will go quiet in patches as the tread approaches each pool and start again behind it as the tread moves on, so that the silence walks around the room, and the sound closes behind the silence, and the mountain breathes under all of it, eleven seconds, twelve, and you understand that this is what lives here: not the bats, not the frogs, not the walkers, but this — the silence moving, the sound closing, the breath beneath — the thing the room does with itself when it thinks no one is listening, and has done, and will go on doing, in the warm and the wet and the dark, for as long as there is a dark to do it in.
