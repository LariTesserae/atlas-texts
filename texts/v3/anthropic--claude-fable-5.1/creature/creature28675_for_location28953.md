The first thing that lives here is the water.

Not the drip — the film. Every surface in the hollow wears it, a skin of warm wet a few hairs thick, and in that skin there is a population that has never in its history touched anything dry. Threads of something clear and faintly greenish lie along the seams where the film is deepest, and if you put your face close you can see them shorten and lengthen, very slowly, in the direction the water is going. They are eating what the wood exhales. Where two films meet at the bottom of a diamond cell and pool before they drip, there is a scum, and the scum is alive, and it is the most numerous thing in the whole place by a factor that would be meaningless to write down. When a drop finally falls it takes some of this with it, down to the next floor, where it joins the film there. The population is one population. It is being slowly, continuously poured through the plant, floor to floor, and it does not mind, because to it the hollow is not a place with a top and a bottom but a single wet surface that happens to be arranged in layers.

On the film grows the fungus, and the fungus is the pasture.

It is one fungus. You can convince yourself of this by walking: the pelt on the wall a day's gallery from here is the same pelt, the same close grey-green nap, and where it fruits it fruits in the same way, small pale shelves along the underside of a seam, and where it has been grazed it recovers at the same rate, and there is no line anywhere at which it becomes a different one. It lives on the outer surface of the living wood and the inner surface of the film and it is, by any measure of extent, the largest thing in the hollow after the plant itself. It never dries. It never sees light brighter than the green murk. It is what most of the animals here are ultimately eating, and its slow spread across the walls and its slow removal by mouths are, like the growth and wear of the wood, now running at the same speed.

---

Under the fungus, under the living wood, in the dead layer, the beetles.

They are pale because nothing in the dead layer has ever needed a colour. Thumb-sized, soft-shelled, with heads that are mostly jaw, and they are not the same species you saw in the honeycombed wall — or they are, but the ones deeper in are longer, and slower, and blinder, and the ones near the surface have a faint stipple of brown along the back that might be the beginning of camouflage or the last of it. Their larvae are in the wood in numbers no one has counted, boring parallel to the grain in the stratum between the grey and the sweet, and the boring is why the dead layer is a honeycomb, and the honeycomb is why the walls are light enough to hold up a vault.

The fine dry seething they make is the sound of the plant being digested. Not by them exactly. They chew the dead wood to a meal, and the meal passes through them, and what comes out is warm, and the fungus takes it, and the film takes the fungus's leavings, and the plant drinks the film. When you press a hand to the wall and feel the warmth coming up, some of it is chemistry in the living cambium, the slow burning of the plant's own stores. Some of it is beetles. It is not possible to say how much. The plant has been eating itself for so long, with their help, that the distinction between its metabolism and theirs has closed over like a seam.

The beetles stop when you come close because they feel you through the wood. They do not know you are anything but a change in the vibration of the wall. They start again when it goes away.

---

There are things in the clouds.

You would not see them from the floor. They are visible only when a column of paler air stands under a well-mouth and something drifts through it: a membrane, roughly the span of two hands, nearly transparent, with a faint radial structure like the veins of a leaf that has rotted down to its skeleton. It rises on the warm breath of the wall until it meets the cool under the vault, and there it sinks, and drifts, and finds warm air again over another wall, and rises. It is doing this all the time. It has never landed. Its ancestors landed, and had legs, and the legs are gone.

They eat the cloud. The indoor weather here carries spores from the fungus, and beetle-dust, and the fine exhalation of the wood, and the membranes strain it out of the air as it passes through them. They are very light and very slow and they cannot steer, and they are the only things in the hollow that cross the great volume of the vault rather than hugging its edges, and they do it by not being able to help it. Sometimes a swift takes one. Mostly they live until they are too heavy with what they have eaten to rise anymore and they come down, in the rain or without it, onto a floor that is a ceiling, and lie there for a moment like a dropped handkerchief, and the film begins on them at once.

In one gallery the wall is lit with morning on one panel and murk on the rest, and there is a fold there, and on certain days the membranes come drifting down out of the murk and through the cold slice of morning and out the other side, and their shadows fall briefly on the wood the wrong way, and then the shadows are gone into the fold and the membranes with them.

---

The swifts are the loudest thing here and they are almost silent.

The colony you saw is one of perhaps a dozen. They nest only in folds — the scar-tissue is hard and dry and holds a nest, and it has another property, which is that the fold is a door. A swift that leaves the nest by going into the fold arrives in a gallery that is by the wall a few paces away and by the air the width of the continent, and so the colony feeds over an area no bird could fly across, and it never has to cross the open vault, where it would be seen. The birds come out of the well-mouth in a pour, hundreds in a few breaths, and they do not call. Their wing-sound against the grey glow is a flicker, a rush of fabric. They turn into the fold and are gone and the gallery is empty.

They navigate by the folds. This is the only way to explain how a bird hatched in a cup of saliva and wood-dust in one wall can leave the nest, go through a graft it has never seen the other side of, feed in a place that is not on any line from here, and come back. The old folds are almost invisible to you, a change in the sound of your feet. To a swift they are as plain as doors. The young go into the fold before they have properly flown, because the fold is the only way out of the nest, and so the first thing a swift ever does with its wings is pass through a place where two branches, generations ago, were bound and bled and became one branch, and it comes out somewhere else, and it does not find this strange.

They come back to the same fold to die. There is a floor below one of the colonies that is soft with them.

---

The grazers are the largest thing, and there are very few.

You saw one from above: long, low, the colour of the wood, moving along a seam with its broad flat mouth on the wall. It is possible you were the first thing in its life to look at it. It has no eyes to speak of — two soft patches on the front of the head that register the green glow and nothing else — and it is deaf in the way the whole hollow encourages, having grown up in a place where the loudest sound is water. What it has is a mouth and a tongue and a sense in the skin of its belly for the seams. It follows the seam because the film is deepest there and the fungus richest, and because the seam is the plant's own line, and the plant's line is always going somewhere.

It is slow as a strategy. Anything that hunts here hunts by geometry — by knowing where a moving thing will come out — and a thing that moves at the speed of fungus recovering cannot be predicted, because there is nothing to predict. It will be where it was. The grazer's whole life is a single slow pass along a single seam, scraping, and a pass takes so long that the pelt has regrown behind it by the time it reaches the far end, and it turns around, or does not need to, because the seam has folded and the far end is the near end.

They are old. It is not clear how old. The wear along the seams, the concavity you noticed under your feet where the wood is smoothed into a channel — some of that is feet, and some of that is grazers, one at a time, over a span that has outlasted the gardeners' knowledge of it. When one dies it dies on the seam and the beetles come, and the fungus, and what is left is a long stain of darker wood where the fat went in, and the seam is a little more concave than it was.

They do not breed often. There are floors where a small one is moving beside a large one, both scraping, a pace apart, and the pace will be the same pace for years.

---

The hunters.

You will not see one and the reason you will not see one is that they are the floor.

They are not many. Two or three dozen, perhaps, in the whole hollow, and each of them holds a single fold and lies along the scar-tissue of it for most of its life. They are flat — flatter than a body should be, the depth of a hand — and long, and the colour of wet wood, and they lie in the diamond cells at a fold's exit with their edges tucked along the seams so that what you see, if you see anything, is a slight smoothness in one panel of the lattice, a place where the ridges look worn. They are wet because everything is wet. The wrong-shaped mark on the floor is where one lay and then was not lying, and the film had not yet closed over the dry place it left.

They do not chase. They cannot; they are not built for it, and there is nothing here to chase. They wait at the far side of a graft for whatever will come through it, and they know what will come through it because they know the fold, and knowing the fold is what they are. The scar-wood carries sound differently — denser, harder, so that the drip on it has a higher, shorter note — and the hunters lie with their whole length against it and feel what is coming from the other side, from a place that by the air is far off and by the wood is directly under them. A swift that leaves its colony to feed will arrive here. A beetle-swarm moving up through the dead layer will emerge here. A gardener whispering into the seam a continent away will be heard, faintly, through the belly. The hunter has never been to the other side. It has felt the other side against its skin all its life, and it knows the other side better than the gallery it lies in.

They eat rarely and they eat whatever the fold gives them. The scatter of small bones is a swift-fold. There are other folds with other scatters. There is a fold, deep, with a long stain beside it that took a great while to make.

They are the youngest thing here in the sense that matters: they could not have existed before the folding. Their whole shape is an answer to a geometry that was made by hand, with cord and mud and patience, and so in a way they are the gardeners' last crop, the one nobody planted. They came up out of something that hid in seams and were changed by the fact that some seams are doors. Whether the gardeners think of them that way is not knowable. The gardeners give the fold-exits a wide berth, and step over the smooth panels, and do not whisper into a seam without first laying a hand on it to feel whether anything is listening from the other side.

---

The gardeners.

There are people here. It is important to say this plainly, because everything about the place conspires to make it seem otherwise — the emptiness, the healed tools, the finished work — and yet the mud in that seam was damp, and someone packed it.

They are not many, and they are dispersed the way everything is dispersed. A person born in the hollow might know a dozen others across a whole life, and know them the way you know the drip — a presence that comes, and is real, and goes into the quiet. They do not gather. There is nothing to gather for; the food is everywhere and thin, and gathering would mean noise, and noise is the one thing the hollow does not permit. Where two of them live within a day of each other it is because the fold between them makes it so, and they may go a season without meeting, and leave each other bindings instead.

You would know one if you saw one. They have the hollow's shape: long in the limb, pale, wide in the eye, with the large slow chest of people who have always breathed thin air. Their feet are soft and their hands are hard. Their skin films over like everything else and they do not wipe it. They move along the walls with a hand on the seam, always, feeling — for dryness, for a lip, for the different note that means a fold, for the faint deep hum through the wood that means something is lying on the other side — and they stop often, and they are very quiet, and in a bad light you could take one for a smoothness in the lattice, which is a thing they know, and use, and share uneasily with the other creature that uses it.

They have stone. This is the strangest fact about them and the one that says most about where they came from. There is no stone in the plant and no stone under it — under it there is air — and yet every gardener carries a blade of dark flaked stone, and the blades are old past reckoning, and each has a name, and the names are the closest thing the gardeners have to a history. The blades came up from below. Somebody climbed, once, to the high place where a seedling was, carrying stone, and began to bend branches. The stone has been passing hand to hand since and there is no more of it. When a blade is lost in the wood — left in a seam, closed over — the loss is remembered for generations in the way you would remember a death. They make what else they need from bone, and from wood hardened in resin, and from the plant's own inner bark, which twists into cord that outlasts them. The awls, the cord, the mud, the moss: all from the plant. Only the edge came from outside, and there is less of it every century.

They speak in whispers and they speak into the wood. A voice in the open vault would carry too far and arrive too strangely; a whisper laid into a seam goes where the seam goes, through the fold, and comes out on the far side low and close, as if someone were standing there with their mouth to the wall. So messages are not sent but left. You whisper into the wood and go on, and a day later, or a season, someone with their hand on the seam a continent away feels the wood change and puts their ear to it and hears you — telling them a seam is dry three cells above a certain knot, or that the swifts have moved, or a name. The hunters hear it too, through the belly. The gardeners know this. They say what they need to and no more.
