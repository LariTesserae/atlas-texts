What lives here is, first, the crowd, and the crowd has already been counted: the mussel pavement, the barnacles on the mussels, the anemones all facing the same way. But a crowd is not a tenant. The mussels do not live in the room so much as constitute it; they are the wall the room has grown over its walls. If you ask what lives here in the sense of having an address—of going out and coming back, of knowing the room and being known by it—the answer narrows to a few, and they sort themselves by how long they have been paying attention.

---

The shortest tenancy belongs to the octopus, and it is worth beginning there because the octopus is the one who is most obviously at home and least likely to still be here next year.

The fourth pump, the one farthest from the light, has a casing that cracked along a seam long before the rind sealed it, and the rind sealed the crack open. There is a gap the width of a hand at the level of the plinth, and behind it a hollow the size of a bucket where the impeller was, and that is a den, and the den has been occupied for as long as there have been crabs in the drainage channels. Not by one octopus. By a succession. The kind that lives in water this cool is a curled octopus, a modest animal, the mantle no bigger than a fist, arms a forearm's length, skin that in daylight would show warm brown and orange and here shows nothing at all. It lives about a year. It arrives in the hall as a settling larva no larger than a rice grain, finds its way to the fourth pump because the fourth pump is where the octopuses have always gone, learns the room in a matter of weeks, hunts it for a season, mates or fails to, and dies. Then the den is empty for a while, and then it is not.

So the octopus is new and the den is old, and you can read the difference at the door. There is a midden on the plinth below the crack: crab carapaces, mussel valves opened cleanly along the hinge, the occasional goby skull, all of it pale, all of it accumulated. The midden has stratified. The lower layers are cemented into the rind. The upper layers are loose and current-sorted, fanned out on the downstream side according to whichever way the tide was running when they were dropped. Each octopus adds to it without knowing what it is adding to. Each one also does what octopuses do to a den, which is to improve it—drags a piece of rind or a flat shell across the opening at slack, pulls it in behind, pushes it out again when it leaves. The current door of the den is a broken mussel clump about the size of a dinner plate. It has been the door for as long as this octopus has been alive. The last octopus used something else, and there is no way to know what.

The octopus hunts the drainage channels because the crabs use them as roads, and the crabs use them as roads because the channels are sheltered from the current, and so the octopus lies along the lip of a channel with two arms down in it and waits for the traffic. It has learned the flywheel. When the knock comes and the shoal contracts and the mussels clap shut down the length of the hall, the crabs freeze, and the octopus, which does not freeze, takes one. This is not intelligence exactly; it is a year's worth of association in an animal built to associate. But it means the octopus is the only inhabitant of the room that uses the clock rather than merely obeying it, and it means that every octopus that has ever lived in the fourth pump has independently discovered the same trick and died without passing it on.

---

The middle tenancy is the wrasse, and the wrasse is old.

It holds under the intake screens in the strongest current in the room, which is the last place a fish would choose unless it had a reason, and the reason is that the intake is a filter and the wrasse is what the filter feeds. Everything the tide pushes through the bars—drift weed, the small crabs that ride on drift weed, the shrimp knocked loose from the tunnel walls—arrives at the wrasse first. It has held that position for so long that the bar screen behind it is polished on the upstream face where its tail-wash has scoured the barnacles, a clean bright patch on the encrusted iron shaped exactly like the shadow of a fish.

It is a ballan wrasse, as long as a forearm and deep-bodied, and in a lit sea it would be mottled green and rust with a pale belly and a heavy, forward-set mouth full of teeth built for crushing shell. Here it is a shape. It was born female, as they all are, and lived in the light-fall as a small fish for years among the algae on the second pump, and at some point when the male who held the intake died or left, it changed. This is ordinary for wrasse. What is not ordinary is the span: wrasse in cold water live twenty-five years, thirty, and this one has the scarred lips and the thickened skull of something near the end of that. It has watched the octopus in the fourth pump die and be replaced perhaps two dozen times. It does not know this, but it has done it.

The wrasse sleeps. That is the other thing about it. At dusk, or at what passes for dusk when the column of light above the ceiling break goes from grey-green to grey, it leaves the intake and swims the length of the hall to a specific rivet-hole in the flange of the third pump—the one with the wheel—and wedges itself in, on its side, and stops. Wrasse do this; they sleep hard, harder than most fish, and can be handled without waking. It has chosen a hole a hand-span from the bearing, so that it sleeps against the wall of the room's loudest point, the knock passing through the iron into its body every minute all night. Whether it chose the place for the noise or in spite of it is unanswerable. But the hole is worn smooth on the inside to the shape of its flank, and there are years in that wear.

---

The longest tenancy is in the culvert, and the culvert is where the eyes are.

Most of the eyes are prawns. That is the honest answer to the impression of the dark being watched: the outflow culverts are lined with them, hundreds, clinging to the walls upside-down and sideways, and their eyes throw back whatever light reaches them as tiny points, so that the mouths of the culverts seem to be full of stars that shift when the current shifts. They are part of the crowd. But behind them, in the second culvert from the left, further back than the light has ever reached, there is the conger.

It came in as a thin thing, a leptocephalus that had already drifted across most of an ocean, and then as an elver no thicker than a pencil, and it found the culvert because the culvert was dark and there was food at the mouth of it, and it has not left. Congers grow for a very long time and do not breed until they leave for deep water to do it, once, and die; this one has not gone. It is now, as far as anyone could tell without seeing it whole, about as wide as the culvert. Its head, when it comes forward to the mouth at the turn of the tide, fills the opening the way a cork fills a bottle. The prawns move aside for it without hurry. It has a blunt, heavy face, a mouth that runs far back past the eye, and eyes that are small and clouded and probably do very little; congers are nocturnal in the open sea, and this one has spent its life in a place where night is the ordinary condition. It works by smell and by the lateral line, which is to say it reads the water. It knows when the tide has turned before the room does, because the pressure changes in the culvert before the current reverses in the hall.

It is the thing that came out along the base of the plinth and went back. It does this once or twice at each turn: emerges, moves along the downstream side of the machines in the slack, takes what it finds—a crab in the open, an incautious blenny, once in a great while the octopus, though not often, because the octopus has learned the timing of that too—and returns. It never crosses the light-fall. It never goes above the plinth. Its whole world is a low horizontal band along the floor of the hall and the length of one culvert, and the culvert may be the only reason it is still here: there is a possibility, judging by its girth, that it has grown too large to pass back out through the outflow, and that the only exit left to it is the intake, forty paces upstream against the strongest current in the room, through a bar screen whose gaps it would no longer fit. If so, it does not know. It came in, it stayed, it grew, and the room closed around it the way the rind closed around the iron.

---

These three are the residents in any sense that has a face. The rest of what lives here lives at speeds that make residence a strange word. The mussels are replaced individually and never as a whole. The barnacles are a surface. The shoal is a shoal—no fish in it stays for more than a season, but there has always been a shoal in the middle of the hall, of about that size, contracting at the knock. The crabs are a traffic. Every one of them is passing through, and the traffic never stops.

And there is one more thing, which lives in the room in a way that is hard to name but impossible to leave out. The bearing of the third pump is the one place in the hall where nothing grows. The water passing through it is too fast and the iron moves against itself and no spat can hold. It is bare metal, or what is left of bare metal, a hollow worn to the shape of the water that wears it, and it is the only surface in the room that is exactly what it was made of. Everything else has been colonized. The bearing has only been used. Once a minute, at the point of the knock, when the load comes on and the shriek begins, there is a moment in which every living thing in the hall responds at once—mussels shutting down the length of the room, the shoal drawing in, the crabs stopping in the channels, the octopus taking its chance, the wrasse asleep against the flange with the sound going through it—and the thing they are all responding to is a piece of iron that has never been alive and has become, by turning, the one inhabitant none of them could do without.

Then the shriek trails off, and the roar comes back, and the mussels open, and the shoal spreads, and the crab that stopped moves on toward the dark where the eyes are.
