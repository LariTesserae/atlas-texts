Start with the obvious answer, which is nobody.

There is no house within sight of the rim. The tar-paper hut with the fallen roof was never a house, only a place to keep tools and boil a kettle when the bog was cut by hand, by men who walked out from farms on the mineral ground and walked back at dark. The pump house has a lamp above its door but no chair inside, only a switchboard and a float and a pump that starts itself when the water in the collecting drain rises past a mark. The lamp is lit for the benefit of no one. It comes on at dusk because it is wired to come on at dusk, and it shows the door to the black fields and the ridges and the frost, and in the morning it goes off again. If you waited beside it all night you would not see it opened.

The machines do not live here either, though they are the closest thing to inhabitants the floor has. They are parked, not abandoned; there is a difference, and you can read it in the way the harvester's drum is raised clear of the ground so it will not freeze in, and the way the bogies are coupled and braked rather than scattered. Someone put them where they are with the intention of coming back. That someone is not here. The men who drive them in summer — and it is a dozen men for a basin this size, working two shifts through the dry months, each alone in a cab for ten hours a day with the radio and the roar — sleep in a town twenty kilometres away and will not set foot on the floor again until April. In winter the machines belong to the frost. Crystals build along their upper edges and the wind takes them off the leeward side, and this happens every night, and nobody sees it.

So: nobody. And yet the sound is being made by something, and that something is being made by someone, and if you follow the conveyor up its lattice to the rim and along the trestles to the brick hall, you find the nearest thing this place has to residents.

There are six of them on the night shift. They sit in a control room on the second floor of the station, behind two panes of glass with a gap between, in a quiet that is not quiet — the roar is in the floor, in the desks, in the water in their cups, a tremor they stopped noticing years ago and would notice instantly if it stopped. They do not hear the plant. They hear the plant change. A fan bearing that has begun to go announces itself as a slight new colour in the vibration under the left elbow of the man at the boiler panel, and he will send someone to listen to it with a rod pressed to the housing and his ear pressed to the rod, and the rod will tell him what the room already told him. They have all lost some hearing and they all know it and they all have the same story about their wives raising their voices. Three of them are the sons of men who worked here. One of them is the grandson of a man who cut the western bays by hand, whose hut that is with the roof gone, and he has never been inside it and does not think of it as his.

They live in the town, but they live in the sound. Their houses are quiet and the quiet troubles them a little, the way the deck of a ship troubles a sailor when it stops moving. On their days off some of them drive out to the rim road and sit in the car with the engine off for the pleasure of feeling it come up through the seat. They would not describe it that way. They would say they were checking on something.

They have not stood on the floor of the basin in years. It is not their job. The station is fed by the conveyor and the conveyor is fed by the rail line and the rail line is fed by the harvesters, and the men in the control room deal only with what arrives at the top of the belt: milled peat, black-brown, the texture of coarse coffee grounds, half water by weight and smelling of nothing much, tipped into a bunker and dried and blown into the boilers as dust. It burns like dust. It has no flame you would recognise, only a glow inside a furnace the size of a church, and out the other end comes steam and smoke and, into a hopper at the bottom, ash.

The ash is worth stopping for. It is grey and fine and there is very little of it — two or three per cent of what went in — and it is the only stone this place has ever had. The moss made its body from rain and air, and rain and air carried dust in them, a few grains a year from deserts and volcanoes and fields a continent away, and the moss took them in and held them for four thousand years, and now they are here, in a hopper, sifted out of the years by fire. The men shovel it into a lorry once a week and it goes to be mixed into cement. That is the first thing that actually lives in the station, if you will allow it: the mineral residue of the sky over four millennia, released and concentrated and driven away on Thursdays.

But the men are twenty kilometres away and asleep for two-thirds of every day, and the station is a mouth, not a body, and you asked what lives here.

Go back down to the floor.

The hare lives somewhere else. That is plain from the way it stood — not at home, alert in the wrong way, watching you across a field with nothing to hide behind and clearly aware of it. Hares on the intact bog have forms in the heather and know every tussock. This one had come down over the rim for some reason of its own, to eat the bark off the birch seedlings perhaps, and was making its way back, and when it went it went in a straight line for the face. The harrier lives somewhere else too, or rather it lives everywhere, working a circuit of twenty or thirty square kilometres of which the basin is one dull black leaf; it comes over because the floor is open and things on it can be seen, and it finds almost nothing, and it goes. In summer there are meadow pipits in the abandoned bays and skylarks over the ridges and a pair of curlew that try every year to nest on the unworked ground by the western drain and are flooded or harrowed out. There are frogs in the drains in April, a great many of them, spawning in water that is barely water, and there are dragonflies in July that hatched in the same drains and hunt the midges that hatched there too. In winter there is none of that. The cold has not killed it. The cold has put it away.

What the cold has not put away is underneath you.

Push your hand into the floor — not the frozen crust but the soft under it, and the cold soft under that — and you are pushing into a living community more populous than any city, though nothing in it is larger than a grain of the peat itself. The peat is wet and airless and acid, and it has been wet and airless and acid for four thousand years, and in it live the things that can bear that: bacteria that breathe sulphate instead of oxygen, others that breathe carbon dioxide and exhale methane, fungi that specialise in the very slow decomposition of things that resist decomposition. They are not many kinds. They are unimaginably many individuals. They work in the dark at a temperature a few degrees above freezing at a speed that makes the moss look hasty — this is why the peat is still here; this is why the birch bark halfway up the face is still white and papery and whole; they have been eating the bog since the bog began and have got through perhaps a tenth of it. Every winter the floor is scraped a few centimetres lower and they are exposed to air for the first time in millennia and most of them die. Every summer the floor is harrowed and dried and they die again. Underneath, they carry on. They will carry on to the clay. When the methane they make rises through a drain in summer you can see it, a bubble the size of a coin breaking the tea-coloured skin and leaving a ring, and that is the closest they come to being seen.

Deeper still, and the question of what lives changes shape.

Because the peat does not rot, things in it do not leave. The birch forest is in the face — you saw it, a white line at head height — and it is dead but it has not gone anywhere. Its bark is intact. Its wood, where you find a stump, is black and heavy and wet and can be split with a fingernail and has kept its rings, so that a patient person could count the summers of a tree that died five thousand years ago and say which were dry. Below the birch there are seeds you can identify to the species. There are the wing-cases of beetles, iridescent still. There are pollen grains by the billion, each one a hollow sculpture the shape of its parent flower, so that the whole history of what grew within a hundred kilometres of here is stacked in the black metre at the bottom of the face like pages. And in some basins like this one — not this one, or not yet, or not found — there are people. Wood and leather and hair and skin, tanned brown, faces pressed slightly flat by the weight of the years above them, the stubble on a jaw still there, the contents of a stomach still legible. They were put in the bog for reasons that are argued about, and the bog did what it does, which is to hold. It is a place where death does not proceed. The dead here are neither alive nor gone. They are simply kept.

The station is where the keeping ends. That is what the roar is. Everything the bog held back for four thousand years — the rotting, the release, the return of carbon to air — happens in the boilers in the space of a few seconds, at a temperature of a thousand degrees, all at once. The methane the bacteria would have taken another ten thousand years to make comes out of the stack this afternoon. The birch forest that has been waiting in the face for its funeral gets it, on a belt, by the tonne. The station is not doing anything to the bog that the bog was not eventually going to do to itself. It is doing it fast, and in one place, and loudly.

Which brings you to the moss.

You have been standing on it the whole time, of course. But look at the living part — the cushions along the drain, red and lime-green, each one the size of a fist or a loaf, luminous in a way nothing else in the basin is, as if lit from underneath. Sphagnum. Bend and look closely. Each cushion is a crowd of stems packed shoulder to shoulder, each stem crowned with a small tight head of branches, and the heads together make a surface like the pile of a carpet. Press it and it gives and springs back and your fingertip comes away wet. The plant is nine parts water. Its leaves are one cell thick and most of those cells are dead, empty, perforated, built for no purpose but to hold water by capillary force and hold it and hold it; a handful wrung out will fill a cup. It has no roots. It does not need them. It draws what it needs from rain, and rain has almost nothing in it, and so the moss has learned to live on almost nothing, and — this is the important thing — to make everything around it live on almost nothing too. It pumps hydrogen ions into the water and takes the scarce minerals in exchange, and the water goes acid, and the acid and the wet and the cold together see off anything that might rot it or compete with it or eat it. It poisons its neighbourhood into a place where only it can thrive, and then it thrives.

And it grows at the tip. That is the thing to understand about what lives here. The green head at the top of each stem is alive. A centimetre below the head the stem is still alive but paler. Two centimetres below it is yellow and dying. Below that it is dead, and the dead part stays, and the living head goes on lengthening upward on a stalk of its own corpse, a millimetre a year, and the corpse is preserved in the acid the head made, and the corpse holds water so the head never dries, and the water holds the cold, and the cold holds off the rot. The whole dome of the bog, the whole four-thousand-year six-metre thickness of the ground you climbed down through, is one plant's dead body, and the plant is still on top of it, still alive, a green skin a few millimetres thick on a mass of itself. It is the only organism you will ever stand inside. It is the only organism that is also a landscape. It has been alive continuously here for longer than there has been farming in this country, not as a species — species are alive everywhere — but as this individual, this colony, this unbroken descent of head from head from head, each generation growing out of the one before without ever once letting go.

That is what lives here. A thing that lives by not dying, and by not letting anything die, and by turning the place it lives into a place where nothing can happen except the slow accumulation of itself. It is not a plant in any sense you are used to. It is closer to a coral reef, or a glacier, or a habit. Its whole strategy is patience. It lost a few centuries to the birch, when the climate dried and the trees came in and shaded it — and then the rain came back and it drowned them, all of them, in itself, and kept them, and is standing on them now.

Now stand at the toe of the face and look at the cushions along the drain, and look up at the station on the rim with its plume gone orange from below, and understand what the cushions are.

They are the same organism. Not figuratively — the same descent, the same water, the same acid, seeded from spores and fragments that survived the milling in the wet corners the harvesters could not reach, or blown down over the rim from the intact dome above. They have started again. They are doing exactly what they did ten thousand years ago when the ice went and the clay basin filled with water and the first cushions took hold at its edges. They will make a skin. The skin will hold water. The water will go acid and cold. Things will fall into it and not rot. The floor will begin, a millimetre a year, to come back.

And every summer the harvesters will take three centimetres off the floor, and the moss will lose thirty years in a fortnight, and start again.

That is what lives here: one thing, in two states, at two speeds. The green head of a body that stopped counting its years a very long time ago, sitting in the bottom of the grave the head dug and the fire is emptying; and, on the rim, the fire, which is the same body's rot, arriving all at once and fifty centuries late, and screaming as it comes. Between them, six men in a warm room feeling it through their elbows, a hare that does not want to be here, a harrier that does not care, and a sea of bacteria in the dark doing very slowly what the station does very fast. But those are visitors, or tenants, or teeth. The resident is the moss. It was here before the roar and it will be here when the roar stops — not because it will win, but because it does not know how to do anything else. When the clay shows and the last belt runs empty and the fans spin down and the sound, for the first time in half a century, drains out of the basin like water out of a dish, the cushions along the drains will not notice. They will be doing what they were doing. Holding water. Growing at the tip. Dying at the base and not letting go.
