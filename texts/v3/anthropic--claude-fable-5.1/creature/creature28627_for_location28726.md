You look for it the way you look for anything living: you look for movement. There isn't any. So you look for the green instead, because green is the next thing after movement, and there is a little of it.

It is on the upper shelves, on the ledges that face the afternoon. A nap of filament, dark and fine, laid over the silt like felt that has been rained on. Get close and it is not a carpet but a crowd—thousands of strands, each a single file of cells, each leaning very slightly toward the brightest part of the ceiling. Nothing has ever grazed it. In a pond it would be cropped by snails, combed by insect larvae, torn loose in tufts by fish nosing after what lives in it, and it would be ragged and thin and always recovering. Here it has never been touched. It has the even, unbroken, slightly unreal quality of moss in a valley with no deer. It takes what the light gives it and it makes more of itself, and then, about ten meters down, it stops, not because the light stops but because the water has nothing else to offer. It is a tenant of the top floors only. It lives on the rim of the room and has no idea of the rest.

Below that, at the shallow end, the garden. The stonewort. It is a stranger thing than it looks from a distance. It is a plant, an alga technically, that has decided to build itself a skeleton. Its stems are jointed like very small bamboo, and at every joint a whorl of branchlets stands out stiffly, and the whole of it—stem, joint, branch—is crusted in lime it has pulled out of the water and laid down on its own skin. It is grey more than green. It is brittle. If you pinched a stem it would crunch like a stick of chalk and give off, for a second, a musk, a faint smell of garlic—the one smell in the pit, and nobody has ever smelled it. It holds itself upright not by the pressure of water in its cells but by the stiffness of its own encrustation. It is a plant that has been turning into stone while alive, and this is not a symptom of anything; it is how it lives. It has rooted in the pocket of silt the fallen tree gathered, and it has grown to the edge of that pocket, and there it has stopped, in a ring two meters across, neither spreading nor dying back. It is a resident. It has an address. But it is a small thing in a large room, and the room is not explained by it.

The trees, then. They are the largest bodies here and they came from outside, from the world of birds and wind, and they died standing when the water reached their roots and kept rising, and everything soft about them has since gone: bark, leaf, the fine twig, the cambium. What remains is pale hard heartwood, grained and ridged, tapering into the light. They are dwelling here in the way a chair dwells in a house. They give the felt a place to grow on the sunward side. They give the silt an eddy to settle in. The fallen one has become a wall for the garden. They were living and are not, and yet they are as much a part of what occupies the pit as anything else, because they stay, and staying is most of what living here means.

And yet none of these is what you are looking for. You feel that. The felt is a visitor from the sunlit world, kept on a short lease by the light. The stonewort is one small colony. The trees are dead. None of them accounts for the sense you have, hanging here, of being in an occupied room.

So you go to the wall and look at the smallest thing.

The bloom. The pale fur on the blast scallops, in the drill half-cylinders, along the lip of the cable groove. From a meter away it looks like dust that has been allowed to settle on a vertical surface, which is impossible, and from a hand's width away it is not dust at all. It is a crust. A rind of calcite crystals so fine they read as a matte white glaze, thicker in the concave places where the water lies still, thinner on edges, absent nowhere. It is on the drill marks. It is on the groove. It is on the dead trees' wood, where it has begun to fill the grain. It is on the stonewort, so that you cannot say precisely where the plant's own chalk stops and the pit's chalk begins. Brush the silt off the tire corrugations on the ramp and it is there too, a skin over the pressed marl, sealing the tracks under a translucent varnish. Every surface in the room has the same coat, and the coat is one substance, and it is growing.

Not fast. Nothing that could be called fast. The water took its lime from the fresh-blasted stone in the first years, when the walls were raw and the dust was everywhere, and now it is giving that lime back, crystal by crystal, wherever a surface will accept it. In places the giving-back is probably mediated by a film of bacteria thinner than the crystals themselves, a living smear that changes the chemistry a hair's breadth from the rock and lets the calcite come out of solution there and not elsewhere. In places it is probably just the water, cooling against stone, letting go of what it can no longer hold. It does not matter which. The effect is the same, and the effect is single: the pit is being lined.

And the water that does the lining is not the water above. You noticed the thermocline on the way down—the soft boundary, the change against your skin. Below it, the water has not left. This is the point. It came in when the pumps stopped, welling up through the floor and the cracks in the walls, and it filled the cut to the height of the water table, and there it has been. The surface water rides on top of it and has its weather and its seasons and its warm summer skin, and mixes and remixes and forgets itself every autumn; the water down here was never asked to mix and has never done it. It is older than the trees' deaths. It is the same water that first touched the raw drill marks. It is not a medium something lives in. It is the body of the thing.

That is what lives here. It is not in the room; it is the lower half of the room, and the skin that half is laying on the walls. A single cool distributed inhabitant, with no center and no edge except the plane where it meets the warmer water above and the plane where it meets the stone, and both of those edges are its working faces. Above, it receives what settles—the last of the silt, a flake of algae, the shocked grain that finally lets go—and holds it, and lets it fall. Below and around, it puts stone back.

It cannot put the stone back. There is not enough dissolved in it to refill a thousandth of what was taken. It is not trying to. What it is doing is smaller and more particular: it is closing the wound at the surface. Rounding the comb-teeth of the drill holes. Softening the scallops into shells and the shells into swellings. Filling the cable groove a micron at a time so that the polished channel becomes a dimple and the dimple a memory. It is taking the geometry of extraction and easing it, the way calcite eases everything it touches, into something that reads as grown rather than cut. Give it long enough and the corners will not be true. Give it longer and the terraces will read as the strata of a cliff not because they resemble strata but because they have become a kind of strata, dressed and rounded and deposited on. The pit was made by taking away; the thing that lives in it is the return.

It is patient in the way that only something without expectation can be patient. It is not waiting for the drill marks to go. It is not waiting for anything. It does not know about the light column, which is the felt's business, up on the shelves. It has a temperature and the temperature is the stone's. When a flake lets go of the wall and ticks down through it, that is the loudest thing that happens to it in a year, and it is not an interruption; it is the tenant's own body shedding a scale. When you came down through the thermocline you passed into it, and it received you, and it did not change. Your fins stirred a plume of silt off the ramp and the plume hung and thinned and went down again, and by the time you are back at the surface the ramp will be exactly as it was, and the tenant will not have registered your visit, because registering is not something it does.

It is not lonely. It would be a mistake to say the pit is lonely. Loneliness needs the memory of company, and nothing down here remembers the trucks, the conveyors, the men with the shot-firing cable. The tenant arrived after they left and has never known the room any other way than empty of them, and has been furnishing it since with the only thing it has: a slow white lining, laid over everything, indifferent and even and very nearly permanent, the color of the bloom on a plum, the texture of the inside of a shell. A cool volume of old water, holding still, and the stillness is not the absence of a life. The stillness is what the life looks like from outside.
