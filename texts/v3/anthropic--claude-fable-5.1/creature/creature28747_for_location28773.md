Start with the bats, because that is what anyone would say, and then set them aside.

A bat does not live here. It roosts here. The difference matters in a place like this. Every dusk roughly half the ceiling detaches itself and goes up the skylights in a column that darkens the shafts for the better part of an hour, and spends the night miles away over the scrub, over ground that this country has no idea exists, eating things that never fall through the roof. The bat that hangs above a given square of gallery floor at noon has a whole second existence in the open air, under weather, under stars, and it carries that existence back down and hangs it up and forgets it, and comes back to it the next night. Its body knows two temperatures. Its ears know two kinds of silence. It is a commuter, and the cave is its dormitory, and the astonishing thing about the ceiling is not that it is alive but that it is a rota — a surface maintained by a population that is never all present at once, the way a road is maintained by traffic. Pull one animal out of the mass and you have a small thing with a wrinkled face and a heartbeat that can slow to almost nothing, and it has seen the sky more times than it has seen the floor, and it does not know the floor is there.

The swifts are worse. They go up in daylight and come down at dark and the roofed edges where they nest are the shallowest part of the system, barely inside, the light still grey enough to see a hand. They are a fringe. They belong to the collapses, to the sunken gardens with their leaning trees, which are already half above-ground places. Nothing that flies lives here. Flight is the way out.

So go down a layer, to the floor, and pick up a cockroach.

Here is something that has never seen the lid. It hatched in the warm mass, in the dark, under the drip line of a roost that has been occupied continuously for longer than its lineage has existed, and it has spent its whole life within a few strides of the point where it hatched, because it has never had a reason to go further. Food falls on it. Water condenses beside it. Warmth comes up from underneath. It has eaten the dead bats, the bat droppings, the shed skins, the moulted casings of its own kind, the mould that grows on all of it; it has been walked over by millipedes and has walked over millipedes; it has felt the surface tremble when the ceiling above it lifted and resettled and has not looked up, having nothing to look with that would help. In a few months it will be taken by a cricket or a centipede or will simply stop, and its body will go into the mass and be indistinguishable from it within a day. It is not so much an inhabitant as a component. The guano is not where the cockroach lives; the guano is, to a first approximation, what the cockroach is, the same matter passing through a hundred thousand mouths and back out and in again, warm from the churning, glittering slightly where the light finds it. To ask which of them lives here is to ask which drop of the pool is the pool.

Still, that is closer. The cockroach at least does not know there is an above. Hold onto that and go up the wall.

On the wall there is a snake.

It is lying along a fracture in the rock, a crack as sharp-edged as the day the ceiling settled and split it, three or four body-lengths above the floor and perhaps a body-length below the lowest hanging fringe of the roost. It is dark, banded in a darker dark, and thin enough that from the floor it reads as a seam in the stone. Its head is turned up. It has been in this position, or one very like it, for most of a day, and before that for most of the day before, and if anyone were counting it would turn out that it has been on this length of wall for years.

This is the one to spend time with, because it has stopped being a candidate and started being an animal.

The snake does not see the roost. There is not enough light on this stretch of gallery for seeing to be worth the trouble, and its eyes are small and cloudy in the way of things that have given up on them without quite discarding them. It knows the roost as heat. The ceiling above it is a warm fog, a low continuous glow on the sensing parts of its face, and against that fog individual bodies register as brighter points when they shift, when a wing opens, when a bat drops a hand's breadth to jostle for a better grip. The snake reads these movements the way a person reads ripples on water. It knows which parts of the mass are settled and which are restless. It knows that arrivals come in from the western end and that the fringe nearest the fracture thickens an hour after the roar changes pitch, when the night-flyers are back and pushing up into the pack. It does not know that there is a night, or that anything is flying, or that the roar comes from wings. It knows that the warm fog above it breathes on a long slow cycle, and that when the fog is thickest and lowest, something will hang within reach.

Then it strikes. It is fast and it is short — it does not launch itself, it merely unfolds the topmost curve of its body upward and closes its jaws on whatever fur is nearest, and folds back. The mass does not react. The bat it has taken reacts, briefly. Then the snake has a weight in its mouth that it must hold against the drag of its own body along a knife-edged crack, and it begins the slow work of turning the weight and taking it in, which will occupy it for some time and after which it will not move at all for days. Its skin, where it presses against the fracture edges, is scarred in fine parallel lines. When it sheds, it sheds against those edges, hooking the old skin on the sharpest one and drawing itself out, and the skin peels back inside-out and hangs for a while like a pale root and then lets go and drifts down into the moving floor and is gone by morning, if there were a morning.

The snake has a country, and its country is this: one fracture, perhaps twenty of its own lengths long, with a wider pocket at one end where it can coil entirely and a narrowing at the other where the crack pinches shut. Above, the fog. Below, the rustling — which it also feels as warmth, a different, lower, steadier warmth, the heat of decomposition coming up through the air. To either side along the wall are other fractures and in some of them other snakes, and there is a slow negotiation of these territories that takes place through scent left on stone and the occasional shove, but the negotiation happens rarely and the snake has held this crack long enough that it no longer happens to it. It has never crossed the floor. It has never been to the far wall. It has never been under a skylight, and if it were carried to one and set down in the grey cone it would experience the light as a wrongness on the skin, a cooling, and would move away from it back into the dark, back toward the fog.

It is old, for a snake. It has struck upward into the mass some thousands of times and been struck at, in its own turn, by nothing — the centipedes that patrol below keep to the lower wall, the owls and the wet-furred cats keep to the light-edges where the streams of animals cross, and up here in the middle dark of a roofed gallery, a body's length under the roost, nothing hunts it. It will die on this fracture. Its body will loosen from the crack and fall into the floor and the floor will take it, and for a while a section of the fog above will go unharvested, and then another snake will find the pocket at the end of the crack and hook itself in, and the harvest will resume. The wall will not have noticed. The edges of the crack, in the whole span of the old snake's life, will not have been rounded by so much as a hair.

What the snake has that the bat and the cockroach do not is a position. It is not carried through this place by the churn of the mass and it does not leave it by the shafts. It has chosen a stretch of rock and it holds it and everything it needs comes to that stretch of rock, and this is as close to residence as the country permits — not building, not owning, but staying, at one point, in the dark, facing the source. If the place has a citizen, it is a thin dark animal lying along a fresh crack in young stone with its face turned up toward a warmth it has never seen.

And yet. Go further in, past the last skylight, down the tube that dips under the black pool and comes up on the other side.

Here the roar has become a hum. It comes through the flooded passage as a vibration in the water and reaches the far chamber as a low steady note in the rock, felt more than heard, the way a person in a building feels machinery on a lower floor. There is no light. There has never been light. In the pool that fills the lowest part of the chamber a fish hangs, white, translucent enough that the dark loop of its gut shows through, with two smooth dimples where the eyes would go, and it is not waiting for anything. It has no fog above it and no falling floor. What it eats arrives as a faint drift of organic dust carried in on the seep from the galleries — the finest fraction of the guano, dissolved and settled and dissolved again — and as the occasional shrimp, and it takes these when the water tells it to and otherwise does not move. It has no cycle, because nothing reaches it that has a cycle. It does not know that the hum is wings, or that the wings go out, or that there is an out. The snake at least faces something; the fish faces nothing, in a chamber that has never been anything but full and never anything but dark and never anything but the temperature it is, and it is entirely at home.

So the answer moves as the light fails. Where the sky shows, nothing lives here — everything passes through. In the grey, tenants. In the roofed dark, a resident, holding its crack. Beyond the sump, in the flooded black, something for which the question has no meaning, because it has never had anywhere else to be.

One more thing. Somebody came down here once with a struck edge and a brand and stood in the grey light at the foot of a shaft and looked at the wall of sound and left, and the flakes they dropped are still on the slab because nothing here has any use for them. That person is the only creature in the story who could have told the difference between visiting and living, and what they did with the knowledge was climb back up. The cockroaches walk over the flakes. The snake, two galleries away, has not moved. In the flooded room the fish hangs in water that has never had a surface anyone breathed at, and the hum goes on.
