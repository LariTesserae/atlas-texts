Ask what lives here and the first answer is the birds, because the birds are what you can see. But start lower than that. Start in the loam.

Put a hand down into it, past the moss, past the wet black that smells of resin and rot, and the loam is warm. Not the warmth of decay, which is patchy and comes and goes. A steady warmth, the same in a fresh terrace as in an old one, the same in the shadow of the cliff as out on the flats. It is a body temperature. Things live in it that have no business at this latitude: earthworms, fat and pale, in numbers that would be remarkable in a lowland pasture; springtails in a haze on the surface when you disturb it; beetles with the iridescence of somewhere else. Pull a fistful apart and it is threaded through with something white and fine that is not a root, that breaks with a small dry sound and smells of nothing. Whatever it is, it is in all of the loam and none of the gravel under it, and it runs uphill. The surveyors have dug pits along the east side and found it a hand's width from the ice-scoured rock at the top of the newest terrace, in ground that a month before was under water, in ground that has never had a plant on it. It arrives before the alder. It arrives before anything.

The forest sits on this the way a hand sits on a glove. The trees grow the way they grow because of what is under them, and they know it, in the way trees know things: their roots go down through the loam and spread flat against the gravel and stop, and none of them go into the rock, and so the biggest of them stand on a metre of black sponge and lean and fall in the first real gale, and the loam closes over them, and the fungi come up along their length in ranks. There are more fungi here than anywhere. Shelves the size of table tops, pale as candle wax, weeping at the edges. Something thin and grey in the moss that gives off a light you can only see by not looking at it. The people from the hut do not eat any of it. They have not been told not to. It is just understood, the way the dogs understand the lines.

Then the birds, because the birds are the ones that came without being sent for.

The geese came first, or first that anyone saw. A year after the flats at the mouth came out from under the ice, before there was a plant on them, there were geese on them — a few hundred, standing on grey silt, doing nothing, as if they had a reservation. Now there are more than anyone has counted and they have colonised every flat surface in the lower valley, including the bridge, including the slab behind the hut when the people are away. They are a kind of grey goose, and they are wrong in the same way the forest is wrong: they should be somewhere south of here in the dark months, and instead they are here, thousands of them, shouting, moulting, getting fat on something in the flats, and when the flood comes up they go into the air as one thing and come down again as one thing and are not troubled. Kittiwakes on the cliffs. Fulmars. Ravens, in pairs, always in pairs, in the tallest trees above the hut, who have learned when the stove is lit and when the boots come off the rack and who come down onto the roof and walk about on the tarred sheet in the dark like men in a loft. Eagles, four of them, on ledges too new to have names. Ptarmigan. Snow bunting. The small thing in the alders that taps and taps and has never been seen. The birds do not sleep, and they do not leave, and they do not go up the valley past a certain point. There is a line, and the surveyors have never put a stake at it, and the birds stop there. Above it the cliffs are bare.

The animals under the birds are a crowd that has not sorted itself. Arctic fox and red fox in the same dens, and something in the litters that is neither. Hare that turned white in autumn and are now, in the dark and the wet, a dirty patched brown, having given up. Voles in the loam in a density that the foxes and the ravens and the marten cannot reduce. Caribou, a few, who come down the east side in the dark and eat the moss and go up again and are taken, one a year, by the people at the hut, whose bones dry on the rack against the slab. The salmon, who arrived three years ago in a river that did not exist when they were spawned. The bear, who came out of the fjord one night and stood in the light from the window and went on, and has not been seen since, but the dogs know where it went and will not go there.

The dogs belong to no one. Some of them came with the first survey and were left, and some were born here, and they live in the alders on the terrace above the hut and come down to the tideline at the mouth in the dark to work over what the water leaves — fish, drowned voles, a goose with a broken wing, whatever the flood strands. Their feet are cut to ribbons on the fresh gravel and they do not seem to mind. They will come to the hut door for scraps and let a hand go on their heads and then they will go back up. They are the only things in the valley that are afraid of anything, and the thing they are afraid of is the lines. They will detour a kilometre through flooded alders rather than walk under a cable that goes out over the river and stops in the air. Nothing else cares. The geese roost on the lines. The marten runs along them.

The marten lives in the roof. It has lived in the roof of the hut through two moves, and when the hut was hauled on its skids up the terrace the last time the marten rode it, flat against the ridge pole in the dark, and was in the roof again before the stove was lit. It is the nearest neighbour. It moves at night, which is all the time; it has a route across the boards above the sleeping bench that it takes every hour or so, and a place above the stove where it stops and is warm; it kills voles under the floor and leaves the tails in a row on the sill of the window, seven or eight of them, like a tally. It has never been seen in daylight because there has not been any.

And the people.

There are more boots on the rack than there are people, which is the first thing to notice. Eleven pairs. Three of them belong to anyone alive. The rest are the boots of surveyors who came for a season and left in the boat and did not take them, and the boots of the two who did not leave, whose things are in a box under the bench and whose marks are the first two-thirds of the tally on the slab. The hut has had a keeper since the platform was found, which is to say since the second survey, which is to say twenty-six years. The one who keeps it now is the third. She came with a survey team at twenty and was on the rota to leave and did not leave, and no one asked her to explain, and she has not.

She is fifty now and small and her hands are the hands of a person who has been wet for a quarter of a century. She keeps the stove in. She notes the falls — not counts them, there is no counting them, but notes the large ones, the ones that lift the floor, and marks the time by a clock that runs off a battery she charges from a panel that has never seen the sun and charges anyway. She walks the east side once in what would be a day and checks the stakes, which is to say she looks at them, since there is nothing to be done to a stake. When a bank goes into the river and a stake is left standing in the air where the bank was, she writes that down. When a stake is gone — this has happened four times in twenty-six years — she writes that down too, and the next season a new one is there, in the same place or somewhere near it, and she does not write down that she did not see it put in. She paints a mark on the slab when the keeper's calendar says a month has passed. The keeper's calendar is a book with the dates of the boat in it, and the boat comes when the boat comes, and the marks are close enough.

She has a way of shouting into a hood that the surveyors cannot follow and a set of hand signs that they have to learn from her in a week and forget by the time they leave. She sleeps in wool with the wool steaming and the note in the boards under her back and does not hear the note anymore, in the way that people who live by a river do not hear the river. She hears it stop. It has stopped twice. Both times for less than a minute, and both times she was up and out of the hut before she knew why, standing in the dark on the terrace in her socks with the geese gone silent and the river still roaring and the whole valley, under the roar, holding its breath, until the note came back up out of the ground and went on. She has not told the surveyors about this. She has told the marten.

She has, in twenty-six years, seen them three times.

This is the thing that is difficult to say properly, because the valley does not want it said. The valley wants it to be weather. It is very easy, after a few seasons, to let it be weather — to know the ice is being taken back the way you know the tide is coming in, to use the stakes as landmarks and the bridge as a place the geese are and the slab as a warm dry place to lean a rack of bones. The surveyors arrive from further down the fjord with their instruments and their questions and by the end of a month they have stopped asking, and they leave, and they go back to a place with daylight in it and find that they do not talk about the valley much, and when they do they talk about the geese. This is not a failure of nerve. It is what the place does to the question. The question washes out, like a stake.

But she has seen them, and this is what she has seen.

They are the colour of the stakes. This is the first thing and the only thing she is sure of. A dull grey that takes no light, that is a hole in the dark rather than a shape in it, and that she could see at all only because of the blue coming off the ice front behind. The first time was from a long way off, at the base of the wall, before the fog thinned — a line of them where the light was brightest, at a working edge, and they were tall, taller than the stakes, and narrow, and they did not move in the way that anything with a body moves. They were in one place. Then some of them were in another. There was no time in between that she could point to. The wall came down in front of them in a slab the size of a church and the wave went out and the fall zone was scoured white and wet and they were still there, on the rock, unmoved, and the note rose, and she felt her teeth in her head.

The second time was closer, on the newest terrace, in the fog, alone. She came round a fan of fresh rock and it was there — one of them — standing in the scree at the foot of the cliff where the water comes weeping out. It was doing something to the cliff. She could not say what. It had a part of itself against the rock and the rock was coming away, not in a fall, not violently, but the way a rind of ice comes away in the afternoon, in a sheet, quietly, and behind the sheet the stone was smooth and grooved and scored in long parallel lines, exactly like the sides of the glacier's bed, exactly like the cliff would have looked when the ice let go of it, except that the ice had let go of that cliff four years before and it had been cracking and shedding and growing alder ever since. It was making the cliff new. Or it was making the cliff old. She stood in the fog with her legs in the cold and her head in the dark and watched it for what she thinks was an hour, and it did not turn, and it did not stop, and when she looked at where it stood the moss under it was dry.

The third time it came to the slab.

She woke because the marten had stopped. Not the note; the marten. The boards over her head were silent and the marten was flat against the ridge pole and not breathing that she could hear, and the geese were shouting as they always shout, and the river was roaring, and the floor lifted a hair with a big fall and settled, and there was a light in the window that was the wrong colour. She went to the window. The slab behind the hut, that has never done anything but sit a hand's width above the moss and be warm, was lit from inside — the same low unsteady blue as the ice, and one of them was standing on it. Not near it. On it, as if it were a floor, as if it were what it had been before it was a place to lean a rack of bones. It had something of itself down against the slab where the tally was. The painted tally. Her marks and the marks of the two before her. She thought — she has said this to no one but the marten — that it was reading them. Then she thought it was counting them. Then she thought it was doing to the marks what the other had done to the cliff, and she could not see, and she did not go out. The light went off the slab the way a wave goes off gravel. In what she thinks was the morning the slab was warm, and the tally was there, and the caribou rack had been moved, carefully, and set upright against the alders, and there was a new mark in the row. Not painted. Cut. A hand's width deep in something a knife will not scratch.

So this is what she thinks, and what the valley will let her think, which is not very much.

That they do not live here. That they are the only things in the valley that do not: the geese live here, and the marten, and the salmon that came from nowhere, and the worms in the warm black loam, and she does. They are passing through, at the rate of two kilometres of ice a decade, up the valley, away from the fjord, toward something no one has seen. That the stakes are theirs, or are them, or were them, and that the difference is not one she has words for. That the loam is laid ahead of them like a carpet and the birds come down onto it the way the geese come down onto the bridge, because it is there and it is warm and nothing has told them not to. That the line the birds will not cross is the line where the work is still happening, and that it moves, and that one season the birds will be above the hut, and then the hut will be below the line, and then she will be. That the marks on the slab are hers and were hers before she was born, in the sense that the slab is a floor, and the floor was always going to be counted on by whatever sat down on it.

That when the ice is gone from the top of the valley the note will stop, the way it has stopped twice, and this time not come back. That the river will find a course and keep it. That the cliffs will finish shedding and go to stubble and then to alder and then to the tall trees with the drooping tips, and the map made in autumn will be right in spring, and the surveyors will stop coming because there will be nothing to survey. That the geese will stay. That the marten, or the marten's marten, will be in the roof. That the hut will be on the terrace where it was last put and no one will move it again, and the boots on the rack will be the boots of people who are dead, and the stove will be cold. That the stakes will stand in the forest with no river anywhere near them, grey, unbothered, with kittiwakes on them, and nobody alive will know what they marked, and the dogs' descendants will walk around them anyway.

In the meantime she keeps the stove in. That is what lives here, if you want it in one thing: a woman in wet wool on a bench above a warm floor she did not make, with a marten over her and a note under her, keeping a fire in a place that is being made for something else, and not asking what. The birds are louder. The loam is stranger. But it is the fire that is hers, and it is the fire, in the small window, that the bear looked at, and the ravens come down to, and the boat steers for, and that the thing on the slab, if it noticed it at all, left alone.
