The easy answer is that the plant lives here, and it is not wrong. The plant is the only thing in the cavern that has never been anywhere else and never will be. But it is hard to say that something lives in a place when it is the place — when the floor you stand on to look at it is its root-bed and the light you look by is its skin. The plant does not inhabit the cavern. It is what the cavern is turning into. The things that live here are the things that could, in principle, leave, and don't.

---

Start small, because that is where the eye lands first.

The pale beetles at the growth-fronts are not quite the pruners they look like. Watch one for the length of a pulse and you see it does not eat the filaments so much as bite them and wait, and what it is waiting for is the bead of oil that rises to the bite. It drinks that. The filament it has bitten stiffens, browns at the tip, and stops; the neighboring filaments, unbitten, go on. So the beetles are not shaping the city by taste — they are shaping it by thirst, and the clean edges of the well-beetled structures are the edges of whatever the beetles happened to drink from first. Their bodies are dry to the touch and weigh nothing. Crush one and there is no wetness, only a smear like graphite and the bread-and-pitch smell of the sap it was carrying. They have no water in them. Nothing here does. Whatever water these animals need they make inside themselves, out of the oil, the way certain desert things do, and they hold it hard and give none of it back to the air.

The tickers inside the columns are never seen whole. What you see is a leg, or the tip of a leg, drawn back into the flute the moment the light of your attention falls on it. The ticking is not a call. It is the sound of them moving up and down the channel, scores of them in each column, and if you lay your palm flat on the fluting you can feel the traffic under it, a faint grainy patter, the column's pulse taken through the column's own skin. When a column goes dark and spent they leave it within a day and the flutes stand open and silent and the beetles do not come either, and the column stands in the district like a tooth pulled and left on the table.

The flat grey warmth-seekers on the glowing beams are the only things in the cavern that seem to have anything like leisure. They lie. They are the color and the flatness of a dropped glove. Every so often one lifts a foot and sets it down again a hand's width along the beam, following some gradient of heat too fine to feel, and that is the whole of its day. They eat the beetles, probably. They have never been seen to do it.

---

The herds are the cavern's motion and they are also, in a way, its strangers. They are the size of a large dog and stand lower, on six legs that move in a rolling pattern that looks wrong for the first hour and inevitable after that. Their bodies are round and their heads are small and held low, and their skin is not fur but a fine dense pile, like the nap of felt, that holds the gypsum dust so completely that a herd standing still is simply not there — a slight roughness on the floor, a hillock, until it moves. They browse the shed slabs and the fallen spools, and they have a way of eating the fiber that involves winding it around a foreleg first, dozens of turns, so that a herd at a spool-heap is a field of small furred spindles slowly rotating.

They do not go inside. This is the thing to understand about them. In all the districts, with all the open doorways and roofed streets and grown halls standing three-quarters walled, no herd animal has ever been seen to step across a threshold. Their trails bend around the structures the way water bends around stones. When a colonnade stands between them and a spool-heap they go the long way. To them the city is not a city. It is an outcrop, a stand of something hard and warm that occasionally drops food and occasionally hides death, and they have learned its edges the way any browsing animal learns the edges of a wood. They live in the cavern. They do not live in the city. They cross it.

---

The one that lives in the city is the one that hunts them.

It takes a long time to see it well, and the first time you see it well it is because it has decided to let you. It is lying along the top of a half-grown wall with its head on its forelegs like a dog on a doorstep, and it is looking at you, and it is the color of everything.

Low, it is long — twice the length of a herd animal, lean through the middle in a way that makes the ribs count themselves under the dust-colored skin. Its skin is bare and dry and has the faint grain of the plant's own tissue, so that lying against a column it is not camouflaged so much as continued. It has four legs, not six, and the forelegs are much the longer, so that when it walks on all fours its back slopes down toward the tail and when it stops it can bring its forequarters up and up and stand.

When it stands it is narrow. Its shoulders are set close, its chest is deep front-to-back and thin side-to-side, and its head, held high on a long neck, is not much wider than the neck. Standing, it is taller than you. Standing in a doorway, it fills the doorway — the too-tall, too-narrow doorway that fits nothing else in the cavern — and stops the light through it like a door.

This is not what the doorways were for. Nothing about the hunter's shape is the shape the plant is building toward; its feet are wrong for the stairs, it has no wrists to speak of, it does not sit. But it is the only thing here that goes inside, and going inside has been shaping it for a long time. The columns are exactly its width. Its forelegs are exactly long enough to rear it into the height of a doorframe. There is a loose skin at the throat that, when it stands still against a wall, hangs in folds that read as the plant's own vascular fluting. You cannot tell — there is no one to ask — whether it found a city that happened to suit it or whether a thousand generations of its kind have been quietly pared down by every ambush that failed because a shoulder showed past a column. Probably both. The city was not built for it, and it is the one the city is fitting.

---

It sleeps in the old district. The oldest buildings there have their doors, grown shut, and the hunter has learned which of them will still open — the grown hinges are stiff but they work — and it lets itself into rooms that hold the last of the warmth from the spent columns in their walls. It sleeps standing. It rears against the wall and lets its forelegs fall forward and down, and the forelegs, at their ends, drop into the row of smooth-bored sockets at chest height, and hang there, and it rests its long jaw on the wall between them and its eyes close.

The sockets were not for that. They are too high for the hunter's comfort and slightly too narrow, and the skin of its forelegs is worn glossy in a band where it rubs the bore. But there is nothing else in the cavern that has ever put anything into those sockets, and the hunter puts its forelegs there every time it sleeps, and its young learn it by watching, and when you find, in a room at the very end of the old district behind a door the plant grew shut long ago, a hunter's skeleton still standing, forelegs still in the wall, jaw fallen, dust to the knee — it is not a strange sight. It looks like something that was meant.

---

It has no shadow to work with. There is no dusk here, no dark corner, no low sun to come out of; the light is even and comes from everywhere. So the hunter does not hunt by shadow or by night. It hunts by stillness and by being the color of the floor and by knowing, better than anything else in the cavern including you, exactly how wide a column is. Its eyes are small and set deep under a heavy brow, and this is not against glare — the light is soft — but against the powder, which is always falling. It blinks slowly and rarely. Its ears are slits. Most of what it knows it knows through its feet and its teeth: the hum of the plant, the patter of the tickers in a column, the felt-soft footfall of a herd on the crust a kilometer off, all of it taken up through the bones.

It eats the herd animals. It also eats the plant, or drinks it: at the fresh fronts, where the growing tissue is soft enough to tear, it bites and waits and licks the oil as it beads, exactly as the beetles do, and it does this most in the hours before a pulse, when the sap is rising. Its breath smells of pitch. Its dung is black and dry and light, and lies on the crust in small cracked pieces that the herds step around and the beetles ignore, and if you break one it is fibrous, and it burns.

It has some sense of the plant's time. That is the strangest thing about it and the hardest to be sure of. But you watch one lie down beside a doorway where the door has grown to knee height, and it lies there through a pulse, and through the next, not sleeping — it lifts its head at every distant footfall — and on the third pulse the door has grown to shoulder height and the hunter rises and steps behind it and is gone. It was waiting for the door. It knew how long a door takes.

You see one climb the stairs to the landing that isn't, one careful step at a time on stairs made for a longer stride than its own, and stand at the top with its forequarters reared and its head against the unfinished lip of the last riser, and look out over the district for a long time, and come down.

---

It does not go into the bowls.

Nothing does, and the hunter is no exception, but the hunter is the only thing that goes to them. It will lie at the lip of a bowl — the twenty-meter grown dish at a district's heart, smooth, empty, faintly warm — with its chin on the rim, and look into the emptiness, and look for a long time. You have seen it do this at three different bowls. You have never seen it set a foot inside. Whatever the bowls are for, the hunter knows it is not for them, and knows that better than it knows almost anything, and waits at the edge as if the bowl were a door of another kind and it had learned how long doors take.

---

It is a solitary thing and there are not many of it. A district holds one, sometimes a pair, and the young leave early and go out across the crust toward the new districts at the far end where the structures are only stubs and the seams are richest and the herds are thickest, and they find a column of their own width and they begin. The oldest hunters are at the old end, in the closed rooms, with the spent columns and the heaps of spools no one will ever come for. The city walks; so does the hunter, at the same pace, a step behind.

It has begun to notice you. Not as prey — you are wrong, you smell of water, you leave wet marks on the columns where your split hands touch them. But you go inside. You are the only other thing in the cavern that walks through the doorways, and it has watched you do it, standing on your two legs, a little too short for the frame and a little too wide, and it has watched you climb the stairs with your too-short stride to stand at the top and look out, the way it does. It lies along the wall in the shadowless light and watches you fit the city badly, and it does not move.

When you sleep, it comes closer. You wake with the hum in your teeth and find its tracks in the powder, four-footed, in a circle, and then two-footed, where it stood, for a while, in the doorway of the room you had chosen, and filled it.
