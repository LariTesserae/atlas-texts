The honest first answer is the plant, and you already know that, and it is not a satisfying answer, because a thing that is the ground is hard to think of as a resident. So set it aside for a moment. Not far. It will come back.

---

Start instead with the scrapers, since they are the ones you will see.

Up close, a scraper is less like an animal than like a piece of the plain that has decided to move. The carapace is jointed in nine or eleven plates, each plate scaled exactly as the mat is scaled, and the scales are the plant's scales — grown from it, not mimicking it. A young scraper, which is the size of a hand and pale and soft and hides in the raw patches behind the herd, is born without them. It rasps and eats for a season, and the tissue it swallows does not all become scraper. Some of it comes up through the shell and roots there, and by the time the animal is the size of a plate it is sheathed in living plant, and the plant on its back pulses with the same eight-second beat as the plain, out of phase by a hair, so that a herd standing still looks like a stretch of the mat with a very slight shimmer running across it. The scrapers do not carry the plant. The plant rides them. It is how it gets to look at itself from above, if a thing without eyes can be said to look.

They have no eyes either. The front edge of the carapace is fringed with hairs that read vibration in the mat, and a scraper knows the plain the way you know a room in the dark — by the drone and its harmonics, by the direction the pulse is coming from, by the tiny flattening in the sound that means a pore is open nearby. They cluster because a herd is a bigger ear than an animal. When a tongue takes one, the ring they scatter into is not panic; it is a shape that lets every remaining animal hear where the pore was, and the herd's slow drift back is the ring closing on the answer.

They mate in the raw. A scraped patch, a day old, still beading with oil, is where they gather at dusk, and they lie on it flat with their plates spread and their undersides pressed to the wound, and the wound and the undersides exchange something, and in the morning the patch is darker and the animals are heavier and there are, in the seams of the raw place, dozens of soft pale nubs that will be scrapers if the tongues do not find them first. It is not clear whether a scraper is an animal that reproduces on the plant or a part of the plant that reproduces through the animal. The distinction has worn away, along with most others.

---

The tongues you will not see, unless you are unlucky, and then you will see one for less than a second. So it is worth taking a moment to say what one is when it is not killing.

It is long. The hooked end that comes out of the pore is perhaps a meter of a body that goes back four or five, and the rest of the animal lives in the plant's vascular channels, in the dark, in the oil, in the drone — which for a tongue is not a sound but the medium it lives in, the way water is for a fish. Its skin is the color of the oil and slick with it, and it breathes it, or does something with it that serves for breathing. It has no armor. It does not need any; nothing eats a tongue. It rides the pulse. When the pressure wave comes down the channel it lets go of the wall and is carried, a few meters every eight seconds, and it knows the channels of the whole plain as a map of currents, and it knows that certain pores open under certain sounds — the grinding of a herd, the stamp of a foot — and it waits below them with the hook resting against the underside of the mat, feeling.

They are patient the way the plant is patient, and they are the only animals here that are ever entirely silent, because they live inside the noise. The people of the town say a tongue can hear a heartbeat through a meter of mat. This may be true.

What the tongues do for the plant, apart from culling the herds and moving them on before a patch is scraped too deep, is this: they die in the channels, and their bodies, which are mostly oil and a little bone, dissolve into the flow and go wherever the flow goes, and the hooks — small, pale, curved — are pumped through the vascular system for years and come out at last through the spire-cords or the vents and lie on the surface of the mat here and there, like fossils that arrived by mail. The town's children collect them. A hook is a good thing to have. It means a tongue has stopped.

---

The gliders are the only inhabitants that do not touch the plant, or rarely. They are enormous and weigh almost nothing — a wingspan the width of a room on a body you could close in two hands — and the membrane of the wing is a single sheet of something pale and veined, through which you can see stars. They ride the warm columns the mat gives off, and above the spires, where the vapor rises, they hang for hours without a wingbeat, turning in the updraft, feeding.

What they feed on is the plant's dust. It comes up in the vapor, the fine oily powder that is the nearest thing the mat has to seed, and the gliders take it on the wing, sieving it from the air with the fringed edges of their mouths, and most of it passes through them and falls somewhere else on the plain, and where it falls the mat thickens. They are the plant's pollen, and its pollinators, and its only view of the horizon.

At night they roost in the spire-crowns, in the fluting, hooked by their small claws to the pale material that is not entirely present, and the violet light comes up through their wings and makes them visible for a moment as long soft shapes folding into the glow. They are born there. Nobody in the town has climbed a spire to see how. Nobody would.

The two-note call is a location call — where I am, where I am — and at dusk, when they all come in, the calls overlap from every crown on the horizon, and the plain between the spires fills with the sound of a hundred things saying only that they exist and where. It is the loneliest sound on the plateau, and the people of the town find it comforting, and after a night or two so will you.

---

Now the people. You have been waiting for them. They will not hurry for you.

They are tall and very thin and their chests are deep — the altitude has been at work on them for a long time — and their skin, where it shows, is dark and waxy and faintly luminous under the violet light, because they oil it. Every morning, a palmful of the warm resinous solvent from the wall tap, worked into the face and hands and throat, and the smell of pine and cold coins that is the plant's smell is also theirs. Skin left unoiled up here cracks like the mat does at dusk. They wear cord — woven from the fibrous vascular sheaths that can be pulled in long strips from an old scraped patch — in layers, wound, so that a person is a bundle with a face, and the face is mostly eyes. The eyes are large and dark-adapted and slow to move.

They speak in the high half-sung register you have heard, and it is worth understanding that this is not a style. It is where the drone leaves room. Below a certain pitch, a voice here does not exist; it is simply absorbed into the hum of the plain. So the language has migrated upward over the generations into a narrow bright band above the noise, and everything they say sounds, to an ear from below, like a question being asked very gently, or a lullaby. Their word for the drone is the same as their word for *quiet*, because it is what is there when nothing else is being said.

And they listen with their feet. This is the thing. A person here in soft-soled shoes on the mat is receiving, continuously, the pulse and the far shudder of stamped messages and the grinding of a herd two kilometers off and the particular flattening that means a pore has opened somewhere close, and they process this the way you process peripheral vision — without attending to it, until it matters. A child who has lost hearing in the ears is not much disadvantaged. A person who has to be carried, who cannot put their feet down, is described by the same word used for the dead.

Names are struck, not spoken. Each person has a rhythm, a short pattern, three to seven beats, given at birth by the mother stamping it on the floor of the room where the child was born, and this pattern is what you hammer on the mat with your staff when you want them, and it arrives underfoot wherever they are, and they come. A spoken name is a translation. When two people marry their patterns are struck together and a new one made from the overlap, and children are given patterns that echo the parents', so that a family, called across the town at dusk, sounds like a theme with variations. The oldest lineages have rhythms so long they are rarely struck in full. Everybody knows them anyway. You can hear, in the daily drumming of the town, who has died recently, because their pattern goes on being struck for a season by people who forget, and then is struck less, and then is not struck, and the mat forgets it too.

They eat scrapers. This is most of what they eat. A herd moving over the town is a herd moving over the larder, and the method is a pore — a real one, kept open by a rhythm that is the opposite of the perimeter-stamp, a pattern that means *come here* — and a person lying in the channel below it with a cord noose, doing badly and briefly what a tongue does well and always. They eat the plant too, the pale soft tissue of a fresh scrape, cooked in its own oil in a hollow grown into the wall, and it tastes of resin and something green and faintly of metal, and it is the taste of everything. They eat gliders never. They drink the distilled fraction of the solvent, warm, in small cups, all day. There is no thirst here as you know it, because there is no water to want, but there is a thing they call *the dry*, which is what happens to a person who goes too long without the oil inside and out, and it begins in the lips and ends in the mind.

---

Their rooms, you know about. Grown around a form. But live in one and you learn what that means.

The walls are warm. Not warm like a heated house — warm like a body you are lying against. They give. Put your back to the wall in the dark and you feel the pulse coming through, and after a night or two you cannot sleep without it; a person from this town, taken down off the plateau, lies awake for weeks listening for a heartbeat that is not there. Infants are laid against the wall, not in cradles. An infant kept away from the pulse for more than a day stops feeding. Nobody knows why. Everybody knows it.

The doors are muscles — a slit in the tissue that opens when you press the seam beside it in the right place, and holds, and closes when you let go — and a door learns its house. A stranger presses the seam and it opens slowly and grudgingly, and for a person who has lived behind it forty years it opens at a touch, and for a person who has been dead a month it will still open, for a while, for anyone who presses the way they did.

The rooms change. This is the thing you must understand about living inside something alive: the plant does not stop growing because you are in it. A room grown around a form is, in the first year, exactly the shape of the form. In the tenth year it is smaller by a hand's breadth on every side. In the fortieth year the ceiling is low and the tunnels to it have narrowed and there is a bulge on the north wall that was not there before. The town is always contracting toward its center, and the people are always growing new rooms at the edges and moving outward, and the old rooms in the middle are sealed by the plant, slowly, into the humps you saw from the spire — and that is what the humps are. They are the rooms of the people who did not move.

Because this is how the old die here. Not by illness, mostly — the cold and the dry take the weak early and the rest are hard. An old person simply stops going out. They stop walking to the spire; the tending is left to the young. They stop striking the perimeter. They stay in their room, which is small now and low and very warm, and the tap still gives oil and the light-filament still glows, and their family comes through the narrowing tunnel with cooked scraper, and then the tunnel is too narrow for a grown person, and then it is too narrow for a child, and they hand the food through, and then one day the door-muscle does not open and the seam beside it is smooth. Nobody presses it again. The pattern is struck for a season. The hump is there for ever.

The town is a graveyard that is also a town. Every hump under the flat straight ridges is someone. When they say the plant will grow you a room they mean it exactly, and they mean the whole of it.

---

They do not mourn this. It is worth saying plainly. The plant is warm and the room is warm and the pulse does not stop, and a person sealed in is not thought of as gone so much as *inward* — gone into the plain, into the thing that is everything, still on the map, still with a rhythm, only unstruck. The word for the sealed rooms is the same word as for the old healed seams that cross the plain, the ones from when the plant was many colonies and not yet one. Boundaries that have stopped meaning anything but are still there.

And here is the thing they think, that they do not say to strangers, that you will work out yourself on the third or fourth night in a warm room with your back to the pulse:

There is no trace of the builders. The plant grew over the trace. But the plant grows over *people*. It is doing it now, in the middle of the town, hump by hump. So the town's oldest ones — the ones who tend the spires' feet and know the long rhythms — hold, without insisting on it, that the builders did not leave. That they grew themselves rooms. That they were the first to lie against the warm wall and feel the pulse and decide not to go out. That somewhere under the mat, hundreds of kilometers deep in the folds where it is thickest, there are the oldest rooms of all, sealed a hundred thousand years, and in them whatever the builders were is warm and inward and unstruck, and the plain is their hump, and the spires are their tap and their light, and the pulse that goes through the whole country is the pulse they lay down to listen to and have not stopped listening to yet.

Nobody has dug to find out. Nobody would. But the people of the town, who think of themselves as one more tolerated animal, also think this, and hold both without difficulty, and so when a child asks who made the spires the answer given is the word for the sealed rooms, the word for the healed seams, and the child understands that it means: the ones who are in the walls.

---

So: what lives here.

The scrapers, which are the plant's hands and its way of keeping young. The tongues, which are its patience, riding its blood in the dark. The gliders, which are its eyes on the horizon and its seed on the wind. The people, who keep its taps clear and drum their names into its skin and become, when they are done, part of the flat.

And the plant. Which you set aside, and which is back, and which is different now, because you know what is in it.

It has no brain that anyone has found. It has a heartbeat and no heart. It is one thing across a continent and it has been one thing for so long that its edges have healed into ridges that no longer mean anything, and it is loud, continuously, at the volume of an engine, and it does not notice you. The people say it thinks. They say it thinks the way the town thinks — slowly, in rooms, by growing around a shape and then, over years, making the shape smaller. They say every hump is a thought it is having. They say it is not finished. They say the pulse is it counting.

And if you press your palm flat against the mat at night and wait through nine or ten of the slow beats, and think about what is inward under your hand — the channels and the tongues and the sealed rooms and the old rhythms and the oldest of all — it becomes difficult to say that anything here lives *on* the plant, or *in* it, or even *as* it. Only that a very great deal is alive and all of it is warm and all of it is pulsing in the same phase, and that the difference between a resident and a residence is not one the plant is interested in, and if you stay long enough, and are useful, it will stop being one you are interested in either.
