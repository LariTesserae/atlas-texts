**What Lives on the Table**

The first answer is the animals, and it is not wrong, but it does not survive being looked at for long.

They breathe. Their eyes are wet. Warmth comes off a flank if one stands close enough, a faint dry warmth like the warmth of a stone the sun has just left. By every test the valleys would apply, they are alive. But the valleys' tests assume that living is something done — a process, a spending, a thing with a rate — and up here the rate has gone to zero without the thing itself stopping. The doe with the lifted hoof is not resting between movements. She is not waiting. Waiting is a posture toward the future, and there is no future here for it to face. She is simply, entirely, a doe with a lifted hoof, in the way a number is simply a number. If she is alive, it is in the sense that a sentence is alive: complete, meaning what it means, saying it whether or not anyone reads.

So perhaps they are not the inhabitants. Perhaps they are the table. The mountain wore down until it ran out of mountain, and what it had left to be, it became — and it became them, because grey rounded shapes standing close are what a surface looks like when it has nothing more to lose. The animals are not on the table the way a herd is on a plain. They are on it the way grain is on a plank. To ask what lives among them is to ask what lives among the fibres of a board, and the answer might still be something, but it will not be the fibres.

---

The second answer is the lichen, and it is closer.

Everywhere else in the world, lichen is what lives on a thing. It is the residency that comes after the residents, the tenant of tombstones. It grows on what has stopped. And here it grows — grew — on the backs of the largest animals, and on the shoulders of the figure with the spear, and on the back of the figure's hand, and nowhere on the bare rock at all. That is worth noticing. The rock is older than the animals, or as old, or is the animals; it has had the same ages of salt and pale light. But the lichen chose hide. The lichen knew which surfaces were the finished ones and which were merely the floor.

And having chosen, it stopped. It reached the size it would be, its edges resolving to that painted definition, and it did not die — lichen that dies goes grey and lifts and flakes, and these are ochre and rust and firm as enamel — and it did not go on. It arrived. If one wanted a clock for the table, the lichen is the nearest thing: not a clock that stopped, but a clock that struck the hour it was built to strike and had no further hours in it. Every rosette says *this long*. This long is how long it takes to be finished here. The hairs of the animal grow up through it and out the other side, so that the animal and its lichen are one woven thing, and neither is on the other, and the question of which one lives there dissolves into the question of what *there* is, which is the table's question, which it does not answer.

---

The third answer is the one who set down the tools.

The knapping floor is arranged. That is the word for it. The flakes are a scatter, yes, but the core is placed on a flat stone, and the awl lies beside it, and the scraper beside that, in the order a hand would want them, and the finished points are set a little apart from the debris as finished things are. Someone was working and stopped, and stopping was not an interruption. It was the last stroke. The last thing made on this floor was the stillness of the tools, and it was made carefully.

The hearth-rings say the same. No ash. Not swept — never lit. A circle of stones is the smallest possible statement of *here, this, mine*, and someone made a great many of them across the table and then declined to sit inside. The cairn of horns faces the way the shadows fall, which is to say it faces the way one faces when one has stopped choosing a direction. The hide staked out in a wind that does not blow is not curing. It is a hide that has been given the shape of curing, permanently, by someone who knew there would be no wind and staked it anyway.

And the figure. Weight on one hip, spear grounded, salt on the shoulders, the lichen on the hand. One reads it first as the last of them — the one who stayed after the others left by whatever way leaves no track. But nothing here supports *last*. The tools were laid down once, in one order, on one floor; the hearths have one plan; there is one cairn. Whoever made the things on the table made them once, and then stood up, and walked among the animals to a distance that felt right, and stopped, and was correct about the distance, because no one has since been able to shorten it. The figure is not the last of a people. It is the whole of them. It is what a person is when the person has done everything it came up here to do.

And the stag is in a hundred places, and the lynx with the hooked scar is in a hundred places, and the figure is in one. Or — the figure is seen in one, from every place, always at the same remove. Whether that is one figure at one distance or the table arranging itself so that the figure is always exactly as far as a figure should be — the difference is the same difference as with the stag, and matters as little.

---

Here the answer begins to change shape, and it is worth letting it.

Consider the click.

A hoof settling. A beak. A wing-joint. One hears it and turns and nothing has moved, and understands that the sound belongs to the table rather than to the moment — a sound that is simply *there*, like a stone, and is heard when one is near enough to it. But a settling is not a static thing. A hoof that settles was, an instant before, not settled. The click is the sound of the very last increment of a motion. It is the sound of arriving.

And the doe's hoof is lifted. The wolf's mouth is slightly open. The hare sits upright, which is not a posture of rest but a posture of the instant before rest, the half-beat in which a hare decides it need not run. The bear's forepaws are set as a bear sets them when it has just stopped walking. Every animal on the table, looked at with this in mind, is not at rest. It is in the last frame before rest. The boar's weight has not quite finished transferring. The bird on the aurochs's back has folded its wings but the fold has not quite closed. The figure's spear has just been grounded — the shaft has met the rock and the hand has not yet loosened.

Nothing here is still. Everything here is *finishing*. And the click is what finishing sounds like, and it has already happened, and it is always about to.

That is what breathes. Not the animals — the finishing. Tens of thousands of last instants, each drawn out across a span in which one could count to a hundred and lose the count, each producing, at its extreme dilation, a single slow rise and a single slow fall of a flank. The hush that lies over the table like a second sky is the sound of an enormous number of things very nearly done.

---

Which leaves the pulse.

One's own, thin-air loud, beating fast against the hush, the only unfinished thing on the table. It felt rude on arrival. The word is exact. It is the rudeness of a guest who has not yet sat down at a table where everyone else is already seated — where everyone else, indeed, has been seated so long that the chairs have grown up through them.

And the eyes are already where one arrives. One turns, and the nearest animal is looking, and was looking before the turn. This was read as attention, and then as a kind of restfulness. Read it once more. The gaze is not following. It does not need to follow. It is on the place where one is going to be, because the place where one is going to be is a place, on the table, like any other; and the table has a place for everything that comes up onto it, and the animals have been looking at that place since before there was anyone to fill it.

The gap between the horse and the aurochs is the width of a hand, and one passes through it, and looks back, and the gap is undisturbed. Of course it is. It was not a gap that one fitted through. It was a gap shaped like one, waiting to be walked into, one of a very great number of such gaps, each the exact size of whoever would eventually find it — and one finds one's own, without knowing, simply by walking where the animals are dense and the way is closed, and passes through, and is on the other side, and is now, in some sense that cannot be checked, slightly nearer the horizon than before.

The figure cannot be approached because the figure is the place one is walking toward. Every step delivers one to the same distance from it because the distance is not between two things on a surface. It is the distance between a person and the end of what the person is doing, and it closes from the inside.

---

So: what lives on the table.

Not the animals, who are what the table is made of. Not the lichen, which is how long it takes. Not the one who laid down the tools, who is what it looks like when it is done.

What lives on the table is whatever is still finishing. It is the last motion of every hoof, stretched across a span that the valleys would call an age and the table does not call anything. It is the fold of a wing that is nearly closed. It is a hand around a spear-shaft, loosening. It is the count to a hundred, and the losing of the count, and the beginning again — and it is the fact that on the third or fourth or hundredth attempt one begins to notice that the interval between one's own breaths has gotten longer, that the pulse is not so loud as it was, that the cold is not so much felt as simply present, like the light.

That is the inhabitant. That is the only thing here that is doing anything: the arrival, extended to the point where it can be mistaken for rest, in every body on the table and in the one that has just come up onto it. Everything else has already become more itself than it can ever become again. The stag has become entirely stag. The lichen has become entirely *this long*. The figure has become the whole of a people at the exact right distance. And the thing that walked up the undisturbed scree with its rude pulse and its questions has begun — is beginning — will be found, by the next one, to have begun — to become entirely what it is, in a gap the width of a hand, with the eyes of everything already resting on the place where it stands, and the salt not yet on its shoulders, and the lichen not yet on its hand, and its foot, lifted, very slowly, coming down.

One hears the click. One turns. Nothing has moved.

It was one's own.
