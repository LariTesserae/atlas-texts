You want to say the animals live there. It is the natural sentence — the bats live in the dome, the crickets on the walls, the mice in the flywheel pit — and it is the sentence the place will not let you finish. *Live* is a verb with a direction in it. It means to take and spend, to be one thing at breakfast and another by night. Nothing on those walls is doing that. The bats are in the dome the way a colour is in a stone. If you had to choose a word for what they are doing there you would not say living; you would say *being kept*, and then you would have to ask by what.

So you look for the keeper.

There should be a man. Engines of that kind do not tend themselves; that was the whole labour of them, the oiling and the watching, the hand on the governor. And there is the evidence of a man everywhere, if you let yourself see it. The stair was cut by someone who knew how to swing a hammer without wasting a stroke. The rails were laid true. The brass plate was polished once, by somebody's sleeve, and has never needed polishing since. There is an oil-can on the frame beside the crosshead, long-spouted, tin, and it is standing exactly where a hand would set it down between rounds. There is a cotton waste rag folded on the cylinder lagging, and it is folded, not dropped. There is a mark on the anhydrite block beside the dynamo that could be where a man's boot-heel rested when he sat with his back to the warm iron and watched the flywheel go. Everything about the engine says: I was started by someone who meant to stay and see how it ran.

He is not there. You look for him in the shadow behind the flywheel and in the square mouth of the drift and he is not there. There are no bones among the beetles. There is no coat on a nail. Whoever set the governor and lit the gas and opened the throttle a quarter-turn until the note settled — he is the one absence in a chamber that otherwise has no gaps in it at all. The oil-can is waiting for him and the rag is waiting for him and the boot-mark is his, and there is a way in which the whole arrangement is a chair pulled out for a man who has stepped away, and the chair has decided, in his absence, that it prefers the room this way.

You think, for a moment, that he might be the sweetness in the air. That hive-smell under the iron and the oil. You think it and put it down again. It is not that kind of place. It has no appetite, and a place that eats a man has an appetite. More likely he walked back up the stair with the note under his boots and the note stopped at the third turning and he went on into whatever weather there was, and the place did not notice him go, because he had already stopped being needed. The engine had found its speed. The bead of oil had formed under the crosshead and had begun, very slowly, to decide. He was a gradient and the room was closing over gradients and it closed over the place where he had been the way still water closes over a stone. He is not what lives there. He is what left so that something could.

You try the rock, then. The seam is the one thing in the chamber that was there before anything else and it is, in its own way, alive: gas-bearing, breathing out through that pipe that comes from no fitting, feeding the engine from its own body. Rock lives slowly. Gypsum grows its crystals over ages, anhydrite drinks water and swells into gypsum and gives it back. That is a metabolism, of a kind, on a scale where a century is a heartbeat. But here even that has stopped. The fracture surfaces are chisel-fresh. The fibrous faces of the anhydrite have not dulled by a grain. Whatever is keeping the flywheel at its speed is keeping the rock at its moment too, and the rock is not the keeper. It is being kept as much as the bats are. It is the body, and you are looking for the thing that has the body.

Which leaves the sound.

You have been standing in it long enough that you have stopped hearing it as a sound and started hearing it as a shape, and the shape is the chamber. It fills the void to the walls the way a fitted thing fills a mould. And you understand — not in words, there is no room for words in it — that this is the wrong way round. The chord did not fill the chamber. The chamber is the size the chord is. The dome is twelve metres up because that is where the bats' whistle wants to break against something. The fallen blocks lie where they lie because the crickets' stridulation is loudest in the spaces between. The note has parts, you noticed that; what you did not notice is that the parts have a body, and the body is the whole room, and the room is not where the thing lives. The room is the thing.

Look at it as you would look at an animal.

It has a heart, and the heart is iron and turns in a pit and has not gained or lost a beat. It has a lung, and the lung is the seam, breathing gas out of itself and taking nothing back, an inhale that never comes because the exhale never finishes. It has a skin, and the skin is bats and moths and crickets and beetles, stratified by warmth, each layer laid where the temperature wants it, so that the whole hide is a map of the heat gradient it has abolished. It has a nervous system, one wire, nine orange nodes, carrying a signal that does not vary. It has a mouth, the square black drift with the rails running four metres in, and through that mouth you came, and it has not swallowed and it has not spoken. It has four pale stalks and a bone-white fern where a creature might have hair. It has a snake laid along its warmest part like a tongue laid along the roof of a mouth.

It has a voice. That is the thing you have been standing inside. Every living thing in the chamber gives to the voice and takes from it, the engine giving the fundamental, the animals holding the harmonics, and the voice going back into the animals and into the engine so that the crickets rub because the flywheel turns and the flywheel turns because the crickets rub — no. You keep trying to find where the pitch is set and there is nowhere. It set itself, once, the way a struck bell settles into its note, and this is the note, and the note is the animal, and the animal has one thought, and the thought is the note.

What does it want? You test the question against the room and the room returns nothing. Wanting is a gradient. It has no wanting in it. It has arrived. Every other living thing you have ever seen was somewhere on the road between hunger and the next hunger; this one stepped off the road and found that off the road there was a place to stand, exactly one, and has been standing there since. It does not want the mice because it is not hungry. The snake is not hungry. The snake will never be hungry again, and it does not know this as a deprivation, because it does not know anything, because knowing is a change of state and there are no changes of state on that cylinder head.

You feel — you cannot help it — that it is content. You have to correct yourself. Content is what a body is between one hunger and the next. This is not between anything. If you must have a word, it is *finished*, in the way a piece of work is finished: not ended but complete, with nothing left that would be improved by another stroke. The men who cut the stair knew that feeling at the end of a shift. They put the hammer down. This place put the hammer down and has never picked it up and does not miss it.

And yet it is not dead. You have to keep saying this to yourself because everything you know insists that a thing which does not eat or drink or breed or age is dead. It is not dead. The flanks move. The oil sits at its level. The bulbs glow, and dead filaments do not glow. It has simply found the one point at which living and not-changing are the same act, and it is performing that act, continuously, with its whole body, and the sound of the performance is the chord. If you had to say what it does all day you would say: it holds. That is its entire behaviour. It holds the note and the note holds it.

You understand now what it made of you. Not an intruder — intruders are resisted, and nothing resisted you. Not prey. Not a guest. You were a wrongness in the sound. You came down the stair carrying your own small chord — pulse, breath, the tick of sweat drying, the widening of your pupils — and none of it was in tune, and the room could not take it in because the room was already full. There was no note left for you to hold. That was the pressure you felt: not a will pushing you out but a completeness that had no vacancy, the way a key that is already turned has no further to go. The bats did not turn their heads because there was nothing in their turning that the place required. You were a drop trying to enter a glass that was brimmed to the exact meniscus. The glass did nothing. The glass did not have to.

And there is one more thing to say about it, and it is the thing you carry up the stair, because it is the only part of the creature that has a future in it and so the only part you can properly understand.

The bead of oil under the crosshead. Perfectly formed, perfectly still. Every other stillness in the chamber is a stillness of things that have nowhere to go: the rock, the rails, the folded rag. The bead has somewhere to go. It is a hanging weight; it is a decision very nearly taken. Somewhere in it, at a scale you could not see with any instrument, the film that holds it is thinning, or would be thinning, if thinning were permitted. It is the last gradient the place did not abolish, and it did not abolish it because it could not — because a drip is what a bearing does, and to stop the drip would be to stop the bearing, and to stop the bearing would be to stop the heart. So it did the only other thing. It held the drop at the lip. It has been holding it since the man stepped away up the stair.

That is what lives there. Not the bats or the engine or the note, exactly, but the act of holding that the note is the sound of — the whole enormous animal, hide and heart and lung and voice, bent with all its perfect stillness on the single task of not letting one bead of oil fall. Every harmonic on the walls is that refusal. Every revolution of the flywheel is that refusal, executed and executed and never concluded. It is the most patient thing you have ever stood inside and the most afraid, if a thing without gradients can be afraid, and perhaps that is the truest thing you can say of it: that it is a creature made entirely of the moment before, and that its whole life is the length of a drop's fall, and it has decided to live forever in that length, and so far it has.

You do not hear the drop fall as you climb. You would know if you had. The note goes with you to the third turning and then it is not under your feet any more, and behind you, in the dark, the thing that lives there is exactly as itself as it has ever been, with its whole weight on the lip of that one bead, holding.
