Start with what is actually there, because the basin rewards that.

What lives here are the mats. Everything else is passing through. The mats have not passed through anything in a very long time; they were laid down in the pools before the sinter floor existed, and in a real sense before the world existed as anything you would recognise, since their kind was making a living out of sulphur and light in water like this when there was not yet enough oxygen in the air to rust a nail. They are not one organism. Each band is a different tenant, sorted by temperature with a precision no landlord could enforce. The orange ones live where the water comes out of the ground at a heat that would take the skin off your hand, and they cannot live anywhere else. A few inches out, where the water has lost a few degrees, they stop, exactly, and the green ones begin, and the green ones cannot cross back. The brown at the outer edge is where the water is merely hot, and it is the most crowded and the most various and the least remarkable, which is the usual arrangement. Between the bands there are no streets. Nobody visits. Each population lives its whole existence in a ring a few fingers wide, fed by water that arrives already carrying everything it needs, and nothing in the ring competes with it, and nothing preys on it except the flies, who take so little from the top that it comes to nothing. This is what the word content means when you stop using it loosely.

They do not know the basin roars. They have no organ for it. The knocking passes up through the water and through them and they are unchanged by it, and this is the first thing to understand about living here: the residents are the ones the sound does not reach.

The flies are next, and the flies are tenants of a different kind, the kind with a short lease. They stand on the mats in their thousands and their larvae are in the shallow warm water at the pool margins, and a fly here lives a few weeks, so that against the pool that slaps its surface every three seconds and has done so since before people, a fly is not an inhabitant but a very rapid flicker in the inhabitant's field of view. They are the only thing here in a hurry. You can see it in them, if you crouch — that stiff quick wading, the way they lift when the bird's shadow comes and settle again a foot along, thousands of them making the same small decision. The bird itself does not live here. It nests up in the rim hills where there is grit to make a scrape in, and it comes down to the basin the way a person goes to work, and it goes home in the evening, and if you took it away the flies would notice for a season and the mats would not notice at all. The hawk is not a tenant on any reckoning. It is the only thing that looks at the basin from above, and the basin, which grows over everything and is never seen, may be said to be seen once a day, briefly, by something that does not want it.

That is the census, honestly taken. But it is not what you feel lives here, and the feeling is not nothing, because the feeling comes from somewhere.

The feeling is that a works has an operator. Nothing runs this steadily on its own — that is what the body believes, standing in the roar with the pool keeping its three seconds and the whistle holding its one note. Somewhere there is a shift on. Someone walks the intervals. Someone reads the pitch of the whistle by ear and knows from it what the pressure is doing in the fissure, and hears the knocking under the floor and does not go looking for it because they have always known what it is and it is fine. You can furnish this person quite fully if you let yourself: unhurried, uninterested in you, wearing something the steam has coloured grey. The basin argues for them the way a footprint argues for a foot.

And then you remember about the objects. A blade left a week begins to be the ground. Nothing stays built. Whoever kept this place would have been kept by it long ago — collared, cased, given a smooth ridged casing of their own — so either there has never been an operator or the operator is what the crust has done. Which is the same thing said twice. The maintenance is the growth. The basin keeps itself the only way it knows how to keep anything, which is by covering it, grain by grain, until it is part of the floor and rings faintly when you walk across it. If you want to see the attendant you are looking at them. They are white and level and steaming in two dozen places.

There is a story, the kind you get from anyone who has spent time on the rim and not much time on the floor, that somebody once did stay. Came down for the reason people come down, and sat with their back to a collar at the northern pool where the flat grooved surface is, and had their breathing taken, and did not go back up. The story locates them precisely: a sinter form on the north side that is broader at the base than the other collars and has, if you want it to, a shoulder. There is no way to check and the basin will not help you. It is exactly as likely to have grown a shape like a seated person by chance as it is to have grown one around a seated person, because it does not distinguish. That is the thing the story is actually about. Not that the crust took someone, but that it would not have been able to tell.

So let the operator go, and let the one who stayed go, and see what is left when you subtract the people you brought down with you.

What is left is something that lives without any gradient. Everything else alive that you have ever met is falling toward something — food, warmth, a mate, the far side of the field — and its life is the shape of the fall. This does not fall. It has arrived. The heat comes up under it at a fixed rate and it turns the heat into sinter and sulphur and sound and puts them down and picks them up again and puts them down. It does not eat so much as it is fed, continuously, from below, by a source that will outlast any interest in the question. It has no eyes, because nothing here needs to be looked at; nothing here changes fast enough to be watched. It has no ears, because it is the sound. When you think you are hearing a roar and a slap and a whistle and a knock you are hearing one organism's several respirations, at the several rates its several openings allow, and the reason the knocking has no source is that a heart has no source either. There is nothing in the chest that knocks. There is only the chest.

The mats are the parts of it that metabolise. The steam is the part of it that remembers to come down. The crust is the part that keeps. The intervals are the part that, if it had a mind, would be the mind — the whole of its attention given over, without strain, to doing the next thing at the same moment as the last thing. It has been doing this for ten thousand years and there is no reason internal to it why it should stop, and no reason external to it that has yet come over the rim and got purchase.

It does not want conversation. This is not unfriendliness. It is that conversation is a gradient — you speak because something is not yet said — and it has nothing unsaid. After an hour you stop talking not because it has silenced you but because you have begun, a little, to be like it, and it does not notice this either. It makes room for you the way it makes room for the bird and the blade and the rain: by continuing exactly as before, and by beginning, at the rate at which it does everything, to include you in the floor.
