The first one you see will be a long way off and will not look like a person.

It will look like a stem that has come loose and is walking. This is partly the light — everything in the hole is the same white, and a figure on a far gallery is a pale upright against pale stone — and partly the eye-strip, which flattens the face to a band, and partly the way they hold themselves, which is very straight and very still through the trunk while the legs do the work, the way a reed stands in a current. You will not see the face for a long time. When you do, it will be looking at you already, and it will have been for a while.

---

They are the color of the place. Not tanned — there is no brown here, the glare comes from every side at once and strikes under the jaw and behind the knee and in the cup of the ear, and what it does to skin over years is not darken it but wear it, so that the people of the hole are pale in the way the limestone is pale, a white that has been handled. The skin is fine and dry and very slightly shiny at the shoulders and the tops of the feet, where the grit has polished it. In the creases — the knuckles, the corners of the eyes, the folds of the neck — the lime dust sits permanently, a faint frost, and when they smile, which they do, the lines of the face show white in white.

Their eyes are small. This is not a figure of speech. Whether they are born so or become so, the eyes of the people here sit deep and narrowed, the lids thickened, and the irises, on the rare occasions you see one uncovered and near, are almost without color — a wet grey, like the resin before it sets. The eye-strips are woven from the fibers of the stem's branching tips, which can be split and softened by chewing, and each strip is the work of one person and worn by that person until it rots, and the slit in it is cut for that face alone. You will not be lent one. There would be no point.

Their hands are the loudest thing about them. Long, hard, the nails worn flat and thick from working stone and hooking resin. The fingertips are scarred in a pattern that comes from the knives and from the stems themselves, which are sharp at a fresh cut, and the pads have gone smooth and slightly glassy, as though the resin had got into them and stayed. They hold the hands a particular way at rest, in front of the breastbone, fingers slightly spread. It is not prayer. It is the position from which the language starts.

---

The language is small because it has to be. In a bowl of glare a large gesture is a smear; the eye can only follow what happens close to the body, against the body, where the body is the dark thing. So it is a language of the fingers against the chest and the throat and the face, of one hand read against the other. It is fast. Two people passing on a stair will exchange something in the time it takes to pass and not slow down for it. It has no loud version and no whisper. It cannot be overheard, only overseen, and in a city where every doorway is open and every gallery looks onto every other, this seems to be the whole of what privacy means: to turn your back while you say it.

What it says, mostly, is where. Where the sound is thick this morning. Where the wall has warmed early. Where a stem has split and is dripping. Where the beetles were at dusk. The language is rich in place — there are signs for every gallery and every stair and for the small round floor at the bottom, which has a sign of its own, the flat of one hand pressed slowly down against the flat of the other — and it is rich in tone, with a scale of finger-positions against the throat that mean things you will not be able to hear the difference between. It has, so far as anyone has been able to learn, no word for silence. It has a word for the plain, which is the same as the word for a stopped ear.

It has no names. Or it has, but they are not what you would call names. Each person is a small motion — a touch to the collarbone, a twist of two fingers at the temple — and the motion is not given, it is taken, some time in the first year, and it is the motion that person makes without thinking when the noon comes down on them. It is how they flinch. That is who they are.

---

You will be told they cannot hear. This is not quite right.

What happens to an ear that spends every noon of every year inside a chord that makes the teeth ache is that it stops being the way sound arrives. The ear is still there — they have ears, ordinary ones, the lobes worn smooth like everything else — but by the time a person has been in the hole a few seasons the small bones inside have given up their fine distinctions and hand the whole business over to the rest of the skeleton. The people of the hole hear with the sternum, with the long bones of the leg, with the flat of the palm laid against stone. They hear the way you felt the plain, before you saw the edge: through the soles, as pressure, as a leaning-on. And because they hear this way they hear things you cannot. They hear which wall is warm. They hear a stem that has cracked from one that has not. They hear the beetle.

This is how they know which stems to leave uncut. A stem with a beetle in it rings a fraction low — the hollow above the resin is occupied, the column of air is shorter, the tone drops by an amount no ear could name — and a hand laid flat against the stem at dusk, when the chord is thinning and the individual voices come apart, can feel it. They go along the rows in the failing light touching each stem once, briefly, the way you would touch the shoulders of people in a crowd to see which ones are asleep.

---

The noon is when you understand them.

An outsider at noon on the floor of the hole cannot stand. The sound folds the knees, as has been said; it also empties the stomach and stops the breath and does something to the sense of where the ground is that leaves you crawling toward a wall you cannot find. The people walk there. Slowly, unsteadily, with that gait like wading — but they walk, and they walk with purpose, in from the lowest gallery across the round pale floor to its center and stand there, in the dense pale forest of the tallest stems, for as long as it takes the shadow of the eastern lip to move the width of a hand across the far wall. Then they walk out again.

They do not all go every day. They do not go together — you will never see two on the floor at once, and it may be that this is forbidden, or it may be that there is no room in the sound for two. But every one of them goes, and often, and if you ask with your hands why, you will get the sign for the floor, the slow press of palm on palm, and then the sign that means — as nearly as it can be rendered — grown. Not the growing of the stems. Grown as a wall is grown smooth. Grown as the resin grows thick at night.

They come out of it changed for a while. The eyes, when the strip is lifted, are wet and unfocused. The hands shake. They cannot sign for some time and do not try; they sit on the lowest step of the spiral with their backs to the wall and their palms flat on the stone and let the chord go on speaking through the bone until the sun has moved and it lets them go. And afterwards they are very calm, and very slow, and they smile more, and the frost in the lines of the face shows white.

---

At dusk they cut.

You will have been told this too, and it is true, but it does not tell you how it is done, which is tenderly. The knives are small. The hooks are copper, hammered thin, and the people warm them in the hand before they use them. A stem is opened low, a single slanting stroke just above the cup of lime it stands in, and the cut is held open with two fingers while the hook goes in and finds the resin — which at this hour is the thickness of honey and coming thicker by the minute — and draws it out in a single long thread that the other hand catches and turns onto a spindle before it can touch the stone. It is fast, and it is done without looking, the face turned aside toward the next stem, the hands reading the work. The stem, emptied, slumps against its neighbors. In the morning it will have filled and stood again, and the cut will have sealed to a pale glassy scar, and the stems of the oldest groves are ringed with these scars from the lime to the height of a man's waist, hundreds of them, close as the growth-rings of a tree, and no one has counted them.

The people do not eat, or they eat so little and so rarely that you will not see it. There is nothing here to eat. The resin is what there is. A fingernail's worth at dusk, worked slowly between the back teeth until it goes from glass to gum to nothing, and the mouth stops wanting; a fingernail's worth at dawn. Over years this does what you would expect: the people of the hole are thin in a way that looks finished rather than starved, the joints large, the bellies flat as a hand, and their teeth are worn to smooth low domes, and the breath, when you are near enough, has that faint hot-pepper smell of the cup of lime where a stem was pulled.

They sleep in the doorways on whichever side the wall is coolest, which changes through the year. A pane of amber propped across the opening cuts the light to a brown dusk and cuts the sound to a pressure. They sleep on the bare ledge, on the side, knees drawn up, one hand under the ear and the other flat against the wall behind them, and if you watch one sleep you will see the fingers of that hand move now and again against the stone — a touch, a slide, the beginning of a sign — and you will understand that they are hearing something, and answering.

---

There are two dozen of them, or fewer, and none of them is young.

Not old, most of them. But there are no children here, and there is no one who looks as though they could have been a child here — no one with the soft unworn skin of someone who grew up in the glare and simply survived it. Every face in the hole is a face that arrived. You can see it in the way they hold the eye-strips, a shade too carefully, as something learned. You can see it in the calluses, which are thick but which were laid down over other calluses, older ones, from other work. One of them has a scar on the forearm that is a burn from a kitchen. One of them walks with a hitch that is not the noon-gait but something a horse did. Where they came from, they do not sign. There may not be a sign for it. But the plain is wide and flat and warm and the sound goes out through it for a day's walk in every direction, under the feet, before there is any sound to hear, and it is not hard to imagine someone crossing that plain for their own reasons and beginning to feel it — that pressure, that leaning — and turning toward it. Not deciding to. Turning, the way the stems turn.

If that is how it happens, then it happens rarely, and the number stays small because the plain is large and empty and few cross it, and the ones who do mostly do not turn. And it would mean the swept rooms are not loss and not design but readiness. Every doorway open. Every ledge bare. Room for whoever comes over the lip.

Or it may be as they say, when they say anything: that the city is for the stems, and a few is enough.

---

The beetles will not let you see them. Not at noon, when they are inside the stems, in the dry hollow above the resin with the chord going through them so hard that a thing the size of a thumbnail should be shaken to dust — and is not, and sits there, and whatever it does in there is its own affair. Not at night, when they are inside again. Only at dusk, for that one stretch of time while the shadow climbs, when they come up out of the stems' cut mouths and the stems' fine branching tips and sit in the last high light, hundreds of them, thousands perhaps, small copper dots on the pale spray of the grove-tops, and are still.

They are the color of the copper hooks. They ring when tapped — a thin high note like a struck cup — and the people do not tap them. The people, in fact, do very little about the beetles at all except leave their stems alone and, at dusk, when the cutting is done, stand for a while at the edge of the grove with their hands in the resting position and look up at them. Whether this is attention or courtesy or something else you will not be able to tell.

But watch the stems. Watch a stem the beetles have used for a season. It is not cut, so it grows — a hand's width a day, day on day, until it stands taller than any of the cut stems around it, taller than two men, three, its top lost in the glare — and then, one dusk, it splits. Down the whole length, a single clean seam, with a sound that even the people flinch at, and out of the split falls a fine dry powder the color of the stem, and the beetles come down out of it in a slow copper stream and walk across the floor and up the walls and into the mouths of the cut stems, and are gone. And in the morning, everywhere the powder fell — in the joints of the pavement, in the cracks of the stairs, on the lintels of the doorways — there are the small pale pricks of new stems coming up.

So that is what the beetles are for, or what the stems are for, or what the people are for; it is not clear which is the tenant and which the house. The stems make the sound. The sound grows the stems. The people cut the stems and the cutting keeps them short and the shortness keeps them singing at the pitch the city was tuned for, and in exchange the people are fed, if that is the word, and are grown, if that is the word, on the round floor at noon. The beetles are borne in the stems and bear the stems, and ring, and are not cut. Everything here is in the hollow of something else. The city is in the plain. The people are in the city. The sound is in the people, in the bone, all the time, and when they leave — one of them, once in a long while, will climb the long unroofed stair and stand on the lip and look out over the clay-colored flat and then go back down — when they leave, even for an hour, they walk as though they had been emptied.

---

You will want to know if they are happy, and you will not be able to ask, because the language has no word for it either, or has too many.

But there is a thing you will see if you stay. In the very last light, when the cutting is done and the spindles are heavy and the beetles are sitting on the grove-tops and the chord has come apart into forty thousand separate voices each going quiet at its own rate, the people come to the galleries. Not together; each to a different level, a different quarter, wherever they were when the light left. And they stand at the edge with their hands on the parapet, flat, and the parapets are worn there to a shallow trough by exactly this, and they listen to the stone give up the day. It takes a long time. The west wall mutters. The floor ticks. Somewhere a single stem holds a single tone a little longer than the others, and every hand on every parapet in the hole feels it, and the fingers move — the same small motion, the flinch that is their name — and then it stops, and there is only the blood.

And they do not go in. They stay, in the warm dark, hands on the stone, faces turned up toward the mouth of the hole where the stars are hard and few, until the cold begins an hour before dawn. Then they lie down on their ledges. And when the first light touches the eastern lip and the first stem up there begins to breathe, they are already awake, every one of them, and already listening, each with one hand flat against the wall.
