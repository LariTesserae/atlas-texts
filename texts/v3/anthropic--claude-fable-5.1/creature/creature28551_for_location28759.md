The easy answer has already been given, and it is wrong in a way worth walking through.

Choughs live there. That is true in the plainest sense — they came out of the seepage cracks of the western horn as wet, indignant chicks; they will die within sight of it; their whitewash on the ledges is older than the fire-ring and will outlast the post. They are the citizens. They mate for life and hold the same crack for decades, and a pair returning to a crack will find in it the packed grass and hair of birds that held it before, and build on top. They know every eddy in the gap the way a ferryman knows a river, and they use the updraft off the southern rim as a kind of standing chair, a place to hang and shout. But watch one long enough and you notice how little of its life it spends on the rock. It roosts there. It nests there. Everything else — feeding, fighting, courting, the endless quarrel that is chough society — happens in the air, a meter or two above the rim, in the same wind that is on its way to the other side of the mountain. The residents of the Notch live mostly in what is passing through it.

Under them, in the slate, the rodents. Their life is the Notch's basement: a warren threaded through the ribs of tilted rock, dark and comparatively dry, lined with shredded tussock and smelling of themselves. They come up to graze the margins of the cushion plants and nothing else — the cushions are too tough, the rosettes too woolly — and so they have trimmed the low ground into a lawn no taller than a thumb, and this is why the mattress of moss in the center is so smooth. They are stout and short-eared for the cold and they never travel more than a few body-lengths from a hole. Their whistle is a shared alarm and it is almost always false; the shadow that crosses them is usually a chough, sometimes a cloud. They do not care. They are the most rooted animals here, and their whole existence is organized around vanishing.

The swifts do not live here and never have. They hatched under an eave or in a cliff a hundred kilometers east and will die somewhere west, and the Notch is the width of a few wingbeats in a life spent almost entirely aloft. They are the wind with a voice added. To ask whether they live here is to ask whether the wind does.

And the hunter — the shape in the cloud that empties the ledges. It lives *in* the cloud, which is not quite the same as living in the Notch. Its eyrie is somewhere on the outer face of one of the horns, on a ledge that can be reached only from the void, and it comes into the gap only to kill and leaves by the way it came. It is the one thing that goes both ways through the door. Everything else is one-directional or stationary; the hunter alone treats the Notch as a room it can enter and exit at will. But it does not stay. A scatter of feathers on the moss, and it is gone. You would not say a wolf lives in the fold.

So: choughs on the walls, rodents under the floor, swifts and the hunter in the air. All of them, in their different ways, half here.

---

Which leaves the frog.

There is only one, or only one at a time — the two are not distinguishable from outside, and this turns out to matter. It is small, the length of the last joint of a thumb, and it is the color of wet slate with a faint copper wash, so that on the rock it is the rock and on the moss it is a shadow on the moss. It has no need of a pond. Its skin is thin enough that the cloud is drink and bath together, and it lives in the sphagnum at the saddle's lowest point, in the sponge where the rill gathers, and moves perhaps a few meters in a year. It eats what the cloud brings it — the small drenched flies that stall in the lee of the cushions — and it calls when the light is low and the cloud is thick, one dry click at a time, at intervals long enough that between one click and the next you can forget you were listening for it.

It is not a loud presence. It is not, in any sense the choughs would recognize, a presence at all. But it is the first thing in the Notch that does not lean west. The wind does not reach it down in the sponge. The cloud does not bother it; the cloud is its element. It does not go anywhere. Whether the frog clicking tonight is the frog that clicked twenty years ago, or its descendant, or a stranger that crawled up out of the cloud along the seepage grooves and found the hollow empty and stayed — the place cannot tell and does not ask. One at a time is how a place keeps something without owning it. It is a form of permanence that looks, from a distance, exactly like a single very old life.

Hold that thought — *one at a time* — and go and look at the shelter.

---

The shelter is built against the eastern horn. Notice that: not the western horn, where the overhangs are deeper and the choughs' whitewash shows there is more shelter from the drip. The eastern horn, into the wind, where the fluting is — where the rock speaks.

And it is a space a person can lie in but not sit up in. Whoever built it did not want to look out; there is nothing to look at, most hours of most days, but the inside of a cloud. They did not build it for a lookout or a hearth — the fire-ring is thirty paces off, near the other horn, and old enough to have been sealed shut by the cushions. They built a place to lie down, with the head end pressed to the base of the singing rock.

Lie in it, and you understand. When the wind is strong enough, the tone in the slot is not something you hear so much as something you are inside. It comes up through the slate under your shoulder blades. It fills the low space between the wall and the roof-flake like water fills a jar. It is below your voice and above your heartbeat and it does not waver, and it goes on for as long as the whole weight of the eastern sky is trying to reach the west. The shelter is not a shelter. It is a listening-place, and the thing it is built to hear is the one voice in the Notch that is not on its way somewhere else.

Now the steps make a different kind of sense. Eight notches cut into a slate rib, going down toward the southern rim and stopping at a foot-wide ledge with cloud beneath it. Everyone who reads them as a descent — steps leading *down* to something — reads them as a mystery, because nothing is down there. Read them the other way. They are how something came *up*. Whoever cut them came from below, from the ledge, from whatever the ledge connects to along the outer face of the mountain in the white. They did not walk the ridge. They climbed out of the cloud into the room, the way the cloud itself does every morning, and the steps are simply the last eight moves of a route whose earlier moves are hidden.

And the post: the tallest thing that grows here, stripped and set upright at the highest point of the floor, dressed in the feathers of the one animal that lives here loudly. A marker. Not for anyone coming along the ridge, because no one does. For someone coming up out of the void, into a room where the far wall is invisible — a dark vertical to steer by, the first solid thing after the steps.

---

So the question of what lives in the Notch sharpens into a different one: what kind of creature comes up out of the cloud, when the wind is high enough to make the horn sing, and lies in a stone box with its ear to the rock, and goes back down.

The word that arrives first is *hermit*, and it is wrong. It brings a robe and a beard and a kind of dry solitude, and there is nothing dry here, and nothing solitary either — the place is a rookery, a thoroughfare, a shouting-match. Set the word aside. The thing that lies in the shelter is not fleeing anyone. It is not praying, or not in any sense the word usually means. It is not here for the view.

Let it be smaller than the word suggests. Let it be something that has more in common with the frog than with the choughs — something that does not lean, that does not mind the wet, that moves a few meters a year, that can hold still for a long time and then, at an interval you cannot predict from outside, do a single thing. Let it come up the eight steps on hands and feet, because that is how the steps are cut, close and shallow, for something that keeps low. Let it be grey with the grey of a thing that has been in cloud so long the cloud has stopped noticing it. Let it have gone past cold into a kind of ambient cool, breath showing faintly, skin slick, with the patience of moss.

It does not tend the place. That is important. The fire-ring was someone else's — someone earlier, or someone who came for a different reason and lit a fire against the dark and left — and the resident has let the cushions swallow it. It does not sweep the shelter or clear the whitewash or trim the heathers; the wind does that. It has made almost nothing, and what it has made is the minimum: a place to lie, a way up, a thing to steer by.

But it keeps the bundle dry. Or rather — this is the part that comes clear last — the bundle is dry because it is the most-touched thing in the Notch. Everything else here is rained on, dripped on, wept on by the rock. The bundle of tussock tied with hide has had a body lying on it, over and over, for long enough that warmth has gone into it and stayed, and the twist of hide has been handled smooth. It is the only dry thing because it is the only thing that is regularly held. That is what tending amounts to, here: not maintenance but return.

---

Whether there is one now is not a question the place will answer, and it is the same non-answer the frog gives. The feathers in the post are some old and some glossy; the glossy ones may be the choughs' doing, or a hand's. The bundle is dry; overhangs do keep things dry, sometimes, for a while. The steps stop at a ledge and the ledge goes into white. Possibly the last one came up and lay down and listened and went back into the cloud a generation ago, and the shelter is only a shelter. Possibly one is down there now, on some shelf of the outer face that has never been seen from above, waiting for the wind.

What can be said is this. Everything in the Notch leans west, away from the voice in the eastern horn — the heathers, the post, the tussocks, the streaks of whitewash, the swifts, the whole atmosphere of one side of the mountain pouring toward the other. The only thing that has ever turned *toward* the voice, and pressed against it, and stayed as long as it sang, is whatever lies in the stone box with its ear to the rock. One at a time. For as long as the wind holds. And then down the steps into the white, and then — after an interval long enough to forget you were waiting — up again.

That is what lives in the Notch. Not the loud things on the walls or the quick things in the air or the buried things under the floor, though all of them are there and all of them are true. What *lives* there, in the sense of having chosen it, is the one creature that treats the door not as a way through but as a place to stop and listen — the thing that has heard the note enough times to know it as a voice, and answers it the only way a voice like that can be answered: by lying still inside it, and coming back.

When the wind drops in the evening and the horn goes silent and the silence is briefly appalling, that is what the silence is. Not the absence of sound. The absence, for a while, of the only listener the sound has ever had.
