Start with the ones you can see, because they are the ones you will be told about.

The salamanders are the town's most numerous citizens and its least ambitious. Black all over, without the yellow you would expect on a salamander lower down, as if the colour were something the altitude had taken along with the frogs' voices. Each one holds its step the way a person holds a seat they arrived early for. If you lift one — they permit this — it is cold, and heavier than it looks, and when you set it down on the next step up it walks back down to its own, and the one whose step you put it on does not move while it does so. They eat what the cloud brings them: small things that land in the water-film and cannot get purchase. They are never seen eating. You come to believe they eat when you have gone into a house, in the same way you believe the stairs are climbed when you are not on them.

The snails are older than they look, and they look old. Their shells have the weather on them that the stone hasn't got yet, and there is an idea, among those who have stayed more than a night, that the snails came up the slope after the fall from wherever they had been before, and that what they are doing on the walls — the slow ascending columns, the glinting tracks — is the same thing the moss is doing: taking the new stone and making it old. Nobody has watched a snail eat lichen here. But the lichen is on the walls, and the snails are on the walls, and the sequence is suggested.

The moths on the lintels are a single species and they do not fly. Or they fly, but not while you watch. They have the palest possible grey with a green in it, the exact tone of the light, so that a lintel fully dressed with them appears to be a lintel made of the weather. They rest with their wings open in intervals so even that people have measured them and found the measurements to vary from house to house but not within a house. Whatever sets the interval is set per door.

The herons are two, and they are always two, and no one has established whether they are the same two. One on the platform in the square, one in the cistern. The one in the cistern fishes, and the cistern has fish in it, small and dark, which is one of the things nobody has explained, since the cistern is fed by nothing but cloud. The one on the platform does not fish. It stands the way a heron stands when it is about to move and does not move, and it stands that way for the whole of a day, and the square is arranged around it the way a square is arranged around whatever stands on the platform. When it does leave, at dusk, in a manner nobody has ever seen begin, the square feels wrong until it or its successor is back.

The hares are the ones that unsettle visitors, more than the stag. They sit up in the doorways with their forepaws hanging and their ears laid a little back, and they look out at the weather. That is all. That is the whole of what they do. But the *looking* is exact — the angle, the height, the little settle of the haunches when the cloud thickens — and it is the look of a person who has come to the door for no reason but to see what the day is doing, and has stood too long, and knows it, and does not go in. It is the most domestic posture there is. When a hare vacates a door and the next hare comes to fill it, the second sits where the first sat, not near where it sat, and looks where it looked.

Then the owls, in the roof-spaces, which are the town's only rapid inhabitants and which spend their rapidity so quietly it hardly counts. The ptarmigan, which sit on the slab roofs like the roofs' own weather, white in the pale parts, mottled where the stone is dark. The voles under the drain-covers, packed together in their dozens, warm, blinking up when you lift the grating and then returning to whatever they were attending to, which is one another. The frogs among them with their mouths working and no sound coming. And in the largest house, the stag.

The stag should be described on its own, because the visitors who have seen it agree on more than visitors agree on about anything else here. It fills the room. Its antlers touch the corbelled underside of the roof and have polished two of the slabs with touching. It faces the door. It is not trapped; the door is wide enough; it went in. It stands the way the heron stands, as a thing that is about to move and does not, and its breath comes out white in the wet cold and rises and beads on the stone. Nobody has seen it eat either. Nobody has seen the room without it, though several have tried, coming at dawn, at midnight, in a thinning of the cloud. It is possible there are several stags and they take the room in turns. It is possible there is one. The people who have stood in that doorway and been looked at by it do not, afterwards, much care to make the distinction.

—

So: those are the inhabitants. That is what a survey would say, and it would be true, and it would leave out the thing you notice after an hour and cannot stop noticing after a day.

It is that none of this is how animals live. Animals crowd or they scatter. They hold territories against one another or they flock and press and jostle for the warm middle. They do not space themselves. They do not fill a doorway and vacate it and refill it as if there were a rota. A hare does not care what a heron is doing; an owl taking a vole is the loudest event in any ordinary field, in the sense that everything in the field knows about it and behaves accordingly. Here nothing behaves accordingly. Here the owl crosses the square with a vole in its feet and the square goes on being a square. The predation is not tolerated, which would be strange enough. It is *unremarked*, which is stranger, because unremarked is a word for what happens between people who have agreed on the terms.

You begin to feel that the animals are not what lives here. They are who is here. What lives here is the arrangement.

Try this on and see if it holds. Something came down the mountain in a single night, within living memory, and three hundred feet lower it found itself on a broken shelf in the cloud, with everything it had been made of lying about in slabs with edges you could cut bread with. And it did what it knew how to do, which was to lay stone on stone until there was a street, and a gutter down the middle, and a square with a platform in it, and to stand in the doorways and look out at the weather. It had no hands. It had the moss and the salamanders and the hares, and whatever else climbed up over the following years into the wet and the quiet, and it took them the way you take whatever is to hand.

People who favour this account differ on what the something is. Some say it is the town that was on the eastern face before the face let go — that there was a settlement there, small, the size of this one, dry-laid because there was no lime to be had so high, and that it came down with the mountain and its people are in the grit that the moss has packed into the seams. The town, in this account, is what survived: not the buildings, which are new, but the *habit* of the buildings, the shape a place holds in the minds of those who live in it long enough that it goes on holding the shape after the minds are gone. The three generations of hands in one generation of stone are the three generations that were on the face. They are laying it again. They are laying it with what they've got.

Others find that too much like a ghost story and prefer the mountain. The *tock* in the rock, the mountain still adjusting to what it did — they hear intention in it. The eastern face was, for all anyone knows, tired of being a face. It came down and made itself a shelf, and on the shelf it is making itself a town, because a town is the thing it saw people make and it wanted to know how it felt from the inside. The animals lift their heads when the rock speaks the way a household lifts its head when the master of the house shifts in his chair.

And there are one or two who say, more quietly, that it is neither, that it is only the place. That some places have a pull toward order the way water has a pull toward level, and that the shelf at Corrach, new and raw and packed with grit and lit like the inside of a pearl, is one, and that everything that has come up into it — moss, snail, hare, stone — has fallen into the order the way filings fall into a field. There is nobody. There is a shape that things take here, and the shape looks like a town because a town is what a shape looks like when living things space themselves and hold still.

—

The walls are where the accounts have to answer for themselves, and they do not answer well.

The new courses are real. Anyone can see them: the split faces still bone-pale, the joints tight, the moss barely started. They are laid with the same hands — or the same something — that laid the rest; the corbels are set at the same pitch, the pierced drain-stones are bored to the same gauge. Whoever is building is building the same town, not adding to it. Nobody has seen it done. Nobody has seen a stone in the air.

But people who have stayed a night have seen this: that in the morning, along the top of a wall that was lower the evening before, the moss on the new course is not the moss of a new course. It is deep and sodden and has the look of years. As if the stone had been lifted from somewhere it was already old and set here, or as if the wall had grown the way a snail's shell grows, from the inside, with the moss going up with it. And along the lane beside the wall, every animal facing it: the hares in their doorways turned from the weather, which they never otherwise turn from, and the salamanders on the steps with their heads toward it, and the moths — the moths, which do not move — rearranged on their lintels in a spacing nobody had recorded there before, wider, or closer, as if the interval had been reset to suit a house that had changed.

There is one more thing, and those who mention it mention it last, because it is the thing that decides whether they come up again.

If you stay long enough — a night is not enough, two nights perhaps — you find that there is a doorway that is empty when you come to it. This does not happen. The doorways are never empty for long. But there is one, and it stays empty while you stand in the lane looking at it, and the hare in the next door along has turned its head, and the heron on the platform has turned its head, and the stag, if you go and check, is facing not the door of its own house but the wall through which, if the wall were not there, it would be facing you.

Nobody who has described this has said that they went and stood in the doorway. Everybody who has described it has said what it felt like to be asked.

That, in the end, is what lives at Corrach. Not the salamanders, though they are its most faithful. Not the hares, though they are its face. Not the mountain, or the dead, or the field that things fall into. Something that keeps a place the way a household keeps a place — with a street and a gutter and a platform in the square, with each door held by someone and each step by someone and each lintel dressed — and that has never yet been full, and is building, one pale course at a time, toward a size at which it will be. The moss is already on the new stone. It is making it old. When it has made it old enough, the shelf will look as though it had been a town for as long as it has been a mountain, and there will be a door with no one in it, and the light will be the light inside a pearl, and the cloud will come through in tides, and something will turn its head.
