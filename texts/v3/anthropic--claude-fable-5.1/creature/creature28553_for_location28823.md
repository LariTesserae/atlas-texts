The first answer is the one the hall gives you itself, and it is the animals. A heron, a second heron, eels, bats, moths, toads, a fox. That is who lives here. You could leave it there.

But you have been standing on the gantry long enough to notice that the heron on the rail has not eaten, and will not, and that the fox in the corner has no smell of fox about it — no musk, no den, no carried scraps — only the mineral damp of everything else. They are not living here the way animals live in a place. A fox in a place leaves the place marked. This fox has been marked by the place. Its fur is beaded like the ribs of the vault. It is being kept, the way the mug is kept, the way the coat is kept. It is one more thing that has been allowed to reach its state and stay there.

So you look again for who lives here, and the second answer is the one in the mug.

There was a person. The handle faces the stair because they set it down, every time, with the handle facing the stair, so that coming down from whatever was above they could take it without looking. The logbook is on a sloped desk because they stood at it; they did not sit. The oilskin is one coat on a row of hooks because there was, at the end, one of them. You can reconstruct a great deal. The height of the worn patch on the handrail gives you their hand. The spoons in the stair treads, deepest on the inside of each turn, give you their habit of hugging the wall going down. They came here every day, or lived here, or came here and then lived here; the distinction wore away like the paint on the flywheel. They watched the water. They logged the strokes. When the near engine held at the top of its stroke a beat too long, they noticed, and their noticing was the hall's noticing, and they wrote it down.

They are gone and the shape they left is exact, and you could say that what lives here is that shape — an absence so precisely moulded that the room still moves around it. But an absence does not step closer along a rail. An absence does not lift its head when the piston hangs.

So the third answer, and you feel yourself resisting it because it is the kind of thing one says about machines, is the engines.

They breathe. That is not a figure. They draw and settle, out of phase, and the interval between them is long enough that you had time to stop counting it and now, having stopped, you find you have not stopped — some part of you is still holding the count in the way you hold the count of a sleeping person's breath in the next room, not listening for it, listening for it to stop. The bats breathe with it. You breathe with it. The flywheel does not know it is turning, but the hall is arranged around the fact that it is, the way a house is arranged around a stove. Once, the near engine hesitated, and every creature in the room turned to the flywheel, and you called that the hall noticing. It was not the hall. The hall is brick.

And here is where the thing becomes more itself, because you realise that what turned to look at the flywheel was not the engine, and was not the animals either, singly. It was one movement. Twelve heads and a dozen small bodies and the fox's ear and your own held breath, all of it turning as one thing turns. The way a shoal turns.

What lives here is the attending.

It does not have a body of its own. It had one once — it stood at the sloped desk, it hugged the wall going down. When that body stopped coming, the attending did not stop; it had been going on too long and the engines were still turning and there was still water to be watched not going anywhere. It simply distributed. It is in the heron on the rail, which holds the watch on the far end and takes one step every few minutes because that is the pace at which the watch can be held without getting tired. It is in the second heron, ankle-deep in the spill, which holds the watch on the return. The toads take the channel, a metre apart, no gaps. The eels take the lamps from below. The bats take the piston housing, which is the part most likely to fail, and they are the ones who lift their heads first. The moths have taken the light itself, and they have dimmed it, and you cannot tell whether that is a cost or a kindness — whether the attending prefers to work in a lower light, the way one turns down a lamp in a sickroom.

The fox does not seem to hold any part of the watch, and then you understand that it does. The fox holds sleep. Somebody has to. The attending cannot be all vigilance or it would be a kind of fear, and there is no fear in this room. The fox lies in the corner where the stair comes down and keeps the part of the watch that trusts the others, and it does not wake when you pass because you have already been accounted for.

You try to give the attending a shape and it will not take one. It is roughly the size of the hall. It is roughly the age of the mineral on the rails. It moves at the speed of the flywheel and no faster, because it has never needed to; nothing here happens quickly except the fall of a single drop, and it does not concern itself with drops. It does not eat. What it wants — and you can feel the want, it is the most present thing in the room, more present than the water — is the next settling. Not the one after that. Only the next. When the settling comes, the want is satisfied wholly and instantly and then begins again, and this is not hunger, because hunger is satisfied by something being carried away, and nothing here is. It is closer to the want of a person who has stayed up with someone all night, and has heard them breathe, and waits to hear them breathe.

It remembers. You were right about that. The hesitations are its only events, and it keeps them the way the logbook kept them, though the logbook is swollen shut and no one will read it. It does not remember them with alarm. It remembers them as the places where it was gathered, briefly, into a single thing, and knew itself, and then let go.

It is not the owner of this place. It came for the same reasons the animals came — the warmth, the steady light, the water that stays — and it has stayed for the same reason the person stayed, which is that the engines were running and someone ought to be here. It will not leave. If the engines stop, it will not know what to do, and so it will do what it has always done: face the flywheel and wait for the settling, one step every few minutes, until the mineral has made a sculpture of it too. You cannot decide whether that is faithfulness or only momentum, and then you notice that you have stopped being able to tell them apart, which is what it feels like from inside.

Because you are inside it. That is the last thing to see. Since the second landing, since the air changed, you have been slowing to match the place, and you took that for the place's effect on you. It was the attending taking you on. Not possessing — it has no interest in possessing — but assigning. It has given you the gantry. Somebody had to hold the gantry; the heron at the far end has been holding it alone. It is why the heron is coming toward you, a step at a time, not to inspect you but to hand over. When it stands beside you it will look at the water, and you will look at the water, and there will be nothing in the water, and you will watch it anyway, because the engines are running and the drop is gathering on the rib above you and in a moment the far one will settle and then there will be the long wait for the near one, and it is important that someone is here when it does.
