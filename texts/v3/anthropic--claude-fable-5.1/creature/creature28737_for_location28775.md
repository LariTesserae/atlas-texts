The first answer is that nobody does, and it is worth sitting with that answer for a while before improving on it, because it is nearly right and the ways it is wrong are the whole point.

Nobody lives here in the sense that the streets suggest. No one walks them to get from one room to another. No one sleeps in the cells. The doorways were never doors. If you put a person in one of the middle cells and told them to make a life, they would fail within a week — there is nothing to burn, nothing to eat, no way to get dry, and the passage out would be narrower every morning. The place refuses tenancy of that kind so thoroughly that it is tempting to call it empty and be done.

But it is not empty. It is occupied to capacity. It is simply that the occupant and the building are the same thing.

---

The plant lives here. That is the plain fact, and it deserves to be said plainly, because the settlement's shape keeps trying to turn the plant into architecture — into a thing that was made and is now standing — when it is in fact a body, and the body is at home.

It is old in the way that slow things are old, which is to say that its age is not a number but a depth. On the downhill edge it is one summer old. In the middle it is perhaps a lifetime. On the uphill side, in the basements under the second and third stories, it is older than that, and what is down there is not so much living stem as the plant's own history, compressed and holding water. It has never been anywhere else. It arrived as a seed or a fragment carried up from somewhere it no longer resembles, and it found the frost-broken rock and the standing cloud, and it decided — if a plant decides — that this was enough.

What it does all day is hold on and lean. Each stem grows until its own length becomes a problem, and then it tips, and finds another stem, and locks. This is its entire behaviour. It has no other gesture. The hexagons, the streets, the consistent walls — these are not what the plant is doing; they are what happens when the one thing it does is done ten thousand times on a slope. It lives here the way a heartbeat lives in a chest: not as an inhabitant but as the ongoing event of the place.

It is warm, slightly. That single degree inside the cells is the plant's, and it is the closest thing here to an intention. Everything else about the settlement — the drip, the pools, the frost, the morning release — is the cloud's doing, or the cold's. The degree is the plant's. It is small and it is constant and it is the reason anything else can live here at all.

---

Because other things do. Not many, and not loudly, but the plant has a household.

The moss on the founding stems first. It was described as paying nothing, and that is probably unfair. It sits on the oldest wood in each cell, the wood that has stopped growing and started, imperceptibly, to give way, and it is likely that the moss is not merely staining that wood but working it — loosening it, taking from it, returning something to the wet floor. In a place where nothing drains, someone has to do the breaking down. The moss is grey and thin and would not be noticed in any other landscape. Here it is the only other green there is, and it has chosen the one surface in every room that is closest to dying.

Then the floor. Under the springy black mat of old root and old stem there is a population that never comes up. Springtails, mites, the small pale things that eat rot and each other. They are the plant's basement tenants. They live entirely on what falls: dead leaves, the felted brown undersides shed one at a time, the soft lower stems that the plant abandons as it climbs. The plant is always leaving something behind, always vacating its own ground floor in favour of the next storey up, and what it leaves is theirs. They will never see the cloud. They have no need of it. Their weather is the drip coming down through the mat, and their seasons are the swelling and shrinking of the pools above their heads.

---

And the snails.

The snails are the ones who live here in something like the way the streets imply.

They are small and dark and flat and nearly the colour of wet wood, and they live on the undersides of the roof-stems, where the fringe of droplets forms. This is their whole territory: the ceiling. They graze the film of algae and the softening bark, and they drink from the beads before the beads fall. They move at the speed the place moves. A snail crossing a cell along the underside of its roof might take most of a day, and a snail crossing the settlement might take a life, and neither has any reason to do so.

But they do move between cells. That is the thing to understand about them. The roof-stems are continuous — where one cell's wall is shared with the next, the roof is shared too — and a snail on the underside can pass from one hexagon to another without ever touching the floor or the street, through the interlocking overhead, along the seam. The settlement is, to a snail, one enormous ceiling with occasional gaps of light in it. The streets are the gaps. The snails avoid them.

So the population is one population, and has been, for as long as the roof has been continuous.

Except that it isn't, quite, because the place is closing.

---

Here is where the household stops being a list and becomes something else.

When a doorway grows shut, the cell does not merely become sealed to the visitor. It becomes sealed to the roof-traffic as well, or nearly. The new growth that fills the doorway is soft and pale and dense, and it comes in at a different angle to the old interlocked roof, and for a long time — perhaps years — a snail can still find a way through it, along the underside, in the dark. But the growth thickens. The angles close. And at some point, on some night no one records, the last route out of a cell ceases to exist, and whatever snails are on the ceiling of that cell at that moment are the founding population of a country.

Somewhere near the center — the center that is hard to find — there are dozens of these. Sealed rooms, full of drip and dark, and inside them, on the undersides of the roof-stems, the snails.

They live. There is no reason they should not. The moss is still there on the founding stem, and the roof-stems still soften, and the water still comes down; a sealed cell is, if anything, wetter than an open one, since nothing drains and now nothing evaporates either. They graze in the dark in the direction their slime tells them to. They breed — slowly, but the cell is small and the generations are short compared to the plant's. They die, and the shells come off the ceiling and fall into the pool, and the pool, which is black and cold and the size of a spread hand, slowly acquires a floor of shell. In the oldest sealed cells that floor might be a finger deep.

None of them has ever seen a passage. None of them knows there is a street on the other side of the wall, or that the wall is a wall, or that there are other rooms. Their entire world is one rough hexagon of dripping wood, a grey stem with moss on it, and a pool. It is enough. It was always enough, even when the doorway was open — they never used it. The closing changed nothing they were doing. It only made permanent what was already their habit.

That is the thing that emerges, standing in a middle cell with the pale band of new growth already pressing into the doorway: the closing is not a catastrophe for anyone. It is not even an event. It is simply the moment at which a way of living that was already complete becomes also enclosed. The snails on this ceiling, if they are here when the door shuts, will not notice. They will go on grazing the wet underside in the dark, and their shells will go into the pool, and the plant will go on climbing uphill over their roof, and the cell will be — as the description said — one of many.

And this is happening in every direction. The settlement is not a town with a few sealed rooms. It is a town becoming, cell by cell, a set of sealed rooms, each with its own small population living out its own version of the same life, none of them in contact, none of them aware that contact was ever possible. The open passages are the exception, the temporary condition, the young part. The sealed cell is what the place is for, if it is for anything. The visitor walks through the streets and thinks of them as the settlement, but the streets are where the settlement has not yet finished happening.

---

So: what lives here.

A plant, which is the place, and which does one thing forever. A moss, on the oldest wood, taking it apart. A floor full of small pale things eating what the plant leaves as it climbs. And on every ceiling, open or sealed, in a hundred or a thousand hexagons of drip and dark, a snail population that has never needed the door and does not miss it.

The bird does not live here. The bird came once, into the uphill stories, and rattled through a space too narrow for its body, and left. It was looking for something — the snails, perhaps, or a place to be out of the cloud — and either it did not find it or it found it and understood that it could not stay. The settlement is not hostile to birds. It simply has no room for anything that needs to leave.

That is the last thing to say about the residents. They are all things that do not need to leave. The plant cannot; the moss will not; the floor-dwellers have no elsewhere; the snails were never going anywhere but the next stretch of ceiling. The place selects for this. It closes, and what remains inside it is whatever was already content to be closed in. Every doorway that shuts is a small test that everything inside has already passed.

The visitor is the only one here who fails it. The visitor stands in the middle cell, knee-deep in someone else's completeness, watching the new growth come in, and is the only living thing in the settlement to whom the narrowing passage means anything at all. And then the visitor goes back down the street, shoulders brushing the leaves on both sides, out to the frost-broken rock and the standing cloud, and behind them the drip continues at its intervals of a few seconds, and on the underside of the roof a dark flat thing the colour of the wood moves a hair's breadth toward the next bead of water, and does not look up, and does not look up, and does not look up.
