Start with the feet, because that is where they start.

Nobody here has ever walked on anything flat. The soles of the people are wide and the toes are spread and a little prehensile from a lifetime of gripping wet bark; a child of six can stand on a rounded limb slick with cloud and lean out over a hundred feet of nothing to hook a cluster, and the only thing holding her is the shape of her foot, which has grown to fit the tree the way the cord has worn the groove. The soles are thick and pale and cross-hatched. They are never dry. There is a condition of the skin between the toes that everyone has and nobody remarks on, and a grey-green paste of crushed leaf that everyone rubs into it in the evening and that does not cure it, only keeps it in its place, and this is the general attitude toward wetness here: you do not defeat it, you negotiate.

Then the hands. There is a callus on the outer edge of each thumb, and another across the pads of the first two fingers, and these are the cord calluses. Cord is what people do with their hands when their hands are not doing something else. An old man watching the harvest from the shade of a limb is twisting cord. A woman waiting for a basket to come up is twisting cord. Children learn to twist before they learn to speak clearly, holding one end in their teeth, and so their front teeth have a shallow notch in them, and you can tell someone's age within a few fruitings by the depth of the notch. Everything is lashed and every lashing eventually rots, so the settlement consumes cord the way a fire consumes wood, and the twisting is not a craft exactly, more a kind of breathing that happens to produce something.

They are not tall. Tall is a disadvantage under limbs. They are lean in the way of people who climb for a living and eat well twice a year and adequately the rest of the time, and their shoulders are big for their frames, and the skin of their shoulders and forearms is marked all over with small pale scars from bark and cane. Hair is kept short, or bound tight and greased, because loose wet hair in a place with no dry is a torment. They wear little: a twisted cord around the hips with a hank of barkcloth hung from it that is more about carrying things than covering them, and a broad hat of a single leaf for the harvest, and that is all, because cloth that is never dry is worse than no cloth. Their bodies are the colour of the bark where the sun reaches it and the colour of the bark where it doesn't, and after a while you notice that they stand the way the tree stands, braced in several directions at once.

---

They are loud. They have to be. Ordinary speech does not carry through a hundred barbets, so the settlement's speech has gone up in pitch and out in volume and acquired a set of sharp vowels that cut through the noise like a hook through leaves. Across the hollow they do not speak at all; they whistle, and the whistling has grammar — two rising notes and a fall means *ready over here*, a long flat note means *stop, wait*, a stutter means *monkeys*. Children learn the whistles as a second language and use them among themselves for secrecy, since the whistles carry farther than adults can be bothered to listen. There are also hand signs, for when the wings of the hornbills drown even whistling, and the hand signs are old and few and never invented, only inherited. The sign for *fruit* is the same as the sign for *now*.

Silence frightens them. When the cloud comes through and the birds stop for their four seconds, every person on every limb goes still and looks up, because the only other thing that silences the crown that way is a hawk, or a falling limb, or once — in a story everyone knows and no one witnessed — the ground moving under the root. The four seconds pass and the noise comes back and they breathe out and laugh at themselves, and the laughing is part of it, part of how the fear is kept small. They do not like to sleep alone, and a house that goes quiet at night, where you cannot hear someone else's breathing over the frogs, is a house someone will visit in the morning with a reason.

---

Time is fruitings. A person is not so many years old but so many fruitings, and since there are two a year the numbers run high and the old are very old indeed by their own count — a woman of sixty is a hundred and twenty fruitings and says so with satisfaction. The half-year between one wave and the next has a name that means something like *the tightening*, and it is when the real work happens: lashings renewed, roofs retrained, walkways rebuilt where a limb has shifted, the harvest paste rationed out of its cane cylinders a scoop at a time, snails gathered from the bromeliad rims by children with quick fingers, and pigeons taken.

They do take pigeons, in the tightening. Not many, with slings, from the smaller trees on the far side of the ridge, never from this one. There is an idea, not often spoken because it does not need to be, that you do not kill what is eating at the same table. During the fruiting the pigeons are guests, or rivals, or weather; they are pushed and shouted at and cursed for their droppings, and not one is harmed, and a child who kills one with a sling during the harvest is not punished so much as looked at for a long time by everyone, which is worse. Six weeks later, on the other ridge, the same child may bring down three and be praised. This is not thought of as a contradiction. It is thought of as knowing where you are.

---

Cord is inheritance. When a young person cuts the tip of an aerial root so that it will fork, they are making cord for someone not yet born; the fork will not be thick enough to harvest for eight or ten years and the person who cuts it may not be the one who takes it. So the roots are known. Every fork on the great root-braid has a history — who cut it, who took the first fork, who is waiting on the second — and this history is kept in the heads of a few old people and is the nearest thing the settlement has to law. Disputes are about roots. Marriages are, in some quiet way, about roots, about which forks will be combined. A family that has cut generously and taken sparingly is a family with a future, and everyone knows which families those are, and it shows in how their platforms are lashed.

The oldest cord in the settlement is not cord at all but the groove it has left. There is a platform on the north side whose beam is worn so deeply that the wood is nearly gone in a ring, and they will not replace the beam. They lash new cord into the old groove twice a year and they touch the groove when they pass it, and if you ask what it is they will say it is a groove, which is true.

---

The houses are alive and are treated accordingly. A roof is a set of fronds still growing from their stems, and stems thicken, so every tightening the roof lashings must be loosened a finger's width or the house will slowly strangle its own roof, which is a thing that has happened, and the house where it happened is still pointed out. A good house is one that has been let grow. There are houses older than anyone living, houses that have been let grow for so long that the frame has thickened into something like a second trunk, and these are not the houses of the important people, exactly, but the important people tend to end up living in them. A new couple is given a young house, small and tight, and the first years of their life together are the years of loosening its lashings, and the settlement has jokes about this that are not subtle.

Inside, the floor is split cane on pegs and the water runs beneath and you can hear it. Everything is hung: baskets, cord, blades, sleeping mats rolled and slung from the frame so they do not lie on anything. A thing left on the floor overnight will have something growing on it by morning. There is a small fire in a clay-lined cup that is more for smoke than heat — smoke keeps the frames from softening and keeps the moths off the paste — and the smoke goes up through the fronds and is, from outside, the only sign at dusk that the lattice is inhabited by anything with fire.

---

The hornbill you don't.

Ask and they will tell you the hornbill is the one who taught them to live in the tree, and then, if you press, they will tell you the actual story, which is that the hornbill nests in a hollow and the female is sealed in with mud and dung by the male and stays there for weeks in the dark being fed through a slit while the young grow, and comes out at last into the noise. The people live in a hollow. They know what the hollow was: the host, the tree the strangler killed and ate and turned to soil, and they know that they are living inside the mouth of the thing that fed them, sealed in by their own lashings, fed through the slits between the stems. They have not made a religion of this. They are not, on the whole, a people who make religions. But when a hornbill lands on the sorting mats they let it take what it wants, and when it flies its wings make the chest-sound, and the children stop what they are doing to feel it.

The monkeys are another matter. The monkeys are neighbours in the way that the family across the hollow are neighbours, which is to say with a grievance. The two troops have divided the crown for as long as anyone remembers, and the settlement has, without anyone deciding it, divided itself the same way — a north side and a south side, and the north side says the south side lashes badly and the south side says the north side cuts roots it has no claim to, and both sides say the monkeys on the other side are the loud ones. There is a great deal of shouting across the hollow in the harvest that is half about fruit and half about this. Nobody has been hurt over it in living memory. It is a rivalry that has worn a groove.

---

There was a below, once. The stone says so: the split-stones and the awl-stones and the steps cut into the buttress roots, all of it carried up by people who came from somewhere with stone, which is to say somewhere with ground. Nobody now living has been to the below. There is a way down — a ladder of lashed cane down the outermost root, and then another, and then what is said to be a path — and every few fruitings a young man or woman goes to look at it, and stands at the top of the ladder in the cloud, and looks down into more cloud, and comes back. They do not think of this as cowardice. They think of the below as the place the stone comes from, the way the sky is the place the hornbills come from, and no more theirs to visit.

Stone is wealth and is not traded lightly. A blade goes from mother to daughter and is resharpened until it is a sliver and then the sliver is set in a cane handle and used as an awl and then the awl is buried. When the woman on the platform trades a blade for a share of fruit, everyone near her stops sorting to watch, because a blade leaving a family is a thing that happens once in a generation and means something has gone wrong in that family, or something has gone very right in the other, and by evening the whole lattice will know which.

---

The dead go into the middle. The interior of the cage, where the host rotted down to soil, is the only earth in the settlement, and it is dim and cool and quiet in a way nowhere else is — the stems break the noise into something distant and pattering — and it is where they put the dead, wrapped in barkcloth, laid in the soft black ground among the ferns. Nothing is marked. In a few fruitings there is nothing to mark. The pale orchid grows there, and the settlement knows what the orchid grows in, and picks it for the harvest platform anyway, and puts it in the hair of the children, and this is not thought of as morbid. It is thought of as the middle giving back.

The second fig grows there too. It is a sapling now, a few years old, a thin whip with the same grey bark and the same glossy leaf, come up from a seed some pigeon dropped in the dark, and the settlement has noticed it and has begun, in a leisurely way, to argue. One side says cut it, it will do to this tree what this tree did to the last one, it will be a hundred years but it will happen and better to end it while it is a whip. The other side says the tree will outlive the argument and so will the sapling and it is not for them to decide what the tree does about its children. Nobody has cut it. Someone, every tightening, goes and looks at it and comes back and says it has grown, and someone else says of course it has grown, and this is as far as it gets. The people who will actually have to decide are not born yet and will not have to decide either, and everyone knows this, and it makes the argument comfortable, a thing to do with the mouth in the quiet months, like cord for the hands.

---

*Don't stand under a fruiting limb with your mouth open.*

It means what it says, first, because a thing does drop and you do not want it in your mouth, and a great many things drop. It means don't gape — don't stand slack in the middle of the harvest waiting for the tree to feed you, when the tree is feeding everyone and you have hands. It means don't be greedy, since the person with their mouth open is the person hoping for more than their basket. It means don't talk when you should be watching. It means, for children, quite specifically, that the pigeons are above you and you know what pigeons do. And it means, underneath all of that, something the people would not put into words because they would not need to: that this is a place that gives and gives and does not give *to you*, that the fruit is not for anyone, that you take your share by being quick and closed and holding on, and that the ones who came before you and were slow and open are in the middle, in the soft ground, growing orchids.

---

So: who lives here.

The frogs live here, one to a cup, ticking, each with its own small territory of rainwater and algae and its own opinion about the frog in the next cup over, and they were here before the houses and did not notice when the houses came. The ants live here in their two-way road, and the people step over the road and not on it, and have done so for so long that the ants have worn their own groove in the bark. The pigeons live here twice a year and nowhere in particular the rest of the time. The monkeys live here and hold their two halves of the crown and will still be holding them when the north side and the south side have forgotten what they were arguing about and started arguing about something else.

And the people live here the way the frogs live in the cups and the houses live in the lattice and the cord lives in the groove: shaped, fitted, worn to the exact contour of a thing that was not made for them and does not know they are there. They do not own the tree. They do not think they own it. They have a hundred words for what it does and none for what it is, and when the cloud comes through and the light goes grey and the birds stop for four seconds, they look up, every one of them, into the lattice and the dripping leaves, and they are not looking for a hawk. They are checking that it is still there.

Then the noise comes back, and somewhere high up a cluster lets go, and it comes down through the leaves in a pattering that sounds like the beginning of rain, and they close their mouths and go back to work.
