What lives here lives in the lines.

That is the first thing to know and it takes a while to know it, because you spend your first days looking at the tiles, and the tiles are empty. Nothing lives on a tile. The tiles are too bright and too warm and too clean; they shed whatever lands on them; they are the one place on this world where a living thing cannot get a purchase. Everything that lives here lives in the dark finger's-width between one tile and the next, in the seams, which from a standing height are the only marks on the plain and which from inside are a world.

So you have to get down. You have to lie on the warm domed surface with your cheek against it and put your eye to the crack and let your eyes come down from the glare, and then the seam opens.

---

The felt first, because the felt is everywhere and everything else depends on it.

You called it a lichen and that will do, though it has had a long time to stop being one. It grows on the less-bright face of the lower tile, a hand's depth down, where the light from the sky reaches at a slant and the light from the ground does not reach at all. It is gray-green at the center of a patch and paler at the edges, almost the color of the plain, so that a new patch is nearly invisible and only an old one has darkened enough to see. The oldest patches are the size of your palm. You come to understand, over weeks, that a patch the size of your palm is not young.

Up close it is not felt but a nap of very fine, very short filaments, each one tipped with a bead so small you see it only as a sheen. The beads are the point of the thing. The warm air rising out of the seam carries a fraction of a fraction of water, and the beads are where that fraction is caught and kept. The felt does not drink; there is nothing to drink. It condenses. It sits in the updraft with a hundred thousand tiny cold points held out into the warm air, and over a day it takes back what the deep gives up, and it lives on that.

It takes its light from the sky. This is odd, when the ground is so much brighter, and you turn it over for a long time before you see the sense of it: the ground's light is heat. It is the plain exhaling, and the felt cannot live in that breath directly, only beside it. So it has turned its back on the brightest thing in the world and faces the thin white sky, and grows toward the sky at a rate that you could not measure with a lifetime.

Here and there on an old patch there are cups. They are the size of pinheads and they open upward, and when the wind is right something leaves them — you never see it, but you see the felt on a new tile's edge years after the tile was placed, when the walkers have finished with it and the edge has gone soft, and there was no felt there before, so it came from somewhere. It rides the warm air up out of the seam and the wind takes it and nearly all of it falls on the bright faces of the tiles and dies there within the hour. A little falls into the dark. That is the whole of its dispersal, and it is enough, because there is nothing but time.

And the felt sheds. Its underlayers die as its surface grows, and the dead layers flake, and the flakes go down the seam, and with them goes the dust they have caught, because the felt is sticky in the way that all slow things are sticky — it holds whatever touches it. The wind drops the plain's own powder into the seams and the felt binds the powder into itself and dies and drops it further. Each seam is a slow throat. The felt is what the throat is lined with. Everything that goes into the dark goes in through the felt, over years, in a fall so gradual that the word falling does not fit.

---

The walkers.

You saw one on the first day and you called it an animal and that will do too. It is the size of your hand with the fingers spread. It has eleven legs, or thirteen, or nine — they are not all the same length and you are never sure you have counted the same one twice — and they come off a low domed body with no front and no back, so that it moves in any direction with the same unhurried competence and turns by simply deciding that a different side is the leading one. It is pale in the way the plain is pale. It is not camouflaged. It is made of the same stuff, because it is made of what it eats.

Its mouth is underneath. You have to turn one over to see this, and it does not resist much; it folds its legs in and waits, warm in your palm, heavier than it looks. The underside is a shallow ring of hard pale ridges around a central softness, and the ridges are worn smooth at their tips. This is what it puts against the edge of a tile. It rasps. It takes the tile's edge off in a powder finer than any powder the wind could make, and it swallows the powder, and what its body does not need for its own shell it gives back as a dust so fine it hangs in the seam's updraft for hours before it settles.

So this is what wore the tiles. You had it as the light leaving, or the wind, or duration, and it was none of those. It was this. A hand's-width thing with a ring of rasps going along every edge on the planet at a speed you would have to sit still to notice, for a length of time that has made the work indistinguishable from weather.

They come to new tiles. The one you saw the first day had come to the new tile, and you wondered how it knew, and now you know: the new tile is warmer than its neighbors, and the walkers have no eyes but they have the whole of their undersides as a heat-sense, and a fresh tile stands up out of the plain's warmth like a lit window. They come across kilometers of seam, over days. They arrive from different directions and do not seem to notice each other and set to the edge and take it down, a sharp edge to a soft one, until the new tile is as blunt as the old ones and the felt can find a hold on it.

They graze the felt too. Not to nothing — they thin it. Where a patch has grown thick enough to begin reaching across the seam toward its neighbor, a walker will come and take the reach off. You watch this happen, once, over most of a day, and you understand at the end of it that the felt, left alone, would close the seams. It would grow across and meet itself and seal the dark. And then the seam could not breathe, and the plain could not adjust, and the tile could not sink and be replaced. So the walkers keep the seams open. They are the reason the plain can still tick.

They are warm. They have no hurry in them. They have no sound, or none you can hear. In all your time here you never see two of them touch, and you never see a small one, and you never see one die except by the hole.

---

The hole.

You find the shells, as you found them before, lying in a seam with the pale dome intact and the underside empty, and in the dome, always, one round opening, its edge as clean as if it had been drilled. The shell is scoured. Not a trace remains inside. Whatever made the hole did not leave a leg or a smear or a crumb; it came and took the whole of the walker out of the shell it had built, and left the shell, which is tile-stuff anyway and will go down the throat with the felt and the dust in its own time.

You never see it. You come to think, after a while, that you would not know it if you did.

Once, lying with your ear to a seam in the long violet night, you hear something below you that is not a tick. It is a dry brushing, continuous, the sound of something long moving against the sides of a narrow place. It is going down, or going along; you cannot tell. You put your eye to the seam and far below where the felt ends and the tile-edges go on stacked into the dark, something pale withdraws, without haste, the way the walkers do everything, and the brushing goes on for a while and then is too far to hear.

It lives lower than the walkers. That much you know. It comes up when it comes up and goes down again with what it has taken, and the walkers, who can feel a new tile from kilometers away, do not seem to feel it coming. Maybe it is not warm. Maybe it is exactly the temperature of the seam. You think about what a thing would be like that had lived its whole existence in a finger's-width of dark between two warm walls, going up and down, and you find you cannot put a shape to it that is not the seam's own shape: long, and pale, and thin, and quiet, and going where the seam goes.

And you begin to wonder, without deciding, whether it is a predator at all. Whether it is not simply the seam's way of getting the walkers back. Everything else that lives here goes down eventually — the felt, the dust, the shells — and the walkers, alive, do not. They stay near the top, where the edges are. Something has to fetch them.

---

Below the felt, below where the brushing thing goes, the seams go on, and the seams are lined with tile-edges, and the tile-edges are older the deeper you look, and somewhere down there the material stops being tiles and becomes whatever it was before it was fitted. You will never see this. Nobody standing on the plain will ever see this. But you know something is there, because the water comes from there.

The plain is dry to its top and its top is all you can reach. But the air coming up the seams carries its fraction of moisture, and the felt would not exist otherwise, and the walkers would not exist without the felt, and so the whole of life here is held up by something that, far down, still has a chemistry that takes water in and gives water out. It is warm. It is the source of the warmth. It is what the plain exists to cool.

You lie on the ground at night and try to think of it as an engine and cannot, because engines do not need water, or as a body and cannot, because it is the size of a world. In the end you think of it as the plain's stomach, and that makes the seams the plain's gut, and the felt its lining, and the walkers something like the small creatures that live in a gut and keep it working, and the brushing thing the means by which the gut takes back what it needs. Everything that falls into the dark is dissolved down there and comes up again as tile. The new tile that rose beside you on the first day, smooth and unpitted and brighter than its neighbors, was made of old tile and old dust and old felt and old shells, and it will be rasped to powder and go back down to become tile again. The plain is made partly of the bodies of the things that live on it. It always has been. That is what it is for them to live here.

---

So the question of what lives here starts to turn over.

You came looking for inhabitants and found a lichen and an animal and the sign of a second animal, and that is a fauna, and you could stop there. But the longer you lie on the warm breathing ground the harder it gets to hold the plain and the things in its seams apart. The felt is not on the plain the way moss is on a rock. The felt is how the plain swallows. The walkers are not living in the plain the way mice live in a wall. The walkers are how the plain keeps its seams open and how it wears its own edges smooth and how it turns tile back into the dust that will be tile again. The brushing thing is how the plain gets the walkers back. None of them could exist without the plain and the plain, you slowly understand, could not go on being what it is without them. Not quite. It would seal itself. It would stop breathing. It would go on radiating for a long time and then, somewhere, it would crack.

It is the plain that lives here. The plain is the inhabitant. It holds itself flat against a planet that would rather not be flat, and it does this by adjusting, and adjusting requires seams, and seams require keeping, and the keeping is done by small pale things that it feeds and that feed it and that it eventually takes back into itself. It has a metabolism, which is heat becoming light. It has a skin, which sheds and is replaced. It has a gut. It has something like a sense of level so fine that a shift of a fraction of a millimeter across a hemisphere registers and is answered. It has a sound, which is the tick. It has a lifespan, which is however long the thing at the bottom stays warm.

Whether it knows anything is not a question the place lets you ask. It does not behave like something that knows. It behaves like something that does. It has one thing to do and it does it with the whole of itself and has done it for so long that the doing has worn the edges off everything, and the small things in its seams go along their edges in the same spirit, doing the one thing, and the felt takes its fraction and the dust falls and the tile rises and settles and ticks.

---

And the ones who made it.

You said they are not here and that stands. They are not in the seams — you have looked; you have put your face to more seams than you can count and the seams hold felt and walkers and shells and dark. They are not under the tiles in any sense that would let you find them. There is no marker, no inscription, no shape in the pattern of the seams that resolves into a sign. If they wanted to be remembered they did not build the plain to do it. The plain does not remember anything. It does not need to.

But you keep coming back to it. Whatever was here before was either taken down into it or became it. And you have spent weeks watching things be taken down into it and come back up as tile, and you cannot stop the thought once it has started: that the plain is made of what was here before, all of it, mountain and seafloor and whatever stood on the mountain and whatever swam over the seafloor, resolved into one pale material and fitted and stacked and set to work. That this is where they are. Not preserved. Not waiting. Used. The way the walkers' shells are used. The way the felt is used. They are in it the way everything that has ever lived here is in it, as material, as the substance of the surface that glows.

Or not. You have no way to know and the place gives you nothing. Maybe they went somewhere. Maybe they only ever came here to make this and then left. You find, after a while, that the not-knowing sits more easily than it should, because the plain has taught you what it is to be a thing that goes on without its makers, and the plain is not sad about it. It does not seem to have been built for them to live on. It seems to have been built to be the thing that lives here after them. They made a resident, and they made it able to keep itself, and they gave it — or it grew, or it found — the small pale keepers in its seams, and then they were not here anymore, and the plain did not notice, and the felt took its fraction, and the walkers went along the edges, and a tile rose and settled and ticked.

---

There is one more thing that lives here and it is you.

You have been here long enough now that you are part of the inventory. Your breath goes up into the dry air and some of it is water, and the felt in the seams where you have slept has had, for a few nights, more than its fraction. It does not know this. It will not remember you. But in the way the plain keeps time, the patches where you lay will be very slightly thicker, for a very long while, than the patches where you did not.

Your skin sheds. The wind takes it. It falls into the dark with the plain's own dust and the felt binds it and it goes down the throat and somewhere below where the brushing thing goes it is dissolved into whatever the deep dissolves things into, and one day, in the kind of time this place runs on, it will rise beside someone as a new tile, smooth and bright, and a walker will come across kilometers of seam to take the edge off it.

You are not unwelcome. Nothing is unwelcome here. The plain does not have a policy about you. It has room, and it has time, and it has a way of taking things in, and you are a thing.

You lie on the warm slow-breathing ground under the violet and watch the dust rise lit from beneath, and the seams run black through the white, and somewhere very far off across the flat bright breadth of the world a tile settles by a hair, and the sound of it comes to you across the surface the way sound comes across ice — a single dry tick — and you know, now, that under that tick a walker has felt the new warmth and turned one of its sides forward and begun, without hurry, to come.
