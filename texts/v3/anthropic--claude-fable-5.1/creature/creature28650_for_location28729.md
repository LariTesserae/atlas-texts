Start with the film, because everything else is a way of being the film for a while.

It is not one thing. Pressed under a fingernail and held to what light there is, the mat in the bowls resolves into a felt of threads finer than hair, and living among the threads, too small to see except as a faint shifting of the bronze at the edges, are the things that eat the film from the inside: grains, motes, specks that are also alive and that the lows swallow without knowing. So the economy has a floor beneath its floor. The film feeds on the water, the motes feed on the film, the lows feed on both, and the lows' shells become the stone the film grows on. It is very tight. There is not much slack in it anywhere.

The lows, seen close, are more particular than they look in their thousands. Each shell is laid down in rings, one for each rise and fall of the sheet, and the rings can be counted; a palm-sized low has lived through fifty or sixty cycles. They have no front and back that matter—the mouth is under the middle and the shell is nearly round—and a low that is turned in place simply continues in whatever direction it now faces, and the front it belonged to closes around the gap and goes on. They have no fear response that anyone has found. Lifted off the rock, a low goes still, then begins rasping at the air where the film should be. Set down, it grazes. They are the most numerous animal on the plain and the least present; there is nothing behind the shell to meet.

The striders are another matter. Their translucence is not uniform: the neck is nearly clear, and you can watch a swallowed low descend through it, shell and all, and see the dark gut take it. The legs are the opaque part—dense, bone-heavy, splayed at the foot into three broad pads that are the only soft thing about them, and the pads are where they hear. A strider stands with its weight rocking slowly from pad to pad, and each shift changes what it hears, and so they are always moving a little, a flock of several hundred swaying slightly out of time, like a crowd listening to something far off. When the wave passes through and the ground answers, the swaying stops all at once, and every foot presses flat.

The bray is not the same in every flock. There are dialects, if that is the word—the wave passes through one flock a beat faster than through another, and the tearing of the call is pitched differently, and the people can tell from a distance which flock is on which bench without seeing it. The bray of the young is thinner. Newly hatched striders make it from their first hour, before they can stand. Whatever the call is for—and the people have several answers, none of them confident—it is not for each other alone. It is for the ground. The flock brays and the plain returns the bray, and something in the return tells the striders where the stone is thick and where it is hollow, where the sheet is deep, where the lows are moving; they navigate by the world's echo of their own voice. The people learned this from the striders. The people say so.

The heavy thing in the water is the only silent animal on the plain.

It is wider than it is long, a shape like a bench that has decided to move, and it lies just beneath the surface of the sheet with only the ridge of its back showing, the color of wet gneiss, the color of the film. Its mouth runs the full width of the front and opens downward. It cruises the edges of the strider-benches at a walking pace and when a strider stands rigid at the close of a wave the mouth comes up under the legs, and the strider is gone from the middle of the flock and the flock does not turn to look. It has no call. It makes no rasp. Its footfalls, if it has feet under there, do not ring. It leaves a wake of cleared shell where it has passed and nothing else, and the people regard it with a feeling that does not have a sign of its own but is made from two signs pressed together: the sign for the inside of a house, and the sign for something that eats you.

They do not hunt it. It is not that they fear it, though they keep their children off the benches when the ridge of a back is moving. It is that it is quiet, and quiet here is nearly holy, and you do not kill the thing that carries it.

The shadow is known mostly by absence. No one has seen it land. What passes overhead under the cloud is a darkness with a shape too broad to be a bird and too slow, and the people know it is coming before it is visible because the ground changes: the striders beneath its path stop braying, all at once, and a lull runs across the plain like the shadow of a hand crossing a floor. In that lull, for a moment, you can hear the drip. Then it has passed and the roar closes over the place. The people have a sign for it that is the same as the sign for the lull—a flat palm passing slowly over the other palm—and they do not distinguish between the animal and the silence it drags with it. It takes striders, presumably. Once, in a long life, a person finds a strider on the open rock far from any bench, killed from above and left, and the bones have marks in them that nothing on the plain could have made.

Now the people.

They are not many; in the distance a ground-strike carries, which is a day's walk in any direction, there may be forty. They are stocky and low, built close to the stone, with thick calves and broad, heavy feet that they never cover. The soles are the color and texture of the rock. From the age of three or four a child is walking barefoot on wet gneiss all day, and by adulthood the skin of the foot is thicker than a strider's pad and, they say, more sensitive—not to touch but to the ring. They can stand on a bench and tell you, by shifting weight from heel to ball, how many striders are on it and whether the flock is settling or about to move. They walk with a particular stopping gait, a half-step, pause, a listening with the leg, that looks like caution and is nearer to reading.

The skin elsewhere is pale and always damp and prone to going wrong. Whatever slickness is in the water is in them too; it softens the nails, it creases the skin at the joints, it gets under the plugs. Ear infections are the common sickness of children and the common cause of their death. The old have fingers that will not fully straighten. Everyone's teeth are worn flat, not from grit but from clenching against the roar, a habit no one is aware of and no one can stop. When they smile, the smile is mostly gum.

Their hair is kept cropped to the skull, because long hair here is never dry and never warm and is a place for the film to start. The film does start on them—a bronze shadow in the creases of an elbow, under an arm, that is scraped off with a low's shell each morning inside the dome. Their clothing is pressed mat, cured and beaten thin, worn as a wrap and a hood, and it is the same color as the film and the stone and the water, so that a person standing still on the plain is another dark knob.

Their language outside is the hand and the ground.

The hand-sign is fast and blunt and made low, at the belt, because the eye here is always half-closed against the wet and the noise and does not want to travel far. It is a grammar of position more than shape: where the hand is in relation to the body, which way the palm faces, whether it is still or moving. There are signs for the film in each of its stages, the pale grazed film and the returning purple and the bronze-edged mature and the thickened mat; there are signs for the sheet at each depth from a skin of wet to knee-high; there are signs for the sound at every pitch and every distance. There is a sign for the moment at the close of a wave when the striders go still, and it is the same sign used to mean *now*.

The ground-strike is slower and reaches farther. A leg bone, or a fist-sized stone, brought down on the gneiss in a pattern; the stone takes it and carries it along its seams and it comes out of the rock a long way off, deepened and delayed, and a person there feels it in the feet and answers. The patterns are simple—where, how many, come, stay, danger, dead—and each person's name is a pattern of three or four blows, and a child is named by being struck for the first time when the parent brings it out of the dome and sets its bare feet on the rock and beats its name into the ground beside it, so that the first thing the plain says back to the child is who it is.

They speak with the mouth only indoors.

This is the thing that takes longest to understand about them. There is a spoken language, small and soft and old, and it exists only under a roof with the curtain down. It has no words for the striders or the film or the sheet or the ring; it has words for sleep, for warmth, for the body, for names, for the few things that happen inside a dome—eating, tending a fire of mat, mending, coupling, dying. It is spoken close, mouth to ear, because even inside the walls the hum is there and you must be near to be heard. Children learn it lying against a parent's chest in the dark. To speak it outside is not forbidden so much as pointless; the mouth opens and the roar takes what comes out of it before it has gone a hand's breadth. So the spoken words become the words of the small enclosed self, and the sign and the strike become the words of the plain, and a person has two languages that never meet, one for the inside and one for everything else.

The domes, then, are not only shelters. They are the only places where a voice can exist. They are built as much for the voice as for the ears—you can see this in the way the door faces away from the nearest bench, and in the thickness of the walls, which is far more than warmth or weather would want. Each takes years. Slabs are worked loose from the benches with quartz picks and levered and dragged across the sheet on sledges of bone, and the building of a dome is the largest thing a few families ever do together, and it is done mostly in the low of the cycle when the roar has spread thin and there is room, almost, to think.

Inside, the quiet is not comfortable. The description got that right. It is a shock, and the shock does not lessen with years. People weep on going in; the old weep every time. There is a word in the inside-language for the first breath you take with the curtain down, and it is also the word for a wound that has stopped bleeding. Some people, usually the very old, usually after the hearing is fully gone and the plain has become only a shiver in the bone, go into the dome and do not come out, and there is a sign for these—both hands laid flat on the belly—and no judgment attached to it. A dome may hold one of them for a long time, lying in the dark, fed by the others, listening to nothing. When they die they are carried out at the next low of the cycle and laid in a bowl where the mat is thick, and the film has them within a few days, and the lows come. The people rise on their own dead as the plain does. Nothing is buried; there is nothing to bury in.

They all go deaf. This is not a tragedy to them. It is the shape of a life. A child hears the most and knows the least; an old person hears nothing and knows everything, reading the plain up through the feet and the shins and the pelvis as a single continuous pressure that they can parse the way the child parses the sky. Deafness has a sign that is made by touching two fingers to the stone and then to the chest: gone into the ground. It is said of the old the way another people might say *wise*.

The plugs are not to prevent this. They cannot prevent it. They slow it. A child is plugged from the first day it is carried outside, and the plugs are renewed and pushed deeper as the ears grow, and by adolescence a person cannot hear the sound of their own hands rubbed together, and the roar is a wall on the other side of the mat that they feel more than hear. The plugs are the difference between going deaf at forty and going deaf at twelve, and between deafness that arrives as settlement and deafness that arrives as pain. That is all. It is enough for them to have made a discipline of it.

Which is the stone.

The single upright stone out on the open plain, with the rope grooves worn into its top, is where the plugs come out.

Every person is brought there twice—as a child, and at the end. The child is brought at seven or eight, when it has learned the sound-map well enough to find its own way home and not so well that it has stopped fearing. It is walked out along a cairn-line at the climb of the cycle, when the sheet is rising and the noise is drawing in toward the benches and thickening, and it is tied at the waist to the stone with a rope of twisted mat, and an adult it loves kneels in front of it and takes the plugs out.

The rope is because everyone runs.

There is no exception to this. A person who hears the plain unplugged for the first time, the whole of it, the rasp and the bray and the ring rising up through the soles and meeting the roar coming down, tries to walk out of it. Not away from anything in particular—in any direction. There is no out. The rope holds them at the stone and they pull against it and the adult who loves them holds their shoulders and puts their own forehead against the child's forehead and stays there, through a full wave and the ground's answer, until the child stops pulling. Then the plugs go back in. Then they walk home. The grooves in the top of the stone are from the rope, and the rope is from the pulling, and the pulling is from every child who has ever lived here, and the stone is very old.

They do this so the child will know what it lives inside. Not to harden it. To make sure it never mistakes the plugs for the plain.

The second time is when the hearing is nearly gone—when the roar has thinned to a shiver and the person can sense that within a season there will be nothing. They walk out alone. They tie the rope themselves. They take the plugs out and lie down full length on the rock with their ear to the stone and their chest to the stone and stay through a whole cycle, a rise and a fall of the sheet, a day and a night, and listen to the last of it with what hearing is left, so that they go into the ground knowing what the sound was. No one goes with them. When they walk back, they do not speak of it, inside or out. They are the ones the children watch.

Their day is short and their year is long. They rise in the dark and eat pressed mat and strider meat and drink the iron water from the bowls, and they go out and read the plain and tend the mat-pits and mend the cairn-lines the sheet has shifted, and once in a few days two or three of them go down a cairn-line to a bench at the close of a wave and take one strider and carry it home by the legs. They gather at the low of the cycle, when the roar is spread thin and the sheet has drained to a skin and a person can be seen and can be struck to from a long way off, and the forty who live within a day's walk come to whichever dome is largest and go in, in shifts, a few at a time, to sit in the quiet and speak with their mouths to people who are not their own household. Matches are made in the domes. So are arguments. Both are made close, mouth to ear, and are over quickly, because there is not much room and the others are waiting outside in the roar.

They are not unhappy. This should be said. There is no hunger here and no cold worth the name and no enemy; the plain gives more than can be eaten and asks only that you stand in it. They have a joke, in the inside-language, that is one of the few things in that language that is not about the body—it is a word that means *outside*, said in a certain way, and it is very funny to them, and no one can explain why to someone who was not born here. They laugh in the domes. Outside, the laugh is a sign, a quick shaking of the open hand, and it is used often.

What they believe is hard to say, because what they believe is mostly the plain. They do not think of the ring as a voice. They do not think the ground is alive, or that it is a god, or that it is answering them. They think the ground is listening. That is the closest an outsider gets to it: the plain, to them, is the great listener, and everything that lives on it—the lows rasping, the striders braying, the people striking their names into it—is speaking into that listening, all the time, and being held. The heavy thing in the water is the one animal that says nothing, and the shadow is the one thing that makes the plain go quiet, and these are the two things they will not hunt, because they are the two things that, in a world made entirely of speaking, seem to be listening back.

At the end they go into the ground and the ground has them, as it has the shells, as it has the hills. The plain rises a fingernail's width in a long life. Somewhere a child is being tied to a stone and pulling against the rope, and somewhere an old woman is lying full length on the gneiss with her ear pressed to it, learning the last of the sound, and somewhere between them a flock brays and the whole world answers, a half-second late, from everywhere at once.
