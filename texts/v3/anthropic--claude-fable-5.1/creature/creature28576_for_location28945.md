Start with what you already counted, because the room asks you to be honest and it will not let you hear yourself exaggerate.

The fish live here. Finger-length, without much colour, hanging in the outer ring of the basin where the water stops fighting itself. They face the fall. They have faced it for as many generations as it takes to lose the pigment out of a body, and they do not startle because nothing in their world has ever approached them except more water. They are the room's congregation, if you want to be sentimental, or its ballast, if you don't. The isopods live here — white, flat, dozens to a channel, moving through the glow and grazing it down, so that the light stays a film and never thickens into something that would dim itself. They are the reason you can see. The slug lives here, or one slug does, on a terrace near the top, going across the calcite at a rate that makes the deposition seem hasty. These three are what lives in the room the way a hinge lives in a door. They are not what you were asking about. You were asking about the doorways.

So try the doorways.

They are too small for a person and the tool marks are a person's thumb wide. The first thing that offers itself is a small people — narrow, quick, wet-handed, tapping the slabs true and slipping into the cells to sleep. It is a comfortable picture and it falls apart in your hand. Nothing narrow makes a thumb-wide mark. Nothing small grinds a bowl rim that even against a stone held in the fist, a stone held in a fist like yours. The hands were your size. Hold onto that and let the rest go.

The second thing that offers itself is that the cells are not for living in. You can put your head and one shoulder in. So put your head and one shoulder in.

It is not quieter. Nothing here is quieter. But the roar, which outside has no shape at all, inside the cell acquires one — the little square chamber rings at its own pitch and the fall, forced through that pitch, becomes a note. Not a musical note. A thick low grain with a centre to it, where before there was only pressure. Pull your head out and the room takes the shape away. Put it in the next cell along and the note is different, because the cell is a finger-width deeper. The one on the terrace below is different again. There are dozens of them. Whoever built this room could not escape the sound and did not try. They divided it. Each cell is a way of hearing the fall, and they built as many ways as there were stones to build them with, and they arranged them so that every one of them looked at the column of water, because a listening-hole that faces away from the thing it listens to is a mistake, and there are no mistakes in this masonry.

That is how they lived with it. Now: who.

You came through the crack sideways. Anything that lives here came through it too, or was born on this side of it, and either way is not larger than you. It eats what there is — the fish, if it is patient, the isopods, if it is not proud. It has no fire, because there is nothing to burn and nowhere for smoke to go and nothing here has ever been warmer than the rock. It does not speak; it has forgotten how, or never learned, or learned and set the skill down where the room could glaze it over. It has the same relation to the light that the fish have to colour. It moves by touch. It tends. When a channel narrows past the point where the water can clear itself, a hand goes in and lifts the lining out in flakes and lays them somewhere they will do less harm. When a cistern lip wears, a stone the size of two fists is chosen from the sorted pile and set. You thought the room had arrived at its own equilibrium. It had help. It has help. The rate of building-up and the rate of wearing-away are the same because something keeps them the same, one stone at a time, at intervals you would not stay long enough to see.

You are looking for it now and you cannot find it, and that is because you are looking for something the colour of a person. There is no such colour here. Everything the mist touches is the colour of old teeth, and the mist touches everything, and something that does not move is a wall. There is a stooped shape on the third terrace that you took for a cell whose lintel had slumped. There is a rounded mass beside the bowl that you took for a stone too large for the masonry and left unexplained. There is, near the drain, where the water leaves as quietly as such water can, a shape like an arch with a ghost of a seam in it, the seam running roughly where a spine would run, and the arch has one end in a cell mouth up to the shoulder.

It is listening. It has been listening to that one note for longer than you can guess, and the glaze on its back is thicker than the glaze on the bowl, and thinner than the glaze on the lintel next to it, which gives you a number if you want one. It may still be alive. The room does not distinguish, and after long enough, with your head in a cell and the fall coming through the stone into your skull as a single grain of sound, neither would you. Whether there is one of them or several — whether the stooped shape on the third terrace was another, and the mass by the bowl a third, all facing the water at the various depths of their own stillness — is a thing the room could tell you and won't. It is not hiding them. It simply has no way of marking the difference between a builder and a built thing once the difference has stopped mattering to either.

So this is what lives in the room under the hill: the ones who made it, at the rate the room permits, which is very slow. They are not trapped. The crack is open. They came for the same reason the cells face the water, and they stayed for the same reason the fish do not startle, and they have become what the room makes of anything that stops moving in it — a surface for the mist, glowing faintly, smooth, the corners rounding, one shoulder in a hole in the wall and the fall in their ears forever. It is not a bad life if the fall was what you wanted. The room was built by people who wanted it more than they wanted speech or weather or the colour green, and they built it well enough that it goes on keeping them.

You were told you could stay as long as you like. That was accurate. The mist lays down its next layer on the lintels and the bowl and the bone pin, and on the shape by the drain, and on you, if you stand still enough, and there is a cell at the height of your head that no one is using.
