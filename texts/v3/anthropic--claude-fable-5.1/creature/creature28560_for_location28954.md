Start with what you can see, because it's nearly everything: the plant.

Not the plants. That is the first correction you make, and you make it slowly, because it takes a while to notice that you have not seen two of anything. The stems are all the same green, the leaves all the same round, the root masses clench the same way around every bolt head. There are no flowers, so there has never been seed. Every stem in the tank was dropped from another stem, root-first, and rooted, and rose, and dropped one of its own. Follow that back and it goes to a single cutting somebody lowered through the hatch on the day the tank was filled. Everything here is that cutting. The church-sized forest is one individual. It has been alive since the water went in and it has never been anything but alive; the yellow stems you saw leaning on their neighbours are not deaths but sheddings, the way a tree sheds, except that nothing carries the shed parts away and so they hang there, in the clear, part of the body still.

It is the only resident the design admits, and it is a strange one to think of as a resident, because it does not do anything a resident does. It does not move except by growing. It does not eat except light and what comes up the penstocks. It does not sleep, or if it does, it sleeps for the twelve hours the water takes to fall a hand's breadth and wakes for the twelve it takes to rise. What it does is hold on, and clean, and climb, and press its faces to the glass. If you wanted a single word for its whole life you might say it attends. It has been attending the machinery for as long as the machinery has existed, and it has learned every slot and tray and pipe run by putting a root in it. There is nothing in the tank the plant does not know by touch. There is nothing the plant has touched that it has let go of.

---

It is not, in fact, alone. This takes longer to find, because you cannot see it and were told it wasn't there.

The roots clean the water. That is the claim of the place, and the water is clear, so the claim holds. But a root on its own does not turn grey water clear. Cup one of the root masses in your hand — they are soft, cool, dense as a wrung sponge — and you will feel that it is slick in a way the stems are not. There is a film on it. It is not on the walls, where you looked, and not on the glass, where anything would be caught; it is down in the root hair, in the dark of the bolt threads and the grating slots, exactly where the strainers cannot reach and the coils keep things warmest. It came up with the cutting. It was on the cutting's roots in whatever nursery the cutting was raised in, and nobody strained it out, because nobody thought of it as an inhabitant, and it went into the water on the first day along with the only other one.

So there are two of them, or one and a multitude, and they have been at this together the whole time. The plant holds and lifts and lights; the film in its roots does the actual eating. Between them they have taken everything the city has sent up and made it clear. Nothing else in the tank eats. Nothing else in the tank is small enough to have got in. In a place built to have no motes there is one vast one, wrapped around every root mass in the forest, and the system depends on it entirely and does not appear to have noticed.

---

And then there is the third, who is the only one who leaves.

The ladder is a service ladder. The hatch has a wheel on it that turns from either side. Somebody comes.

You do not see them, but you can read them in the tank the way you can read a hand in a garden. The outflow grate at the bottom is clear when, given how the shed stems hang and drift, it should by now be a mat. The crane rail under the dome has a hook on it, and the hook has a loop of line, and the line is not new. Certain of the yellow stems are not leaning on their neighbours but lying along a catwalk in a row, cut clean at the base, laid out where they can be gathered. Nothing in the tank cuts. Somebody has been through with a blade, and has not finished, and will be back.

They come up the cold ladder, then, with their fingers aching, and open the hatch, and there is nowhere to stand, so they go in. All the way in, to the neck, into water the temperature of themselves, and they do the work swimming. They part the stems the way you did and the stems close behind them the way they did behind you. They pull the shed threads off the outflow by hand. They lay the cut stems on the catwalk and haul them up on the crane line and lift them out through the hatch, into the thin air, where they freeze on the way down. They check the paint stripe on the shaft. They rest a palm on the coil housing and feel the hum in their teeth, and know from it whether the current is right.

They are, in the strict accounting of the place, the only contamination it permits. Their skin comes off in the water. Their breath goes into it. Whatever they carry up the ladder on their hands goes in with them. A system that will not admit a snail admits a person once in whatever interval the outflow takes to mat, and either the designers decided the roots could handle one body's worth of city, or the film in the roots decided it for them. Either way the water stays clear.

You wonder how long they stay. Longer than the work needs, you think. There is nothing in the city below that is this warm, or this quiet, or this clean. The hatch is a door that the cold closes behind you, and on the other side of it is the one place the whole lifting works exists to make, and it is the temperature of a body, and the leaves press up into the light, and a person floating there with their face in a square of it would find it hard to say why they should climb back down.

---

So: three, if you count that way. One that has never left and cannot. One that is in every root and was never invited and does the eating for the whole tank. One that comes, and cuts, and floats a while, and goes back down the ladder into the haze.

And the fit between them is the thing you keep returning to. The plant grew to the machine; the film grew to the plant; the keeper's hands, cutting only what has yellowed and only where the outflow needs it, have grown to the shape of the plant's shedding. Nobody decided this arrangement. It was designed as a filter with a plant in it and it has become a household, with a body and a gut and a visitor, and each of the three has learned the other two by touch, and none of them is in a hurry, and none of them has anywhere else it would rather be.

Below you, in the warm and the clear, a root that has never once let go of anything is finding the next slot.
