Start with the eyes, because the eyes are what the light has done.

They are narrow to the point of being closed. Not squinted the way you squint stepping out of a doorway, a thing the face does and then undoes, but set — the lids thickened and heavy, the opening between them a horizontal line that admits the glare a little at a time, the lashes pale and dense and by midday furred white with salt that has dried out of the sweat at the lid's edge. The whites, when you get close enough to see them, are pink. The irises are the no-color of thin brine. Children are born here with their eyes wide and dark like anyone's, and within the first season they have learned the line, and within a few years the flesh around it has taken the shape for good, so that every adult face has the same expression, which is the expression of someone about to disagree with you, or of someone looking a very long way off at something very small. It is the only expression. It cannot be adjusted. A face here tells you almost nothing, and after a while you stop looking at faces and start, as they do, looking at hands.

---

The hands are the place to read them.

They are large in proportion to the rest, and the skin on them has gone the texture of the floor: whitened, crazed, every crease packed with salt that will not wash out because there is nothing to wash it out with. The nails are worn down flat and blunt from the block-work. On the pouring hand — whichever one a person favors — there is a hard shiny callus in the fork of the thumb where the neck of the hide bag rests, and the fingers of that hand hold themselves in a permanent slight curl even at rest, ready for the bag. On the other hand the callus is across the palm, from carrying. You can tell a cutter from a carrier from a pourer by which hand is which, and you can tell a floor-reader because a floor-reader's hands are not calloused at all, or not in those ways; a floor-reader's hands are dry and open and light, and they touch the floor the way you would touch someone's forehead to check for a fever.

The hands are also where the talk is.

There is a spoken language. It is used within arm's reach and almost never beyond it, because beyond arm's reach the air eats it. It is a close, unvoiced, breathing sort of speech, short words made mostly of the tongue and the teeth, and to hear it you must be near enough to feel it, which is how it is used: two people bent over a joint, one pouring, one steadying the block, saying to each other whatever there is to say about the pour. Anything meant to travel is done from the shoulder. The gesture-language is big and slow and unmistakable, built to read at a hundred paces through glare without shadows, and it has, as far as you can make out, no small talk in it. It has *swelling*, which is a hand rising flat and slow from the hip. It has *split*, two hands drawn apart. It has *wet it*, *leave it*, *come*, *bring*, *not there*. It has the circle. It has a gesture for *the herd is coming through*, which is a ripple of the fingers held low, and which is made without urgency, because a herd coming through is not an event.

It does not seem to have *mine*.

---

They live on what the light grows.

At the edges of the brine pans where the ceiling is thinnest and the white glare is strongest, the films come up — the pink and the orange scums, a finger's depth of something between slime and felt, warm to the touch, with a taste of iron and old egg and salt, always salt. The people go to the pans in the brightest part of the day with flat bone scrapers and take the films up in sheets and lay them to dry on the crowns of the hemispheres, where they go leathery and darker and can be rolled and carried. This is the color of their food: the only color in the place besides the amber of the roof, and they wear it. Not as ornament. The stain gets into the hands and the mouth and does not come out, so that every person here has orange at the fingertips and lips, and the children, who eat with their whole faces, are orange to the cheekbones.

The rest is what turns up. The finger-long eyeless fish, when the quarry breaks into a void and they come flapping into the light — the crew nearest goes down on hands and knees and gathers them before the herd arrives, and eats them raw and whole, there, in the pit. The thumb-length things that come out of the joints to drink the poured brine, which are picked off a wall by the handful and swallowed like seeds. And at the seam, where the ceiling comes down to meet the floor and the light goes from white to amber to a deep cool honey, the gray fringed colonies that hang from the gypsum can be reached, and are pulled down in wet dripping armfuls and eaten, and they are the only thing here that tastes of anything other than salt. They taste of the water they drip. The children are sent for them. It is, you gather, the closest thing here to a treat.

Fresh water is the thing they cannot make and cannot build for.

The ceiling sweats it — condensation, pure, before it ever touches the floor — but any drop that reaches the salt is brine in a breath, and there is no vessel here that will hold fresh water without dissolving from the inside. So every family, or crew, or whatever the unit is, owns a single hide, and the hide is stretched on bone pegs in the amber stretches under the thickest ceiling where the drip is heaviest, angled so the drops run to a lip and fall into a second hide sewn into a bag. And the bag is carried. It is the one possession that is treated as a possession: it hangs on a peg driven into the inside wall of whatever hemisphere they are sleeping in that season, and when the hemisphere fails it is the first thing taken out, before the blocks, before anything. It sits against the body. You can tell how a season has gone by how the bags hang.

---

You realize, after some days, that you have been giving yourself directions by the people.

There is nothing else to do it with. The humps move. The pans fill and drain and reform. The fold you crossed this morning will not be there tomorrow. But the crew that is cutting to the left of where the light goes gold will still be a crew, and will still be there or thereabouts, and if not there then a few dozen paces along in the direction of the floor's drift, and when you ask — when you gesture — where something is, the answer you get is not a place but a person. *Past the old one who reads the floor. Beyond the pair with the child.* The people are the only things in the cavity that last longer than a season. A body here holds its shape for forty or fifty years. Nothing else, not a wall, not a hollow, not the ground itself, holds its shape for one.

So they are the landmarks. They are, without there being any word for it, the fixed points — the only masonry in the city that does not dissolve at the base. And they seem to know it in the way they carry themselves: not proudly, there is no room here for that, but with a certain steadiness in the standing, feet wide and low on the shifting sugar, weight even, the stance of something that has been set down and intends to stay set. You see it in the children as soon as they can walk. They do not toddle. They plant.

Names go with this. A name here is the one thing given to a person that is not expected to move. It is a breath-word, short, said close, and it stays for life, and when a person is not there the name is a gesture made toward wherever they were last seen, which is the nearest thing this place has to an address.

---

The floor-readers are the old ones.

Not all the old ones read the floor, but all the readers are old, because it takes that long. What they do is walk. A reader goes out alone in the first white of the morning with a bundle of bone pegs and walks a slow line across the open salt between one cluster and the next, and every so often stops and kneels — the light dry hands go flat to the surface — and stays there. What they are feeling for you can only guess. The warmth, perhaps; salt that is being squeezed is a little warmer than salt that is not. The grain of the crust, whether it crumbles toward or away. The way the brine in a shallow lens sits, level or listing, and if listing then toward what. Then a peg goes in, and the reader stands and walks on and puts in another, so that by midday there is a straight line of pegs across a stretch of floor, and by the next midday there is a line that is no longer straight, and by the third day the bend in it has a direction and a rate, and the reader stands at the end of it and makes a shape with two hands that the crews can read from a long way off, and the crews begin — without hurrying, nothing here hurries — to take the nearest hemisphere down.

No one argues with a reader. There is nothing to argue with. The pegs have leaned.

When a reader dies, the pegs are pulled up and given out. There is no other inheritance.

---

They put the dead into the floor.

Not buried — there is no digging a grave in something that will not hold a hole — but laid. A reader is asked, and the reader walks, and finds a place where the floor is closing: a fold that is rising on both sides, a shallow trough that will be a low shoulder by the end of the season and gone by the end of the next. The body is carried there on a hide by the crew it worked with. It is wetted — brine poured over it the way brine is poured over a joint — and left, and the herd is kept off it for the first day and night by people standing round, and after that the salt has crusted it enough that the herd is not interested, and after that the floor takes it. Slowly. It goes under the way a stone goes under sand in a tide. Within a season the trough has closed over and there is only a place where the salt is a little grayer, and then not even that.

Somewhere down in the halite, then, creeping with it, at whatever pace the floor creeps, are all of the dead. All of them. However long this has been going on. They are moving in the same direction the city is moving, if the city is moving, and a little below it, in the dark under the white.

And the quarrying goes down.

Every so often a crew cutting blocks — grooving, levering — lifts a slab and there is a shape in it. A curve of something browner than the salt, cased in the crystal like a fly in amber, sugared and dry and very old, or not old, there is no telling. The crew stops. There is no gesture for this; nothing is said at all. The slab is set back into its bed. Brine is poured along the cut. The cut seals. The crew moves forty paces and begins again, and the floor carries the slab wherever it was going, and that is the whole of the rite. You never see one refused a second time. It is understood that you do not ask the floor for the same block twice.

---

Of the outside they know what the light tells them.

They know there is a sun, because the glare moves and because they have watched the sliding bands from the healed fractures cross the floor their whole lives. They know the sun has a season, because the light comes in at a different angle at different times and the gold of the evening lasts longer and shorter. They know there is a plain above the ceiling; they have a breath-word for it that means, as near as you can get, *the other floor*, and if you press — if you gesture upward and make the questioning shape — the answer is a look at the roof, the line of the eyes not changing, and then the same gesture made downward, at the salt, and then the shrug. The other floor is where the light lives. It is the one made thing here that they did not make and cannot move, and they do not build toward it, and they do not touch it except at the seam, and the children are told not to strike it. It is not worshipped. It is left alone in the way you would leave alone the one wall in a house that is holding the rest up.

The flint comes from the seam.

Not from any one point of it. Certain people — not many, a few in any cluster, the ones whose hands are calloused in no pattern you have learned — walk the seam. They go where the ceiling comes down to meet the floor and they follow that line, in one direction or the other, for a long time. A season. More. They take a fresh-water hide and films rolled in a bundle and they walk in the amber light along the place where the city can go no further, and they come back with flint. Nodules of it, black and gray and cold, heavier than anything here, wrapped in hide against the salt. They give it out. They do not say much about where they went. When asked, the gesture is along the seam, into the far distance where the amber goes to brown, and then a pause, and then a second gesture that you do not have — a flat hand turned, once, as if showing you both sides of something. You think it means *there is a place where it opens*. You think it might mean *there is a shadow that does not move*. The ones who have come back from the seam have a way of standing in the evening when the shadows arrive that the others do not: they turn and look at their own, for a moment, as if checking it is pointing the right way.

---

The animals are the other people here, and they are treated as such, which is to say they are not treated at all.

The herd-things — long, plated, many-legged, the size of a dog, blind — move through the city in their hundreds at every hour of the light, and the people move through them. That is the whole relationship. A herd crossing a work site parts around the cutters and the cutters do not look up; a cutter walking across the open floor walks into a herd and it parts around him and he does not look down. They are not hunted, because there is no need and because a herd will take a fist's depth off a wall in a night and no one wants to be remembered by the herd. They are not tame, because there is nothing to tame them with. They are weather. You gesture that the herd is coming through the way you would gesture that the light is going gold: a fact about the day, low, with the fingers.

Watch them long enough and they resolve. The plates on their backs are not the salt-color from any camouflage; they are the salt. A herd-thing that lies in a brine pan long enough grows a crust, and the crust thickens, and the animal carries it, and when it dies the crust is what is left, a low pale hump like a very small hemisphere, which the herd eats out from within and the floor takes. Their mouths are underneath, a slot between the first legs, and they feed by moving — they do not stop to eat, they eat by passing over, the whole herd taking up the pan-edge film in a single fluent pass that leaves the salt behind them scraped clean and faintly warm. They lick the walls the same way, in passing, in a night, and are gone by the white of the morning.

The ones in the joints — the translucent, thumb-length, thousand-haired ones — are the builders' only instrument. They are attracted to the freshly wetted seam, and a pourer will finish a joint and stand back and wait for them, and if they come, a pale fringe seeping out of the wall and gathering along the line of the pour like frost along a crack, the joint is wet enough. If they do not come the pourer pours again. Nobody names them. They are eaten off the wall afterward, when the joint has taken, by whoever is hungry.

And the gray colonies on the ceiling drip, and the eyeless fish wait in the voids, and none of it has ever made a sound, and the people move among all of it as one more slow pale thing that eats the films and lives in the light.

---

Inside a hemisphere, when the light has gone.

Low. You cannot stand. The walls glow, dully, the way a stone warmed all day gives its heat back, and the glow is the last of the daylight caught in the halite and letting itself out; it is orange for a while, then the color of weak tea, then nothing. The air is warmer by a degree and thick with the sweat of the walls, and everything drips a little, and every drip that hits the floor ticks once as it dissolves. There is a hide on the floor and people on the hide, close, closer than people elsewhere sleep, because the breath-language only works close and this is when it is used. What is said is short. Where the pegs were leaning. Who is cutting tomorrow. Where the fresh-water hide is hanging — on its peg in the wall, and the peg has sunk a finger's depth into the salt since the season started, and someone reaches up in the dark and pulls it out and drives it in again higher, and the bag swings, and settles.

The children sleep in the middle. They lie the way they stand, wide and planted, small landmarks. In the last of the wall-glow you can see the orange at their mouths.

Outside the floor is moving. It does not stop because the light stopped. Somewhere a block settles with a tick; somewhere a herd is at a neglected wall, licking; somewhere along the seam someone with a hide bag against their body is walking toward a place where it opens, or does not. Inside the hemisphere the glow goes out of the salt entirely and for a few hours there is dark, the only dark these people ever know, and they sleep in it as if it were a thing they had built.

Then the roof lights. The line of the eyes takes the glare. The hands find the bag, the scraper, the pegs. And they go out onto the floor to see where it has carried them.
