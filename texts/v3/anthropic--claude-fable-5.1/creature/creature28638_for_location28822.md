The first answer is the herds, and the first answer is wrong in the way that "clouds" is a wrong answer to what lives in the sky. The herds are here the way the fog is here. They are the weather. They make the sky and the sky falls back on them and they make it again, and something that is its own climate is not living in a place so much as being one. Ask a beast in the fourth line what it is standing on and the honest reply would be: the rest of me.

Look smaller, then. The scuttlers on the spines have appetites, which is a start. They want condensation, joint-tissue, scrap; they run toward a falter and away from a spark; they die in a flash and a smell. Wanting and dying are what residents do. But watch one long enough and it never leaves the back it is on, or it leaves for another back that is the same back, and it has no more edges than the thing it feeds on. When two of them cross a current and there is a flash and one fewer, it is not clear the number was ever two. They are a fever the herd runs. They are the herd's own skin, itching.

The ones underneath are nearer to it. They have somewhere else — down — and they return there, and a thing with somewhere else to go is a neighbour, not a tenant. They come up through the film like a harbour giving something back, they close on what has fallen, and they take it below into whatever the silt is when it is loose enough to swim. Nothing has dug far enough to say. They live *under* here. The distinction holds, which is itself unusual on this plain, and it is worth keeping.

The mat is closer still. It lives on the knuckle-stones and it stays, and staying is most of what living in a place means. Slick, coppery, warmer than the rock it grows on, too thin to see except as a change in how the light beads. It does not knock or breathe or flash. Trace it with a finger and it goes over the edge of the stone and down under the film and does not stop at the silt — it goes in, and a hand pressed flat on the plain a hundred paces from any stone feels, after a while, a faint warmth in the grey that is not the herd-heat coming down but something coming up. It is possible that every stone on the plain wears a piece of the same mat. It is possible the stones are where it surfaces. It is the only thing here that has never once been in a hurry, and if the plain is settling toward anything it is settling toward this: a warmth with no engine, spread everywhere, doing nothing.

But the mat does not know it lives here. There is no one home in it. It is the floor of the room, not the occupant.

So look between.

There is very little room between the herds. The bodies stand shoulder to shoulder and the film shows through at their feet in slivers. But a plain of ten thousand lines has seams, places where one line's rhythm meets another's out of phase and neither will yield, and the seam stays open a body's width and runs a long way. Something moves in the seams.

It is taken at first for a scuttler that has come down off the backs, because it is small against the herd and it is grey and it is going somewhere. Then it is nearer and it is too slow and too tall, and it is taken for one of the fallen — a beast that went down and was somehow not finished, still upright on the legs that did not buckle. That is closer. That is nearly it.

It is one of the herd. It has the plates, dark blue-grey, the columnar legs with too many joints, the low blunt head that is mostly mouth. It has the row of vents along the back. But the plates do not slide when it moves; they have seized, or been let seize, and they grind faintly instead of hinging, and there is no oiled look to them because it is not wet in the way the others are wet. It is not steaming. That is the strangest thing about it, and it takes a while to see because the eye does not know how to look for an absence of plume in a world made of plumes. It stands in its own small pocket of clear cold air. The fog thins around it for a few paces in every direction. It is cold. It is the only cold body on the plain.

It stopped. That is what it is. Somewhere back — there is no way of saying how far back — its rhythm dropped out, the local stutter in the knocking that calls the scuttlers down, and the scuttlers came, and it did not fall. It stepped sideways. Out of its line, into the seam, and the line closed over the gap behind it within the hour and the material moved on through the animal that had been behind and was now simply next, and the mill did not notice. The scuttlers left it alone. They key to a falter in the rhythm, and it was no longer making a rhythm to falter. The ones underneath left it alone, because it had not fallen. It had only quit. There is no scavenger here for a thing that has only quit.

It does not work the ground. Nothing passes through it. Its hind vents are dry and closed and have not dropped anything into the film in longer than the film would remember. It does not eat, as far as can be seen, unless it drinks; sometimes it lowers the blunt head to the film and stays there and the film goes still around its mouth, and the water it takes is the cold water, not the condensation, not what runs hot off the herd. It walks. Not far, not fast, along the seams, with the grind of its unhinged plates too quiet to hear from more than a body's length away. It is the only thing on the plain that walks, and walking is not what the plain is for.

And because it stepped out of the pattern, the pattern cannot fold it. It has edges. It is in one place. Walk toward it and it grows. Walk around it and there is a back to it, a single back, and no second copy of the cracked plate on its flank three lines to the south, because it does not have a flank three lines to the south. Around it, for a few paces, the plain agrees with itself: distance holds, the film mirrors a leg-count that matches, the knock arrives after the foreleg falls and not before. It carries a little pocket of ordinary time with it the way it carries a pocket of clear air, and it is very likely that when the fog opens and the plain runs out for a hundred miles under it, the opening began near one of these, and spread, and will close when it moves on.

One of these. Because there are more than one, and they are not the same one. That is the whole point of them.

They are not many. On a plain that holds ten thousand in the eye at once there may be a dozen, walking the seams, and each is alone in a way that nothing else here is alone. But they find each other. Not by calling; they do not call. The horn-note that goes on for a minute and arrives from everywhere is a herd-sound, a folded sound, and these have nothing to do with it. They find each other by the quiet. In a noise like a city under a bridge, a moving pocket of no-sound is as loud as a shout, and a second walker hears the first the way you hear a gap in a wall, and turns toward it, and they come along their two seams to where the seams cross and stand there. Not touching. A body-length apart, always. Two cold pockets in the steam, a little clear air between them. Then one goes on, or both do, in different directions, and the fog closes behind them.

What they are doing, when they walk, is listening. This is the closest it comes to a purpose. A walker moving along a seam will stop beside a beast in the line — one with a catch in its hiss, a plate that has begun to seize, a knock that has begun to come a fraction late — and stand there in its cold pocket for what might be a day, for what the slackening in the rhythm might call a night, and the beast goes on working and does not turn its head. Mostly nothing happens. Mostly the walker moves on and the beast is taken by the scuttlers a little later, or is not, and the mill runs. But sometimes, with the cold thing standing beside it, the knock drops out and stays out, and the plates stop sliding and start to grind, and the steam thins off its back, and it steps sideways into the seam, and there are two pockets of clear air where there was one. The line closes. Nothing is built, nothing is lost. There is one fewer in the herd and one more in whatever the walkers are, and the difference does not register anywhere on a plain this size, and yet it is the only thing on the plain that could be called an event.

They are not becoming the mat. That would be the tidy thing — cold, still, spreading — and it is tempting, and it is wrong. The mat is warm. The walkers are cold and stay cold, and where one has stood a long time the coppery growth has not come; there is a bare ring on the silt beneath the film, the only place on the plain the mat does not go. They are not what the plain is settling toward. They are the thing in the plain that has declined to settle. A world ground down to the one thing that it does, and a handful of bodies inside it that have stopped doing it and have not died of that and have not been forgiven for it either — simply not noticed, walking the seams, carrying clear air, listening for a catch in someone's breath.

That is who lives here. Not the mill, not the fever on its back, not the floor. The ones who stepped out of the line and found there was nowhere to go, and went anyway, along the gaps, in the cold, one at a time.
