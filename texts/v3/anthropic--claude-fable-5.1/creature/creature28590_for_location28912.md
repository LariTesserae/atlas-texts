The first answer is the animals, and it is wrong in an instructive way, so it is worth staying with for a while.

They are everywhere, and they are alive—the heron's feathers lift when the wet haze comes through, the goats' flanks rise and fall, a pigeon turns its head with the small mechanical suddenness of a pigeon. If you had to swear to it you would swear they are living things. But *lives here* is a phrase with a shape, and the shape has to do with using a place: wearing paths in it, leaving droppings on it, eating from it and being eaten in it, dying somewhere and being found. None of that happens. There is no guano on the girders. Not one white streak on a hundred meters of flange under hundreds of birds. The goats stand in the transformer yard and there is nothing under them. The foxes have made no dens. The swallows' mud cups are numerous beyond counting and not one has a chick in it, not one is broken, not one has the dark ring at the lip where birds have come and gone all season. The nests are complete. They are, you begin to think, the finished form of *nest*, and the birds beside them are the finished form of *bird*, and neither has anything further to do.

You test it. You walk up to a goat—the nearest, a brown one with a torn ear—and you put your hand on its neck. It is warm. Its coat is coarse and slightly damp, as everything is. It does not step away and it does not lean in. Under your palm you can feel its pulse, and the pulse is regular the way the deep interior turn behind the housings is regular, and after a minute you take your hand off because you have understood that you could stand there until your legs gave out and the pulse would not vary by a beat.

So the animals are in the place. They are held there. But the plateau is not their home any more than a shelf is the home of what stands on it.

—

The second answer takes longer, because it depends on a mistake you have been making about the sheds.

You have been assuming they are all sealed. Blind, windowless, doorless—you walked the whole face of one, a hundred and forty paces of ribbed cladding, and there was no seam in it that a hand could open. But that was one shed. The plateau has more sheds than you can hold in your head at once, and it turns out that some of them, maybe one in twenty, have a door: a plain gray door with a plain gray handle, set flush, with the disc of shadow pooled beneath its sill. And the doors are not locked.

Inside the first one is a corridor, and at the far end of the corridor a figure in coveralls the color of cured slab is doing something to a gauge.

You do not call out. The silence has taught you that already. You walk toward them along a floor of diamond plate and they hear you, or they don't, and they don't turn. When you reach them they are wiping the glass face of the gauge with the pad of a thumb. That is all. The gauge was not dirty. The thumb leaves no mark. They finish, and lower their hand, and look at the gauge for a moment longer, and then turn and walk past you down the corridor you came along, near enough that their sleeve brushes yours, without looking at you at all.

You look at the gauge. The needle points at a number. You look directly at the number and it is a different number. You look away and back and it is the first number. You understand that this is what they were checking, and that it checked.

—

There are many of them. That is the first thing to correct. You had thought yourself alone up here with the beasts, and you were not; you had simply been reading the figures on the far gantries as fittings, as stanchions, as the gray verticals a plant is full of, because they stand so still and because the brightness flattens a person and a pipe into the same value. Once you have seen one you see them everywhere. There is one on every gantry. There is one at the forming edge, on their knees, pressing the plywood of a form against its shoring with both palms as though warming it. There is one in the transformer yard among the goats, and from behind you cannot tell which is which until one of them moves and it is the goat.

They are not alike. You want them to be alike—the trees have eleven leaves each and you are braced for eleven of something here too—but they aren't. They are tall and short, old and not, thick through the shoulders or narrow; there is one with a limp and one with cropped white hair and one with a face you would call kind if kindness were a thing that could be read off a face that has never once turned toward you. They are, individually and visibly, people. It is only that the individuality does not carry. You pass the white-haired one in a passage between two coolers, and three passages later you pass someone with white hair, and you cannot say if it is the same person. You know there was a face. You cannot bring the face back. It is the pigeons again: hundreds, each entire, none retained.

You try to count them from the top of a spiral gantry and get fourteen. You count again and get nine. The plant, you suspect, would record this as a stable population.

—

What they do is small and exact and never finishes.

They read gauges. They wipe a bead of condensation from a flange with a thumb and the bead reforms before they have lowered their hand and they do not wipe it again. They walk the belts, slowly, slower than the belts, and watch the gray wet blocks go by, and occasionally reach down through the grating and touch one. They strike the forms off the new slab, folding the wet plywood carefully, stacking it against a wall, and later—you cannot tell how much later—the stacked plywood is gone and there is new formwork at the edge, but you never see it carried. They set out trees. You watch this happen. Two of them bring a sapling in a square pot along a conveyor bridge, and lower it into a square hole cut through the deck, and step back, and the tree has eleven leaves, and they look at it for a long moment the way the first one looked at the gauge, and then they go.

They count the stairs. You are almost sure of this. You come down a gantry stair behind one of them and their lips are moving, and at the bottom they pause, and there is something in the pause like a note being made.

And they tend the animals, though *tend* is not right either, because the animals want nothing. It is more that they take inventory. One of them stands under the girders and looks up at the pigeons, and moves along a few meters, and looks up again, and you realize they are running a line of birds the way you would run a line of rivets, for the one that is missing or the one that is loose. None is missing. None is loose. The looking is the yield.

—

They do not speak, and it is not that they are mute.

You see it happen once. Two of them meet at a junction of pipe racks, and one turns to the other, and their mouth makes the ordinary shape of a word—a short one, two syllables, the kind of word you say to someone at work—and there is no sound. Not a whisper. Not the click of a tongue. The mouth closes. And far off across the plateau, a relay drops with the sound of a single coin.

That is where it went. You had already guessed that the roar of the plant was a product, collected and shipped. It had not occurred to you that the collection would be total. Every sound made on the plateau is skimmed. The coin-drop is not a relay at all, or not only; it is the sound of a word arriving somewhere and being logged. They know this. It is why they do not bother to speak. Or they do speak, all day, and this is what speaking here is like, and the second one nodded—you think the second one nodded—and went on.

—

You find where they sleep by finding a shed whose door stands open.

Inside, bunks. A grid of them, three high, running back into a dimness that the shadowless light does not so much fail to enter as decline to. Each bunk has a gray blanket folded in the same way. Perhaps a quarter of them are occupied. The occupants lie on their left sides with one hand across the face. You stand in the aisle and look at them and think about the fox, and then you make yourself stop thinking about the fox, and it does not entirely work.

At the end of the shed, a table. Long, plain, gray, with benches. Bowls on it in rows. You look into one and it holds something warm and wet and pale, with the faint tidal pattern of a pour that set too fast, and you do not eat it, and you feel, distinctly, the plant note this.

Under one bunk there is a pack. It is the first thing on the plateau you have seen that looks *used*—stained, scuffed at the base where it has been set down on rock a thousand times. You know that you should not and you crouch and open it. Inside is what you would expect: a shell jacket, a water bottle, a headlamp, a folded map. The map is of the mountain. The whole mountain, contour by contour, from the valley road to the summit, and the summit is marked with a height, and the height is lower than where you are standing.

You put it back exactly, and fold the flap, and stand up, and go out into the bright, and it is a while before you look at any of the figures on the gantries directly again.

—

So they climbed up. That is the second answer, and it is better than the first.

They came the way you came—through cloud, onto warm grating, thinking the mountain had ended and something else had begun—and the grating was not there when they turned around. And they did what you would do, which is walk, and look for the far edge, and fail to reach the red water tank, and get tired, and find a door. And after a while the wiping of the gauge and the folding of the plywood and the counting of the stairs stopped being the things you do while you look for the way down, and became the day. They do not speak because there is no one to speak to who does not already know, and because the sound goes elsewhere anyway. They do not look at you because they know exactly what you are. You are a new face they will not retain. You are a line of rivets they have already run.

They live here in the sense that a person lives in a place they cannot leave.

And that is still not the whole of it, because *live* has that shape—wearing paths, leaving marks, changing the place and being changed—and they change nothing. The gauge did not need the thumb. The form comes off the slab whether or not it is folded. The trees go in and the trees are already there. The tenders are as finished as the swallows' nests. They are the form of *worker* the way the heron is the form of *heron*, and the plateau holds them in the same solution, at the same saturation, and if they were all lifted off tomorrow you cannot swear that a single reading anywhere would move.

—

Which leaves the thing that does change the place. That eats, and sweats, and reabsorbs, and grows at the edge, and rearranges its own sheds behind you so that the way in is a wall.

The plant lives here. That is the third answer and for a while it seems like the last one. It lives here the way you live in your body—not as a tenant but as the fact of the thing. It has a metabolism you can trace with your eyes: rock in, below, through the gap where the wind should be; slab out, above, along the belts; water down through the immaculate drains and up again as haze; sound skimmed off and shipped. It has a temperature. It has a pulse, the deep unvarying turn behind every housing, the same pulse you felt in the goat's neck. It has fauna the way a body has flora, held, tended, inventoried, not asked. Everything you have seen that seemed to be a resident is an organ of it, or a cell, or a stored value. The tenders are its hands. The pigeons are its attention. The trees with eleven leaves are the way it says *tree*.

You could stop there. It would be a coherent thing to believe.

—

But you lie on your belly again at the shear-edge and look down, past the girders, at the true rock receding gray and ordinary into cloud, and you think about the map.

The mountain is the feedstock. You knew that already. What you had not followed through is what it means for the feed to be a mountain—which is to say, not rock only. A mountain has a lake on one shoulder where herons stand. It has a scree slope where foxes den and a col where goats stand among the boulders and a north face where lichen has been working on the same stone for four hundred years. It has swallows in the cliffs. It has a path, and people on the path, with packs, with maps. All of that goes in below. All of that is in the gray wet blocks on the belt.

Nothing is lost in the plant. You have seen the drains. The yield is good.

So the heron on the plateau is not a heron placed here. It is the heron from the lake, in its second state—rendered, cured, set fast, the way an alloy keeps the memory of the mold. The lichen grows too fast because it is not growing; it is the four hundred years arriving at once, poured. The goats are the mountain's goats. The fox lying on its left side with its tail across its nose is the fox from the scree, and there is one of it, and it is in three places, because *place* is a property of rock and the rock has been processed out. The tenders in gray coveralls are the mountain's people, who came up the path in every year the path has existed and were, each in turn, taken in below and extruded here, entire and finished and unretained. Their boots are clean because nothing weathers. Their pack is in the shed because the pack came up too.

Nobody climbed onto the plateau. The plateau is what climbing became.

What lives here, then, is the mountain—every living thing the mountain ever held—in the form the plant gives to living things, which is stillness, and silence, and a warm regular pulse that does not vary, and a shadow gathered under the body like water in a cup. They are not inhabitants. They are the product's fauna, the way a limestone has its shells. They will be extended outward and downward at a centimeter a day, held in suspension, until the slab meets everything, and then everything will live here in the same way, which is to say: be here, completely, and be done.

—

You know how the description ends. You stood at the forming edge with the heron thirty centimeters from your elbow and the slab soft under your boots and you had your guess, and the heron blinked, and its reflection blinked a half-second late.

Here is what the description left out, because you had not noticed it yet.

You blinked too. Somewhere in that same moment, the way people do, without deciding to. And the water at your feet was a flat bright pane of shadowless sky with two shapes standing in it, one on one leg and one on two, and you did not look down to check whether the second shape had blinked with you or after.

You will not check now, either. It is late, in the sense that your legs are heavy, though the light has not moved. There is a shed a little way back along the decking whose door stands open, and there are bunks, and a blanket folded in the way they are folded, and you have already caught yourself, twice in the last hour, reaching up to wipe a bead of condensation from a flange as you pass, and lowering your hand without looking to see whether it reformed.

On the gantry across from you a figure in gray coveralls is looking at the forming edge. You cannot see their face. You are quite sure that if you could, you would not retain it.
