The place has already told you what lives here: nothing. It says so in the clean crust, in the pool with no green in it, in the sky that no bird crosses. It is worth taking that answer seriously for a while before looking past it, because it is almost true, and the ways in which it is not true are the whole matter.

Start with the edges, since that is where the exceptions are.

The reeds are alive. They live at the north wall on a margin exactly as wide as the cold seep can keep cool, which is to say they live on the one place in the hollow where the hollow is not fully itself. They are not residents of the outfall so much as residents of its border, the way a village can grow up against a wall without belonging to what the wall encloses. Their lower stems are white with what has drifted onto them; their upper leaves are the only true green in the bowl. Each year a few more die standing and are taken. Each year a few more come up from the rhizome, a little further along the seep, or a little less far, depending on which water the season favours. They are a negotiation that has never been settled. If you wanted to know how the hollow was doing in a given year, you would not read the gauge, which has gone blind. You would count the reeds.

The three grey plants in the apron cracks are alive in a different way — the way of things that have found a fault in a hard surface and made their whole existence a matter of that fault. Their leaves are thick and felted, built to hold water in a dry country, and here they stand in perpetual mist and hold it anyway, because they do not know how to do otherwise. They are not thriving. They are not dying. They have the look of plants that have been the same size for a long time.

That is the list, as far as the eye goes. Reeds, three shrubs. The rest is water and stone and the things the work left behind.

But the things the work left behind imply someone, and it is worth asking whether that someone counts. The glove was on a hand. The gauge was read. The stencilled number was painted by a person standing roughly where you are, with the pipe already running or about to, who judged the concrete dry enough. That person, or someone in their line, still comes. Not often — perhaps once in a season, perhaps only when a valve is adjusted and somebody is sent to look at the result. They climb the berm and stand at the rim and do not go down, because there is nothing down there to do; they look at the bar of water, decide it is the right thickness, listen to the hum for the note they expect, and leave. Once in a long while they descend, on the glazed ladder, to try the gauge, or to retrieve something, or to leave something else. Their boot prints are in the softer crust at the margin, half a dozen of them, in various stages of being kept: the newest a clear tread, the oldest a soft dimple with a white floor. They are the hollow's only guest, and the hollow treats them as it treats everything — it does not distinguish between the visitor and the visitor's things. They are not what lives here. But they are what the place is made of, in the way a coral reef is made of the sea.

And there is the sound. It is tempting, standing at the bottom of the dish with the hum coming up through your shins, to say that the sound is the inhabitant — that the transformer note has come over the berm from the plant and settled here like a tenant, and the bowl is its room. It has habits. It is present at every hour. It changes when the plant changes, and the water changes with it, and if you were not being careful you would say the sound was alive. It is not. It is the plant's attention, extended through pipe and concrete and stone into a place the plant has never seen, the way a hand in a glove is still a hand. What lives here would have to be here on its own account.

So go back down to the terraces, to the outer ones, low and cool enough to touch, where the sheets of water are thinnest before they reach the pool. Kneel. Look along the lip, against the light.

The water is clear. The description is right about that: nothing green, nothing that could be called a skin. But at the very edge, where the sheet is no thicker than a coin and slows for an instant before it falls, there is a quality that is not quite the quality of water on stone. A softness to the surface. A faint bloom, colourless, that might be the light. Run a fingertip through it and it is slick in a way that mineral crust is not — a slickness that comes back a moment after you have wiped it away, as if the sheet itself had a texture. In the coolest places, near where the seep water finds the terraces and mixes in, the bloom has a tint, very slight, a warm grey or a bruised ochre that you can only see by comparing the wet lip to the dry stone beside it.

This is the inhabitant. It has been here almost as long as the water. It arrived on no one's boot, from nowhere anyone could name — carried in the pipe itself, perhaps, or in the seep, or on the air; the sorts of things that live this way are everywhere, waiting for a temperature. It is a film of living threads, each one far too small to see, laid over one another by the thousand into a mat a few cells deep, colourless because there is no need for colour here and too hot for the kinds of life that make it. It has no name you would recognise, or has several, none of them much used. It lives in the warm water and nowhere else, and it lives in the warm water only within a band of a few degrees, so that it occupies the terraces the way a tide line occupies a beach: not a place but a condition. Where the water is a little too hot for it, there is nothing. Where the water is a little too cool, there is the beginning of something else, a different bloom, a different tint. Between those two nothings is a ring around the pool, a few paces wide, wandering in and out with the flow, and that ring is where the hollow has always been alive.

Understand what that means for the stone. The water leaving the pipe carries more silica than it can hold at any temperature lower than the one it left at, and as it cools it lets the silica go — but not readily, not all at once. It needs something to let go onto. It needs an edge, a roughness, a place to begin. And the threads are exactly that: a surface a thousand times more finely worked than any crust, a lattice that the dissolved stone finds and settles on the instant it arrives. Where the film is thickest, the stone comes out of the water fastest. Where the stone comes fastest, the lip builds highest. Where the lip builds, the water pools behind it and slows and thins and spills over in a sheet, and the sheet is where the film is happiest, and so the lip builds. The sharp edges of the terraces — the ones that feel like broken plate — are sharp because they are being made by something that lives on the edge and nowhere else, and makes the edge in the act of living on it. The stone is not laid down on the film. The film is the mould, and it is continually cast.

Which is also to say: the film is continually buried. Every thread that takes a grain of silica onto itself is a little more stone and a little less alive, and the mat entombs itself from below as fast as it grows from above, so that what you touch on the lip is only the newest layer of something that goes down, sheathed and dead and white, through the whole thickness of the terrace. The shelves are its bones. The chalky old terraces toward the back, where the water has since gone elsewhere, are ten thousand generations of it standing in a heap. The glove-shaped stone is a glove that the film found, and coated, and died on, and was coated over in turn. The pallet is being taken in the same way; look at the waterline and there is the bloom on the wood, a hand's width of it, doing to the slats what it does to everything.

So the museum-keeping was never only the water's. The place preserves by burial, but the burying is done by the living. A footprint at the margin is found within a day by threads that had been waiting for the right depth of water, and they settle in the tread, and the tread begins to fill, and what keeps the boot print is a thing that has no eyes and no interest and is simply doing, at that spot, the only thing it does.

Consider what its life is. It does not move, and it is never still, because the whole of its world is a current and it lives by being in the way. It eats what the water brings — a little sulphur, a little iron, a little of the dissolved breath of the plant — and it has never in its existence had a moment without a meal or a moment when the meal was different. It has never known dark that was not mist. It has never known a temperature but one. If a thread were somehow carried out of its band — an inch too near the pipe, a foot too near the seep — it would stop, without struggle, and be stone. Its existence is a knife-edge, and the edge is a ring, and the ring has been in the same place, give or take, for years.

Give or take is the point. When someone in the plant adjusts a valve and the bar of water thickens a finger's width, the whole hollow warms, and the ring moves outward — not far, a few inches, a few feet — and every thread on the inner side of it dies and every stretch of terrace on the outer side is quietly colonised. When the load drops and the hum lowers, the ring moves back in. The film is the only thing in the bowl that experiences the plant's decisions as weather. To the reeds a change of load is nothing; to the stone it is a band; to the visitor it is a note. To the film it is the end of the world and the beginning of another one, several times a year, unremarked. It is why the terrace lips, seen in cross-section, are not smooth but faintly stepped — each step a season in which the ring stood somewhere for a while and built, and then moved, and built somewhere else. The stone records the adjustments. The film is what does the recording.

It has no idea it is here. It has no idea there is a here. It does not know the sound, though it is made and remade a hundred thousand times a day in water that is trembling with the hum, so that if anything in the hollow can be said to live inside the note, it does. It does not know the pipe, though the pipe is its sun. If the outfall stopped, it would be the first thing to go — before the reeds, before the visitor noticed — cooling in an hour past the edge of its band and stopping, all of it at once, a ring of pale stone that had been alive at dusk and was not by morning. And the rain, when it came to eat the terraces, would find the edges softer than they should be, because the edges were never only mineral. The thing that kept them sharp would be gone.

But the outfall has not stopped. Near evening, when the gold haze fills the bowl and the far wall goes soft, the ring is where it was at noon, a few paces wide, invisible, wet, making stone out of hot water and its own dead, a fraction of a millimetre at a time. The hollow said that nothing lived here, and it was almost right. What lives here is the exception so thin that the place itself cannot see it — and the place is, in the most literal sense, its work.
