Begin, as the room does, with who cannot live here, because that list is what shapes the other one.

Nothing that listens. That is the first cut, and it is nearly total. The roar is a wall with no door in it, and anything that needs to hear — hear a footfall, a wingbeat, a mate, its own young — is excluded not by cold or dark but by the simple fact that the sense it depends on has been switched off at the source. The dipper knows this and works only the fringe, where the noise is still a thing with an outside. The bat, if it is a bat, comes in once and finds its own voice erased and does not come again. The people came, and used the place, and could not stay, and the reason is in the description itself: no way to speak, no way to hear anything approach. They were animals with ears. The room did not want them.

Nothing that needs light for making. Nothing that needs to be dry for even part of the day. Nothing that needs to be warm.

So the residents are what is left when you subtract all of that. They are the un-eared, the un-eyed, the things that take their water from the air and their heat from nowhere. That is a narrow gate, and the room is small, and what comes through it is small too — but it is not nothing, and it has been here a long time.

---

The moss first, because the moss is most of it.

It is tempting to call it a coating, a lining, as if the room were a box and the moss a felt glued on. But stand with your palm flat against the wall for a while and it is harder to think of it that way. It gives. It is cold and then, slowly, less cold where your hand is. Water comes up through it against your skin. It is a single continuous body wrapped around the whole interior — walls, ceiling, the undersides of ledges, the sides of the hearth-stones — with no seam anywhere, one pelt without the animal inside it, and the animal it is missing is the room. The moss is the room's fur. If you could lift it off in one piece and lay it flat it would be the shape of the hollow, inside out.

It lives on the noise. That is the strange, literal truth of it. The roar is the sound of a river being shaken apart into drops, and some of those drops drift inward as spray, and the spray is what the moss drinks. Every hair of it is wet because the water outside is loud. If the fall went quiet the moss would die. It has grown, for ten thousand years, at exactly the height the spray can reach, which is all the way up, and it has never once been thirsty, and it has never once heard a thing.

In among it, lower down, where the wet is heaviest, the liverworts lie flat and scaled and very dark, like the skin of something that has been turned inside out and pressed against the rock to cure. They are older than moss, as a kind of thing. They were doing this — lying on wet stone in the dark, not flowering, not needing to — before there were ferns to do it beside them, and they do it without any sign of urgency, and they will still be doing it when the moss above them has been replaced several times over. If the room has an old family, it is these.

And the filmy fern, one cell thick. Hold a frond toward the curtain and the grey light comes through it as if through nothing. It is a plant at the absolute lower limit of how little a thing can be and still be that thing — a green sheet a single cell deep, unable to hold water in itself and so living only where the air is already water, unable to stand up and so lying against the moss, unable to be seen and so, mostly, not seen. It is a resident the way a breath on glass is a resident. It lives in exactly one kind of place on earth and this is that place.

---

Then the slug.

You will not find it looking for it. You find it by putting your face to the wall to see the fern and having your eye, at some point, snag on a darker dark that is smoother than moss and slightly wet in a different way — glossed, not soaked. The length of a finger. Black, or a brown so deep it might as well be. It does not move while you watch, and then when you look back it has moved.

The slug is the only creature here for whom the room is not a partial thing. The dipper's world is the whole river and this is one corner of it. The trout's world is the pool below. Yours is the valley and the walk back down. The slug's world is this wall. It has never been outside; it was hatched in the moss from an egg the size of a seed-pearl and it will die in the moss and be taken back into it, and in between it will cross the wall a few hundred times, eating the wall as it goes — the fine algae in the moss, the softer liverwort, the fern when it can find it. Its eyes are on stalks and in this dark they see nothing and have never seen anything, and the slug does not know this, because there is no version of the slug that could know it. It reads the world through the underside of its body: wet, less wet, this taste, that. It has no ears at all, none, not even the rudiment of one. The roar that stops your speech and gets into your teeth is for the slug not even silence. It is not a thing that failed to arrive; it is a category that does not exist.

Perhaps it feels the vibration in the rock. Probably it does. But it has felt it every moment since it hatched and so it is not a sound to the slug either. It is what the rock is. The way, for you, the ground is heavy.

How did it get here? Along the ledge, most likely, some ancestor of it, in a wet autumn, a few inches a night, over years — or carried, on a hide that someone brought in and hung on the pegs, or in the crack of a piece of driftwood laid by for a fire. It does not matter. It is here now, and there are others of it in the moss you will not find, and they have been here long enough that the slug and the room have become one another's fact.

---

The pegs are becoming residents. This is worth saying because they did not start that way; they were put here, driven into holes cut for them by hands that meant them to hang things from, and for a while they were tools. Now the wood has split in the wet and the moss has found the splits and is growing in them, and each peg is by now perhaps half peg and half wall, and in another few centuries they will be moss with a hardness at the centre, and then not that. The room is taking them in. It does this to everything that stays long enough.

The hearth-stones it is taking more slowly. They are the only circle in the room, the only made shape — fist-sized river cobbles carried in from outside and set close, and they have been sinking into the softening floor for longer than anyone alive has a word for. They are, in the most literal sense, lodgers: brought from another place, put here, staying. Nothing grows on them because the heat that cracked them killed the rock's willingness. They are the one dry patch of ground the room contains, and the room is filling them in around the edges, a fingernail's width each hundred years.

---

And the pebble.

It sits on top of the cache in the dry niche, on the grey knot of sinew and the broken flint and the antler gone soft, and it is pale, and it is from somewhere else. Not this valley. Someone carried it here in a hand or a pouch across a distance you cannot measure from the pebble, and set it down last, on top, as if it were the point. Everything else in the room arrived by water or spore or wind or by creeping. The pebble is the only thing here that is here on purpose.

That makes it a different kind of resident. It does not eat the wall or drink the spray. It was not driven in or set in a ring for a use. It was placed, and then a lid-stone was set over it, and the lid-stone has slid a little, and whoever placed it did not come back. Meant to. That is what the lid says: not a burial, not an offering, a keeping. Someone left a piece of somewhere else to live here on their behalf until they returned for it, and the something-else has been living here ever since, in the dark, deaf as everything is deaf here, waiting in the way only a stone can wait, which is without expecting anything.

You could say, then, that what lives here is that. Not the person — the person is long gone into the ground somewhere with ears and eyes and a name. But the intention to return, made solid and set on a shelf. The pebble is the room's one tenant that is not a plant or an animal or a piece of the rock's own furniture. It is a thought, left.

---

So: who lives here.

The moss, which is the room's own skin and drinks the noise. The liverworts under it, older than ferns. The fern that is one cell thick. The slug, black and glossed and finger-long, for whom the wall is the world and the roar is what rock is. Two pegs halfway to being wall. A ring of cobbles that came from outside and are being taken in. A pale stone, chosen and carried and set down and covered and left.

All of them deaf. All of them past needing light. All of them cold and wet and in no hurry. All of them, in their different ways, things that came in from outside and stayed long enough to be absorbed — because that is what the room does, and the only way to live in it is to let it.

You are not on the list, and you know it by the fact that you are holding your breath. But sit a night with the fire lit and hidden, back to the dry wall, the pools black at your feet, and something of the room gets into you: the roar in the marrow, the cold where your warmth was. You will carry that out along the ledge and down through the ordinary noise and the birds and it will fade, but not entirely. And so the room does live, a little, in the other direction too — in whoever has sat in it and left. That is what the pegs and the hearth are, after all. The infrastructure of people who did not stay and did not forget. Which is a kind of residence. The slug would not understand it. The pebble, in its way, is it.
