The easy answer is the eel, and for a while the easy answer holds.

It is worth staying with. It is dark the way the water is dark, which is to say not a colour but a condition, and it is long — longer than the room by some distance, if distance is a word the room permits. It came up with the water. That is the plain fact underneath the puzzle of it having been here before the room existed: it was in the water below, in whatever lightless seam the water rose from, and when the ground gave way the room came down to meet it. It did not arrive. It was arrived upon. The hollow opened around it like a mouth around a tongue.

Its channel through the weed is the only line in the room that goes anywhere, and if you follow it — by the lean of the stems, by the faint smoothness where the same flank has passed a thousand times — you find that it does not loop. It does not go from a lair to a hunting ground and back. It goes once around and then it is elsewhere in the room, having crossed itself without meeting itself, so that the eel is a single body threaded through the fold, head in one part of the water and tail in another and the middle nowhere you can put a hand. It is the stalk that grows both ways. It does not eat, or does not seem to. It does not hurry, because there is nowhere in the room that it is not already partly present.

But the channel bends. That is the thing you notice once you have followed it long enough. Not the bends of a swimming thing choosing its way — those are everywhere, and mean nothing — but one bend, always in the same place, always the same shallow arc away from something and then back. The eel goes around a shape. Whatever it is going around does not move, and the eel has never once, in all its circuits, touched it.

So the eel is a tenant, not the one the room belongs to.

The snail, then. It is on the threshold stone, in the groove, and the groove is a direction, and the snail is taking it. A fingernail's width of soft body, a shell the colour of wet paper, a motion so slow that the word motion is a courtesy. It has covered the width of your thumb since the water came in. It is going where the feet went. If you crouch — if crouching is something the room lets you do — and look along the groove ahead of it, into the dark, toward the place the feet were going when they wore the stone, you find that the groove does not run off the edge of the block. It stops. It stops a little way in from the far edge, in a slight deepening, the way a path ends at a doorstep and the doorstep is worn deepest of all.

The snail is not going away along the groove. It is going toward the place where the last foot came down.

And the plants lean. This needs saying carefully, because they do not lean the way plants lean, toward a window, all together. They lean the way a crowd leans that is trying not to look at something. Follow a blade of weed up from the silt and it bows, a fraction, always in the same direction; follow a root down from the ceiling and it bows the same way; and the place where the up-growing and the down-growing meet and pass through each other, the middle that is not fixed — that middle is not at the centre of the room. It is low, and to one side, and it is over the far end of the threshold stone, above the deepening in the groove, above the place the snail is going. Everything green in the room, growing in the dark as toward a light, is growing toward there. That is what the dark feeds them with. Not nothing. Something standing.

There is someone on the stone.

Call it that — someone — and then let the word soften, because it will not stay a person for long, and it was not only a person to begin with. It stands at the far end of the block, where the groove ends. It is the temperature of the water exactly, which is why you could pass your hand a finger's breadth from it and feel only water. It has been standing since before the water came in, and before that it was standing somewhere dry, on this same stone, in a doorway that is no longer anywhere, and the ground did not open under it so much as around it. It is the grit. The room is the pearl.

It is mid-step.

Not falling, not caught, not the strained arrest of a thing stopped by force. Mid-step the way the drop in the ceiling is mid-fall: the rings have spread, the sound has landed, and the drop simply has not got round to it yet. One foot is down in the deepest part of the groove. The other is lifted, a little, behind, the heel just off the stone, the weight already gone forward out of it. It is the posture of someone leaving a house. Of someone who has put a hand on a doorframe and is looking, for the last moment, at a room, before turning to the outside — except that the frame is gone, and the outside is the field, and the field is above, and the only thing left of the frame is one nail standing point-up in the silt a few paces off, driven from beneath, the last nail of a threshold's last post, holding the doorway up from the wrong side because there is no longer a right one.

What it wears has gone to moss. Not rotted; gone to moss the way the walls have, taken over face by face until the cloth is a rumour under green. Its hair, if it has hair, has grown up and joined the root-mat, or the root-mat has come down and become its hair, and there is no point along any single strand where you could say which. Its hands are at its sides, and the weed grows up between the fingers and holds them slightly apart, the way a person holds their hands when they are about to reach for something and have not decided what. Its face is turned the way the feet went. Toward the deepest wearing. Toward the door.

It breathes. This is the slow cooling and the slow warming, the cycle that is not the seasons. A year drawn in and a year let out. At the bottom of the breath the water by the stone tastes of dust, because the breath is coming from the dry place, the house, the doorway that was, and it is bringing a little of that up the way the stone does. At the top of the breath the root-mat above it lets go a pale drift, and the drift sinks through the weed and never reaches the floor, and this is the thing it exhales, and the plants take it, and they are green.

That is what they grow on. Not light. The slow breath of a thing that has not finished leaving.

It does not know about the eel. Or it knows the way you know about a draught in a house you have lived in for a long time: not as a fact but as a habit of the air. The eel goes around it and has always gone around it and there is no more thought in the arrangement than there is in the way water goes around a post.

It knows about the snail. It has been waiting for it. The snail is coming along the groove in the direction the feet went, toward the deepest wearing, toward the one foot that is down, and when the snail reaches the foot — a long time from now, and also, given how the room handles time, already — the step will finish. That is not a threat and it is not a promise. It is just the order of things down here. The groove was worn before the step that wore it. The sound came before the stone let go. The snail will arrive, and the heel that is lifted will come down, and what is standing on the threshold stone will have gone through the door.

Where the door goes is not something the room says.

It does not want anything from you. It is important to be plain about this, because the dark and the water and the standing figure will want to make it into a thing that wants, and it is not that. It is not waiting for you. It is not aware of you as a presence any more than a doorway is aware of the weather. But it hears you before you come, because everything here is heard before it happens, and so when you enter — pressing into the plants, finding the water between them — you will find that it has already made room. Not by moving. It cannot move; it is in the middle of moving, which is the same thing as being unable to. It has made room the way a full dark room makes room: by being what the room is full of, so that there is nowhere in the packed green that is not already, faintly, leaning toward it, and you, once you are inside, will lean that way too, and will find you have taken up no space at all.

You could walk over it in fourteen steps in the field above, and it would feel the steps in the water, and the roots would carry them down to it, and it would hear each one before it landed. It would not turn. It is facing the way the feet went. It has one foot down and one lifted and a snail coming along the stone toward it at the speed of something that is certain of arriving.

The edges of the fracture are still sharp. Nothing has softened them. Nothing will, for a long time, because the one who is leaving has not left, and until it does, the room is only just opened, and will stay that way — new, and full, and holding its breath over the course of a year, and under the moss and the weed and the cold, standing in a doorway that is now made of water, someone in the middle of a step.
