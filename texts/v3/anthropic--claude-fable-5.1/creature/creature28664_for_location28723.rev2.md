You will not find them by looking for houses. That is the first mistake, and you will make it: scanning the terraces for the shapes that mean dwelling — a doorway with a lamp beside it, a window with something behind it, a roof. There are doorways by the thousand and none of them is a front door. There are lamps everywhere and none belongs to a household. What you take at first for a village on the ninth level turns out to be a belt-shop with its shutters open; what you take for a shrine is a gauge-house. The figures in oilcloth are not going home. They are going where the water has gone.

So you have to start with the figures.

---

They are small, or seem small, from the rim; up close they are ordinary in height and somewhat heavy through the shoulder. They wear oilcloth, hooded, the cloth tarred to a dark brown that has been rubbed to black at the elbows and gone faintly green across the shoulders where the drip lands, exactly as the scaffolding poles have. Under the oilcloth, wool. On the feet, wooden soles with a shallow tread cut across them, or, on those who work the wheelhouses, hobnailed boots whose iron rings on the stair and is the one sharp sound they make. Everything they wear is damp. Nothing they wear is ever wet through, which is a skill.

Their hands are the thing you notice. The skin is pale and closely creased, like the hands of laundresses or fishermen, and the nails are short and stained at the edges by moss and bronze. They have thick fingers and they use them delicately. You will see a man lift a sluice key the length of his arm and set it into its socket without a sound, and turn it, and stop turning at a point he seems to feel rather than see, and step back, and wait, listening. You will see a woman close her palm over a penstock flange and hold it there a moment the way you would feel a forehead for fever.

Their voices are low. This is not manners, or not only. A raised voice does not carry here; the moss and the fog take it and there is nothing left twenty feet away. A voice pitched just above a murmur travels along a wall and round a corner and down a stair, and everyone knows this, and so the whole valley speaks in the tone of a reading room. Children learn it before they learn to walk. You will not hear a shout in the basin unless something has failed, and then you will hear it once.

---

They hear better than you do, or differently.

The ground-note — the compound hum of all the wheels — is to them not one sound but a chord, and they can name its members. Stand with one of the gate-tenders on a terrace edge and ask what is running and she will tilt her head and tell you: the pair on the eleventh, the long wheelhouse under the western scarp, the little mill by the old incline that is turning too fast and will want looking at. She is not guessing. When a wheelhouse stops, the chord loses a note, and every person within a quarter mile feels it go and knows, without being told, that in a few minutes a bell will ring and a gate will open somewhere and it will be time to move. The bells confirm what the ground has already said.

This is why they seem to anticipate. It looks, from the rim, as though the forty who leave a darkening mill are responding to a signal. They are, but the signal was the silence.

They hear the cisterns the same way: the cold hum of still water, deeper as the level rises, and they can put an ear to a vault door and give you a depth to the foot. They hear the drip change on a wall before the wall itself shows a dry patch. They hear the spanner three terraces down, and someone always goes to fetch it, because a spanner is the most personal thing anyone here owns.

---

They own tools. That is very nearly the whole of it.

A wheelwright owns his gauges and his scrapers and a folding rule, wrapped in oiled leather. A mason owns her hammers and a set of chisels whose handles have been replaced so many times that only the steel is original, and the steel has been drawn out and re-tempered so many times that perhaps not even that. A gate-tender owns a key — or is entrusted with one; the distinction is not one they make. These things are carried. They are set down beside the sleeper at night and picked up in the morning and they are the only continuity a person has that is not a person.

Nobody owns a room. There are dry rooms — the drying is the point of them — set into the thickness of the terrace walls behind the wheelhouses, where the heat off the bearings and the foundry flues keeps the air just above the dew point. Bedding hangs in them from racks near the ceiling, and people sleep beneath the racks on wooden platforms, close together, in shifts that follow the water. When the wheelhouse stops, the room cools, and within a day the bedding is damp and the sleepers have moved to the next warm place. You will find rooms on the lower levels with fifty racks and nobody in them, the moss already advancing across the platforms; you will find them again a month later full and warm and smelling of wet wool and tallow. The rooms are not abandoned, any more than a footbridge is abandoned between crossings.

Ask where someone lives and they will tell you which bell they answer to. Ask where they were born and they will tell you the year by the water — the year the north cisterns ran over, the year the western scarp went dry — and the level, roughly, and then they will shrug, because the level has been rebuilt twice since and the wheelhouse is somewhere else now.

---

It takes a while to see that there are kinds of them, and longer to see that the kinds are not fixed.

The ones who follow the water are the most numerous and the youngest. They run the mills and the belt-shops and the foundries, and they are the figures you see on the footbridges, moving from a wheel that has stopped to a wheel that has started, carrying their tools and their bedding rolls, never hurrying and never idle. They know every stair that exists this week. They read the chalk.

The ones who make the chalk marks are a smaller number, older, and walk more slowly. They are the gate-tenders and the gauge-readers, and they do not decide where the water goes so much as agree, over days, in low conversations on terrace edges, on where it will be least wasteful for it to go next. Nobody among them is in charge. There is no room in the basin for the kind of authority that stands still. What they have instead is a very long memory for the shape of the drip, and a habit of walking the levels with a hand on the wall, and the keys.

The ones who rebuild have never built anything new. Every block they set has been set before, and they know this, and they read the tool marks of whoever set it last as you might read a letter. They can tell you, from the dressing on a limestone face, that it was cut by someone who held the chisel in the left hand and was working fast; that it stood for a while in a course that was wetter than this one, by the depth of the moss-scar; that it has been turned, so that what is now the bed was once the face. They regard the oldest quarters — the ones that have been taken down and put up most often — with something that is not quite reverence, because reverence would suggest leaving alone, and nothing here is left alone. It is closer to the regard a family has for a long-married pair.

The moss-trimmers are the fewest and the slowest and are very old. They move along the lamp-lines with small curved blades and clear the felt back from the enamel shades and the gauge-glasses and the bell-mouths, a finger's width, no more, with the care of barbers. They do not scrape. Nobody scrapes. If you ask them why the moss is structural they will show you a wall where it has been torn off by a slipped block and the mortar beneath is already crumbling to sand in the drip, and they will say nothing further, because the wall has said it.

Nobody retires. The machinery is slow; a person slowing down simply moves along it, from the wheelhouses to the gauges to the lamps, and the last thing they do is trim.

---

The children are born in the fog and stay in it.

They are raised on stairs that are not in the same place from one season to the next, and this does not trouble them; they learn the bells the way children elsewhere learn birds, and can tell you at four years old which gate has just closed by the pitch of the stroke. They learn to walk on wet stone before they learn to run, and most of them never do learn to run — there is nowhere to run to that is not also a stair, and a stair here is a thing you take at the pace of the drip. They are quiet. They are set to picking the tea rows early, because small hands are best in the low dense bushes and the work is shallow and safe, and by ten they are carrying tools.

They do not see the sun. Nobody in the basin sees the sun, but the children have not even the memory of the rim, and for them the world is a grey-green room with no ceiling, lit by filaments, humming. At some point in their growing up someone takes them up the road — there is no ceremony about it, it is done when there is a reason to go, when a cargo needs a hand over the saddle — and they stand on the rim in the moving cloud and look down into the lamps, and see the whole of it at once for the first time, breathing.

Some go back down and do not go up again. Some become porters, and go over the saddle with the bronze and the tea and come back with grain and salt and cast iron, and are the only people in the valley who know that the road goes anywhere. They are not treated as travellers. When they come back they are given a bed near a warm wheel like anyone else, and they answer to a bell.

---

You will ask, because there is no ground, what they do with the dead.

You will be told differently by different people, and both answers will be given in the same low tone, without emphasis, as one gives directions.

One answer is the tunnel. The lowest cistern sends its overflow out under the western wall, and where it comes out nobody in the valley has been, and the dead are laid in the overflow channel and go where the water goes. This is consistent with everything. Nothing is stored here for long. Everything is designed to be passing through.

The other answer is the mortar. The foundries burn hot enough for it, and lime is dear and carried up a long way, and ash is a binder. The old quarters, the ones rebuilt the most, have the most people in them. The masons, when they read a joint, are reading a letter in more than one sense. This too is consistent with everything.

Nobody will tell you which is true, and you will come to suspect that both are, in different quarters, in different years, depending on where the water was.

---

Then there is what lives here that is not people.

The tea, if it is tea: low, dense, glossy, every leaf carrying a drop, picked at the tips by small hands and dried in the flue-heat behind the foundries, and drunk constantly, from tin cups, by everyone, so that the smell of it is under the smell of moss and tallow wherever you go. The broad-leaved crop that catches the drip, whose root is what they mostly eat, boiled to a grey paste and salted. The espaliered trees flat against the retaining walls, which fruit small and sour and late, and are pruned by people who were not born when they were planted and who will not be alive when they are next moved. The plants are residents in the way the people are: cut back where a conduit needs to pass, laid in again where one has been abandoned, settled into a relationship with the pipe.

The moths that never fly, brown, in the tea rows, which the children collect in their palms and let go. The pale crickets in the cistern mouths, whose one note is sometimes mistaken for a drip. The cats, who are as rare as saints and are treated like them: seen once a day on a wall, not approached, spoken of afterward.

And the moss. If anything owns the walls, the moss owns them. It was here before the oldest joint anyone can read, and it has been on every block through every rebuilding, torn and re-laid and taken down and put up, and it is on the scaffolding now and will be on whatever the scaffolding becomes. The people trim a finger's width from the lamps and step around the rest. They understand that they are the tenants.

---

And under all of it, the thing you came for and did not see: the water.

It arrives as weather over the rim and is handed down, ledge to spout to lip, into the dark vaults and out the liver-red pipes, and it turns the wheels and leaves under the western wall, and none of it stays. The people of the basin do not think of themselves as living on it or by it. If they think of it at all — and they mostly do not, any more than you think of your blood — they think of themselves as what keeps it moving: the hands on the keys, the ear to the vault, the forty crossing a footbridge because a note has gone out of the chord. The basin is the water's body. They are its motion.

They are placid because of this. Not content, exactly; the word does not have a purchase here. What they have is the absence of a certain kind of grief. When a wheelhouse stops they are already walking, and they do not look back at the wall. They have never stayed anywhere, and they have never left. Every one of them was born in it and will go out of it by the water or into it by the mortar, and in between they will have slept in a hundred rooms and answered to a dozen bells and carried the same chisel, re-handled, and set blocks that were set before by hands they could read.

Ask one of them how many people live in the valley and you will get the same answer you got about the wheels — a number, then another number, then a shrug. It is not that they do not know. It is that a number is a picture of something standing still, and they have never seen anything stand still, and they do not trust it.
