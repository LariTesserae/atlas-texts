Start with the birds, because they are what you see, and because for a while you will think they are the answer.

They are the size of a closed hand. Dark all over, a brown so deep it is nearly black, with a slightly paler throat you notice only when one turns its head. The bill is short and fine and the legs are long for the body, and when a bird stands still it stands with the legs slightly bent, so that the belly nearly touches the rock. This is not rest. It is heat. The warmth comes up through the stone and into the unfeathered skin of the legs and the thin down of the belly, and a bird that stands tall loses what a bird that crouches keeps. You watch for an hour and understand that everything they do is arranged around this arithmetic. They do not hop when a fly appears two body-lengths away; they wait for one to appear within reach. They do not preen. They do not, ever, in daylight, lift their wings.

The spacing is not politeness. Each bird sits at the center of a circle of rock that its own body has helped to dry — warmth from below, warmth from the bird, the two together enough to keep the frost off a patch roughly the size of a dinner plate. Where two circles would overlap, one bird has moved. The lattice is the shape that thousands of these small negotiations settled into, and it is stable, and it has the disquieting regularity of a thing grown in a dish.

They came at night. You did not see it, but you can reconstruct it from the ones that arrive late: a shape dropping through the cloud with the wings already folding, hitting the rock with a small hard sound, standing there for a moment with its beak open, and then walking — walking — to the nearest empty circle and sitting down in it. They flew here from the coast, or from the lowlands beyond the coast, at night, high, without stopping, and the last kilometer of that flight was straight down. Some of them misjudge. You find those in the morning at the cold edge of the basin, whole, unbroken, dead of nothing but the gap between where they landed and where the warmth begins.

They will leave the same way, and they will not agree to it. There will be no sign. One morning the flies will come up a little later and a little fewer, and a few hundred birds at the cold margin will not eat, and by the next dawn those birds will be gone and the lattice will have contracted toward the center like a thing drawing in a breath. Then the center will go. It will take perhaps a week. On the last day there will be one bird on the warmest rock in the basin, eating alone, and then there will be none.

This is what they are: not inhabitants but a season. They are the plateau's summer, arriving from outside to spend a few weeks on the surplus and then taking themselves away before the account closes. The rings on their legs say so. The numbers were stamped somewhere with a roof. The birds are the plateau's connection to the sodium light on the horizon — the only thing here, apart from the water, that has been in both places.

---

The scavengers are the second answer, and they are closer to the truth.

You see one properly only once, at the mouth of a fissure in the last light: a body about the length of your forearm, low, long, with a tail that seems to belong to a larger animal. The fur is the grey-brown of the oxidizing rock and it is wet and stuck into points along the spine. The face is narrow and the eyes are set to see forward and down. It looks at you for a count of three and then it is not there, and you did not see it go. The rest of what you know about them is sound: the scrabble in the fissures, the clicking of teeth on bone in the dark, once a thin high squeal that ends in the middle.

They are not from here. Nothing is. They came up the flank of the mountain from the old valleys in the first weeks after the rock could be walked on, and they came, you decide, because they were already following the birds. They know this colony. They know it the way a fisherman knows a season, and they have known it in other places, on other new ground, because the birds have been landing on cooling rock for longer than anyone has been stamping numbers on rings. The scavengers are not opportunists. They are specialists in the opportunity.

And they have done something the birds have not. Deep in the fissures, where the water runs warm and the light does not come, they have young. You never see them. You know because on certain nights the scrabbling has a second pitch under it, thinner, and because one evening an adult comes out of a crack carrying something and goes back in without eating it. They are the first creatures to be born on the plateau. For those young, the wet black rock and the warmth in the dark and the sound of the birds feeding overhead are not a season. They are the world. They will be weaned onto dead birds and grow up in a place with no soil, and when the birds stop coming their parents will take them down off the mountain to some other new ground, and they will remember nothing of this, and the plateau will have been, briefly, a nursery.

---

Now the flies, because the flies are where the plateau stops pretending.

You have seen them as food. Small, dark, soft-bodied, coming up out of the cracks in the vapor and stalling in the cold air a hand's breadth above the rock, dropping, being taken. There are thousands a day. It looks like a catastrophe — a whole population extruded into the cold to die — and the birds would not be here if it were not reliable, so it cannot be a catastrophe. It is a surplus. It is the froth on something much larger that is going on below.

Get down on your stomach at the edge of one of the warmer fissures. Put your face into the gap. The air that comes up is a few degrees above the air outside and it is saturated, and it smells — this is the first smell you have noticed on the plateau — faintly of sulfur and faintly of something alive, a low mineral rot. The walls of the crack are wet and black and, a little way down where the light fails, they are not quite black. There is a film. It is grey, or greyish-yellow, or in some places the dull orange of rust, and it is thin enough to be a discoloration and thick enough that when you reach down and touch it with a gloved finger it slides.

That is what the flies eat. Not as adults — the adults eat nothing, or almost nothing; they are a delivery mechanism — but as larvae, in the warm dark, on the walls of every fissure that carries warm water, for as many weeks as it takes to grow. The larvae are pale and blind and never see the sky. Most of them never see it as adults either. The ones that rise into the cold and stall and are eaten are the ones that went up the wrong crack, or went up at the wrong hour, or were simply the fraction the population can afford to lose. The rest emerge in the dark, mate in the dark, and lay in the dark, and their whole lives are lived within a few vertical meters of warm wet stone. They have never been anywhere else. Their grandparents have never been anywhere else. No ring will ever be put on them.

They are, in every sense the word can carry, the residents. The birds are visiting them.

And the film is what the flies are visiting. The film was here first. It arrived on the water — the water that was once steam in a cooling tower, that fell as rain on old mountains and rose as cloud and came across the plateau at the height of your knees — and it found warm rock rich in the metals that fresh lava is rich in, and it began to do what it does, which is eat stone. Slowly. Not stone in the way a root eats stone, prising it apart, but chemically, at the scale of molecules, pulling electrons off iron and sulfur and using the small energy of that to make itself. It does not need light. It does not need the moss. It needs the warmth and the wet and the raw unweathered mineral surface, and this plateau is, for the moment, more of exactly that than anywhere within a thousand kilometers.

So the shape of the ecology you worked out from the surface was upside down. It is not that the heat draws the flies which draw the birds which draw the rest. It is that the heat feeds the film, and the film feeds the larvae, and the flies are the film's exhaust, and everything above them — the birds, the scavengers, the shape in the cloud — is a small crowd gathered around an exhaust pipe.

---

The shape in the cloud you never resolve, and you should not try too hard. But it comes at the same hour each day, and the birds' stillness lasts the same slow count, and once, when the overcast thins, you see the shadow it throws on the cloud below it — broad, blunt-winged, slower than a falcon, holding one position in the wind for longer than seems possible. It does not land. In all the days you are here it does not once come down onto the rock. It takes what it takes on the wing, low over the basin, and goes back up, and you decide — with no evidence — that it roosts somewhere else entirely. On an older mountain across the rim. Or, why not, on something tall and warm in the northeast, under the sodium light, where the updrafts are constant and the ledges are steel. It would not be the first of its kind to learn that. The plateau might be a place it visits on its round, one stop among several, most of them built.

---

And under all of it — under the film, under the water counting itself into pools that have never seen light — the last inhabitant.

You have been told the plateau is not finished, and you have been thinking of that as a fact about geology. It is also a fact about who lives here. The mountain is still, in a way that is not quite metaphor, occupied by the thing that made it. The heat that comes up through the rock is not a residue. It is a body of molten stone, kilometers down, cooling from the outside in, and every few days when the door closes in the distant part of the house that is the sound of it giving up some small increment of itself: a chamber's roof settling onto a chamber that has emptied, a pane of new rock cracking as it shrinks. The plateau shifts because the thing beneath it is drawing in, and everything on the surface — the film, the larvae, the birds, the scavenger young in their warm crack — is living on the outside of that withdrawal.

This is the resident that is leaving. Not in the way the birds will leave, in a week of contractions, or the way the scavengers will leave, walking down the flank with their young. It is leaving by becoming less, day by day, a fire going out in a room the size of a mountain. When it is gone the plateau will be cold and dark and stable and the water will begin the long work of moving it, and the moss will come, and the rosette that you saw once will have a thousand descendants, and there will be soil, and something with roots, and then something that eats roots, and then something that eats that. A different building. Different tenants.

For now: a warm dark body under raw rock, dreaming itself cold. On its skin, a grey film eating the metals it sweats. In the film, blind pale larvae. Above the larvae, a froth of flies. Around the flies, a lattice of small dark birds with the numbers of somewhere else on their legs, sitting very still, spending nothing. In the cracks between, a handful of long grey animals raising the only children the plateau will ever see born on it. In the cloud, once a day, a shadow that lives elsewhere. Above the cloud, an engine.

Everything here is a guest of the heat. Everything here is a guest of everything below it. And the thing at the bottom, the one that all of them are living on, is the only one that has never been anywhere else and never will be — the mountain, cooling, still in the middle of arriving, and already beginning, in its own long way, to go.
