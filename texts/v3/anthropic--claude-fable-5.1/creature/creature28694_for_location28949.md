You will not see them at first, and when you do you will think you are wrong.

The pale grazers are the size and colour of the walls, and for the first hour every pale shape crouched at the base of a wall is a grazer until it lifts an arm. Then it is a grazer with an arm, for a moment, until the whole animal reorganises itself in your eye and stands up — not far — and is a person, and has been a person the entire time, and has been watching you since you came off the flats.

They are low. That is the first thing about them, as it was the first thing about the city, and it is the same thing. Nothing here reaches higher than a few storeys and nothing here reaches higher than a few feet if it can help it. They squat by default. They stand only to move, and they move with the knees bent and the weight down, so that the standing is really a kind of raised squat, and the tallest of them is shorter than you and looks shorter than that. Their feet are wide and long-toed and splay on contact. Their hands are the same. The wrists and ankles are thick, and there is a settled, banked quality to the whole frame, as if it had been built by something that knew the floor could not be trusted and had designed for the floor rather than the sky.

The skin is the colour of the material. Not painted — the colour of it, greyish-buff, faintly translucent at the edges of the ears and the webbing between the fingers, and stained the way the walls are stained: rust in the creases, a greenish shadow at the knuckles and heels, and at the elbows and the backs of the calves, where they rest most against stone, a thin hard crust of mineral white that they do not scrape off. On the old ones this crust is thick. It cracks when they bend and reforms when they sleep. It is not decoration and it is not neglect. It is what happens to anything that stays in contact with this water long enough, and they have stayed.

The eyes are large and dark and wet, and they do not blink much in the steam. The ears are small, and lie close, and there is something about them that takes a while to see — the opening is narrow, almost a slot, and the flesh around it is dense and cushioned, as if the ear had been packed down. When you finally get close to one you will see that the ear moves. Not a twitch. A slow orienting, like the tilt of a dish.

---

They do not speak. You were told this and you will still find yourself trying to talk to them, and they will watch your mouth work with an interest that is not comprehension.

They are not deaf. This takes a while to understand, because you will assume that anything living inside this roar must have gone deaf as a mercy, and then you will watch one of them turn its head toward a section of wall thirty paces off, a moment before the wall begins to lean, and you will realise the roar is not noise to them. It is not even sound. It is where they live. They hear in it the way you see in daylight — not the light itself, which you have stopped noticing, but everything the light falls on. A floor that is about to lift makes a sound inside the sound. A channel that is about to drain makes another. The pitch of the grinding under a court changes when the court is about to close, and they hear the change from farther away than you would hear a shout, and they do not hurry, because they heard it early enough not to need to.

What they cannot hear, or have no use for, is a voice. A voice is a small sound made by a soft body and the roar simply takes it. They have not used voices in longer than anything remembers, and the apparatus has gone the way of the appendix — the tongue thick, the larynx present but unpractised. Once, at night, you will hear one of them make a noise in its sleep, a low unformed vowel, and it will be the strangest thing you have heard in this place, because it will be the only sound here that is not the city.

They speak with their hands. Not in the air — the steam is too thick, the light too flat, and a hand in the air is a hand out of contact. They speak on skin. Two of them squatting side by side will have a forearm laid across the other's thigh, or a palm flat on the other's back, and the fingers will be moving in a slow, pressing, complicated way, and the other will answer with a hand on the first one's neck or shoulder or the inside of the elbow. It is not gesture. It is closer to writing, if writing were done directly onto the reader. The pressure has grammar; the location has grammar; the speed has grammar. A group of them at rest is a group in continuous quiet conversation, every body touching two others, and what one of them knows the group knows within a minute, passed hand to back to hand along the whole heap without anyone lifting their head.

They also speak to the stone, or listen to it, and the difference is not clear. You will see one press the flat of a palm to a wall and hold it there, and then press an ear, and then — this is the one that unsettles — press its teeth, the mouth open against the wet surface, jaw locked, for a long time. The skull hears what the ear cannot. When it takes its mouth away there is a clean pale oval in the varnish and it moves off in a direction that did not previously look like a direction, and a little while later that direction is a passage.

---

They own what is on the body and nothing else.

There is nothing to keep a thing in. There is no room that will be a room tomorrow, no shelf that will not be a floor by dusk, no crevice that will not close on whatever was put in it. Anything set down is given away. So they set nothing down. Each of them carries a small weight of gear — a cord of twisted swallow-down and sinew, a scraper made of grazer rib, a crab claw for the fungus, a little pouch of something pale and folded that turns out to be the dried gut of a fish — and all of it is lashed to the body, and none of it would be missed. Their wealth, if they had a concept for it, would be what their hands know.

They eat what the city exposes. When a wall rises out of a floor it shears the layer beneath and turns up the things that live in that layer, and the glassy swarms come to feed on the exposure, and the people come to feed on the swarms, scooping them out of the air with a fine cord net and eating them off the cord with their teeth. When a channel begins to drain they are already at the low end of it, where the blind fish will pool as the water goes. They eat the shelf fungus raw. They eat the snails. They very rarely eat a grazer, and when they do it is a grazer that a rising floor has already caught by the leg, and they do not so much kill it as finish something the city started. They drink the milky water from the pools and their bodies are full of the same suspended mineral as the walls, and this is presumably where the crust at the elbows begins — from inside.

They do not cook. There is no fuel here. The floors are warm and a fish left on a fresh floor will cure in a day, and they know which floors, and they sit around the fish while it cures with their backs to the wall and their hands on each other, and by the time it is ready the wall is somewhere else and so are they.

---

They sleep in heaps.

Not for warmth — nothing here is ever cold — but because a body alone on this ground is a body no one is listening to. A heap of eight or ten of them in a corner where the varnish is thick, limbs over limbs, faces down, every one in contact with at least two others. Someone is always partly awake. Not the same someone; the wakefulness moves through the heap the way the conversation does, one and then another lifting a head, laying a palm on the floor, feeling what the floor is doing, laying the head back down. When a wall begins to rise under them the heap shifts as one thing, rolling a body-width to the side without anyone opening their eyes, exactly as the grazers do, and you will think of the grazers, and you will not be wrong to.

Children are carried. From birth until they can squat and hold, a child is against skin — on a back, on a hip, in the crook of an arm — and the language is pressed into it before it can see well enough to read a face. The first thing a child knows is what a hand on its spine means. The second is what the floor is doing. You will see children who cannot yet walk laid belly-down on the stone with a parent's hand between their shoulders, and the child's palms spread wide and flat on the surface, and both of them very still, and it will take you a while to understand that this is a lesson. They are learning the roar from the inside. They are learning it the way you learned to see.

They do not have homes and they do not have places. They have readings. A group will stop where the crust is thick and the pitch is steady and the seep at the base of the wall is slow, and that is where they will be tonight, and it will not be where they were last night or where they will be tomorrow, and this does not seem to trouble them any more than the movement of the sun would. You will look for the thing they return to — the fixed point, the shrine, the grave, the market — and there is none. Or there is one, and it is everywhere: the material, which is the same material in every direction, and which they touch continuously, and which has never once in their lives been anywhere but under their hands.

---

The dead are given to the floor.

That is the whole of it. There is no digging, because nothing dug would stay dug. A body is laid on a clean surface — clean, not varnished, which means moving — and the heap sits around it with their hands on it and on each other, and the floor comes up. Slowly. Over a day or two the pale lens rises around the body the way it rises around anything, and takes the shape of it, and then does not take the shape of it any more, and then is a wall. The heap stays until the wall is a wall and then moves on. Nobody comes back to it, because nobody could find it, and because there would be nothing there to find: within a season the wall has flowed on and the body has gone into the flow, mineral into mineral, and is somewhere in the terraces now, or in the seep, or in the milky pool that the children are drinking from.

They know this. You can see that they know it in the way they touch the old walls — the thick-varnished ones, the ones that have been still a long time — with a particular flat attention, the palm held longer than it needs to be held for reading. Whether they think of the walls as their dead or the dead as walls or whether the distinction has any purchase for them at all, you cannot ask. But the rust streaks in the seams are iron, and they have been drinking the water for a very long time.

---

You will want to know whether they steer it.

You will watch one of them press both hands into a rising lens, hard, leaning its weight in, and hold, and the lens will seem to slow — and you will not be able to say whether it slowed because of the hands or whether the hands went there because it was about to slow. You will see a group of them lie down in a court that by every reading you have learned should close by evening, and it will not close, and you will not know if they knew that or made that. You will see the grammar of the shapes — the arches of one proportion, the channels of one depth — and you will see that the people sit in the arches and drink from the channels and you will wonder which came first, the proportion or the sitting, the depth or the drinking, and whether over enough time there is a difference.

They will not tell you. Not because it is secret. Because there is no one to tell it to. You would have to stand outside the city to ask how the city works, and they have never stood outside it, and the question has no shape for a hand to press.

Some of them do reach the edge. You will hear about this — feel about it, rather, one of them pressing it into your wrist in a slow deliberate way that you will understand is meant for you and that you will fail to read, until later, when you see it happen. A young one, mostly. Restless, or following a channel too far, or just walking in one direction longer than anyone walks in one direction. The city thins. The shelves get lower and further apart and the roar drops — not much, a little, like a hand lifting slightly off a drum. And the young one stops. You can see it from the last terrace, a small pale shape standing very still on the cracked white mud where the ground still belongs to itself, and it is standing because it is deafened. It has walked into silence and the silence is the loudest thing it has ever heard. It cannot feel where the floor is going because the floor is not going anywhere. It cannot hear its heap. It cannot hear anything, and after a while it turns around and walks back in, and the roar comes up around it, and its shoulders drop, and it is home.

---

By the end you will have given up on the question of what they are. Descendants of the makers, maybe — the ones who set the tide running and then forgot they had done it, whose grandchildren's grandchildren live inside the machine the way fish live inside the current, needing it, not owning it, not needing to. Or something the city made, later, out of the same material, the way it made the arches. Or the animal it keeps. You will have noticed that everything alive here is the colour of the walls, and that the people are only the most watchful of the animals, and that the difference between a person who has stopped speaking and a grazer that has never started is smaller, up close, than it looked from the flats.

And you will have noticed the other thing. That the roar does not stop and the walls do not stop and nothing here has a name for the city, because a name is for something you can stand beside. And that every one of them, all the time, waking and sleeping, eating and dying, has a hand on the stone. Not for balance. Not only for reading. Because a hand on the stone is the way you stay in the conversation, and the conversation has been going on since before the ground was dry, and it is going on now, in the grinding under your feet, and if there is anything in this place that is alive in the way you meant when you asked, it is not the pale people in their heaps or the swallows in their two-day eaves.

It is what they are all listening to.

One of them will come up to you before you go — before the floor decides for you — and lay its wide wet hand on the inside of your forearm and press something there, slowly, carefully, three or four times. You will not know what it says. You will feel the crust on its fingers and the warmth of it, blood-warm, floor-warm, and the small deliberate pressures that are its whole voice, and then it will take its hand back and squat down again against the wall and turn its slotted ear toward something you cannot hear.

Later, on the flats, in the terrible quiet, you will find the marks are still there. Four pale ovals in the skin, where the mineral came off its fingers onto you. They will wash off. But not for a while.
