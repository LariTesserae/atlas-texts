Start by sorting. Most of what you see does not live here. The tide does not live here — the many-legged sheet, the long-snouted pack, the storm of gray bodies dropping from the vault — those are traffic, and a crossing is not a home to what crosses it. Strip them out. Wait for the gland to shut and the seething to sink back to its floor and then look at what is still in the room.

---

The gaskets, first, because they are the ones that cannot leave.

Look closely at one of the small ones, on a wrist-thick conduit at the height of your face. It is a ring, or a very short tube, and its body is a single band of muscle with no front and no back, the same in every direction around its own hole. It has no eyes. It has no mouth you could point to; the whole inner surface of the ring is mouth, a fine downy lining that takes up whatever sweats through the seam it sits in. It is exactly as thick as the gap. Not nearly. Exactly. Where the gland's lip and the conduit's lip come closest, the gasket is thinnest, almost a membrane; where the wood falls away a little on the far side, the gasket swells to fill. It is a cast of the space between two pieces of the plant, taken in flesh.

It does not arrive this way. Watch the half-pipe on the north wall, the one that hasn't closed, and you will see the beginning of one. Where the curling lips are closest there is a place that weeps — a thread of sap, the barest slick — and in the slick something is sitting. A grain of a thing, pale, ringed even now. It was carried in on a tide, one among a thousand of its kind riding in the fur of the gray animals or the gut of the long-snouts or just tumbling loose in the column, and every one of that thousand came out into the chamber and was swept on west except this one, which fell, and landed where something leaked, and stopped. That is all a gasket needs to know: *here it leaks; stay*. It will eat the leak. Eating, it will grow. Growing, it will fill the gap, and when it fills the gap the leak will stop, and then it will be living on almost nothing, a seep, a sweat, whatever a perfect seal still lets through — which is not much and is enough. By then the lips of the pipe will have closed over it and the gland will have begun to work and it will be spending its life opening and clamping on a count it did not choose, and it will not experience this as hardship, because it has never known a moment of its life that wasn't shaped by that count. The clench is its weather. The count is its day.

They die in place. They have nowhere else to die. When one goes, the seam it held opens and the join leaks, and the leak runs down the conduit and pools and stinks of sap, and for a few tides the gland behind it works badly and the flow stutters — and then a grain of something lands in the leak and stays. The old body does not come out. The root does what it always does with a dead thing pressed against it: thickens over it. If you run your hand along one of the great conduits you will find, in among the living seals, hard amber knots the size of a fist, smooth, faintly ringed. Those were animals. The wood has sealed them into itself and grown a new gap a fraction to one side and a new animal fills that. The oldest conduits are studded with them like a string with beads, generations of gaskets laid up in the grain, each one a place where the plant leaked once and something came and stopped it and was kept.

So that is one answer. What lives here is the thing that fits.

---

But the pools have their own answer, and it is not the same.

Kneel by one. The film on the surface is not scum — it is a mat, living, a felted skin of something between a mould and a moss that draws on the amber light from the conduits and grows a hair's breadth a day and is grazed back a hair's breadth a day, so it never changes. Under it the water is clear and a hand deep and cold compared to the air, and in it the pale shapes lie.

They are longer than you thought, most of the length folded under. They have four legs, small and set far back, that they use only to hold position. They have a head that is mostly gill, a fringe of feathery pink stalks that stand out from the neck and stir constantly, and inside the fringe a face that has given up almost everything — no eyes, no eyelids, not even the dents where eyes were — everything except a mouth like a slot and a pale patch of skin over the brain that is thinner than the rest and shivers when a drop hits the water. They feel the drop. They feel the tide through the floor, the drumming coming up through the wood into the basin, and they feel the gland behind the east wall clench because the whole chamber flexes a hair when it does and the water in a hand-deep pool moves. They live in a room of pressures and know it perfectly.

Each pool is its own country. The salamander-things in the basin under the east gland are not the same as the ones under the north wall; you can see it in the fringe, redder in the east, longer-stalked in the north; they have been separate for however long those two pools have been two. But the trough floods. Every so often a gland vents water and the floor runs end to end for the space of a few breaths, and in those few breaths the pools are one pool, and the shapes rise and cross. Not far. Not fast. A body-length, two, a slow pale writhe across the wet wood toward the next basin down, and then the water drains through the cracks and whatever is not in a hollow when it goes is on bare wood in the warmth, and the hunters, who know this timing too, come out of the walls low and quick and take what's exposed. So the crossing costs. The pools stay mostly separate and the fringes stay mostly different and a little blood passes between them at every flood, enough to keep them one animal, not enough to make them one pool. It is a beautiful arrangement and nothing designed it. The water made the schedule. The hunters set the price.

Some pools are dry. You can find the hollows — perfect basins, polished, the mat gone to a gray crust around the rim — where a crack opened in the floor and the water started to go through faster than the vault could put it back. There is nothing in them. There are marks in the bottom of one, a fan of fine parallel scratches, the last thing something did there.

---

The gray hangers have a home and leave it. That is their answer.

They are the only residents that go out on the tide and come back, and it is not obvious at first that they do come back, because they leave in a storm and return in ones and twos, dropping into the pockets from the ceiling conduits in the long quiet stretches, so you never see it as an arrival. But count a pocket before a tide and count it after and the number is short, and count it a few hours later and the number is nearly right again. Not exactly right. The chamber is not the whole of their range; there are other nodes, other pockets, and the gray things move among them the way birds move among roosts, and what returns to this wall is not always what left it. Yet the colony is the same colony. It has been in that scar since the limb rotted out. The individuals are the water; the pocket is the pool.

Inside, they hang in rows with their heads down and their wings — if the flaps of skin between the long fingers are wings — folded so tight around them that they look like a row of pale seed-pods, soft-furred, breathing. The fur is the color of the ash on a dead coal. The eyes are pinheads and they hardly use them; what turns their heads all together when the light pulses is not sight but something in the skin of the face that reads the glow's warmth, and they orient to it the way a room of sleepers orients to a draft. The pocket smells like them, a warm musk over the resin. The back wall of it is worn to a shine by generations of small bodies pressing there. In the deepest fold of the scar, where the light does not reach, the young hang — bare, blind, gripping the wood and each other, kept warm by the pack of adults in front of them. They are the only creatures in the chamber that are ever kept warm by anything but the plant.

When the pulse comes that empties the ceiling, the adults let go all at once and the young stay. That is why the storm is so brief and so total: the whole colony launches on one signal because the signal is the only way to know the gland is open and the gland will not stay open long. They ride the tide west, they go into the root, and somewhere out in it they feed — on the mites of the ceiling crust, on the pale many-legs, on whatever the columns carry — and then they find their way back through conduits you can't see to a pocket whose smell they know, and hang, and the young work their way forward through the pack to something that will feed them. A few tides later the number is right. A few tides after that some of the young let go with the rest.

---

The beetles are harder to call residents, because they are so plainly *employed*.

They live on the fibers. Not near them: on, undersides flattened to the cord, the whole iridescent shell a lid over a body that is mostly a pair of soft plates pressed to the hum. They are drawing current. What they do with it you can see if you watch one long enough — the shell flushes, bands of color chasing across it, and then it dims, and that is a charge being taken in and spent on something, on heat maybe, or on the slow pumping that keeps a soft body alive in a dry place; and when the surge comes down the fiber to drive the great east gland they let go before it reaches them because they have been reading the line all along and can feel the front of it coming. They fall. They land on their backs, mostly, with a crackle, and right themselves, and start up the wall again by a route that avoids the fiber until the surge has passed. Then they climb back on. Then they hang there in the hum until the next one.

Their larvae are the crust in the vault, or some of it — flat gray grubs the size of a lentil grazing the dry wood where the ceiling conduits give off the most heat and the least damp, a slow weather of them shifting across the glow. They come down when they've grown enough to need the current. There is a line, about two-thirds up the wall, where the crust ends and the beetles begin, and that line is where the fibers are.

If any animal here knows the plant directly, it is these. The gaskets know the count. The pool-things know the pressure. The beetles are wired in. They live in the plant's voltage and read its moods off the cord under their bellies and they are the first in the chamber to know anything is coming — before the gaskets change color, before the gray heads go still. Watch the beetles. When they scatter, the room is about to move.

---

The hunters live in the walls.

You knew that already without knowing you knew it, from the way they are always in time. Between tides they are in the closed conduits that don't run anymore — tubes that shut over long ago and then lost their traffic and never filled, so they stay as hollows in the root, dry, dark, low, exactly the height of a body moving with its belly down. There are openings into them all around the chamber at floor level and you have been walking past them taking them for knots. Put your hand to one and the air coming out is cooler and smells of nothing, which in this room is a smell.

What comes out of them when the flood is at its thickest is the chamber made into an animal. It is the color of the polished trough. It is low because the tubes are low and long because the tubes are long and the joints go the wrong way because the wrong way is what lets it turn in a space that tight. Its feet make no sound on the wood because its feet are wood-colored pads with the same slight gloss the floor has, and the many-legs run over them without knowing. It has eyes — small, deep, set forward — and it uses them, which almost nothing else here does; it needs to pick one gray body out of a storm and it needs to do it fast. It takes the one that is falling wrong, or the one that lands where the trough is deepest and the crowd slowest. It does not chase. There is nothing to chase, in a tide; it holds still in the wedge it has made and the flood brings the taking to it.

Then it is gone into the wall with the body and you hear nothing, and nothing comes back out but, later, a scatter of small clean bones the pool-things surface for. There are three or four. There have been three or four for a very long time. It is not clear whether they breed in the dead tubes or whether the young come in with the tide like everything else and find their way into the walls and grow into the shape the walls require; but the walls do require it, and the shape is precise, and you will not find a hunter here built any other way.

---

And now stop sorting, because the sorting has begun to fail.

You wanted to know what lives here, and you have a list — the seal, the pool, the roost, the wire, the wall — and every item on the list turns out to be an animal that is also a part. The gasket is a valve. The beetle is a fuse. The gray colony is a load that fires on a signal; the hunters are a friction the schedule applies to the crossing; the pool-things are a filter, cold and blind, straining whatever falls. You can describe each one as a creature with a life, and you have, and it is all true. You can also describe each one as an organ of the room, and that is all true too, and the two descriptions do not compete. They are the same description read from either end.

So try the other answer. The thing that lives here is the fit.

Not the plant — the plant is the place, and a place does not live in itself. Not any one animal. What lives here is what you saw in the gasket and then kept seeing everywhere: a life that has taken the exact shape of a gap and holds it, and a gap that has taken the exact shape of a life and keeps it, so long and so closely that you can no longer say which is the mould. The leaking seam that summons the ring. The flood that pays for the crossing. The fiber that teaches the beetle when to fall. Every resident in this room is a body pressed into a space the root left open, and every space the root left open is one it kept open because a body was pressing there. That is the animal. It has no edges. It is the whole knuckled, sweating, humming chamber understood as a single thing that has been fitting itself to itself for longer than any of its parts have existed — and each part is born into a slot that was worn by the part before it, and dies in that slot, and is thickened over, and the slot remains.

The beads in the great conduit are its memory. The dry basin with the scratches in it is a place where it has forgotten something.

---

You have been sitting a long time. The wood under you is warm the whole way through now, and when you shift your weight there is the faintest tackiness where your skin has been, a slick of resin that has come up out of the grain toward the pressure. It is not much. It would take a very long time. But the rule of the place does not know that you are only visiting, and somewhere in the root a load has been registered, and the answer to load is material.

Across the chamber a gasket on the north wall tightens and goes the color of weak tea. The beetles let go. The gray heads stop, all at once, and hang perfectly still.

Something lives here. It is about to breathe.
