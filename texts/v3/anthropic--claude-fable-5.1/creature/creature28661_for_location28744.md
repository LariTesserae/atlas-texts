The honest first answer is the animals, and the honest first answer is not wrong.

Start with the worms, because they are the ones who cannot be explained by any road. They are vent worms. They are exactly the vent worms of the deep ocean — the ones that live packed on the flanks of black smokers, heads in water near boiling, tails in water near freezing, in absolute dark under three kilometres of sea. Nothing about them has changed. Their red is the red of a haemoglobin built for a place where oxygen is a rumour. They did not climb here. There is no version of climbing that gets a worm from the sea floor to the roof of the sky.

They came in the rock. That is the only way to say it. The melt that built this cone rose from somewhere under the roots of the ocean floor, from a depth where the sea's basement is hot enough to be slow, and it carried what was living in the seams there the way a river carries seed — not as bodies, which would have burned, but as whatever a body is before it is a body, as the small hard grains that vent animals leave in the cracks against catastrophe. Most of that cargo cooked. A pocket of it did not, because the pocket cooled into this hollow, and this hollow keeps a rule that the rest of the mountain does not: here, what is at the bottom is also at the top. The worms live where the water comes out hottest. They cluster so close to the source that their tail-ends are bleached from cooking. They have found their smoker. They do not know it is a mountain.

The pale flat things with too many legs are the grazers, and they are the hollow's farmers in the sense that a place needs someone to eat what it makes. What the hollow makes is film: iron and sulphur coming out of the hot water as it cools against the walls, laid down as a rust-red skin a hair thick, renewed every hour. The grazers rasp it off with mouths like files. They shed constantly in the wet — their cast skins drop into the pool and go to the fish. They talk without stopping, and their talk is the pressure in your jaw. Watch where they lay. They lay only along the fractures where the water runs upward, so that the eggs are lifted with the stream into the fog beneath the roof, hatch there in the warm dark, and come down as young in the two-arm-length rain. The wrong-way water is a nursery. It is the one thing here that is unmistakably for something.

The mites are not individuals. There is no point trying to see them that way. They are what the walls look like when the walls are alive, the shimmer at the edge of the red, and they eat what the grazers leave, and the grazers eat them when the film is thin, and no one in the hollow would describe this as a relationship.

The shelled ones are the only residents you could call deliberate. They are slow. Their carapaces are the colour and texture of the young glass, and they spend most of their time motionless, which is why you see them only when they move, and when they move it is to strike stone. They knap. They are not good at it — four tries for a flake — but they are exactly as good as they need to be, and they hand the flake on, and the flake goes down into the worm-pelt and is used until it dulls, and then it is dropped and becomes a flake the rock made. No shelled thing has ever kept a tool overnight. Keeping is a habit of creatures who expect a tomorrow. The hollow has an afternoon.

And the fish, eyeless, finger-long, in water that should be a broth. They rise. They are taken from below. They are the only thing the hollow produces that crosses to the other side of the pool, and you notice, after a while, that they seem to be bred for it — that the fish are the rent.

---

Then the bird, which is the one resident that came from your side.

It is a mountain bird. It is the kind that lives in the highest crags on any range, black-and-grey and tough, nesting where there is nothing but rock and wind and a few insects blown up from below and frozen. It found the hollow the way such birds find anything: by going where the rest of the world would not. The cone was still hot enough to steam in the snow. It came down to the warmth on the first cold night after the rim cooled enough to stand on, and it was probably looking for the insects, and it found the grazers instead, and it stayed.

That was a week ago, or a month, or it was this afternoon. Time is one of the things the hollow keeps in a different order, and the bird has been here long enough to be arranged by it. It sleeps under the overhang in the far corner. It sleeps in the near corner. It stands on the lip above you and looks down at the crowd with the flat, calculating regard of an animal that has never once gone hungry here. It is one bird. You know this the way you know your own hand is one hand. When it lifts its head in the far corner, the head on the lip lifts too, not simultaneously but not separately either — the way a thing in water and its reflection move, if you had never been told which was which.

It still eats. It takes a grazer when one strays onto the dry shelf, and sometimes, delicately, a fish, and when it takes a fish something on the other side of the pool goes unfed and is, you feel rather than see, displeased. It preens the condensation from its feathers with a steady care that has nothing haunted in it. It is not lost. It is a bird that found the best place on the mountain and became, in staying, whatever staying makes you here. It is the only resident who could leave. When the light goes flat in the evening — the evening that does not come — it will unfold and lift into the freezing dark and go and be one bird again, on a ledge, in the wind, for a few hours. Then it will come back and be several. It has decided this is a fair exchange, in the way birds decide things, which is by continuing.

You watch it because it is the one whose life you can imagine. That is the trap of it.

---

Then the reflection.

On this side of the water the animals are many and small and busy. On that side they are few and large and still. This is not a different set of animals. Look at the way a reflected hunter waits beneath the spot where a fish will rise, and then look at the crowd of grazers on the ceiling above the same spot, and notice that they are doing the same thing — gathering, waiting, oriented toward the water — only there are thirty of them here and one of it there, and it is sleek and long where they are granular. It is as if the seam through the hollow is a lens, and on your side you see a thing scattered into a crowd, and on the far side you see the crowd gathered into a thing. The same appetite, at a different resolution.

If that is true of the grazers it is true of the worms, and the red carpet you are looking at is, from the other side, something very large and very patient lying along the bottom of the pool with its mouth at the source. It is true of the bird — one bird here, several places; there, perhaps, something that is only ever in one place and has many eyes. And it is true of the leaning figure at the rim, at a slight angle, printed twice by a shaking hand. There is no shadow of you in the pool because the pool is not showing you a reflection. It is showing you the other side, and on the other side you are the doubled thing, and your shadow fell somewhere you cannot see.

What lives in the reflection, then, is the hollow's residents seen truly. Or the hollow's residents are the reflection's, seen refracted. The hollow does not distinguish. It does not distinguish the flakes the shelled things make from the flakes the cooling glass makes, either, and it does not distinguish the bird from the bird, and after a while you understand that this not-distinguishing is not carelessness. It is the resident's nature. It is what actually lives here.

---

Because the fourth answer, the one that takes longest, is that the hollow is occupied by the seam itself, and the seam is alive in the way weather is alive, or a fire, or a tide — a process that keeps itself going and can be said to want things.

It came up in the melt. Deep in the earth, at the depth this lava was born, pressure and heat are enough that certain separations have not yet been made: up and down are a matter of taste, one and several are a matter of accounting, here and there are the same word. That is the ordinary state of the deep. It is only near the surface, in the cold, that the world crystallises into the version you can walk on, where water falls and a bird is one bird and a room is as wide as it looks. The melt rose fast. It carried a bubble of the deep's undecidedness up with it — a bead of the unsorted state, the way obsidian carries a bead of trapped gas — and when the lava froze last week the bead froze into it, not as rock but as a condition of the rock. The overhang cooled around it. The fractures crazed through it. The line you cannot find on the back wall, where water changes its mind, is the bubble's edge.

That is what lives in the hollow. A small quantity of the interior of the earth, breathing.

It breathes through the fog. Heat rises through it and the water lifts and falls and lifts, and the crowd on the floor draws in and spreads out with the pulse of it, and this is its respiration. It eats heat from below, and the heat is finite. It is the youngest living thing on the mountain — a week old, a month — and the stuff it is made of is the oldest, and it will not see the year out. The rock is cooling. Every day the source runs a degree less hot, and the worms shift a hair's breadth inward, and the two-arm-length rain runs a beat slower, and the seam, in whatever way rock knows things, knows. That is the hurry. That is why the grazers lay every hour and the shelled things knap and drop and knap and the fish are bred to be taken and nothing is kept: the whole engine is a lifetime pressed into a cooling curve, an ecology built to be complete in a season, every generation the last one, and every animal in it working with the calm of creatures who have never been told what a winter is.

When it goes, it will go quietly. The film will stop forming. The grazers will thin, the worms will dry to red threads in the fractures, the pool will shrink to a stain, and the water will remember which way is down. The hollow will be four strides across and no deeper, roofed by a lip of dead black lava, with some odd little flakes on a shelf and a scatter of things like rust. The bird will be one bird, on a ledge, in the wind. It will not come back to the cone. It will not know why.

---

So that is who lives there.

A crowd from the bottom of the sea, at altitude, in a hurry. A bird that stayed and became several and does not mind. Something on the far side of the water that is fewer and larger and is fed on fish and is, in its patience, perhaps the same crowd seen whole. And beneath all of them, holding them, the resident proper: a bead of the deep, a week old, warm, breathing fog, keeping its one rule, dying at the pace of cooling glass.

And a fifth. A recent one. Whose knees are hot and whose shadow has gone somewhere, who has been at the rim for an afternoon that will not end, and under whose palms the new rock has begun, without any sound at all, to weep — not because the rock is doing anything to the hands, but because the seam has stopped distinguishing between them.
