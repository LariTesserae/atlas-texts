Most of what lives on the plateau has already been described, because up there the distinction between a place and its inhabitants does not hold. The grazers are the insides of the cushions. The red predators are the tunnels' maintenance. The amphibians are their cups, one to a cup, and the cups are the ledges' drip, and the drip is the rock's ordinary condition. You cannot describe the basalt without describing what drinks from it. Whoever asks what lives there has already been handed most of the answer.

But there is one thing more, and it is the last to be noticed, because it looks like the birds.

Not at first. At first it looks like nothing — a slightly taller hummock in the canopy, grey-green, beaded with wet, steaming a little on a cold morning along with everything else. Then it moves. It is standing up. It has been standing within twenty meters of you for some time, and it is a person, or near enough that the word will have to do until a better one arrives.

They are small. Chest-high to an ordinary adult, heavy through the trunk, with short limbs and broad, flat, splayed feet that spread their weight across the moss skin the way a snowshoe spreads it across snow. They are never dry. Their skin is the grey of wet lichen, and in fact wet lichen is on it: crusts on the shoulders and the backs of the hands, ruffs at the throat, a fine dark liverwort in the creases of the elbows and the backs of the knees that they neither remove nor appear to notice. Their hair is long, because nothing here is cut, and it has felted into a mat that holds water like a cushion does. Their teeth are the dull blue-grey of tin. Their eyes are very large and very pale and slow to move. Under the cloud deck the light is even and low and there is little contrast to track, and so they look at one thing for a long time before they look at another.

They are cold to the touch. But if you lay your hand flat on the chest — as they do to one another, in greeting or in nothing — you feel the warmth a few millimeters down, exactly where you would feel it in a cushion. They run cold outside and warm inside and exchange across the wall. Up there this is not a figure of speech. It is a job.

They sleep inside. The oldest cushions, the house-sized ones, so tight-grown and ancient that their many chambers have coalesced into one, have hollows in them large enough to lie down in, curled, and this is where the people are at night and through the long cold of the early day. The hollow is lined with the moss's dead tissue and is warm and dark and smells of clean rot and animal. The grazers are in the walls; you can hear them planing. The people reach into the wall and pull them out and eat them. They eat the moss too, from the inside, at a rate the cushion tolerates, and what the people give back the moss draws up through itself the way it draws up everything. A cushion with a family in it is warmer than one without and grows faster on the outside. The people know this. They know it without a word for it, the way the birds know their routes.

A child is born in the hollow, pale and nearly hairless, with its eyes closed. It stays there for some years, in the dark, eating what is put in its hands, and its eyes open slowly over that time and are enormous when it first climbs out onto the canopy into the grey day. Some children do not climb out. They stay, and grow pale and soft and very large, and eat and are eaten from, and become the interior of that cushion permanently — one to a cup, a lineage, like the amphibians. Nobody thinks of these as unfortunate. They are the ones who went in rather than out. The cushion runs hotter for it.

The ones who go out, walk. That is the whole of their occupation.

There is no edge to reach, so they walk in loops — vast, shallow ovals, a lifetime across, following lines in the canopy that are a slightly different color and have been for longer than the birds have been walking theirs. Six or eight in a line, as the birds go. They pause at intervals and kneel and drive an arm through the moss skin to the shoulder and come up with a grazer, or a fistful of the warm chewed stuff from the tunnels, and eat it, and lift their heads, and go on. They do not go at the birds' speed or on the birds' routes, and where the two cross the moss is a third color, and both parties pause there a little longer than elsewhere.

What they are for, in the plateau's accounting, is carrying. The red predators move material from cushion to cushion in their gut. The people move it farther. Everything on them is colonized: the lichen on their shoulders seeds the cushions they sleep in; the spores in their hair fall wherever they lie down; the grazer eggs that pass through them intact — and a great many do — are deposited a season's walk from where they were swallowed. They are the plateau's long circulation, the slow current that moves what the tunnels cannot. A person is a corridor that walks.

They eat fern-rib for the mineral in it, peeling the ribs off the fronds with a thumbnail, and this is why their teeth are the color they are. On still afternoons, when the cloud is thickest, one of them will stand with a palm flat against a fern trunk and wait. When the thread comes down, the fern takes it and the person takes a little, and for an instant the whole room-sized patch — fern, moss, the veins in the back of the hand — lights faintly through its own tissue, and then doesn't. They do this the way you would drink. Afterward they are warmer, and their eyes are wider, and they walk a long way without pausing.

They speak, though not in a way you would at first recognize as speech, because sound does not carry there. Talk is mouth to ear, or else mouth pressed to the moss so that the other, ear pressed to the moss a meter off, hears it arrive through the cushion along with the ticking. A conversation, then, is something that happens lying down, faces to the ground, and it is slow, and there are long stretches of listening to the plateau work in between. Their language has few words for distance and none for direction, since there is only along-the-route and off-it. There is no word for down. There is a word for the underside of the cloud that means, more or less, *the place the drip goes*, and it is not a word anyone uses often. There is a word for edge, but it means the rim of a failure.

Because the failures are theirs — in the way that everything up there belongs to whatever uses it.

When a cushion dies somewhere and its chamber caves and there is, for the first time in centuries at that spot, a hollow with bare weeping basalt at the bottom, the people come to it. Not quickly. They bend the loop of their walk to pass it, and when they arrive they kneel at the rim as you would and look down at the stone. The drip is suddenly audible. The air smells of iron. They are cold there in a way they are cold nowhere else, and they say very little even in their fashion, and after a while one of them — the oldest, usually, or one who is sick, or one who has simply decided — climbs down and lies on the basalt, in the wet, and the rest go back up onto the canopy and take up the route again.

The neighbors move in over the following days. They move in faster than they otherwise would. They grow across the person the way the moss grows through a dead predator in a tunnel — the warmth first, then the rest — and the failure closes and is sealed, and there is a new cushion there, slightly warmer than those around it for a long time afterward. Eventually it has a chamber. Eventually there is a family in the chamber.

None of the ones who lie down in the failures are dead when they do it. This is the one thing about them that is difficult, and there is nothing to be done except say it plainly. They are cold, and the stone is cold, and the moss is warm, and they lie down, and the moss comes.

They have no account of this. They are not a making people — they shape no stone and stack none, and they do not stack explanations either. What they have in place of belief is timing, worn as smooth as the birds'. They can tell from the ticking through the moss when a cushion is about to fail, well before it does. They know how long a failure takes to close with someone in it and how long without. They know, walking their loop, when they are approaching a place where one of them went down, and they pause there, on the new moss, and kneel, and reach an arm through the skin, and eat what they find, and lift their heads, and go on. If that is a ceremony it is indistinguishable from lunch, and they would not understand the question.

They know something is beneath them. The water they weep into the moss goes down through the fractures, warm and rich and carrying, and out below the cloud whose underside none of them has seen. They do not wonder what it feeds. *The place the drip goes.* The word is exactly as large as it needs to be.

So the one standing near you turns, finally, having looked at you as long as it looks at anything, and walks off along the paler line in the canopy, not fast, and after some minutes there are others beside it, six or eight, and the line pauses, and kneels, and each drives an arm down through the moss to the shoulder and comes up with something small and pale and eats it. Then they stand, and the hiss resumes — it never stopped — and behind them the moss along the route is a slightly different color, and in front of them it is too, and nothing has changed, at any scale you could measure, in any direction you could walk. That is what lives there. It would be wrong to say they are people. It would be wrong to say they are not. They are the part of the works that walks, and they have been walking for so long that the route has taken on the form of the walking rather than the other way around.
