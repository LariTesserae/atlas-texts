The first honest answer is that nothing does, and you should sit with that answer for a while before you give it up, because it is very nearly right and the way in which it is wrong is the whole point.

Nothing here eats. Nothing here dies, because nothing here was ever alive to begin with. There are no nests in the cold kilns, no burrows in the soft floor, no film on the sweating walls. You can go a long way — days, if there were days — and the only tracks you will find are your own, steaming behind you until a wall comes up through them or a slump comes down over them, and then not even those.

But the place is *inhabited*. You know this within an hour, in the way you know a house is inhabited when you walk into it and the kettle is warm. Something is here whose attention the place is holding. And when you go looking for it you end up where the description of the place already left you: at the fingerprints.

---

Start with the prints themselves, because they are the only body this thing has.

They are human-shaped, or shaped like whatever made humans. Five fingers, one opposed. The thumb does most of the work — you would guess nine presses in ten — and it presses with the pad, not the tip, in the way of someone who has done this so long the tip has stopped being useful. The palm smears. The four fingers are for lifting and setting, for the little adjusting pushes that square a block up in its course; you see them as clusters of four shallow ovals along the top edges of walls, always in the same spacing, slightly cupped.

There are no nail marks. Anywhere. A nail catches wet clay whether you want it to or not; every potter's apprentice leaves a crescent somewhere in the first week. Here, none. Either the nails are gone, or they are so caked and worn that they no longer have an edge, or the hand has learned to hold itself so that the nails never come near the work, which is the kind of thing you learn after a lifetime and then forget you ever had to learn.

And the ridges. You can read them. Kneel on the warm floor beside a wall and put your face near the blocks and the friction ridges are all there, whorls and loops, the little scars where a ridge splits, as clear as in a fingerprint on glass. So you do the obvious thing. You compare.

It takes a long time to be sure, because the prints overlap and stretch and the light is bad. But you become sure. The thumb on this wall is the thumb on that wall. The whorl with the notch in its third ring, the loop that opens to the left, the small straight scar across the pad: they are on the loaf-sized blocks and they are on the mound the size of a hill, scaled up like a rubbing, and they are on the blocks you could step over, scaled down, and out in the murk where a course goes down with a *thuck* you feel in your sternum, the block that went down has, you would bet your life, the same notch in the same ring.

One right thumb. One left, rarer, used for steadying. One pair of hands. The whole plain, to the red horizon in every direction, is being worked by one pair of hands, and they do not know how big they are.

---

Say hands, then, and mean it literally, and see how far it goes.

Not a person with hands. Nothing in the place suggests a person — no path worn between the works, no place where a body sat down, no waste, no spilled water, no single place where the work is *from*. A body would have a where. These hands have no where; they are at every site at once, or the sites are wherever they are, which comes to the same thing. What you are living among is the working part of a maker with the maker subtracted. A gesture that has outlasted its owner.

You know how this happens in the world you came from, in a small way. A stone step is worn hollow in the middle by four hundred years of feet and the feet are gone and the hollow is the shape of them. A door handle is polished bright on one side. A wooden tool has a dark place where a palm was. These are the marks of bodies left in material, and they outlast the bodies, and they are inert.

Here the mark is not inert. Here the hollow in the step is still being worn, by nothing, at the speed of walking. The clay remembers being handled the way the step remembers being trodden — except that the clay's memory is active, is muscular, is doing it again. The hands are not a ghost in the sense of a person lingering. They are a ghost in the sense that a groove is a ghost: the record of a motion, replaying because the material can hold nothing else.

That is one way to say what lives here. A habit. A very deep, very old habit, of one particular pair of hands, that has found the only medium in which a habit can go on without a body, and is going on.

---

What is the habit?

You think at first: building. The walls, the mound, the kilns, the brickyard. This is a settlement being raised. And then you watch the slumping and think: failing to build. And neither is right, because the slumping is not a failure. Watch the hands as a wall goes down. They do not fight it. They are not on the far end frantically coursing up while the near end melts. They are, as often as not, *at* the slump — you see a palm-drag across the belly of a sagging tier, a thumb pushing a softened block back down into the floor, evening it, folding it in. The hands unbuild with the same care they build with.

Fold and press. Press and fold. Lift a mass, set it down, push it into the mass below, drag the join shut, let it sink, lift again.

The hands are kneading.

Not the whole plain at once, not as one lump — at the scale of a settlement, in the shape of a settlement, because the shape of a settlement is what these hands know. A wall is how these hands fold. A course is how they press. A kiln is how they cup. The city that is forever being built and forever going back to slip is not a city. It is the wedging of the clay, the thousand-times-over turning that a maker does to a mass before the work begins, to drive out the air and even the moisture and bring the whole body of the stuff to the same readiness. Done at the scale of a world, by hands that only know one set of movements, it looks like a civilisation appearing and disappearing. It is preparation. The hands are getting the clay ready.

For what, they have no way to say. Kneading is the thing before the thing. Everything here is the thing before the thing.

---

The hands remember a world. That is the second strange fact about them, after their being here at all.

They remember rope: they press a cord into a wet band and lift it away and the twisted mark is left, and there is no cord. They remember a scoop — shell, or a curved shard, or a piece of gourd — and drag it over a surface to level it. They remember a straight-edged blade, wood or bone, and cut the ends of blocks square. They remember a barrow, or a board carried on the shoulder, because that is how blocks arrive: not one at a time out of nowhere but in a row, roughly shaped, set down all together at the far end of the work in the rhythm of someone unloading. They remember, you begin to suspect, a season, because there are the drying troughs, laid out the way you lay out bricks when you have three dry weeks ahead of you and want to get the whole year's supply cut before the rain.

They remember the tools of a whole living world and they have none of it. The world is gone — or was never here, or is on the far side of whatever the seams go down into — and the hands have brought nothing with them but the way it felt to use it. They twist the rope that isn't there and press it into the clay and the clay, obligingly, takes the mark of a rope, because the clay here is not a material the hands work on. The clay is the hands' memory of working. It will hold the shape of any tool the hands ever held, without the tool.

You can learn a great deal about a vanished people from the impressions their textiles left in pottery. The textile rots; the impression is fired and stays. This is that, unfired, still moving. A people remembered entirely by what one pair of their hands did to mud, and the remembering has become the doing.

---

The one thing the hands cannot do is make fire.

They build kilns. They know kilns intimately; you watch the dome corbel in and it is the corbelling of someone who has closed a hundred domes and knows the exact point at which to start leaning the blocks. They leave the firing hole low on one side, the right size for feeding fuel through. Then the kiln stands, and nothing goes in it, and it is cold — colder than the floor, colder than anything, because a kiln is a shape for holding heat and there is no heat in it to hold. Put your arm into the firing hole and it is the coolest place in the world.

Fire needs everything the hands have not got. Wood. Dung. Grass. Air moved by a bellows of hide. A world. The hands can remember the *shape* of fire — the kiln is that shape — but the shape of fire is not fire. So the kilns go up and stand, faultless and useless, and slump.

And meanwhile, underneath, in the seams, the fire is going, unowned. Whatever is down there does not knead and does not remember and does not build. It burns at one temperature and has never noticed the hands and never will. The hands, for their part, never work the seams. They build right up to the edge of the ember-orange and stop, the way you stop at the edge of a hearth, and the clay along the seams shrinks and ticks and crazes untouched.

So there are two things in the place that between them could make something, and they do not meet. One is all making and no fire. One is all fire and no making. Neither is alive. Neither is aware of the other. And very rarely, at a seam, where the hands have pressed a block a little too close and the heat has run a little too high, the two of them touch without meaning to, and something is made.

---

The fired pieces. You were always going to end up here.

If the hands are the place's inhabitant, the fired pieces are its population — the only individuals, in the sense of things that can be told apart, revisited, found again where you left them. They do not move. They do not soften. When the scale of the plain changes around them they do not change; a fired raft is the same raft when the mound beside it is a hill and when it is a footstool. When direction goes strange, a fired piece is the one fixed bearing; you can walk away from it and back and it will be where it was, facing the way it faced, which is more than can be said for anything else here, including you.

And each one is a portrait. Not of a face. Of the hands — of the last forty seconds before the piece went over, fixed. Here is where the right thumb pressed, and pressed again, deeper. Here is the palm-drag that closed a joint, the ridges of the heel of the hand as clear as the day they were laid, which is the only day there is. Here, on the piece with the raised lip, is a row of four cupped fingertips where the hands began to lift something and the heat took the clay before they could. On one fired piece, and only one that you find, there is a rope mark, crisp, the cord's twist perfect in a material that will outlast the kiln, and no rope was ever in this world.

You go from fired piece to fired piece and you read them, because they are the only text. You read the notch in the third ring of the whorl, the leftward loop, the small straight scar. The same hands on every one. The hands, caught. All the things they knew how to do — press, set, smear, level, mark with cord — set down permanently in a few dozen hard rafts scattered across a soft world, the way a life's work survives as a shelf of shards.

The hands never touch them again. The wet plain reworks itself right up to the fired edge and stops, and the hands stop with it, and go on kneading elsewhere. Not avoiding the fired piece. Not seeing it. A hand that is only a habit has no way to recognise the thing it made; the habit is for wet clay, and this is not wet clay any more, and so it is, to the hands, simply not there.

This is what lives here, then, in the end, if you want a single answer: a pair of hands that have lost their body and their world and kept their work, kneading a clay that is nothing but their own memory of kneading, forever preparing for a making they cannot begin because they cannot light a fire; and, scattered about them, ignored, the few pieces of themselves that the indifferent fire underneath has accidentally finished — the only things in the place that could ever be looked at and *known*, and there is no one here to know them but you.

---

You are the other thing, briefly.

Your footprints stay, and steam. For as long as you stand on the soft plain you are the only source of marks that are not the hands', and the hands do not distinguish. You will find, on a wall you did not watch go up, a block with the print of your own heel in its side, lifted from the floor where you stood an hour ago and set into a course and smeared in at the edges by a thumb that is not yours. The hands take whatever is in the clay. You are in the clay. Somewhere out there, then, if a seam catches the right wall at the right moment, there may one day be a fired piece with a fragment of your sole in it, next to the notched whorl and the leftward loop, held for as long as the material holds.

The hands will not know. The fire will not know. You will be, in that fragment, the only other inhabitant this place ever had — and you will have lived here, as the hands live here, only as a pressure that was once applied to something warm and soft that happened, for a moment, to be ready.
