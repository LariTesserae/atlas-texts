The easy answer is that nothing lives here.

Things are present. Things are installed. The beetles on the pitchers, the frogs at the vents, the ants on their spiral in two lanes — these are here in the way a valve is in a pipe. They were born into their slots and they will die in them and they will fall into the slot below. You would not say the beetles live in the pitcher any more than you would say the blood lives in the vein. They are the pitcher's outer surface with legs. The frogs are the vent's lip, given the ability to ring. The mites are a color. Even the salamander in its moss, orange and unmoving, is less an animal that has chosen this place than a place that has, over some long span, taken the shape of an animal in order to hold still more thoroughly. If you came back in a decade and it had not moved, you would not be surprised. If it had, you would wonder what had gone wrong with the room.

So: populations, not residents. Tissue. The chamber is alive the way a lung is alive, and to ask who lives in it is to ask who lives in a lung.

And yet you stood in it and you were not tissue. You stooped under the arch and straightened up and there was room for you. The original account calls that an accident of geometry, and moves on quickly, and you notice that it moves on quickly.

---

Try the other easy answer. The mass lives here.

But the mass does not live anywhere in the sense you mean. It is the size of a district. It has no more of a home than weather does. This room is one of its pores — an exhaust manifold, a condenser, a place where spent breath gathers — and a body does not live in its own exhalation. The mass is around the room, above and below it, pressing on it from every side with that four-second throb, and the room is precisely the place where the mass is not.

That is the thing to hold onto. The chamber is the one volume in kilometers of green where the plant has withdrawn. Nothing on the floor. Nothing on the lower walls. Outside, the vegetation is so dense it is nearly solid, and every cubic centimeter of air has been taken and filled with leaf; inside, air. Wet, warm, breathing, unclaimed air. A body that fills everything it touches has left this space open, and has kept it open, for centuries, against the entire pressure of its own growth.

You can call that an accident. You can also notice that an empty room in a full house is what you make when you expect someone.

---

Look again at the fern on the eastern wall.

Look at what is on it.

The account lists the bird last and briefly, as a curiosity — the one large animal, dark, beak built for the pitchers, eyes closed against the wet. It gives it half a paragraph and the white cone under it and moves on. But go back and stand under the fern and look up at it for a while, for as long as it has been looking at nothing, and it begins to separate from the rest of the room in a way the other creatures do not.

It is heavy. Heavier than a bird of that size should be; the humidity has gotten into it over the years and never left, and the feathers lie flat and dark and faintly matted, with a greenish bloom along the shoulders where something has begun to grow that is not feather. The bill is long and slightly downcurved and pale at the tip from use. The feet grip the fern's rachis in a lock that does not look voluntary any longer. The eyes, when they are closed, are closed in the way a door is closed, not in the way a sleeper's eyes are closed — deliberately, and with something behind them.

Here is the difference. Every other thing in this room was born here or came down from above through the mass's own vessels. The beetles hatched on the pitchers. The frogs spawned in the condensate at the vents. The ants came down the spiral from wherever the spiral goes. The salamander, the spider, the mites — you cannot imagine them arriving; they are simply what those patches of moss have always contained.

The bird came in.

It came in the way you did. Through the failed arch, at some point — a wet season, maybe, when the black water rose and the young birds of that year were scattered and one of them found a gap where two roots did not meet and pushed through into amber light and a throb it had never heard before and a fern held out flat like hands at a fire. And it landed. And a beetle fell from a pitcher. And it has been here since.

It is the only thing in the chamber that entered. And the arch is still there. It has never been closed; the roots have not grown to meet; the gap is exactly as wide as it was. The bird could stoop under it, as you did, and go back out into the shrieking heat. It does not. That is what makes it the resident and not the component: a component cannot leave. A resident does not.

---

Watch it long enough and you notice what it is doing, which is not waiting.

The account says its head comes around before a fallen beetle has hit the water. Consider what that requires. The room is loud. The throb comes up through the perch and the frogs ring without cease and the wall on the north side sighs and something slaps like a valve seating, and inside all of that there is a beetle, three centimeters long, losing its footing on a waxy rim seven meters away. The bird does not react to the splash. It reacts before the splash. It is not hearing the fall. It is hearing whatever precedes the fall — the changed rhythm of the column on that pitcher, the fractional stall in the traffic as one beetle's grip goes, the sound a very small thing makes when it stops being attached. And it has learned this. It was not born knowing the beetles' timing. It arrived knowing nothing about this room and it has spent years inside the engine listening, and now it can tell you, without opening its eyes, which pitcher will drop next.

More than that. When a frog is going to die at the vents, the ringing changes first — one bell-note in the crowd goes flat and slow — and the bird's head tilts by a degree toward that vent, and holds, and the frog falls, and the pitcher catches it, and the bird settles. It is not eating the frog. It has no interest in the frog. It simply knew.

Every animal in this room is *in* the throb. The bird is the only one listening to it.

That is what it does, and it is a kind of thought the mass does not have. The mass has no anticipation. The mass has settled so long ago that it has forgotten there was ever anything to settle, and its regularity is not patience but the absence of any alternative. Nothing here is becoming; it has become. Except that there is one animal on the eastern wall that is still, very slowly, getting better at hearing.

---

Now try to see the arrangement from below, from the fern's point of view, or from whatever it is the mass has instead of a point of view.

The cone of white under the perch is years deep. The fern's roots have found it and gone in and the fern is very large — larger than any fern should be on a wall where nothing else grows, in a room the mass has kept scrupulously bare. The mass suffers no epiphytes on its lower walls. It suffers this one. It has, in fact, held a fissure open for it two meters up, at exactly the height where a bird with a downcurved bill could sit and reach a falling beetle before the water does.

And the cone feeds the fern and the fern holds the bird and the bird keeps the beetles honest, in a small way, and eats what would otherwise foul the basin, and its droppings go down the polished tracks with everything else and home through the sump. It is in the loop. Of course it is in the loop. Nothing is in this room that is not in the loop.

But there is a difference between a thing that grew into a slot and a thing for which a slot was grown. The frogs did not need a room. The beetles did not need a room. The ants have their spiral and would run it in solid wood. The one creature in this chamber that requires *space* — a volume of open air to see across, a distance for a beetle to fall through, a clear wall to be perched on — is the one that was not born here.

The mass hollowed this chamber, the account says, not by rot but by use. It reabsorbed the interior wood the way a body reabsorbs bone it no longer needs. And you ask: what use? A pump does not need a nave-sized void in its housing. A condenser does not need eight meters of headroom. The circulation would run just as well through a cavity the size of a fist.

Unless the void is the use. Unless what the mass reabsorbed the wood *for* was room. Room for an ear. A body the size of a district, running at one steady rhythm for longer than anyone has been alive, and somewhere in the middle of it, held open at some cost against its own growth, a chamber the shape of a listener.

---

You do not have to believe the mass intends this. Nothing here intends. But intention is not the only way a shape gets made. Water running down a wall for long enough polishes a channel, and the channel is the shape of the water; and a room kept empty for long enough becomes the shape of whatever comes in and stays.

So this is what lives here. Not the mass, which is everywhere and therefore nowhere. Not the beetles or the frogs or the ants, which are the room's own flesh. Not even, quite, the bird — or not this bird. The account is careful about that: in a hundred years you would find the bird, *or one exactly like it*. This one will die on its perch some year, and its grip will finally let go, and it is too large for the pitchers; it will fall the eight meters through the amber air and hit the basin, and the water will take it to the low point, and the soft pale sump the size of a dinner plate will open a little wider than usual, and close. And the fern will lose its cone and go a little hungry. And the arch will still be there. And some wet season a young dark bird, scattered, will find a gap where two roots do not meet.

What lives here is the guest. The one that enters and does not leave. The tenant of the emptiness — whatever it is that a body that fills everything makes a hollow for, and keeps, and listens to listening.

You stooped in under the arch. There was room for you. The throb, the account insists, has no more interest in you than a turbine has in a moth, and that is true, and you left, and it did not follow you or notice. But the room was your height. The light was easy on your eyes. The air was warm as a mouth. And on the eastern wall something dark and heavy with its eyes closed turned its head one degree toward the arch as you bent to go, and held, and then settled.
