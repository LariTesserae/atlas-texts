Start with the birds, because they are what you see.

They are smaller than they look from below. Below, the movement of them against the wall makes them seem the size of thrushes; in the hand — if one ever came to the hand — each would be scarcely heavier than the wet mud of its own cell. They are dark all over, a brown that reads as black in the fog, with the long stiff crescent wings of an animal that does almost everything in the air and the tiny hooked feet of an animal that does nothing at all on the ground. They cannot perch. They land onto a vertical face and hold, and shuffle sideways along the ledge with their wings half-raised like someone carrying a tray, and then they duck and vanish into the bottle-neck of a cell.

Their eyes are large and set forward and always look wet, and in the fog they are of very little use. What the birds use instead is a sound too small to carry: a soft, dry, ticking click, made in the throat, repeated as they come down through the white. You do not hear it unless one passes within an arm's length, and then it sounds like a fingernail on a coat button. They are reading the wall with it. They know their own cell the way you know your own door in the dark — by the count of ledges, by the shape the click comes back in, by the smell of the mud, which is mixed with their own saliva and belongs to them the way a fingerprint does.

They go up. This is the thing about them that nothing else in the trench does daily. In the morning, while the floor is still full of milk, the birds pour out of the galleries and spiral up through the fog and come out above it into the sky over the plain, where the warmth off the grass lifts columns of small insects, and there they hang all day — a smear of black flecks turning in the blue, half a kilometre up, visible from the plain as a kind of weather. Then they come down again with their throats full. Every fed nestling in the city has been fed on the sky. The trench is dark and cold and slow, and its most numerous citizens make their living in bright fast air and only sleep here; they are the one thread that connects the place to the world it is set into, and they carry the thread up and down a thousand times a day without anyone on either end noticing.

They lay two eggs, sometimes one. The young sit for weeks in the dark of the cell hearing the drip and the traffic and the clicking of returning parents, and by the time they come out into the fog they already know the city by sound. They do not learn it; they were raised inside it.

Old birds die in their cells and are not removed. The next season's building goes over them. So the lower tiers of the city — the pale, calcite-hardened tiers with moss on the ledges — are also its cemetery, and if you broke into one you would find small bones gone the colour of the rock, and mud, and older mud, and older bones under that.

---

Now the snails, because they are what you step on.

They are the size of a thumb-joint, with shells a deep glossy brown, thick — there is lime in everything they eat — and slightly translucent at the lip where the new growth is. The bodies are near-black and very wet, and they move in crowds, always in crowds, twenty or thirty in a loose file across a moss cushion, the followers keeping to the slime of the leaders because the fog condenses first on whoever is in front. They eat moss, and the film of algae on wet calcite, and the soft mud of fallen nest-fragments, and a bird that has come down dead, which they will cover entirely and reduce to feather and bone in three days. At night they climb the city itself and rasp at the lower galleries for the lime in the mud, and the birds let them, or eat the small ones, and press the empty shells into new cells as aggregate. There are shells in every wall. Where the city has fallen, the rubble underneath is white with them.

They have no defence but slowness and numbers and the shell, and in this place that is enough. Nothing here is fast except the birds, and the birds are not interested in anything that does not fly.

---

Under the snails, in the moss, is the oldest population, and this is the point at which the place begins to explain itself.

Lift a hand's depth of liverwort off a block and there is a second landscape underneath — dark, dripping, cold, a chambered thing of stems and rootlets and standing water. It is full of small pale animals. Springtail-like things that flick. Woodlouse-things the colour of candle wax, slow, their many legs moving in a wave. A blind shrimp, colourless, in the water that runs through the moss and out along the seeps. And in the deeper seeps and beneath the oldest dams, where the water pools in the dark under stone, a soft, long, white, four-legged thing with a flattened tail and no eyes at all, only two dents in the skin where eyes would be, which breathes through frilled red gills and moves perhaps a metre in a night.

These were the cave's animals. They lived in the river in the dark under the plain for longer than there has been a plain in its present shape, and one wet season the roof came off their world, and they did not die. They went down. The moss came over the rubble faster than the light could kill them, and the moss is a cave — a cave diced into a million small chambers, dark, wet, constant, cold, with water moving through it — and they simply moved house into the walls of their own house, which had been rearranged. There is more dark in the trench now than there was when the roof stood. It has been divided finely and spread over everything, and they live in it as they always did.

The white thing with gills has never seen the sky and never will. It knows the collapse only as a change in the taste of the water.

---

The fish are the same story from the other side.

They are pale, finger-long, with skin you can see the blood through and eyes that are either very small or gone under a film. They hunt by touch and by the pressure of the water on their flanks, in the milky pools where the silt has not yet settled — the upper pools, closest to the arch where the river comes out of what is left of the cave. In those pools they are safe: nothing can see them, and they need to see nothing. But the dams settle the silt. Each pool down the flight is clearer than the last, and in the clear ones the fish are visible from above, and they die. So there is a slow pressure on them, generation by generation, that pushes them upstream — back toward the arch, back toward the dark they came out of — and the lower pools are nearly empty of them, and the heron-thing knows this, and stands mostly in the middle ones, where the water is clear enough to see and cloudy enough that the fish have not yet learned to be afraid.

---

The builders. You will not see them, so this is mostly inference.

They are about the length of a man's arm, heavy, low, and wrapped in a fur so dense that water beads off it as off the moss. Their ears are tiny and can be closed. Their eyes are small and, it seems, not much used. What they have instead is a face full of whiskers — long, stiff, sensitive, spread in a fan — and hearing that works through water better than through air. They listen to the river. This is the whole of their craft. A dam is right when the water going over it makes almost no sound, because a dam that roars deafens the household that lives inside it, and a household that cannot hear cannot tell a rising flood from a settling silt from something walking on the bank. They build quiet the way other animals build strong, and the quiet turns out to be the same thing as strong, because water that is not hurried does not cut.

They eat the bark of the willow-things and the fat roots of the sedge, and they cut willow in the autumn and sink it in the pools beside the lodges and eat it under the ice all winter, moving between the store and the lodge in water that is black and a little above freezing. Each dam is a family: a founding pair and two or three years of young, the eldest of whom will leave, when they leave, by going downstream to the next unclaimed stretch of river and starting a dam of their own. The oldest dams — the ones that have become islands with trees on them — belong to the oldest line, and the lodge inside has been rebuilt over itself so many times that the current occupants sleep two metres above the floor of their great-grandparents' room.

The young are born in the lodge in total dark. The first thing they know is the sound of water going over the dam correctly.

They never go up onto the plain. They have no reason to, and they do not like open sky, and there is a tendency in the place, which you begin to notice, for everything that lives here to prefer a ceiling.

---

The grazers have the opposite history, which is what makes them interesting.

They are plain animals. Their ancestors lived in the grass and the wind above, and some of them — a few each generation — came down the rubble ramps after the collapse, for the water or the sedge or because they were pushed, and having come down they stayed. They are the size of a small deer and the colour of wet slate, and the coat has grown so dense that when one stands still in the fog the fog collects on the outer hairs and does not reach the skin. Their hooves have spread. Their legs are shorter than the plain form's. They have lost most of the plain animal's habit of looking up — there is very little to see, and what hunts them here does not come from where a plain animal would look — and they graze with their heads down in a settled, unwatchful way that would get them killed within a week on the grass.

What they listen for is rock. When a block shifts in the wall — a sound like a door being tried, far off — every grey head comes up together and they stand and wait, and if the sound comes again they move, not away, but toward the middle of the floor. They have learned where things fall.

They can leave. The ramps go both ways. A few go up each year, into a world of wind and wolves and dry grass, and a few come down. But the population is a population of stayers, and the stayers have children, and the children are a little greyer and a little shorter-legged and a little less inclined to look at the sky than the ones before.

---

Two predators, and they divide the day between them.

The heron-thing owns the light. It is a pale bluish grey that is exactly the colour of the fog at noon, and it is the one animal in the trench that seems to have been made for this exact place — legs long enough to stand in a pool, neck long enough to strike from stillness, and a patience that outlasts everything. It stands in the middle pools where the fish are visible and unwary, or on the meadow edge where a frog will sit under a crowd of snails, or at the foot of the city after a fall, walking the rubble and picking nestlings out of broken cells. It takes anything slow. It also goes up: it lifts over the rim in the afternoon and is seen on the plain at the edge of pools there, and then comes back to roost in the trench because the trench is where the food is at dusk. It is the only thing here that lives in both worlds by choice and treats neither as home.

The other predator owns the dark, and it lives in the arch.

At the upper end of the trench, where the collapse stopped, the river comes out from under perhaps two hundred metres of roof that did not fall. That is the last of the cave. Nothing green grows more than a few body-lengths inside it. It is entirely black, and it drips, and it is the coldest place in a cold place, and something lives there that the trench never sees and always hears. Broad-winged, soft-feathered, with a face that is mostly a dish for gathering sound — an owl or something very like one, coloured like wet stone. It hunts by hearing. It comes out of the arch after the fog has closed and drifts down the length of the trench at the height of the city, and it makes no noise at all, and it takes what makes noise. A bird that calls. A snail-heavy frog that moves too fast. A grazer's foal that bleats. A builder that surfaces with a splash.

It is the reason the place is quiet. Not the fog, not the moss — those hold sound, but the owl is why nobody makes any. The birds' click is pitched below what it can hear, and the dams are tuned so the river does not cover the sound of an approach, and the grazers tear sedge softly, and every animal that has lived here more than a few generations has been made silent by the thing in the arch, which has been there since before the roof fell and has simply continued to hunt the cave after the cave became a valley.

---

The moss should be counted, and the ferns, and the willow-things, because they are the landlord.

The moss is not many plants. It is a few kinds, each in vast extent, and over the rubble ramps the cushion moss especially is probably a small number of enormous individuals, each one covering scores of blocks, each one older than the collapse only in the sense that it came from spores that were already in the cave's mouth when the ceiling was still up. It does two things. It holds water — a hand's depth of it holds more water than the same depth of soil, and releases it slowly, which is why the seeps trickle all year rather than in pulses — and it holds cold, because a wet sponge does not warm. Everything else in the trench lives in conditions the moss maintains. It is the ceiling now. It is what keeps the dark and the wet and the constancy in a place that lost its roof, and it grows over every new face within a season, which is the closest thing the trench has to healing.

The ferns are stiff and bluish and their fronds hold water in their curls; they are the trench's rain gutters, catching and channelling. The willow-things along the water are shrubby, many-stemmed, and grow from every cutting the builders drop, so the builders are also their gardeners. The sedge on the meadows was seeded by the grazers' dung. The cold-country herb with the thick leaves arrived on the feet of the heron. Nothing here was planted, and everything was.

---

On the rim is what does not live here.

A small lizard warms itself on the white snapped stone in the afternoon and never goes over. A hawk from the plain sits on the highest fracture and watches the birds pour out of the fog in the morning and takes one, sometimes, at the very top of the spiral, but will not follow them down. Plain grass grows to within a body-length of the edge and stops, killed by the cold breathing up. The wolves of the plain come to the lip and look and go away; there is nothing in the trench they can catch on that footing, and the smell that rises is of a place that is not theirs.

People come now and then. Herders, mostly, whose animals will not graze near the edge. They lie on their stomachs on the warm white rock and look down into the milk and wait for it to thin, and when it thins they see the wall of eyes and the flight of stairs and the grey shapes moving in the sedge, and they go away and tell it, and the telling is always a little wrong. Nobody has stayed. There is nowhere to stay. There is nowhere to put a foot that is not already the property of a living thing, and the living things have been there longer, and are quieter, and are not leaving.
