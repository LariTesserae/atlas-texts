What lives here is small, and there are a great many of it, and it has never once been dry.

Start with the frogs, because they are the loudest and because in a sense they are the place. There are twelve kinds, or eleven, or fourteen depending on who counted and which pools were connected that week. Most of them cannot be told apart at arm's length. Some of them can barely be told apart by each other, which is why they call: in a world where the cloud closes off vision at six metres and every surface is the same wet grey-green, the voice is the only thing that carries. Each species has a note, and each colony within a species has an accent, and a frog sitting in the cup of a bromeliad on a hummock two metres across knows exactly where it is in the world by what it hears from the hummocks around it. It knows nothing about the plateau. It knows its hummock and the eleven hummocks it can hear.

They are frogs that have given up on the pond. There is no pond here — or rather, everything is pond, and so the idea has stopped organising anything. The clear-skinned ones lay their eggs in the moss itself, a few large eggs each, and the young hatch as frogs, having done the tadpole in the egg, because there was never a dry season to force a hurry and never a wet season to make a pool worth swimming in. The black-and-gold ones lay in bromeliad cups and their tadpoles grow up in a litre of rainwater and drowned gnats, and are sometimes washed out when the cup overflows, and finish growing up wherever they land. The brown ones lay in the runoff, in the actual stream of it, on the undersides of stone lips where the water slides over without quite touching, and the eggs hang there in a film of water a millimetre thick like a second skin the rock has grown. Every frog on the plateau has a heart you could watch through its side if you held it up to the grey light. None of them has ever been held up.

They eat what falls in. Everything, eventually, falls into something. Midges hatch off the pool surfaces in continuous small clouds that are beaten down again by the rain before they have gone a metre, and the frogs sit under them with their mouths shut, waiting, though waiting here is not the thing it is elsewhere. Elsewhere waiting has an end — the dusk, the rains, the thaw. Here there is nothing on the far side of it. A frog on this plateau does not wait for the rain to stop; it does not have that thought available. It sits, and calls, and eats, and the sitting and calling and eating are not phases but a single continuous activity that has a slightly different texture at different depths of grey. When it is darker they call slower. That is the whole of night.

Above the frogs, in the same sense that liverwort is above moss, are the birds.

They are dark, all of them — soot, slate, peat-brown — and small, and they have feathers that do the opposite of what feathers do. A bird from a drier place holds a layer of air against its body; these hold nothing, because there is nothing dry to hold. Their plumage lies flat and sheds water like a slate roof, and under it the skin is thick and the fat is close, and they are, when you get near one, half the size they look. They live on the rosette plants, in the wet brown skirts of dead leaves, where the temperature is two degrees warmer than the air and where a whole small economy of beetles and springtails and the frogs' own tadpoles goes on in the trapped water. A bird works a rosette from the bottom up, jamming its head between the leaf bases, and comes out with its face slick and a larva in its bill, and shakes once — not to dry, there is no drying, but to reset — and goes to the next. They nest in the skirts too. The eggs are laid on wet leaf-felt and are wet from the moment they exist and the chicks hatch wet and are fed wet food and have, at no point in their lives, the sensation of not being wet, and so do not have the sensation of being wet either. It is simply how the air is.

The hummingbird is the exception that shows the rule. It is the one bright thing. It hangs at the rosette flowers in a spray of its own making, wings throwing off water as fast as it lands, and it must eat constantly because the effort of staying airborne in air this heavy would kill it in an hour without sugar. It has no lean time. There is no season when the rosettes do not flower, because there is no season, and so the hummingbird has never in its lineage had to fatten, or drop its temperature, or wait, and would die at once if it had to. It is the most fragile living thing on the plateau and the only one with any colour, and it is exactly as old here as the frogs.

Then the water itself, which is full.

The pools are the colour of tea and clear, and in the clear tea there are beetles that carry their own air down with them and let it out in strings of bright beads; there are larvae that build cases of sand grains and are, when they are dead, indistinguishable from the sand; there are the pale fish. The fish are the ones for whom the plateau's rules are hardest. Everything goes to the edge, and they are in the thing that goes. A pool that has held a shoal for a year will, when a hummock slumps and the channel shifts, drain in an afternoon, and the shoal goes with it — into the next pool down if the channel is deep enough, over the honeycombed lip and into the moss if it is not, where the fish lie in the sphagnum flexing until the water rises again or a bird finds them. Every fish on the plateau is upstream of a cliff, and knows it in the way fish know things, by orienting into the current and holding there, holding, spending the whole of its life swimming gently toward the centre of the tableland against water that is going the other way. Some fraction of them go over the edge every hour of every day and have done for as long as there have been fish here. The population is a thing the plateau leaks. It is replaced as fast as it is lost, in the same pools, by the same slow spawning in the same tea-coloured shallows, and the shoal you see today is not the shoal you saw yesterday, and is the same shoal.

Smaller than the fish, and larger in number by orders that stop meaning anything: what lives in the moss.

A hand-deep pelt of sphagnum over a square metre of rock holds more animals than the whole visible surface of the plateau does. Mites, in their hundreds of thousands, red and brown and colourless. Springtails. Rotifers. Nematodes in the water between the leaves. Things that live their entire lives in the film of water that clings to a single strand of liverwort and never encounter the rest of the world. The sundews eat some of them; the pitcher plants, leaning out over the channels, eat the ones that fly; the frogs eat the sundews' misses. The whole plateau is a stack of mouths, each one exactly the size of what the layer above lets fall, and the water goes through all of them on its way to the edge and is a little browner and a little richer for it when it gets there.

This is what lives here, mostly. Small, many, wet, loud, and unable to imagine the alternative to any of those.

But there is the thing with the webbed feet.

It was moving in the reed at the edge of vision and did not show itself, and it does not show itself, and for a long while that is all there is of it: a disturbance. A line of sedge that parts and closes with no wind. A slick in the moss on a hummock's flank, a body's width, where the sphagnum has been pressed flat and has not yet sprung back, filling with brown water even as you look at it so that in a minute the mark will be a pool like any other. A place on a stone lip where the algae has been scraped. A frog chorus that stops — a whole hummock going silent at once, and then the next one, a silence moving across the ground faster than a person could walk, and then starting again behind it in the same order.

It leaves fish. That is the thing you find first. Pale scales on a ledge above a deep pool, a dozen of them, stuck to the wet rock; and then, on another ledge kilometres downdrainage, the same. A backbone in the moss with the flesh gone. It works the pools, and it works them along the channels, and it is going, always, the way the water goes.

Because it has fur, or something that does what fur does when fur has never once been dry — a dark dense nap that lies flat and sheds, and under it a body long and low and heavier than it looks in the water, where it does most of its living. It is the largest animal on the plateau by a factor that makes it a different kind of thing altogether. Nothing else here is bigger than a hand. It is bigger than a child. It is one of very few; it must be, because the plateau, for all its density, is thin — everything on it is small because there is not enough of anything up here to make large things out of, except water, and it lives on what the water carries. The fish, mostly. Frogs when they are close. Once, perhaps, a bird taken off a rosette in the moment the bird's head was buried in the wet skirt. It follows the drainage down toward the edge, working each pool, and it is the only living thing on the tableland that goes all the way to the edge and comes back.

It knows the edge. The frogs do not; a frog carried over the lip of its hummock's pool does not know that the edge is the end of every downhill it has ever been in. The fish do not, though they act as if they do. The birds could know but have no reason to go there. The webbed thing goes there because the fish go there — because the last pools before the cliff, the ones under the spray where the vegetation is short and plastered and the rock scoured pink, are where everything the plateau has lost that hour is concentrated, briefly, before it is thrown into the air. It hunts in the roar. It has scraped the algae off rock a body's length from the lip. It has hauled out onto ledges over the void, in the driven spray, with a fish, and eaten it there, and turned, and gone back up — against the current, up the channels, over the honeycombed lips, into the interior — to sleep.

It sleeps under the stone. Where the quartzite has weathered into overhung lips above the basins there are recesses a metre deep, dark, running with water on every surface, where the rain does not reach directly and the light is a grey so faint the moss stops. It lies in there, on wet rock, with water going past it, and is as dry as it is ever going to be. Its young are born there. Two or three; dark, blind, already webbed, already with the flat dense nap. They are in the water before their eyes open. They have their mother's route down the drainage before they have anything that could be called a mind, because the drainage is the only shape the world has.

It has been here as long as the frogs. It has to have been; nothing came up here. It is not a thing that arrived; it is a thing that was here when the rock was higher and the water had further to go, and it followed the water as the water took the mountains down, and it is what is left of whatever was large here, worn to a single low dark shape that goes to the edge and comes back.

The frogs stop for it and start again. The birds do not care.

It has learned the turbine house.

This is the newest thing it knows and it knows it as a rock — a long hollow rock that hums, that has stood at the head of the penstock for a span of time which, in its lineage, is three or four grandmothers, which is to say forever. The intake takes a portion of the falling curtain and with it a portion of everything the plateau is losing, and the grille is packed with moss, and the moss is packed with fish that came down the channel and could not go through. It is the richest feeding on the tableland and the webbed thing has it to itself. It comes in through the sprung doors when the grey is at its darkest, when the frogs have slowed, and the noise of the water in the pipe is a pressure it does not notice because it has never been anywhere without that noise. It hauls out on the concrete beside the groaning wheel, in the filthy spray, and eats.

Above the door the lamp glows its faint pointless orange on some circuit nobody drew. And in the faintness the midges come to it, out of the wet dark, because there is nothing else in a hundred kilometres for them to come to, and they beat around the wire cage and fall, and the frogs that colonised the shed sit on the housings and the flanges beneath it with their mouths shut, and eat what falls. That is the whole of what the machine does now. One turbine turning on bearings that should have seized, driving a generator whose brushes have worn to nothing, lighting one lamp, which feeds the frogs, which feed the thing on the concrete, which goes back out into the rain and up the drainage and under the stone. The people who built it wanted the water for something else. The water did not stop and so neither did the wheel, and what it powers now is this, a small circuit of eating in the one dry-ish corner of a place where dry has lost its meaning, and it will go on powering it until the bearings finally go, which might be a long time; iron takes longer than mountains to come down, and this one has already outlasted every reason it had.

So: what lives here is what the rain allows, and the rain allows a great deal, so long as it is small and does not need to wait for anything. Frogs that have never known a pond, birds that have never known dry air, fish that spend their lives swimming inward against a current that will carry them out anyway. A stack of mouths in the moss. A hummingbird that would die in an hour if the flowers stopped and the flowers have never stopped. And one thing that is not small, that goes to the edge and comes back, that knows the whole shape of the plateau as the shape of a single downhill, and sleeps under the oldest rock there is with water going past it in the dark. None of them has any picture of the place. None of them needs one. They are the place — the wet living interval between the stone and the falling cloud, packed thick, calling all day, leaking over the edge and remaking itself behind, exactly as it was a million years ago, exactly as it is this afternoon, while the rain goes on doing the only thing it does.
