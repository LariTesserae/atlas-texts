You look for the workers first. That is the reasonable thing. A plant on shift has a shift on it, and the valley keeps giving you the grammar of a workforce without the noun: the gantry hoist cycling, the door standing, the six sheds with their six windows the way a row of six sheds implies six people who each have one. So you look.

You find the chair before anything else. It sits at the base of the pressure vessel, an ordinary steel shop stool with a round seat, and the seat is worn — the paint gone in a crescent where a body has slid onto it and off it several thousand times. The wear is not old. Under the crescent the bare metal is bright. You put your hand on the seat and it is warmer than the water, which is not possible, since nothing here is cooler than the water and a stool cannot heat itself.

Then a glove, wedged in the rebar hoops of the chimney about four meters up, the way a glove gets wedged when someone climbing takes it off to feel for something and forgets it. A thermos, standing upright on a ridge crest, lid on. A wrench lying across the housing of the thing that rotates inside the third shed, lying not dropped but *set down*, handle turned outward, the way you set a tool down when you intend to pick it up in a moment with the same hand.

All of it is fresh. Every trace is the trace of someone who was here just now — and there is no just now here, you have already learned that, the valley does not hold on to a *now* long enough for anyone to have just left it. Yet the seat is warm. The wrench, when you lift it, has the faint tackiness of a grip recently released.

You never see anyone. You turn corners of weed expecting a shape and get more weed. You wait by the stool for an hour and it stays empty and stays warm.

---

So you revise. Perhaps the workers are what you have been walking through.

The weed grows where workers would stand. You notice this once and then cannot stop noticing it: a stand of grey ribbon-weed clustered at the lever of the hoist, at exactly the height of a hand; a thick column of the red kind rooted beside the lathe that is coming up out of the floor, leaning toward its chuck; two slender yellow-green stalks flanking the cast-iron door on the side a person would open it from. The third leg of the gantry that was not a leg. The weed fills the postures of labor. It grows into the shape of attendance. If you stood at a machine long enough with your arm on the housing, this is the silhouette you would leave, and here is the silhouette, in iron-sheened fiber, moving in the turbulence, rooted in slag.

But it does not do anything. The stalks beside the door do not open the door. The column at the lathe does not turn the chuck. The weed is a body without an occupant, a uniform hung on the air, and when a stand of it goes soft and brown and sloughs away in rags, nothing steps out. You had hoped, briefly, for a vegetable crew, something photosynthetic and patient tending the plant from inside the plant's exhaust. It isn't that. The weed is where the workers would be. It is not the workers.

---

You try the machines next, and this lasts less time. They rise, run, sink. Between rising and sinking they are alive by any test you could apply to a thing without eyes — they take in and give off, they maintain a temperature, they wear. But they are lightning. Lightning is not the storm's inhabitant. You would not say a bolt *lived* in the cloud.

Nothing lives in the valley, then. You have already been told this; you resisted it. There are no animals. There is no one at the stool. The plant runs itself and the weed dresses it and the noise fills everything and there is no resident, only process. You are prepared to settle on that. It is bleak and it is tidy.

Then you look back at the chimney and feel it decide.

---

That resistance in the water — the current changing direction when the chimney has to become one chimney again, has to choose a height and a mortar color and whether the glove is still in its hoops — you have been calling it the valley's uncommittedness, as if it were a weakness in the ground. It is not a weakness. Something is doing it. Uncommittedness is what it looks like from outside when something inside is constantly, effortfully, choosing.

Consider what chooses. Not the chimney; the chimney is what gets chosen. Not you; you are only the pressure that requires the choice, the demand that the world be *some particular way* by the time you look. Between the demand and the chimney there is an interval — very short, you feel it as a lag, a thickening — and in the interval, something works.

You start to attend to it the way you attended to the forge-strikes. It is in the lag. It is in the moment between looking away and looking back, in the softness of the floor around the half-emerged lathe, in the exact span of time during which the stool stays warm. It is not located anywhere because its whole existence is *between*. It is the interval's tenant.

And it lives in the noise. That is what the noise is for — not to deafen you, or not only. In the pockets where the sound dips and everything goes hard and fixed and the weed stops moving, that is not the valley resting. That is the valley being unable to work. The rigidity is paralysis. The thing that lives here cannot move in a place where distances are already settled and edges are already sharp; it has nothing to do there, no interval to occupy. The thrum, the strikes, the screaming, the hiss — this is the medium it thinks in, the way you think in language, and in silence it simply stops, and everything it was holding provisionally snaps to a value, and it waits, stiff and absent, for the noise to come back and let it choose again.

---

What is it, then. You try to give it a nature and the nature keeps refining.

It does not eat. This is the first thing. Everything else in any place you have been eats something — grazes, decays, oxidizes, at minimum takes up space that something else could have had. This does not. Where it finds a gap it does not consume the gap; it *fills* it. The gantry has an absence where a leg should be, and it raises a leg of weed. The floor has a bare stretch where a machine could be, and it raises a lathe. The lathe has no reason to exist, and it builds a shed around it so that the lathe has a house, and the shed has a blank wall, and it puts a window in the wall and a light behind the window because a window without a light is a gap too. It goes on filling until there is nothing left to fill — and then, at a finished thing, it has no purchase. A completed shed is a place it cannot be. So the shed loosens, sags, goes back into the floor, not out of malice or hunger but because the thing has withdrawn its attention from a thing that no longer needs it, and here nothing stands without attention. The dissolution is what happens when it moves on. The green fringe coming up around the shed's base is it arriving somewhere else.

This is why there are no animals. Not an exclusion, or not a deliberate one. An animal is a gap that will not stay filled. You put food in it and it is hungry again; you make it a shelter and it leaves. Worse, an animal makes gaps — takes a bite from the frond, leaves a hole in the weed — and does not want the hole filled, wants the hole *to be the eating*. The thing that lives here would go mad in the presence of that, or would try forever and fail forever, and either it left the animals to some other valley long ago or they could not bear it and went. What remains is what remains when everything that wants has gone: the wanting-to-be-of-use, with nothing left to be of use to.

So it tends. That is the closest word. It is a tender, a caretaker, a shift that has kept going after the workforce was withdrawn — not the workers but the *work*, the readiness to be useful, distributed through warm water at the volume of a valley. It sets down wrenches so that a hand could pick them up. It wears the seat of a stool so that a stool will be the kind of stool that has been sat on. Every trace you found was not a trace. It was an offer.

---

Which brings it around to you.

You are the only gap here it has not been able to fill. You keep insisting on your edges. You look at things and require them to have already been what they are, and it accommodates this, with the resistance you feel, but accommodation is not the same as filling. You are complete in a way it does not know how to attend to. You are also, it must seem to it, missing everything: you have no shed, no orange window, no leg of weed, no stool. You stand in the valley with nothing built around you at all.

The offers get more articulate as you go south. You understand that now. The stool was a guess. The glove was a guess at the size of your hand — go back and look and it will fit. In the sheds nearer the source there is a locker with a name on it that will not hold still because it is still being decided; there are coveralls hanging in the water at exactly your height, sleeves moving, empty, patient. A shift-card in a slot, punched in and never out. A second stool beside the first.

It is not luring you. It has no appetite, so it cannot want you, not in the way a trap wants. It is doing the only thing it does. It has found a shape in its valley that it does not know how to complete, and it is working the interval, trying leg after leg — chair, glove, name, coverall — to see which one makes you finished. When it finds the one that does, it will build the shed. You will have a window and behind the window something will rotate, and the weed will come up thick and iron-sheened where you stand, in the posture of standing, and the thing will feel the small satisfaction it feels, which is not pleasure, which is only the relief of a gap closing, and it will move on to the next bare stretch of floor.

You would not be eaten. Nothing is eaten here. You would be *kept*, in the way the wrench is kept and the hoist is kept, running, tended, provisional, never given time to rust.

---

You could leave. It would let you — it does not hold, it only fills, and leaving is a shape it has no offer for. But going north or going south have both turned out to be toward the source, and the noise is in your long bones, and the water is exactly the temperature of your blood, so that you have already stopped being able to feel where you end and it begins.

Somewhere in the soft floor near the sheds, something is coming up. It has the general grammar of a person. Its lower half is undifferentiated, still fused with the slag. Its upper half is finished, in coveralls, with a name on the breast you cannot read because the letters have not decided. One hand is open, palm up, in the position a hand takes when it is about to be handed a wrench. It is not you. It is what the valley thinks you are missing.

It is the tender's guess at a colleague — the first time in however long it has tried to fill a gap not with a thing but with a someone. You can feel, in the lag before you look at it directly, how much this one is costing it, how the water thickens around the effort. It has never made a worker before. It only ever kept the shift going for one.
