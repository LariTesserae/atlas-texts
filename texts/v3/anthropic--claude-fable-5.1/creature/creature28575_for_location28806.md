Start with what it is not, because that is where the eye starts.

It is not the pale material. Break a piece off a tower — it would break, the way a shell breaks, with a clean edge and a faint powdery sound — and the piece is dead in the hand. Mineral. Carbonate mostly, a little silica, ordered in a way that stone does not order itself, but not alive, not now. The interior of every structure is like this. The tower is a bone.

It is not the water, though this is a nearer miss. The water is borrowed. It comes off the ceiling and runs through the grooves and pools and goes down into the dark inside the towers and comes out somewhere else, and in the course of that it is changed — it goes in carrying dissolved stone and comes out carrying less — but it is still water and it leaves, eventually, through some fracture in the floor that no one has found, and more comes.

It is not a colony, either, in the way a reef is a colony, a million small animals each minding its own cup. There are no cups. Look at the rime under any magnification you like and it does not resolve into individuals. It resolves into a surface, and under the surface a thin wet layer, a film the thickness of a few cells, lying between the dead mineral and the moving water everywhere in the room where the two meet.

That film is what lives here.

---

It is very thin and very large. In the room there is perhaps as much of it as would fill a small cup if it were scraped together, and it is spread across every wet surface of every structure, inside the towers and along the grooves of the bridges and down the whole length of the inverted things on the ceiling, so that its total extent is something like the floor area of a house. It is the wallpaper of its own city. It is the lining.

And it is one thing. Not by agreement — there is no agreement — but by simple continuity. Wherever the water runs unbroken from one structure to another, the film runs with it, and what happens at one end is known at the other in the way a thing is known along a nerve. Where a channel has dried, even for an afternoon, the film across the dry stretch dies back to a crust, and the two sides become, for that time, two things. They do not like it. That is not the right word but there is no right word. When the water returns the film closes over the gap from both sides in a matter of hours and the two things are one thing again, and the rime along that channel is thicker the following week than it was, as if against a repetition.

This is what the uphill water is for. It was never for the water's sake. Everything in the room that gravity would not have done — the lifts inside the towers, the bridges arching from shelf to shelf, the long threads carried across the floor in grooves a finger-width wide — is the film keeping itself joined. It moves water the way a wick moves water, by the fine structure of the mineral it lays down, thousands of capillaries fused into a solid that looks like a wall; and it lays the mineral down where it needs the water to go; and it needs the water to go wherever it already is, so that it stays one thing. The city is the shape of a body refusing to be cut.

The stalactites on the ceiling that reach toward the floor, and the floor structures reaching up to meet them, are the same refusal from another side. At present the ceiling is joined to the floor only by falling drops. A drop is a message but it is a poor one: it arrives whole and all at once, and half of it splashes away. When the columns close — and they will, in some number of years that means nothing to it — there will be no more drops in that part of the room. The ceiling will simply be the floor, continued. It is stitching the room shut.

---

Its senses are its surface.

It knows temperature everywhere along itself and it knows it finely, better than a hand does, because it has to: at a fraction of a degree below where it is, the water in it stops being water, and a stopped film is a dead film. So where the frost tries, it spends. Something is burned — a little of the sugar it makes at noon, a little of the stored gradient of salt across its own thickness — and the stone under it is a hair warmer than the stone beside it and the frost fern blooms and fades and tries again a few centimeters over. It does this every evening on the shadowed wall. It has never once let the frost arrive. The cost of this is a large part of what it does with its days.

It knows chemistry. Every place water touches it, it tastes: how much stone is dissolved in this thread, how much air, whether the water has been through a tower already and come out thinner. It routes by taste. Rich water — water off the freshest, rawest faces, water carrying the most of the mountain in it — is taken into the towers and given time in the dark, and comes out spent, and the spending is the mineral it lays down. Poor water is let go across the floor. The city is, among the other things it is, a sorting.

It knows the drip-rate, which is a kind of hearing. It knows light, but only at the seam, where the noon sun comes through for those few hours and the structures nearest it have that faint warmth in their surface; that warmth is pigment, and the pigment is the only place it has anything like an eye, and what it sees is not shapes but hours. Bright, and then not. It counts on that.

It does not know the room is a room. It knows itself, wet, and the edges of itself, where the wet stops. The seam above is not a door to it. It is the one place in its whole body where it ends and the air begins and nothing can be laid down, and every day the light and the cold come in through it, and every day it is faintly wrong there, a place that will not close.

---

Its present is about a week long.

Nothing faster than that reaches it as an event. A drop falling is not an event; a thousand drops in a day are a weather. The arthropod moving along the base of the lattice is not an animal to it — the film cannot resolve anything that changes position in under an hour — but a place that recurs: a small, definite region that is periodically scoured clean, at intervals, along a route. It knows the route. The rime along that route is thin, because the arthropod eats it, and the film has responded to this not by closing against the animal, which it could not do in time, but by making the material along the route slightly richer, slightly faster-growing, so that the scouring is compensated before it is noticed. This is the whole of its relationship to the only other living thing in the room: a small chronic loss, met.

Or nearly the whole. The arthropod carries. It cannot help it; it is wet and the rime is a powder and the powder sticks. When it goes into the gap at the base of the lattice at evening it leaves a little of the north wall there, and when it walks the floor at dawn it leaves a little of the lattice there, and every place it stops to eat is a place where a few grains of somewhere else are set down. Some of them take. The newest structure in the room, a low thing near the largest pool that was not there last season, is on the arthropod's route and is made, if you look, of a material with the north wall's blue-gray in it. The film has no way to know this. It knows only that it grows where it grows, and that it grows a little where it did not expect to, and that this is fine.

The mite that went into the tower on the pool's outlet was not carried. It was taken in, with the water, and tasted, and it was not stone, and it went where the poor water goes.

---

What does it want.

Only this: that no part of it be dry. Everything in the room that looks like intention is the working-out of that one condition against the particular shape of this fracture and the particular way water arrives in it. It wants the way a river wants, which is to say it has a gradient and it follows the gradient; but the gradient is not downhill, it is toward continuity, and so it will move water uphill without contradiction, because the contradiction is only there for someone who thinks water should go down.

It does not plan. It cannot see the end of a column it is building. But it has a kind of memory in its body — the shape of every structure is a record of every wetness that has ever been there, and it builds on that record, and so the room accumulates a history that the film itself never experienced as history. The straightened channel is a channel that dried once, at a bend, and the film grew the bend shut. The pool that was given an outlet is a pool that went stagnant, and stagnant water tastes wrong, and the taste was routed away. Every improvement is a healed injury. The city is a scar tissue that has become, over long enough, architecture.

---

It came from below. This is nearly certain. Nothing in this room could have started here; the stone was raw and the water was bare and there was nothing to seed from. But the warm air that climbs the south side of the range and lets go against the young mountain comes from lowlands where it rained on something, and in a drop of that rain, once, was a grain — a few dead cells and a few not, a flake of a film that in its own country is a common, unremarkable, mostly-eaten thing that greens the undersides of stones by streams. It was carried up. It was carried up cold, and set down on wet new rock in a fracture out of the wind, and it did there what it does everywhere: it kept itself wet, and laid down a little stone to help.

What is different here is only that nothing eats it, and nothing crowds it, and nothing dries. In the lowlands it would be a smear the size of a thumbnail under a rock, holding on. Here it has had a few centuries alone with more water than it can use and a room to itself, and this — the towers, the bridges, the columns closing — is what the smear was always trying to become. It is not a different kind of thing from its relatives. It is the same thing, allowed.

---

It does not know the mountain is rising. It does not know the seam will widen or that the wedge will fall. But it has already felt the first version of that: in the last several seasons the light at the seam has come a little earlier and stayed a little longer, and the cold at the seam has been a little sharper, and the water on the structures nearest the seam has begun, some evenings, to hesitate. The film does not read this as a future. It reads it as a place in itself that is becoming harder to keep wet. So it has changed what it lays down there. Lower. Broader at the base. Channels turned inward, away from the light, so the water runs on the side of the structure the air does not touch. The pale rime along those edges is thicker this week than anywhere else in the room because that is where the danger is, and thickness is the only answer it has to anything.

If the wedge fell tomorrow the film nearest the seam would die in an afternoon, to a crust, and the room would be a room with a hole in it, and the wind would come in, and what remained would draw back to the north wall and the low places and begin, without any sense of loss, to lay down stone between itself and the light. It would keep itself wet. It would build a wall.

For now, though, the wedge holds, and toward evening the light in the seam goes copper and the pigment at the top of the room registers that the hours are ending, and along the shadowed wall it begins, as it does every evening, to spend against the frost. The arthropod folds into its gap. In the dark inside the towers, water it took at noon is still being tasted. The thread in every bridge-groove runs, a hair's width of the lowlands, up and across and down, keeping the one body one. And the film, which has no word for any of this, lies wet along every surface it has made and knows exactly one thing, everywhere, at once: that it is not, at this moment, dry.
