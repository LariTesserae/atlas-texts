What lives there begins smaller than you can see, and it is worth starting there, because everything else is built on it the way the city is built on the stone.

In the thinnest water, the water that is only a shine on the lip of a terrace, there are films. Orange where it is hottest, near the head of the valley; then saffron, then a green that goes toward black as the water cools and slows. They are not plants. They are the first, simplest kind of living, sheets of cells one on top of another, and they do two things: they eat the heat and the mineral, and they die. Their dying is not incidental. Each layer that dies is sealed under lime and becomes one of those bands finer than hair, and the next layer grows on top of it, and so the color of the stone is a record of what lived on it, laid down and shut. Cut a fresh ledge open and you can read it like the rings of a tree, except that it never stopped and never will, and except that there were never any years to count.

The flies live on the films. Nobody has named them; there is no need, there being only the one kind. They are dark and short-winged and they do not so much fly as lift, a few inches, and settle. Where the film is thick the flies are thick and you cannot see the stone for them. A doorway can be flies. You put your hand into it and the doorway opens a hand's breadth around your hand and closes again behind it. They lay in the wet margins and their young are a paler crust under the dark one, and they are born and mate and die in a span so short that the people, who measure time by how fast stone grows, have no unit for it.

Then the birds, and the bats, which together are the ceiling of the place as the films are its floor.

The swifts never land. That is the first thing anyone notices about them, and it is very nearly true: they have feet, but the feet are for clinging, not walking, and a swift on the ground is a swift that is dying. They nest in cups of their own hardened spit against the undersides of overhangs, and the spit takes lime from the steam and turns to stone as it dries, so that a swift's nest and the rock it hangs from become one thing within a season. The birds inside are sooty and small and their eyes are large for their heads and they scream. It is not song. It is a thread of sound pulled tight across the whole valley, a frequency chosen — not by them, by whatever made them — because it is one of the few that will cut through the water. The people speak on the same frequency. Nobody thinks this is strange.

The bats are the swifts' night, in a place that has no night. They hang where the swifts do not, in the deeper hollows and the older vaults, so dense that the ceiling seems upholstered, and when one shifts the whole surface shifts, a slow shrug traveling across a roomful of sleeping bodies. Their dung falls and dries on the warm stone and the people gather it in reed scoops and burn it, and so in a sense the bats are the hearth of every house. They are larger than you expect and their faces are folded and patient. They come and go without any cue of light. A portion of them is always out over the pools, taking flies, and a portion is always hanging, and which portion is which changes so gradually that if there is a rhythm to it no one has ever found where it starts.

In the pools, the fish. Not blind, quite — there are eyes, small and filmed — but they live in water you cannot see a hand's depth into, and the eyes are not what they use. They are the color of the water. When a shoal turns, the pool seems to clot and then clear. They eat what falls from the roosts and what drifts down from the films, and the people take them in baskets and eat them, bones and all, since the bones are soft and the fish are small and there is nothing else. Their flesh tastes of the mineral. Everything here does.

And on the terrace lips, the snails, which are the emblem of the place if it has one. They graze the films. They move at the pace of snails. And the stone grows at the pace of stone, which is not much slower, so that a snail that lingers too long in one place finds its shell has been welded to the rim by a bead of fresh lime, and it must pull free or stay. Most pull free. Some stay. On every lip there are a few shells, white as the stone, sealed to the stone, and inside some of them the snail is still alive, still grazing the small circle of film its neck can reach, sealed in and living. The people have a touch for this — two fingers pressed and held — that means both patience and something worse.

The people are the largest thing that lives here and by numbers the least, and they are shaped by the basin more thoroughly than anything in it except the stone itself.

They are pale, but not the pallor of sickness; it is the pallor of skin that has never dried. Their fingertips are permanently whorled and soft, like the fingertips of someone who has just come out of a long bath, and their feet are broad and the soles thick and white and slightly translucent, the same fingernail white as the ground, so that an old person's foot seen from beneath is hard to distinguish from what it stands on. Hair grows out and the ends stiffen with lime; most people crop it with tufa blades, but some of the old let it go and it forms a kind of cowl, brittle and pale, that clicks when they turn their heads. Their teeth are very good and heavily crusted. Their joints set early. By the time a person has seen three roofs grown — the phrase they use — the knees and the knuckles have begun to lock, and the very old move like something being carefully lifted from a mold before the mold is done.

They cannot hear each other, and they have never been able to. A child born here is born into the roar and the roar is in the child's ears before its eyes open, and within a few seasons of growth the ears have given up the middle of their range for good. What survives is the high short shriek — the swifts' frequency — and it is used for what shrieks are for: a warning, a call across a pool, a name thrown out to see who turns. Everything else is done by touch.

The language is spoken on the forearm. The speaker takes the listener's arm, or the listener offers it, and talks with fingers and the heel of the hand against the skin of the inner wrist and up toward the elbow: pressures, drags, taps, the flat of the palm laid still for a moment. It is a full language. It has tense, though its tenses are odd. It has a great many words for kinds of wetness and kinds of thickness and very few for distance or color. It is spoken exactly the same in the dark as in the light, which is why the people have never much cared about the light. To talk to someone you must be within reach of them and touching them, and so there is no talking across a room, no calling from the door, no conversation that is not also an embrace of a sort. A group talks in a chain, hand to arm to hand, and what one person says travels down the chain arm by arm, delayed and slightly altered, so that a joke told at one end arrives at the other end as a different joke. Children learn to speak with their whole bodies laid against their mothers, the way children elsewhere learn by listening, and a mother talks to an infant by holding it and moving her hands on its back.

Because you cannot overhear, there is no such thing as overhearing. Nothing is said that is not said to someone. A secret is not something withheld from a room; it is something one person tells another with a hand cupped so the fingers cannot be seen moving, and even then it must be told skin to skin, so that the person who keeps a secret has always, once, held the person who gave it to them. Lying is possible but difficult; a hand on the arm feels the arm tense. They have a word for the outside people — the reed-bearers and the traders in black glass — that means, roughly, those who speak without holding, and it is not a kind word, and it is not an unkind one either. It is the word for a strange thing.

---

They keep no hours because there are none to keep. There is no morning. A person sleeps when the body wants sleep and wakes when it is done, and since bodies differ, the city is never asleep and never awake but always partly both, some rooms breathing and some working, someone always standing a screen in a channel while three walls away someone is dreaming. The measure of time is the stone. The smallest unit anyone bothers with is the skin, the day or two it takes fresh deposit to firm underfoot. Then the screen, which is how long a screen takes to become a wall. Then the roof. Then the house, which is the whole life of a room from the first screen to the moment its door has narrowed too far to pass and its people go up — and a house is about the length of a human life, or a little more, which is not an accident, since it was the human life that decided how big a house should be and the stone that decided how long that would take. A person of two roofs is a child. A person of a house is old. Nobody is older than a house, in the sense that nobody has watched a room they were born in close.

Their eyes are made for the near and the grey. The horizon of anyone born here is forty feet of pearl, and past it everything is a suggestion, and the eyes have grown into that: quick to catch movement close at hand, quick at the fine work of adzing a channel or lifting a bowl off the rock, indifferent to anything far. They have one word for far and hidden. Those who go up over the rim — the young sometimes do, a few, for reasons they can rarely put into fingers afterward — come back speaking of the sky as something painful, a light that hits from one side and makes a dark copy of you on the ground that follows and will not be shaken off. They come back deaf, or rather, they come back hearing, and for a day the roar is a roar to them again and they cannot sleep, and then it sinks back into being the shape of silence and they are home.

Their work is the water's work, guided. Someone is always cutting. The adzes are tufa, fresh-cut, and they last a few screens' time before they have grown their own crust and gone blunt with their own material, and then they are set down and become part of a floor. Someone is always standing screens, always leading a sheet of water this way instead of that, always cutting the groove that tells a vault it is thick enough and may stop. Someone is always gathering — nests from the low overhangs, eggs, bat dung, fish in baskets, the little natural cups the drip leaves that become bowls — and someone is always cooking over a dung fire in a room whose ceiling drips into the pot, and the drip is part of the recipe and no one has ever tasted food without it. Children do what adults do, smaller. There is no one whose work is to think about the whole; the whole thinks about itself.

The reed-bearers are the seam between this and the rest of the world. They are born here, mostly, but they spend their lives walking: up the terraces, out over the rim into the dry scrub where the reed grows in the wet places, and back down with bundles taller than themselves, into the steam, hearing for the first hour and then not. They bring reed. They bring, sometimes, a blade of black glass, or a dried thing to eat that tastes of a color, or news. The news is never useful. The outside has weather and kings and years and none of these translate. A bearer will lay a hand on someone's arm and try to explain a thing that happened and then stopped happening and will not happen again, and the listener will feel the hand fumble for a tense that does not exist, and will pat the wrist twice, which means: all right, and also, never mind.

---

The dead go into the stone, because there is nowhere else for them to go.

A body is carried down, not up — down to the lower tiers, the old rooms whose doors have narrowed, where the water still runs and no one lives. It is laid on the floor beneath a lip, and the water is led over it, and it is left. Within a skin's time it has a bloom on it. Within a screen's time it is a shape. Within a roof's time it is a swelling in the floor that a child could walk over without knowing, and the room goes on closing around it, floor rising, ceiling coming down, until the room is solid and the person is somewhere in the middle of the solid, a hollow perhaps, perhaps not even that. Every closed house in the bottom of the valley has people in it. Every lived-in house higher up stands on those houses. When they say the ancestors are in the walls they are not being figurative; they mean the grandmother is about there, at about the height of the knee, a few rooms down, and the stone has been growing over her since, and the drip you sleep under has passed through the stone that passed through her.

They do not grieve the way holding-less people grieve, or they do, but the grief has a different shape, because the dead have not gone. They have stopped being touched, which is the same thing as having stopped speaking, and there is a touch for that too — the palm laid flat and still, longer than is comfortable. It is also the touch that means a wall is finished.

---

So what lives in the basin, if you stand in it long enough for the question to come apart in your hands, is not quite the people and not quite the birds and not quite the films.

What lives here is the letting go. Hot water comes up carrying stone in solution and meets the air and cannot hold it, and gives it up, and the giving up is the only thing that happens and it is happening on every surface at once, on the lips and the rims and the ceilings and the shells of the snails and the backs of the bearers' hands and the faces of the old and the bodies of the dead, a valley-wide continuous act of deposition that does not know it is doing anything. The films are the letting go, colored. The city is the letting go, guided. The people are the letting go, walking around inside itself for the length of a house, speaking on each other's arms, sealed in a little more each skin's time and grazing what they can reach.

They are not sad about this. There is nothing to be sad about. They have the water's steadiness, which from outside looks like patience and from inside is not a virtue but simply the condition of the place. They know, in the way of knowing that goes without saying, that the stone is not indifferent to them and not kind to them either; it is what they are made of, only guided, and it goes on. A child laying her cheek against a wall to feel the water in it is not listening for anything. She is doing what the snails do. The wall is warm, and faintly gives at the surface, and under that it is hard as porcelain, and inside it, at about the height of her knee, is everyone who has ever done this before her, and she thinks nothing of it at all, and then she gets up, and someone takes her arm to tell her something, and she is held.
