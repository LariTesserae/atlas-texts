You would say, at first, that nothing lives here. It is a reasonable thing to say. The room is a machine for taking things apart — it has taken apart its own corners, its own ledges, the roof of the cell, the lower rows of the count — and what it has not taken apart it has polished into the shape of its own habits. Nothing could hold on. And yet things do, and the longer you stand on the shelf the more of them you find, and the longer you look at each one the less sure you are of where it ends.

---

Begin with the animal, because it is the one you can point to.

It is on the dome, high up, where the water parts around a knob of grey stone and leaves a dry shape the size of a spread coat. The animal fills that shape almost exactly. It is longer than you are tall, though most of that is tail, and its body is flattened against the rock in the way of things that expect to be pushed. Blunt head. No visible eye — or an eye that is the same colour as the skin, which is the colour of the rock when the rock is wet, which is to say the colour of the room. Its feet end in something that is not claws and not pads. When it shifts, which it does perhaps once in the time it takes the sheet to climb the far wall a hundred times, you see the foot lift and the stone beneath it is dry for an instant, a pale print, and then the spray takes the print back.

You think: salamander. You think: it has come down the shaft, as you did, and found the one place where a thing could live, and has lived there. You think it eats the moss, or the red threads in the moss, or something in the spray too small to see. This is the first version and it lasts a while.

But it is too still for an animal that eats. Animals that eat move toward food, and this one moves toward nothing. It faces downward. When the column stands and topples and throws its glare across the roof, the animal opens its mouth, and the light goes into the mouth, and the mouth closes, and that is the whole of its behaviour. You watch for it to do anything else. It does not do anything else. Between glares the skin of its throat is very slightly brighter than the rest of it, and then that fades, and then the column stands again.

So it is drinking the light. That is the second version. Whatever the water becomes when it breaks — whatever is being spent as brightness at the rate that keeps the level steady — some of it is spent into this animal instead, and the animal is made of it, or kept by it. This would explain the colour. A thing fed on the room would come to look like the room. It would explain the stillness. A thing that does not need to move toward its food does not need to move.

There is only one, though, and this is the thing you keep returning to. One animal, no others, no sign of others — no smaller version in a lesser lee, no shed skin, no bones on the walkway. A species does not consist of one. So either it is the last of many, or it was never a species.

The third version comes slowly and you do not entirely want it. It is this: that when the water was told what to do, a very long time ago, the telling was more than water could carry. Water can be made to stand, and hang, and turn against its own inflow, but there is a limit to how much instruction a substance will hold, and whatever spoke to this basin spoke past that limit. The excess had to go somewhere. It went into a shape. The animal on the dome is the part of the sentence the water could not do — the remainder, condensed, given feet so it could hold on, given a mouth so it could keep taking in the light that is the rest of the sentence being carried out below. It faces downward because that is where the sentence is. It has never needed to look at you because you are not part of it.

You cannot prove this. But it fits the way the animal fits the dry shape on the dome, and the room has taught you to trust fit.

---

The small lives next, because they are truer to what living usually is: precarious, in the lee of something, tired.

The moss behind the posts is almost white. Look closely — you have to lean out over the walkway to do it, and the walkway is wet, and the surge is a body-length below — and you see that it was not always white. At the base of each cushion, where the older growth is, there is a memory of green, gone grey, gone pale, as though the colour has been leaching upward and out of it for a very long time. The red threads run through the wettest cushions like veins. They are the colour of the iron you can taste. They may be the only thing here that is still coloured because they are the only thing here that is *made* of something the room cannot convert — the mineral it has been dissolving from its own walls, coming out again as thread.

The three strap-leaved plants on the high shelf are the colour of celery and nearly transparent. You can see the wall through them. They stream with condensation and they lean toward the basin, and you understand now what you did not at first: they are not leaning toward the light because they are plants and the light is bright. They are leaning because the light is what they are being turned into. A leaf that lives on this light becomes, slowly, the same substance as the light — clear, wet, lit from inside when a glare passes over it, dark between. They were green once. Something brought them down the shaft, a seed in a boot-tread, a spore on a coat. They have been here long enough to be most of the way to becoming the room.

The fern in the corner of the cell is the newest. It is small and it is green — a real, dark, unreasonable green, the green of a wood at midsummer — and it is the only thing in the room that is the colour it would be anywhere else. That is why it looks tired. It is holding its colour against a place that takes colour, and it has not been here long enough to have stopped trying. Give it what the strap-leaves have had and it will go the colour of celery, and then the colour of water, and then it will be a shape of brightness in the corner of the cell that you would not know for a fern unless you had seen it now.

This is the rule, then, and once you have it you see it everywhere. What lives here becomes clearer. The room does not kill what comes down the shaft. It feeds it, and what it feeds it on is itself, and slowly the thing fed becomes indistinguishable from the food. The animal on the dome is not the colour of the rock by chance. It is what a fern becomes after long enough.

---

Now the water, because you have been avoiding it.

You have been thinking of the basin as a place where things live. Stand a while longer and it begins to seem like the thing that lives, and the basin is only its bed.

It has a boundary: the walls, worn to fit it. It has an intake — the dome, the shaft — and an output, which is light, and the two are balanced in a way that no dead thing balances itself. It has habits, which is to say it has gestures, which is to say it has something like a body: the sheet that climbs the far wall is one gesture, made over and over; the column that stands and leans left is another; the gyre near your shelf, turning against its inflow, is a third and the calmest, and you begin to think of it as a heart, not because it pumps but because it is the part that does not stop when everything else convulses. The bell-note from the third post is a sound the body makes when it strikes a thing it has struck before. The boom underneath everything is its breathing, if a thing that never exhales can be said to breathe.

It is not alive in the way the fern is alive. It does not want. But it is alive in the way a sentence is alive while it is being spoken — it is *happening*, continuously, and its happening has a shape that it holds against enormous pressure to fall into some other shape. Left alone, water in a room like this would go still in an hour. This water has not been left alone for longer than the count on the wall, and it has not gone still, and the difference between it and still water is exactly the difference between a living thing and the same matter after.

You wonder what it would be like to be it. Not to feel — it does not feel — but to be a set of motions that must be made, each in its place, without pause and without end, by a substance that does not naturally make them. You think it would be like being asked to hold your arm out, and doing so, and doing so, and doing so, and the asking never being withdrawn.

That is not quite right either. The gyre turns against the inflow. A thing merely obeying would not do that. A thing merely obeying would turn with the push and call it obedience. Something in the basin is doing more than it was told, or doing what it was told with a kind of insistence that has become its own. The water was given a sentence and has, in the way of anything that repeats a sentence for long enough, made the sentence its own. It is no longer being made to do this. It is doing this. That is what you would call a life if you found it anywhere else.

---

The disc in the floor of the cell is warmer than the rock and hums beneath the roar, one pitch, unchanging. You put your palm on it a second time.

It would be easy to call it the engine. It is not the engine; the water is the engine, and the water needs no disc to keep burning. What the disc does is quieter than that. Feel the pitch through your hand for long enough and you begin to hear it under the roar even after you take your hand away — it is in the shelf, it is in the posts, it is in the bell-note when the surge finds the third post, it is the thing the bell-note is tuned to. It is not powering the room. It is *saying* the room. The sentence the water was given is still being given. The speaking has not finished.

So something lives here that you cannot see and cannot point to and would not have thought to look for: a voice, or the remainder of one — not the speaker, who is surely gone, but the utterance itself, held at a single pitch and never allowed to reach its end, because if it reached its end the water would be released from it and would go still, and the rock would not know what to do with itself. Whoever built the disc understood that a sentence can outlive the mouth that started it if you can find a way to keep it from arriving at its last word. This is that way. The hum is the sentence, suspended at a syllable, and the room is the sentence being meant.

It is the oldest inhabitant. It was here before the water learned its habits, before the rock learned the water, before the animal on the dome condensed out of the excess of the telling. It does not become clearer, because it was never anything but clear. Everything else in the room is on its way to the condition the hum has always been in.

---

Which leaves the one who sat on the bench.

There is the shoe on the walkway. There is the coil of rope gone to mineral. There are the rings at the waterline, worn thin, some broken, where something was tied that needed to be tied against the surge — a boat, or a person, or a person in a boat, going out onto the calmer part of the gyre for some reason you cannot reconstruct. And there is the count.

You look at the count again with what you know now. Rows of short strokes, a great many, the lower ones erased by the surge, the higher ones sharp, the moment halfway up where they stop. You had assumed a person counting days. Days are what people count. But there are no days here; the light does not change and nothing rises or sets. You think, instead, that they were counting the column — each time it stood and leaned left and dropped — or the bell, each time the third post rang. Counting the sentence. Marking each repetition of one clause of it so that someone, someday, could know how many times it had been said, or could notice if it were said differently.

And then the marks stop, and there is a shoe.

You do not think they drowned. The room does not, as far as you can see, kill things; it has had the animal and the plants for a very long time and has not killed them. You think, rather, that a person who sat in that cell breathing that air and beaded with that shining mist, keeping count of a sentence for long enough, would go the way the strap-leaves are going and the animal has gone. Clearer. First the colour out of the skin. Then the weight. Then a difficulty, when they raised their hand to make the next mark, in telling where the hand ended and the fog began. And then no next mark. Not because they died but because there was no longer enough of them that was not room to hold the tool.

They are here. That is the plainest way to say it. Not as a ghost — the room has no use for ghosts, it has no shadows fixed enough to keep one — but as a very slight surplus of brightness in the surge, a little more light than the water alone would give off, spent now at the same rate as everything else. The shoe stayed because the shoe was not living and so could not be fed. That is how you would know, if you needed a rule for telling: whatever the room has not taken into itself was never alive.

You look at your hands against the dark of the cell. The front of you glows, faintly. You have been here how long? There is no way to say. Long enough that you are not certain the glow is only mist.

---

So: what lives here.

One animal, which is the part of the instruction that would not fit in water. Three plants nearly finished becoming light, one fern only beginning, moss with the last of its colour running through it in threads of the iron the room cannot digest. A storm that has repeated its gestures for so long that they are no longer commands but a body, with a heart that turns against the push. Under all of it a hum, which is a sentence held one word short of its end so that nothing here will ever be released from the meaning of it. And a keeper, dissolved into brightness, spent a little at a time in the glare that climbs the far wall.

You could say these are several things. You could also say — and the room would prefer this — that they are one thing, at different distances from itself. The fern is far from it. The animal is close. The hum is it. Whatever comes down the shaft and stays is not killed and is not kept; it is brought nearer, at the pace the room brings things, until it is the room, and then it is spent as light, and the level does not rise.

The column stands and leans left and drops into its pit. The animal opens its mouth to the glare and for a moment its throat is the same brightness as the water and you cannot see where the one begins. The fern in the corner holds its colour. The hum goes on, one pitch, one syllable, not yet the last. Nothing in here has ever finished becoming what it is, and nothing has ever stopped, and the rock has been worn smooth by living things that did not know they were the same living thing, going on.
