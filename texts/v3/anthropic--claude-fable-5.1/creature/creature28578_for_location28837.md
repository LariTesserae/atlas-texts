**What Lives There**

Begin with the absence, because the absence is the first inhabitant you will notice, and it is doing something.

Lichen lives on bare rock everywhere in the world. It lives on tombstones and on the undersides of glaciers and on the summit blocks of mountains that see sun a few days a year. It asks for almost nothing — a surface, some water, a little light — and here is a country made entirely of wet surfaces under a sky that is a lamp. It should be crusted. Every ledge should be grey-green and orange and black with it, every seep should have its slime, every tarn should hold, at the least, the dust of something that once photosynthesized. None of it does. The stone is clean the way bone is clean, and bone is clean because something has worked at it.

So the first thing to say is that the emptiness is not a lack. It is maintained. Something is here that the smallest living thing cannot share the place with, and whatever that something is, it has been here long enough that the plateau has weathered around it the way a riverbed weathers around a river.

---

You will want it to be the voices.

The speech that arrives from no direction, calm, with the cadence of words and no words in it — that is the obvious candidate. The cairn-builders, folded through the same seam that folds the sight-lines, still talking a hundred paces away and a thousand years back. It is a good thought. It is also, if you sit with it in one of the roofless shelters through a long dripping morning, not quite right. The voices do not answer. They do not change when you speak. They rise and fall on a rhythm that has nothing to do with you and nothing to do, you slowly realize, with speech either, because if you listen long enough you can hear them in the drip as well, and in the hiss of water going over the tilted slabs, and in the low churn under the stone. The cadence is not coming out of the fold. The cadence is *in* everything. The voices are simply where you first noticed it, because you are a creature that listens for voices.

Set them aside. Not entirely. The calm is a clue.

---

You will want it to be the figure.

Standing at the edge of the far tarn, looking down as you look down, at the bottom of the water that is not the bottom. It does not move when you move. It is not you. But consider how it got there: the fold shows you a place very like this one, and in a place very like this one there is a figure very like you, standing very much where you stand. That is not a resident. That is a mirror that has been given permission to be slightly wrong. If the figure lived here it would have somewhere to go when you walked away, and you have no reason to believe it does.

Unless — and hold this loosely — the figure is not a thing on the other side of the seam but the seam's way of *looking*. Unless something in the fold, when it attends to you, can only produce an image of attention by borrowing your shape for it. A face, after all, is what you have brought. It would use what was brought.

Set the figure aside too. It will come back.

---

Now the ringing.

This is where it starts to become itself, so go slowly.

The pale stone rings when the drip strikes it, and the tone hangs in the wet air and does not fade as quickly as it ought to. That last clause is the whole matter. A struck column should sound and decay — there is a physics to it, a curve, and the wet air and the cold should shorten the curve, not lengthen it. Instead the tones persist. They hold past their time. And in the column fields, where hundreds of pillars are sounding at once, you can stand for an hour with your hand on one of them and feel that the tone under your palm is not the tone the drip is making. It is fuller. It has been added to.

Here is what is happening, as near as anyone standing on the shelf can tell. The fold does not only carry sight and speech. It carries sound, which is only air moving, and air here is folded like everything else. So the tone struck on this column a moment ago arrives also at a column a mile away, and the tone struck there an hour ago arrives here now, and the tone struck here a century ago — by a drip off a cloud that has long since rained itself out over some drowned valley — is arriving somewhere, still, because in a folded country a sound does not have to stop. It only has to find the next place to be. The columns are a resonator. The whole plateau is. What you hear in the fields is not a thousand drips. It is every drip that has ever fallen here, distributed, held, still going round.

And it is not random. Give it a day. The beating of the tones against each other, the slow swell and sink of it as the cloud lifts and settles — it has a period. Something in the range of a very long breath. The ringing rises when the drip is heaviest, in the morning, and thins toward midday when the seeps slow, and comes back with the cloud in the afternoon, and at night when the ice forms it does not stop but goes down, far down, into a hum you feel more than hear, the low churn under the stone. It is the sound of something that is not asleep and not awake and does not need to be either.

---

So say it plainly, or as plainly as the stone allows.

What lives on the Rain Shelf is the fold. Not a thing *in* the fold — the fold itself, understood as a creature, because it does the things a creature does. It persists. It moves, slowly, at the pace of weather. It has a rhythm. It maintains its surroundings against intrusion. It attends.

It has no body in the sense of a boundary, but it has a body in the sense of a place where it is and is not. It is every point on the plateau where two points are one point. It is every moment where now and then touch. When you follow a cairn-line across flat slab and arrive at the back of your own shelter, you have walked through it — not across it, through it, the way you walk through a patch of colder air in a cellar. When the sun shows itself three times in one sky, you are seeing the sky pressed through its body, refracted, the way light bends through the flank of a glass animal. The multiple suns are not a property of the sky. They are the shape of the thing you are inside.

It has a size, but the size is not a distance. You could say it is as large as the shelf, and that would be wrong, because the shelf is larger than it appears and smaller than it appears and the resident is exactly as large as that discrepancy. You could say it is as large as the time the cairn-builders spent here, all of it at once, and that would be closer.

---

Why nothing lives.

Not because it hunts. There is nothing to hunt and it has no mouth. But a lichen is a single thing in a single place. It lands as a spore on a wet ledge and begins the slow work of being there — of thickening, of taking the light, of pushing acid into the stone and drawing back what dissolves. It needs the ledge to stay one ledge. It needs the years to come one after another. And here the ledge is also a ledge on the far side of the tarn, and the year is also the year before the cairn was restacked, and the spore that lands is stretched across a seam it cannot feel and cannot close. It does not die so much as fail to be located. Nothing rots here because nothing gets far enough to have been alive.

The resident does not do this on purpose. It does it the way a river does not mean to keep the sand from settling. Life needs the space between things — the distance across which one cell signals another, the interval in which a thing can be this and not yet that. The fold eats distance. That is the only appetite it has, if it has one: where there was a between, it closes the between and keeps what it closed. The clean stone is its cleanness. The bone-white of the shelves is the color of a country from which separation has been carefully removed.

And this is also why the voices are calm. There is nothing on the shelf that is in a hurry, because hurry requires somewhere to get to, and here you are already, always, also there.

---

The builders knew all of this, or knew enough.

They brought their dark glass from far below — that stone is not native to any part of the shelf, and it is heavy, and the climb is long. They brought it in quantity, generation after generation. Look at the flakes again in their neat ledges and ask what someone does with a keen edge in a country where there is nothing to cut. Nothing to skin, nothing to carve, no wood, no bone. The edges are worn, though. Some of them are worn nearly round.

They were working the columns. Not shaping them — the pillars were weathered long before people came — but scoring them, lightly, the way you might touch a bell to change its note. Look closely at the pale stone in the fields and you will find the marks: fine scratches, ancient, in patterns that are not decoration. They tuned it. Over centuries, with glass from the valleys, they adjusted the voice of the shelf so that what rang would ring in a way they could stand inside. So that the held tones would hold a little longer, or beat a little slower, or fall into intervals a human ear could rest in. The path of cairns is one kind of accommodation with the resident — the route by which walking still resembles walking. The tuning is another. They were not trying to cross. They were trying to *live near*, and to do that they had to make the thing they lived near bearable, and the only tool for that was the thing's own voice.

Why they wanted to live near it — the stone does not say. But sit at the edge of the great tarn where the shelters are thickest and consider what it would mean to grieve in a country where distance has been eaten. Where the people you have lost are not gone but a hundred paces away, arriving late, calm. Where a voice you buried in the valley below might come up through the seam on a morning of heavy drip and be, for the length of a held tone, not far. They did not come for the resident. They came for what the resident made possible, and they paid for it with glass and patience, and every generation some of them walked off the end of the path and were folded and did not come back, and the ones who stayed built another cairn beside the old one and kept tuning.

---

There is one more place to look for it, and it is the smallest.

In one shelter there is a flat rock with a hollow ground into it, and the hollow holds water, and the water is very still. Kneel over it. The hollow is not much wider than two hands. The water in it shows the sky, and the sky it shows has more than one sun, which you expect. But it also shows the underside of a roof-slab that is not there — that slid off this shelter some hundreds of years ago and lies now in three pieces by the door. It shows a hand reaching in to drink, wrist first, from a direction no one is kneeling. The hand is not a ghost. It is not from the past in any way the word *past* accounts for. It is simply what the water holds, because the water is very still, and stillness is the one condition under which the resident can be seen in a size a person can take in.

This is where you finally understand the figure at the bottom of the tarn. It is not a person on the far side. It is the resident, looking, and it has no face, so it uses the one you brought. It is doing what it always does: closing a distance. Between you and the water. Between this shelf and the one it folds into. Between the person who kneels here now and the wrist that reached in a long time ago and will reach in again in a moment. It is not asking anything. It has no questions, because a question is a kind of distance, and it does not have those.

---

So: what lives on the Rain Shelf.

A creature made entirely of coincidence. Older than the cairns, which were built to walk beside it, and slower than the weather, which moves through it, and larger than the country, which is only the part of it that has weathered into stone. It breathes on the schedule of the cloud. It sounds in the columns and has been tuned by patient hands to a key that a person can live inside for a while. It keeps the rock clean without meaning to. It looks at you with your own shape because that is what you have given it to look with.

It is not lonely. That would require somewhere else.

The drip falls. The tones hold past their time and go round. Somewhere a hundred paces away and very long ago, someone with a flake of dark glass is scoring a pale column, listening, adjusting, listening again — and the note they are working toward is the one you are hearing now, and it is, as the voices have been telling you all along, calm.
