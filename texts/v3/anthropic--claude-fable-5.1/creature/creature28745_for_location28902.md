They have hands you notice first.

Not large. Not especially strong. But the palms are pale and thick and slightly shiny, the way a palm gets from holding one thing for a long time, and the thing is a wooden scraper, and the shine is from the handle. The fingertips are blunt. The nails are kept short, filed on the walls, because a nail is a tool here — you test a surface with it, run it across a step or a sill or the rim of a bowl to see whether the skin has come yet, and the little squeak or the little catch tells you whether you have a morning or whether you have already lost one. Everyone does this. They do it without knowing they do it, to doorframes as they pass, to the edge of a table as they sit, to each other's shoulders. A hand comes to rest and the thumb moves once, checking.

Their skin is damp. Not sweaty — cool, and damp, always, the way the walls are. In the folds of the fingers and at the corners of the eyes there is a faint whiteness that is not dirt and does not wash off with water, because it is what the water leaves. Old people are pale to the elbows. Children are scrubbed with sand brought from the marsh edge, once a week, and they hate it, and it does not much help.

The hair is stiff. The water is so hard that whatever grows on the mound grows heavy, and hair worn long becomes a coarse ashy rope that clicks faintly when it moves. Most of them cut it short, close to the skull, men and women, and the old ones shave it, and the shaved skull under the grey light has the same soft pallor as the roofs. From the reed line, if you saw a group of them standing at the lip, you might take them for part of it.

Their teeth are very good. This is remarked on by outsiders. It is the water.

---

They do not set things down.

This is not a rule they follow so much as a shape they have taken. Watch one of them eat: the bowl stays in the hand, or in the crook of the arm, and when it is empty it is turned upside down and hung by its loop on a peg, and the peg is scraped when the bowl is lifted off it. Watch one of them work: the tools are held, or tucked into a belt, or passed to someone. Watch one of them rest, which is rare, and you will see that they rest standing, one shoulder against a wall, and that the shoulder moves — a slight roll, every little while, breaking the contact. They sleep on hung mats and they sleep in a curl and they turn in the night more than other people do. Babies are carried, always, in a wrap against the chest, and when they are laid down for a moment on a step the mother's foot is under them, and the step is scraped after.

A child of three has learned this. A child of three, given a shell, will hold it for the whole afternoon and then hand it to you rather than drop it. People who come from off the mound find this unbearable to watch, and then, after a few days on the crust, find themselves doing it.

Those who leave — and some do, a few in a generation, poling out across the marsh with the traders and not coming back — are said to keep the habit for the rest of their lives. They cannot put a cup on a table. They lift their doors. They wake in the night and run a thumbnail along the bedframe, and lie there with the small ordinary wood under their hand, and do not know what they were checking for.

---

They speak low, and close, and with their hands on each other.

The stone takes the voice out of the air before it has gone ten paces, so there is no calling across the town, no shouting down from a roof. If you want to say something to someone you go and stand beside them. You touch the arm or the back of the neck, and you say it near the ear, and the words are short. Their speech has few long words in it and almost no ornament. There is a great deal in it about water — where it is going, where it was yesterday, whose runnel is running clear and whose is starting to climb — and very little about anything you cannot touch.

There is no word for a thing left alone. There is a word for what happens to it, which is the same word used for a wound closing, and for ice, and for a person who has stopped speaking.

They laugh, but quietly, with the mouth mostly shut, a sort of shaking. Children are hushed not for noise but for stillness. A child standing too long in one place will feel a hand on its back moving it along, gently, the way you would move something off a hearth.

---

Households are not counted by walls. They are counted by water.

Each runnel that leaves the crown basin has a line of houses along it, uphill to downhill, and that line is a family in the sense that matters: the people whose neglect can seal your door and whose door your neglect can seal. Marriage is a matter of channels — a person from one runnel who goes to live on another has left, in a way, and taken their scraping with them, and the runnel they left is one pair of hands poorer. The eldest along a channel is not its head. The head is whoever knows the channel best, which is usually but not always the eldest, and what they know is where it has run before. Where it turned in their grandmother's time. Where it tried to leave its bed the winter the basin overflowed. Where the stone under it is thin, because there is a filled room beneath, and a cistern cut there would go through.

These people are called, roughly, the ones who remember the water. There is one for each runnel and they meet, irregularly, standing in the crown basin up to their ankles in the cold, because that is the only place on the mound where everybody's water is still the same water. They do not decide much. They mostly remind each other. When a runnel is to be recut, the one who remembers it walks the line first with a wooden rod and marks the bed, and the marks are in the same places as the last time, and the time before that.

Quarrels between runnels are the only quarrels there are. They last. The filled houses are the record of them — the white masses with the ghost lintels, leaned upon by their neighbours — and everyone knows which one is which and whose fault it was, and the fault is inherited, and so is the grudge, downhill, with the water. There has never, as far as anyone knows, been a killing. There has been, more than once, a family that woke to a threshold that had not been scraped by the people above and had climbed in the night, and had to cut their way out, and did not forgive it.

---

The dead are let go.

When someone dies, their room stops being scraped. That is the whole of it. The body is laid on the floor, on its side, in the curl they sleep in, and the door is lifted out and set against the outside wall and not put back. The family moves what they need into other rooms — the rooms are small; there is not much — and then they do not go in again. The water does the rest. Within a season the body has a skin. Within a few years the floor has risen around it, and the walls have thickened, and the doorway is a slot you could put an arm through, and then not even that. The house next door leans in. The room becomes a lump in the mound with a person-shaped hollow at its heart, or, in time, without one.

They do not think of this as burial. Burial is what the marsh people do, in the black mud, and the townspeople find that horrible — to put someone where nothing holds, where the ground itself gives. They think of it as the person staying. The town is thick with them. When a cistern is cut and goes through a floor into an old room and the diggers see a bench-shaped swelling with something curled against it, they are not afraid and they do not stop. They cut around. They lay the new floor. They say the old word for a wound closing, once, and go on.

A person who is dying is not left still before they are dead. That is the one thing. Right up to the last hour someone sits with them and moves them a little, turns them, lifts their hand and puts it down elsewhere, so that the skin does not come while they can feel it. Nobody says why this matters. They just do it.

---

The children are the wettest of them.

They are always in the runnels. The runnels are the only places on the mound where anything moves fast, and the children lie along them on their bellies with their arms in the water and let the cold go up to the shoulder, and come out blue and shaking and pleased. They race things down the channels — reed stems, a seed, a shed feather from the dipper — and where the runnels fan out over the lip and go into the marsh, the losing things are caught on the terraces and stone over, and years later there are little white feathers and little white stems in the scallops of the edge, hundreds of them, from a hundred years of racing.

They make stone on purpose. This is their play and, later, their courting. You take a thing — a leaf, a snail shell, a knot of grass, a lock of hair — and you set it in the crown basin where the water is hardest, and you leave it, which is the daring part, and in a season you have it back turned white and heavy and made permanent. Children give these to each other. Young people give them to each other. An old woman will have, in a lidded box with her copper needle, a stoned leaf that a boy gave her sixty years ago, and the boy will be a lump under a floor somewhere downhill, and the leaf will look exactly as it did.

Children are not named at birth. They are named when they have learned not to set things down — when a parent watches them hold a shell for an afternoon and hand it back. Until then they are called by the name of the runnel, with a small word after it that means *new*, or *soft*, or *not yet skinned*. Most are named before they are three. A child who is slow to learn it is worried over. A child who never learns it — there are stories — was taken away with the dogs.

---

The traders come in the dry weeks, poling flat boats through the reeds, and they do not come onto the crust.

They stop at the lip and stand in their boats, and the townspeople come down to the pale edge and stand there, and the trade is done across a stride of white terrace with everyone's feet in an inch of moving water. The marsh people bring wool and grain and salt and the small copper things and, once in a long while, a dog, which is refused. They take away the green stone, the moss-lintels, cut and polished so the lace shows through. It is heavy and they do not take much of it. Where it goes after them nobody on the mound knows or asks.

The marsh people do not like the town. They think the people of the mound are what happens to you if you stay too long near the spring — that the whiteness at the elbows goes inward, that the low voices are because the throat is stoning. They have a story in which the town is a single family that stopped moving a very long time ago and is still, slowly, becoming its own house. The townspeople have heard this story. They do not find it as insulting as it is meant to be.

---

Very little else lives with them.

The dipper does. It has always been one dipper, in the sense that there is always one, and it works the outflow channels from the basin down to the lip and back, walking into the cold water and coming up, and it is the only thing on the mound that is allowed to stand still. It stands on a stone in a runnel for the length of a breath and the children watch it and do not move it on. If it were ever to stone, they say, that would be the end of the town. It never has. It is too quick.

The snails come up the walls in the morning wet and are scraped off, and the ones the scrapers miss are found later in the crevices, white and shut. These are the only unlucky thing. A stone snail in the wall of a house means a year of something — a sealed door, a sick child, a runnel that climbs — and it is cut out, carefully, with a shell, and carried down to the lip and thrown as far into the marsh as an arm can throw it, and the person who threw it does not look where it lands.

The cat belongs to nobody and sleeps nowhere twice. It is grey, or it is white with the crust, no one can say. It is fed by whichever house it walks into. It has never been seen to stand still. There are people on the mound who think it is the same cat there has always been, and people who think it is many cats, one after another, learning the town the way the children do, and there is no way to tell which is right, and they do not argue about it. It is the one thing they do not argue about.

---

And under them, the others.

Not ghosts. They do not have ghosts; a ghost would need somewhere to stand. But they know how many floors there are beneath the ones they walk on, roughly, from the cisterns, and they know that every floor had rooms and every room had people who did what they do — who lifted their doors in the morning and ran a thumbnail along the sill and scraped, and scraped, and let a little powder fall, and were in the end let go into the white. They know that the town they live in is standing on the shoulders of that town, and that one on the next, and that there is no bottom to it that anyone has found. And they know that the water that comes up cold through the crown basin, and runs over every roof, and beads on every lintel, and leaves its finger's width on everything they clean, is the same water that filled those rooms and is filling theirs. It has been the same water the whole time. It does not stop. It is the only thing on the mound that has never once been moved along.

If you asked them what lives there, they would not say themselves. They would look at you with the grey light on their pale skulls, and one of them would put a hand on your arm and roll the shoulder against the wall, and say, low, near your ear, the name of the spring. Which is also the word for what happens to a thing that is left. Which is also, they would not tell you, the name they will each be called by, eventually, downhill, when the door is lifted out and set against the wall and not put back.
