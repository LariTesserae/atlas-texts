Start with what is smallest, because on the massif the smallest things are doing the most.

The pools are not coloured by minerals. That is what you assume, and it is half right — the ochre and the rust are iron, the white is silica and lime — but the tea-brown and the milky green and the strange soft yellow are alive. Each pool is a population. Where the water leaves the ground it is too hot for anything with a nucleus, and there, in the throat of the spring, the floor is a pale bare crust and the only life is something you would need an instrument to see: threads and films of bacteria that have made their peace with temperatures that would cook an egg, laid down in sheets so thin they show as a faint iridescence on the stone. A metre from the source the water has lost a few degrees and the films thicken and begin to have colour — a dull yellow first, then orange, then, where the pool is cool enough to put your hand in without losing it, a green so dense it looks like felt. The bands do not mix. Each has its temperature and keeps to it, so that every pool on the plateau is a target of concentric rings, hottest at the bull's-eye, and if you know how to read it you can take the mountain's temperature by colour alone.

The terraces are their work. The rims that look carved were not carved; they were grown. The mats take up what the water carries and lay it down as they die, layer on layer, a fraction of a millimetre a year, building the lips of the pools upward and outward so slowly that the pools appear to have always been the shape they are. They have not. A pool that is round today was a crack a few thousand years ago. The mats are still building. Put your fingertip to the rim of one and it comes away with a faint chalky bloom and, under that, a slickness — the living surface, invisible, indifferent to being touched.

The mat on the rock between the pools is a different thing, and it is barely a thing. Grey-green, dry to the eye and damp to the hand, it is neither moss nor lichen but the partnership that becomes lichen when there is enough light and enough time — an alga and a fungus that have found each other and not yet fully decided what they are to one another. It grows where the drizzle is steadiest and the rock is warm but not hot, in a band a hand's width wide around the cooler pools and along the drip-line of the basalt columns, and it grows nowhere else. Where the stone rises a few degrees beyond its tolerance it stops as if at a wall. Where the drip falters it browns and curls and waits. It can wait for years. It is the one green thing on the mountain and it is not properly green and it is not, in any sense a plant would recognise, growing. It is persisting.

The sedge is a different matter. The sedge is a plant, a real one, with roots and seed, and it has no business being here. It lives in the deepest cracks, where the condensation drip is heaviest and a pocket of grit has collected, and each hummock is a single individual, possibly very old — the sedge does not appear to flower more than once in a decade and does not appear to set seed even then, or if it does the seed does not travel, because each hummock stands alone with metres of bare rock around it and no young. There are perhaps two hundred on the whole plateau. You could count them. Someone has.

Now the insects, because everything above them depends on them.

They are beetles, or the thing that was a beetle a long time ago and has been simplified by the place: small, black, hard, with wings that are barely wings, more like the stubs left after the wings were taken away. They do not fly so much as fall upward. A beetle at rest on the warm rock at the foot of a steam-plume is caught by the updraft and carried aloft, tumbling, to the point a body-length or two above the ground where the plume goes cold and collapses; it is wet then, and heavy, and it falls with the drizzle back to the rock, and dries, and warms, and is caught again. That is its life. It rises and falls in the same column of steam from the day it emerges to the day it is eaten, and it never goes anywhere else, and it is, as far as you can tell, entirely content.

The larvae live in the film. Every warm wet rock on the plateau — which is every rock — carries a skin of condensate a few molecules deep, and in it, grazing on the bacterial haze and the spores of the mat, the young beetles crawl. They are pale and soft and almost transparent and they are everywhere, so that when you kneel and put your eye close to the stone you see the surface is not still but faintly, endlessly stirring. The lizards do not eat them; they are too small and too many, and the lizards' attention is elsewhere. When they are ready they crawl to a plume and harden in an hour and go up. They mate in the air, at the top of the rise, in the instant before the fall, and the female lays in the drip. The whole cycle, from film to steam to film, takes about a month. They do not overwinter because there is no winter. They do not disperse because there is nowhere to go. The plumes are separated by cold dry rock a beetle cannot cross, and so each plume, over the years, has become its own small country, and if you had the patience and the lens you would find that the beetles of the eastern basin differ, very slightly, from the beetles of the north.

And then the lizards.

You have been walking on them, or nearly, for an hour before you begin to understand what they are, and the understanding does not come all at once. First you see that they are not one species. Most are the charcoal blunt-headed ones, but among them, lying in the same crowds, are others — a smaller, finer-boned kind with a paler throat; a heavier one with a keeled back that seems to prefer the pipeline; here and there something almost black with a blue sheen that lies apart from the rest at the very hottest ledges, where the others will not go. They do not fight. They lie across one another without regard for kind. Whatever separates them in the desert below is not relevant here, because here there is only one thing that matters and they are all here for it.

They do not bask in the sun. That is the first strangeness, once you know to look for it. A desert lizard turns its back to the light and flattens to catch it; these lie with their bellies to the ground and their backs to the sky and take no interest in the sun at all. The sun is too far away and too thin. The heat is below. Their whole orientation has turned over. The sensing is in the throat and the belly, in a patch of naked skin under the jaw that they press to the stone and hold there, so that a lizard on the massif looks like an animal listening to the ground, which in a sense it is. When the ground shifts — when a fissure opens a fraction and a ledge warms by a degree, or the turbine's note changes and something in the deep rock changes with it — they know through the throat, before any sound could carry, and that is the ripple you see.

The flush is blood. The orange along the flanks is the skin gone translucent over vessels that have opened wide to carry heat inward, and it deepens with the temperature of the rock, so that a lizard on a cool ledge is charcoal all over and a lizard at the lip of a hot pool glows along its sides like a coal in a grate. The very hottest ledges, where the blue-black ones lie, are hot enough that you would not hold your hand there. They lie there for hours. Turn one over — you can; they barely resist — and the belly is not orange but white, dead white, a plate of scale as thick as a fingernail, and it is dry, and it is hot to the touch in a way that is not quite pleasant, and the animal in your hand is looking at you with an eye that has a dark sclera and a heavy brow and a pupil drawn to a slit against a light it has no use for, and it is waiting to be put back.

Put it back. It will go to the exact place it was, and the crowd will close over it.

They stack. On the best ledges they lie two and three deep, and you take this at first for crowding, but it is not crowding; it is a process. The bottom layer is hottest and the top is coldest and the top can see the sky. Every so often, without any signal you can detect, a lizard at the bottom of a pile will begin, very slowly, to move — not away, but up, working its way through the bodies over it as if through a loose drift of leaves, until it lies on top, and the pile beneath it has shifted to close the space, and some other animal has found itself at the bottom. It takes about an hour. It goes on all day. Watch a single ledge from morning to dusk and you will see the same pile turn itself over and over, like something being slowly kneaded, so that every animal in it spends its share of time at the hearth and its share of time on watch. Nobody organises this. Nobody has to.

The watch is for the birds. When the dark shape drops, the top layer scatters — a lateral scatter, sideways off the pile and flat to the rock, an inch, two inches, enough — and the bird takes what it takes from the ones that were slow, and the pile is a lizard lighter, and within a minute it has closed and reordered and is turning again. They do not look up afterward. They do not, so far as you can tell, remember. There are a hundred thousand of them and one is taken every minute somewhere on the plateau and the numbers do not change, and you begin, standing there, to feel that the pile is the animal and the lizards are its cells, and that a cell being taken is not a death the animal notices.

But they die in other ways, and those deaths the mountain keeps.

Walk to the edge of the warm zone — anywhere, on any side, the place where the rock goes from wet and hot to dry and merely cool — and you will find the line marked. Not by anything living. Along the boundary, in a band a few metres wide, the ground is covered in small dry bodies: lizards that went too far in the night, or were pushed to the outside of a crowd at the plateau's edge and did not get back in before the cold came up, and stopped where they were, and dried where they stopped. The cold here is absolute and the air is dry and nothing rots. They have been accumulating for as long as there have been lizards on the massif. In places they are heaped, a low rampart of husks, charcoal and white, every one of them lying with its throat pressed to a stone that had, by the time it got there, nothing left to give. In the morning the living come out onto the rampart to lie on the dead, because the husks hold a little warmth from the dawn sun, and by midday they have gone back inside. The line does not move. The mountain's heat reaches so far and no farther and the lizards have drawn the boundary in themselves.

Their eggs go into the mountain. In the mineral grit at the rims of the cooler pools — the green ones, the felt ones, where the terrace is soft and the temperature is exactly what it has been for a thousand years — the females dig, a hand's depth, and lay, and cover, and go back to the crowd. The mountain does the rest. There is no season. The clutches go in year round and come up year round and there is never a time when there are not hatchlings — small, grey, almost transparent, no longer than your thumb — working up out of the crust and into a crowd that takes no notice of them whatsoever. They go under. They lie beneath the adults, in the warmest place there is, and for the first months of their lives they do not see the sky at all. Some are eaten by the blue-black ones, who will eat anything small enough and slow enough. Most are not. The mountain incubates them all at the one temperature, and if the sex of these animals is set by heat, as it is in so many of their kind, then the mountain has set it, the same way, for every clutch since the first — and perhaps that is why the numbers do not change. Perhaps the mountain has been deciding.

The old ones you will know when you see them. They are the ones the pools have begun to build on. An animal that has lain at a hot rim for twenty years, thirty, forty — nobody knows how long they live, but it is long — carries the same mineral bloom the stone carries, a crust of white and ochre along the spine and the shoulders where the drizzle falls and the water leaves what it has brought. They are being terraced. They are becoming rim. The oldest are patched so thickly with deposit that you cannot see where the animal ends and the pool begins, and they do not move, ever, that you can see, and their flanks do not flush, and only when you kneel and look very closely at the throat, at the pulse under the pale naked skin against the stone, can you be sure the mountain has not taken them entirely.

The birds are two, and they belong to the mountain differently.

The pale-bellied ones are a kind of swift, or near enough. They nest in the columns — in the gaps between the organ-pipes of basalt, where the drizzle drips and the stone is warm and there is nothing that can climb — and they have built the nests, over a very long time, of the only material there is: their own spit and the mineral crust, which sets around it. The nests are stone. Some are so old and so thickly plated that they have merged with the column, and a bird arriving at one seems to be flying into a hole in the rock. They come out at dawn and rise on the plume-tops, mouths open, and take the beetles at the crest of the fall, and they do not land except to nest. They sleep on the wing, probably, high in the cold air where the massif's own heat lifts them; it is not a thing you can prove but it is a thing you can believe, watching them circle up at dusk into the last light and not come down. There are perhaps a few hundred. They raise one young a year and the young are fed on beetles and leave the nest and never leave the mountain.

The dark one is a harrier, or a kite, or a thing that has become both — long-winged, long-tailed, a slow tilting flight that never seems to end. There are very few of them. You could see the same individual all day and be right. They do not nest on the plateau; they nest in the cold cliffs of the north face, below the warm zone, and they are the only creatures on the massif that leave it and come back — up in the morning on the mountain's thermal, which is the strongest for a hundred kilometres in any direction, and down at dusk, heavy, having eaten. They take a lizard a minute between them and it is nothing. They have been taking a lizard a minute since before the wellheads, and the lizards have been stacking and scattering and closing the gap for as long, and the two behaviours are so old and so exactly matched that they look, from the rim, like a single motion — the fold in the air and the ripple on the ledge, and then stillness, and then the next.

And then there is the machine, which you have to decide whether to count.

You will decide to count it. Not because it is alive — it is not — but because it has been here long enough to be lived in, and lived with, and lived by. The turbine that still turns is a small unit at the north end of the house, geared down, fed from the three wellheads that still hold pressure; the other units are seized and cold and the lizards lie on them as on any other rock. This one they lie around. The casing is too hot even for the blue-black ones, and the floor around it is a ring of warm concrete a few metres wide that is the most densely populated place on the mountain — bodies to the depth of your hand, turning, kneading, a low continuous whisper of scale on scale that is the closest the lizards come to a sound. The condensate channel runs out under the eastern wall and the mound there is a half-metre high and has been building for years and its lower layers are almost certainly not alive.

The machine does one thing. It turns, and in turning it drives a generator, and the generator feeds a line that runs to the rim and down. It also drives a pump, a small one, that draws from the coolest pool on the plateau and pushes the water through a jacket around its own bearings and lets it out again, hot, into the channel, so that the machine is in some sense bathing itself in the mountain and returning what it takes. And it drives a fan. The fan is what the lizards are listening to. When the pressure in the deep fissures rises — and it rises on a schedule that is not daily and not quite monthly and that nobody has ever explained — the governor opens, the turbine quickens, the fan quickens with it, and the note goes up a tone. The lizards feel it in the concrete before the sound has reached the door. The ripple goes out from the turbine house across the whole plateau, a wave through a hundred thousand bodies, and it is not fear; nothing scatters. It is attention. Something that is a part of them has changed its breathing, and they have noticed, and then they have accepted it.

Whether they think of the machine as a part of the mountain, or the mountain as a part of the machine, or whether they think at all, you cannot know. But you can see that they have lain against warm iron for long enough that the iron has been taken into whatever the lizards understand as ground, and that the plateau has grown a new organ and the body around it has adjusted and not rejected it, and that this happened without anyone intending it and will go on after the machine stops, because the lizards will simply lie on it cold, as they lie on the seized units, as they lie on the dead.

Whoever else lives here, you will not see.

But you will know. In the turbine house there is a room — a partition of the corrugated iron, a door that closes, a window with the glass still in it. Inside there is a cot with its legs standing in four cut-down cans that once held water and now hold a crust of scale and a few very small husks. There is a shelf with tins on it, and the tins are not old; the labels have not gone pale. There is a stove that runs from a tap off the condensate line, hot water piped straight through it, so that a kettle set on the plate comes to the boil on the mountain's own heat. There is a table and on the table a book, a ledger, ruled for pressures and outputs, and in it, in one hand, pages of pressures and outputs — the turbine's readings, taken twice a day for years, in a script that begins careful and becomes, without ever getting worse, brief.

And then, some way in, the readings begin to have company. A number in the margin, no units: 3. Then 4. Then 3. It takes you some pages to understand that this is the dark birds, counted at dawn from the rim. Later there is another column, and this one is the sedge — a count that does not change for two years and then drops by one and then does not change again. Later still, in a different ink, a note that is not a number at all: the note went up at noon, they turned before it did. And a page after that, alone: they always do.

The most recent entry is not old. The kettle, when you lift it, has water in it. The cot has a blanket and the blanket has a shape and the shape is a body's, and it is warm — but everything here is warm, and you cannot say if that means anything. You will not meet them. You can stay on the massif a week and you will not meet them. They will be on the far side of the plateau when you are on the near, or at the wellheads when you are at the rim, or asleep in the room when you are on the ledges at dusk and awake and gone when you come in. It is possible they are avoiding you. It is more likely that the mountain is simply large, and that they have arranged their days around its rhythms for so long that they move through it the way the lizards do, without haste and without being seen to move, and that a visitor is a thing the plateau notices and then closes over.
