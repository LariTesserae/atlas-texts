The birds first, because the pit was dug for them and everything else is a consequence of them.

They are pigeons, and they are also no longer quite pigeons. Somewhere at the beginning there were rock doves brought in a covered basket from a cliff somewhere, or coaxed off a granary roof with spilled barley, and set into the fresh niches while the brick was still damp enough to take a thumbprint. Since then the blood has closed on itself. No one has brought a new bird in living memory. Strays arrive — a lost racer with a brass ring on its leg, a white fantail blown off some rich man's roof, a wild dove from the hills — and they either learn the wall or are driven off it, and most are driven off, and the few that stay are swallowed within a generation. So the flock has drifted the way any shut population drifts. They are smaller than the pigeons of towns, and denser, as if compressed. The two clean black bars on the wing that the wild bird carries have smeared into a checkering and the checkering, in many of them, into a near-black, so that the whole flock in the air reads as smoke rather than slate. The eyes are the colour of hot iron. The feet are a raw red, scoured by brick. Someone from the villages could pick one out from a hundred others walking a threshing floor and say without looking twice, *that is a pit bird*, and mean by it something slightly disparaging and slightly respectful, the way you might speak of a family that has never left its valley.

Each bird knows one niche. Ten thousand mouths open in the wall, most of them identical to the width of a thumb, and each bird knows its own out of the spinning column of the return and drops to it and folds. Nobody understands how. The niche is not a home in any sense a person would recognise. It is a claim. A pair holds it — they pair for life, which in the pit is three years, four, rarely six — and the cock spends a good part of every day at the lip of it, bowing, turning full circle, dragging his fanned tail across the brick, inflating his throat and letting out the low rolling note that, multiplied by five thousand, is the pulse inside the roar. Behind him, in the dark of the niche, the hen sits on two eggs, always two, laid a day apart, and when they hatch the squabs are blind and the colour of wet putty and covered in a yellow wisp that is almost not feathers at all, and both parents feed them a thick pale curd they make in their own throats. The squabs double their weight in days. They are fat before they have eyes. In a month they are at the lip themselves, clumsy, enormous-footed, begging with a shrill wheeze that is the other sound inside the roar, the high thread through the low. The pit nests in every month. There is no season when the wall is not asking to be fed.

The niches are not equal, and the birds know this too. The high courses, the last body-height below the rim, get the first sun and the first clean air and are too far from the damp for the snakes to bother; they also lie directly under the kestrel's brick, and so the birds that hold them are the strong ones, the ones that can afford the risk, and they guard them ferociously and rarely lose them except to her. The great middle of the wall is the great middle of the flock — most birds, most eggs, most noise. The lowest active courses, a body-height or so above the floor, are held by whoever can get nothing better: yearlings, widowed birds, the pair whose eggs keep failing, the old. And then there are the birds with no niche at all, several hundred of them at any moment, young birds mostly, fledged out of the wall and finding it full. They roost along the outer edge of the ramp, shoulder to shoulder, and on the fig, and up on the rim in a long grey fringe, and every day they test the wall, dropping onto a lip that is briefly unattended and being driven off with wingblows and pecks, and they are waiting — that is the truest description of them — waiting for a death to open something. In a pit this size something opens most days. The kestrel sees to that, and the snakes, and age. A niche is rarely empty for longer than it takes the dead bird's smell to fade.

At the exodus they go up and out over the plain and they scatter. Some go a short walk, some go half a day. They walk the barley stubble after harvest picking up what the gleaners missed. They walk the threshing floors when the grain is being winnowed and are cursed and stoned and come back the next morning. They stand in the mud of the sheep folds and take the spilled fodder. They eat grit from the wadi beds to grind it all. Every one of them comes back by evening with its crop swollen hard as a plum, and that grain, carried in the bodies of five thousand birds, is the only thing the pit is fed. Nothing grows here. Everything that grows here grows on what was eaten somewhere else. The pit is a mouth that the plain puts food into without knowing it does so, and a gut that gives back, in baskets, a few times a year, a black cake that makes the plain's fields heavy. The birds do not know they are the middle of this. They know their niche, and the kestrel's brick, and the way home.

And then there are the birds that do not go.

You notice them after a while, once the roar has become ordinary enough that you can see through it. At the bottom of the pit, around the trough and along the lowest turn of the ramp, there are always a dozen or two dozen pigeons walking. Only walking. They have a wing that healed wrong, or one that never worked, or they are simply too old for the chimney; one or two in every hundred are born to the wall unable to leave it. They live on what falls. The fig drops its fruit for them, green and then purple and then split and rotten, and they eat it at every stage. The returning flock sheds grain from overfull crops in the jostle at the trough, and the walkers pick it up. Squabs fall from the high niches and die on the floor, and the walkers do not eat these, but they stand about them. They are fat and slow and they do not fear you. They know the trough as the others do not, from the edge, all day, and in the hour before dawn when the water is clearest they are the only birds drinking. They have their own niches, the lowest of the active ones, and some of them even breed, and the squabs of the walkers fly out with everyone else on the day they are ready and never come back to the floor, and this seems to be understood. A walker lives a year or two on the floor. When it dies it dies where it stands, and the floor takes it.

The kestrel is one bird, and she is also all the kestrels there have been. That pale brick on the rim is pale with a hundred years of talons. It is always a female — the male is smaller, and hunts the plain for mice and lizards, and comes to the rim only to pass her something in the courting season — and she holds the brick the way the pit birds hold a niche, as a claim, and when she is old and slow another comes and takes it from her, and the exchange is not gentle. She nests a little way round the rim on the sunward side, in a niche that some kestrel long ago found half-collapsed and has since been widened, generation on generation, by scraping and by the sheer wearing of bodies, until it is the size of four pigeon niches and shaped like nothing else in the wall. Her chicks are the only creatures born in the pit who eat pigeon. They are fed, from the first day, on what she takes twice daily out of the column: the slow, the young, the one that hesitated, and — always, if there is one — the white one, because a white bird against a smoke-coloured flock is the easiest thing in the world to follow with the eye. The pit birds throw a white one now and then, some old fantail blood surfacing. It rarely lives to hold a niche. She is, in her way, the flock's breeder. She has been keeping it dark for longer than anyone has been keeping records of anything.

Below her, behind the wall, the snakes.

There are three kinds, or three that are seen. One is thin as a bootlace and fast and the colour of the dry brick, and it works the wall in the heat of the day, threading through the honeycomb after lizards, and the pigeons ignore it because it is too small to be a danger and they have learned this. One is thicker, a slow heavy rope with a blunt head and a pattern like scattered sand, and it moves at dusk and in the dark, a hand's breadth at a time, niche to niche, and it takes eggs whole — you can see the egg travelling down it as a lump, and then a crack, and the shell comes back up crushed and is spat — and takes squabs when it can pin one, and this is the one the birds shriek at and will not leave their eggs for, though leaving would save them, because the niche is the only thing they have. The third is almost never seen at all. It lives in the floor itself, in the warm damp under the cake, a sand-coloured thing with a tail like its head, and it feeds on beetles and beetle larvae and on the mice that come in from the plain for the spilled grain, and the only sign of it is a smooth-sided tunnel at the base of the wall and the way the cake sometimes shifts underfoot in a line. All three shed in the low stopped niches, where the damp keeps the skin soft enough to come off in one piece, and if you put your hand into one of those niches you will pull out something like a length of yellowed paper printed with scales, and there will be more behind it, years of them, the way a family keeps old letters.

The lizards the snakes eat are two kinds also. By day there are the small brown ones on the sunlit brick, quick, flat, watching the flies. By night the geckos come out — pale, soft, almost translucent, eyes like black beads — and walk the warm wall upside down and sideways and click to one another in the roar, a small dry sound like two pebbles, and take the moths that come down out of the dark to the warmth. The geckos live in the cracks between the courses, in the healed joints, and there are more of them than you would think possible, because the pit at night is the warmest thing for a day's walk in any direction and every small cold-blooded creature that can find it does.

Under all of that, the floor.

The floor is a country of its own and its people are beetles. The cake you walk on is only the roof of it. Below the trodden crust, in the warm dark where the dung is still soft and breathing off its heat, there is a mass of life so dense that a handful of it lifted to the light seethes and drips. Broad black beetles that roll and pack and tunnel, whose larvae are fat and white and curled like a thumb. Small oval ones, brown and hairy, that do nothing but strip flesh from bone, and do it so fast that a squab that falls in the morning is a clean small skeleton by the third day and a scatter of chalk sinking into the cake by the tenth. Long thin ones with pincers that eat the others. Their larvae, and the larvae of the flies, and the flies themselves rising in the heat in a haze so thick it has a sound of its own, a finer thread in the roar, and the flies feed the lizards and the lizards feed the snakes and the kestrel takes the odd lizard too when the pigeons are being difficult, and the whole thing is a wheel with dung at the hub.

And in the wall — this you will not see, though it is the most numerous thing here — the ticks.

They are flat and grey and soft, the size of a lentil when they are empty and the size of a chickpea when they are not, and they live in the cracks of the brick, in every healed joint and every spalled edge and behind every patch.
