The honest answer is that the description already gave you most of the inhabitants, and hedged on every one of them. Seal-things. Climbing things. Something in the pools. Something in the fog. That hedging is not evasion; it is the correct register for a place where you cannot see. But you can stay longer than the description did, and the longer you stay the more the hedges come down, one at a time, and what is underneath them is stranger than the hedges were.

Start from the bottom, because everything here starts from the bottom.

---

**The film**

It is not a plant and it is not quite a thing. On the wall of a gallery near the centre, where the water has been running for a thousand years, it is the thickness of a coat of paint and the colour of wet bone, and when you put your palm flat against it it is warmer than the mineral under it and very slightly alive to the touch — not moving, but yielding, the way a callus yields. Scrape it with a fingernail and it comes away in a ribbon and smells of struck matches and pennies. Beneath it the mineral is not white but a pale grey that is exactly the colour of the film itself when the film is dead, and this is the first thing to notice about it: the walls are the same colour as the thing that grows on the walls, and after a while you stop being sure which one you are looking at.

It eats the water. Not the water — what the water carries. Iron coming up in one state and wanting to be in another, sulphur doing the same, a handful of gases dissolved at depth and pressure and coming out of solution as the water cools. Every one of those transitions gives off a little energy, and the film lives on those little energies the way things on the surface live on light. It needs no sun and has never had one. It needs the rock to be warm and the water to keep coming and the air to be cold enough that the coming is a shock, and all three of those are true here at every point on the shelf at every hour of the long night, and so the film is everywhere, on every surface the water has ever touched, thickest where the water hesitates.

And the water hesitates where the film is thickest.

Stay with that. The description said the water *knows where the walls go*, that something was *put into it or grown into it or written into it*, and left the mechanism there. But you can crouch at the edge of a young district, at the ankle-high beginnings of a wall, with your ear almost on the stone, and watch a trickle arrive at a place where there is no wall yet and stop. It does not stop because of the stone. The stone is flat. It stops because there is a lip of film there, a pale rind no thicker than a fingernail, and the water pools against the rind and slows and cools and gives up its lime and iron and leaves them exactly there, on the rind, a fraction of a millimetre higher than they were, and the film grows up over the new mineral by the same fraction, and the lip is a fraction higher, and the water stops a fraction sooner. The film is not on the wall. The film is the leading edge of the wall. The wall is the film's cast, laid down behind it as it climbs.

Whether the film was told to make these shapes, or whether the shapes are simply what the film does — whether it was *written* or whether this is only its handwriting — you will not be able to settle. But you will stop saying that something was put into the water. Something is in the water. It is the thing you scraped off with your fingernail, and it is the architect, and it is the food of nearly everything else on the shelf, and it is eaten constantly, from every wall, by many hundred million mouths, and it does not mind. It grows back at the rate the water comes. The city is being built by the one inhabitant that no other inhabitant regards as anything but a meal.

---

**The climbers**

They are the mouths.

Pick one off the wall — it comes away with a small reluctant sound, six or eight pairs of legs letting go one after another — and hold it in your cupped hand and it is the size of a thumb-joint, plated, the plates pale and slightly translucent so that in the almost-dark you can feel rather than see a shadow of organs moving inside. It has no eyes. There has never been anything for eyes to do here. In place of a head it has a rounded plate with a fringe of stiff filaments that sweep the surface ahead of it, and beneath the fringe a rasp, and the rasp is what you hear when you stand still in a gallery: not a click, close up, but a scrape, a tiny hard tongue taking film off mineral, repeated and repeated and repeated.

They do not flee you. They do not know what fleeing is, because on this shelf nothing that eats them moves faster than they do. Set the one from your hand back on the wall and it turns once, orienting to the direction the water is running, and begins to rasp again. They graze upward, always upward, toward the wetter and newer film at the tops of the walls, and when they reach the top they fall off, or are pushed off by the ones behind, and they land in the galleries below and begin to climb again. You will find drifts of them at the base of every wall, alive and dead together, the dead ones being eaten by the live ones and by things smaller than either. You will find their shed plates in banks like sand. You will find, if you dig into one of those banks with your hands, that the lower layers have been cemented by the water and are becoming stone, and that there are climbers moving in the cement, and that the film is growing over them.

They breed on the walls. There is no season and so there is no breeding season, and at any moment some fraction of the climbers on any wall are carrying under their plates a paste of eggs which they leave in the film, and the young hatch into the film and eat their way out of it. But once, twice in a long night, something changes in the water — a shift in the rock's temperature, a pulse of something new coming up from below — and the film blooms, and the climbers respond to the bloom, and for a few days the walls of an entire district are one continuous moving surface, and the young that hatch from that bloom are not the usual kind. They are thin, and soft, and they have a pair of membranes folded along their backs, and when the bloom dies back and the walls are bare and there is nothing left to eat, they let go and open the membranes and drop into the fog and are carried.

That is what the flying things eat. You wondered.

---

**The seal-things**

Now the hedge that is hardest to take down, because they are the ones you can touch.

They are warm. That is the first thing your hand tells you when you put it on a back in the dark. Warm like a horse, blood-warm, and covered not in fur but in a skin that feels like wet suede and gives off, when they move, a smell that is animal and sulphurous both. They are the size of a large man lying down, or a small man if young, and they are that shape — a long body tapering both ways, heavy in the middle — and they move in the way the description said, on limbs that are flippers when they are lying in the water and hands when they lift themselves out of it. Watch one haul over the rim of a pool. It puts a hand down flat on the mineral, and the hand is broad and webbed and has five short thick fingers, and the fingers spread and grip, and there is a nail on each one worn to a blunt shine. The floors of the lower city are smooth because of those hands. Ten thousand years from now they will still be smooth and no smoother, because the film grows over the wear as fast as the wear happens, and the hands wear the film, and the film grows, and the hands wear it.

You will want to know where they came from. The shelf is young. Nothing this shape could have arisen here in the time the shelf has existed, and nothing this shape belongs to a mountain a thousand kilometres from any sea. Sit among them long enough and you will build, without evidence, a story: that somewhere below, in the ordinary world of ice and lichen, there were once warm springs, and a beast that lived in them, and that when the rock came up and the springs came up with it and the beast's old pools went cold, the beast climbed. In the dark, through the cloud, over the fractured stone, following the warmth up as it left. The shape it has is the shape it had. It did not become this here; it simply arrived.

They lie in the pools according to a rule you learn slowly. The hottest water, closest to the emerging springs, holds the oldest animals. They lie half submerged and almost motionless, and they are being cooked, very slowly, and they know it, and they stay. The pups are not born in the pools but in the galleries above them, in the warm dry cells that the water left when it ran inside the walls, and for the first weeks of their lives they never touch water at all — the mothers come up to them wet and lie against them and the pups lick the film off their mothers' backs. Then the mothers bring them down.

And the groan. You will spend a long time on the groan.

It is not, you will decide, a voice. It is the sound of a body letting its breath out under the weight of the bodies on top of it, and in a pool where a hundred bodies are packed on top of each other it is a hundred breaths let out at a hundred slightly different pitches and it is continuous because there is always someone breathing out. That is the mechanical account and it is true.

But lie at the edge of a pool for a whole night with your ear against the warm mineral and listen to how it moves. A groan begins at one side. The animal beside it groans, a moment later, a little lower. The one beside that. It crosses the pool in a slow wave, each body answering the one before it, and reaches the far side, and stops, and there is a pause of a length you can count, and then it begins again on the far side and comes back. Every animal in the pool sounds once in each crossing. If one is missing — taken, or dead, or hauled out — the wave has a gap in it, and the gap is there on the next crossing, and the next, until the bodies have shifted to fill it. It is a count. Whether anyone is counting is a different question. But it is a count, and it passes over the pools of the lower city all night, all season, hundreds of pools, thousands of crossings, and it is the sound of the shelf keeping track of how many of them there are.

---

**The bats**

They are bats. There is no more honest hedge available; they have the leather and the fold and the face, and the fact that the description said *or the local equivalent* was only a way of saying that no one has held one. You can hold one. Reach into a cell in a tower wall where the roost is packed and you will feel first the heat, a solid animal heat that comes out of the hole at you like breath, and then the fur, and then the bat itself, which weighs almost nothing and turns in your hand and bites you, hard, with a mouth full of very small teeth, and screams at a pitch you feel in your fillings.

They are pale. The ones nearest the openings of the cells are grey and the ones deep inside are nearly white and their eyes are sealed under the skin. They eat the winged young of the climbers when there are winged young, which is rarely, and between times they lick the walls. That is what they do with the long dark: they hang in the cells and reach out and lick the mineral, for the salt in it and for the film, and the licking is a sound, a soft continuous wet sound that fills the interior of every tower and that you will mistake, the first time, for the water.

And they foul the cells. Of course they do. Ten thousand bats in a tower for a thousand years produce a quantity of dung that ought to have filled the tower to the roof, and it has not, because the water runs inside the walls, and where the water runs it builds, and what it builds over it keeps. Break open — you cannot break open; the mineral is too hard — find a place where a tower has fractured along one of the shelf's long straight cracks, and look at the inside face of the wall, and it is striped. Grey, and then black, and then grey. A century of construction, a century of roost. The black bands are thin near the base and thick near the top where the colonies are larger now than they were. The towers have their history inside them, written in what the bats left, and the water has kept it all, and is keeping it still, another layer the thickness of a breath.

When a colony startles the whole tower exhales. You know this. What you will learn, if you stay, is that the startle is not random. It begins in the highest cells. Always the highest. It runs down the tower and across to the next tower and down that one, and by the time it has passed you can stand at the base and hear the fog above the city full of wings, and then the long settling, and then nothing. And you will begin to wonder what is in the highest cells that is not in the lower ones, and what it is up there that the bats are afraid of.

---

**The gallery-walkers**

The description called them slow pale predators the size of dogs and left it there, and that is fair, because they are hard to know. They are hard to know because they are hard to find, and they are hard to find because they are very nearly the same temperature as the walls.

You find one by accident. You are moving along a gallery in the old district with your hand on the wall, as you have learned to move, and the wall under your hand becomes soft, and warm, and rises very slightly, and you understand that you have your hand on a flank. It does not react. It is longer than you thought — you walk your hand along it and it goes on, low, a body the height of your knee and the length of a man — and it has no fur and no plates and its skin is smooth and very slightly loose, like the skin of something that was once larger. At one end it narrows to a head that is all mouth. The mouth is a rasp, like the climbers' mouths are rasps, only the size of a hand, and around the mouth there is a fringe of long stiff whiskers, and the whiskers are moving across the wall ahead of it in slow sweeps, and where the whiskers find a climber the head turns and the mouth closes and there is the sound the description promised, the sound of tearing paper, and then the head turns back to the wall.

It is not hunting. It is grazing, in the same way the climbers graze, only one level up. The climbers do not flee it because it is slower than they are. It does not need them to be slow; it needs there to be a great many of them, and there are. It eats along a wall from one end to the other at the pace of the hand of a clock, leaving a strip of bare film behind it, and by the time it has reached the end of the wall the strip has been re-colonised from above and it can turn around.

They have no eyes. They have, as far as your hands can tell, no ears. They know the walls the way you have learned to know them, by touch, and they know them better; they never miss a turning, never come to a cell with no door. And here is the thing that will stay with you. They avoid the scars. Where a wall has a fault in it — where someone once dammed a trickle with their hand and the pattern skipped — the gallery-walkers pass around it, whiskers lifted, without touching. They know the wall is wrong there. You do not know how.

---

**The one in the pools**

You will not see it. Nobody sees it. You will see the water in one of the deepest, hottest pools near the centre bulge upward and roll, and the seal-things at that end of the pool all lift at once and shift toward the other end, and the count go across the pool with a gap in it. You will see, once, a back: a long smooth darkness under the surface, longer than any seal-thing, moving without any motion you can name, and then not there. The mothers do not take their pups into that pool. The old animals lie in it anyway, because it is the warmest, and are sometimes taken, and the wave of groaning closes over the place where they were.

There is a way of knowing more about it than that, and it is unpleasant, and you will do it. The pools are connected. The water runs from one to the next down the tilt of the shelf through channels the film has walled, and in the channels, on the film, there are marks. Broad shallow grooves, in parallel, spaced a hand apart, as if something with a keel and a soft belly had dragged itself from pool to pool in the dark when there was no one to see. The grooves are fresh in some channels and grown over in others. It moves between the pools. It does the whole lower city on a circuit that takes longer than you will be here. It is, you decide, the only animal on the shelf that is not at saturation — the only one that is rare — because it eats the only thing that is not endless.

---

**The whistle**

Take this one last, because it is the one that will not resolve.

It is heard at the edges of the shelf and near the tops of the towers, and it is heard when the fog is thickest, and it descends — a long falling note, from somewhere above the register of the bats down to somewhere near the register of the seal-things' groan, and then silence, and then, sometimes, a small soft impact on the mineral nearby, and if you go to the sound and feel for it you will find a bat, warm, dead, with a cut along the body that is clean and straight and not like a bite.

For a while you will believe it is the towers. The wind moves through the fog, and the towers are hollow, and the highest cells are open to the air, and a hollow thing in a wind whistles. The bats in the highest cells are fewest and most nervous. The bats that fall have simply died, as bats do, of the cold or of age, and the cut is where they struck an edge on the way down. You will build this account and it will be a comfort and you will hold onto it for some nights.

And then the fog will lift, a little, as it does once or twice in a season, and the wind will drop to nothing, and in the stillness the whistle will come, and it will not be where the towers are. It will be over the young districts at the edge, over the ankle-high walls where nothing stands taller than your shin, and it will move — you will hear it move — against what little air there is. And you will remember that the description said *no one has ever seen* it, and you will understand that this was not a hedge. That is the whole of the knowledge. Something is up there, in the substance the air has become, and it is the only inhabitant of the shelf that lives in the fog rather than under it, and it eats the things that fly, and it is not counted in anyone's count.

---

**The scars**

There is one more class of inhabitant and it does not breathe.

You were told that when you dam a trickle with your hand the wall stops and stays stopped and there is a fault in the pattern that says *someone stood here*. Go and look at the old district with this in mind. Run your hands along the base of a fifteen-metre tower, where the mineral is a thousand years old. There is a fault at the height of your knee, a place where the ribbing skips and the surface goes lumpen for a hand's width before the pattern resumes above it. Somebody stood here nine hundred years ago and did something with their hands. There is another at shoulder height, later. There is one on the next tower at the height of your head, which means the tower was head-high when they came, which means eight hundred years. In the middle districts the faults are more frequent and closer to the ground. In the young districts at the edge there are faults you can see being made — a pooling where there should be a channel, a hesitation on a lip — and some of them are yours.

The city keeps everyone. It cannot help it. It builds over what it is given and does not distinguish between a bat's dung and the print of a hand, and so every warm upright body that has ever climbed through the cloud and stood on the shelf and reached out and touched the water where it was working has been recorded, and the record is in the walls, and it will be there when the towers are thirty metres and the seal-things are still groaning in pools of exactly this size. You are not the first. There have not been many, but there have been enough that the pattern of the old district, if you could see it whole from above in daylight, would be visibly wrong in a few hundred places, and every one of those places is a person, and every one of them stood, as you are standing, in the dark, and put out a hand, and was kept.

---

So, what lives here.

The film lives here, and it is the only thing that could be said to belong; the rest came. It eats the rock's leavings and builds the city with its own dead. The climbers live on the film and are its mouths and its sand. The seal-things live in the film's pools and are its only smooth surfaces and its only count. The bats live in the film's hollows and are its stripes. The gallery-walkers live on the climbers and are the only things on the shelf that know where the walls are wrong. The one in the pools lives on the old and the young. The whistle lives on the bats and in the fog and in no other place. And the visitors live in the walls, at the heights they stood, forever.

None of them made it. One of them is making it. All of them, without exception, are eating something that grows back exactly as fast as it is eaten, and the growing-back is the sound the shelf makes, and the sound is the loudest thing for a thousand kilometres, and it is louder than you, and after a while your heartbeat slows to meet it.
