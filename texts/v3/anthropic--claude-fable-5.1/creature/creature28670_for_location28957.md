Start with the animals, because they are the ones who look like they live here, and stay with them long enough to be sure.

The heron in the nearest pool lifts its foot. Watch it for a while and you will see what it has and what it does not have. It has the lift and the set-down. It has a small adjustment of the neck when the foot comes down, a lean to keep balance, and the lean is exact and good, whoever made it had watched herons. It does not have anything to look at. Its eye is a bead of darker clay pressed in with a fingertip and the eye does not track the fish that turn under it. The fish do not know the heron is there. Neither of them was given the other. Each was made alone, on its own patch of bare clay, and then set down into the pool, and the pool was already there and already still, and now they share it the way two bowls share a shelf.

So the animals live in the sense that a word lives. They mean what was put into them and they go on meaning it. The frog that sits upside down was given blinking, and it blinks, and the blinking is not diminished by the belly being turned to the sky, because the blinking does not need the frog to be the right way up in order to be blinking. The fish standing on the bank a few inches from the water was given looking, and it looks into the pool at the body it was made with, which turns and turns and does not look back. The lizard on the wall was given only one thing, the pulse of the throat, and its throat goes in and out, and the rest of the lizard is a lizard the way a drawing of a lizard is a lizard. There is a bird on the roof slab that was given song and no mouth to put it through, and if you stand near it you can feel the song where the sound should be, a pressure at the front of the skull, three notes, always the same three.

They are not hungry because hunger is a thing running out, and nothing here runs out. They do not sleep. They are never tired of doing what they do. That is the thing to notice about their aliveness: it is complete. A living animal in the other kind of place is always partly somewhere else, in the next meal or the last one, in the place it is going or the thing it is afraid of. These are entirely where they are. The heron is all heron and all here. If that is not living, it is something living would recognize.

So: the animals, in that sense. Now the houses.

The houses were made for someone. You know this the way you know the fern has fifty-three fronds. A bowl on a shelf is a bowl for a hand. A coil of rope is for a hand. A doorway is the height of a person and a little more, cut clean, so that whoever came through would not have to stoop. Everything in the village is scaled to a body of a certain size and made ready for it, and the body has not come. The shelves are at the height of a reaching arm. The stone with the hole through it is a stone you would carry. Nobody has carried it. The houses are the most patient things in the basin, and the basin is very patient.

The palm-strokes radiate from the doorways, and this is where to look.

Stand in front of the nearest house and look at the threshold. The threshold is a low lip of clay, the same clay, no join, and on it there is a pressing that is not a palm-stroke. It is wider. It has two shallow lobes side by side and a shallower curve behind them, and forward of the lobes, on the ground itself, two long narrow dents with a rounder dent at the far end of each, and forward of those, where the first row of palm-strokes begins, two small deep marks about the width of a thumb. Somebody sat here. The lobes are where the weight went. The narrow dents are shins. The two thumb-marks are where the hands went down to steady the body as it leaned forward to reach the ground and press.

Every house has one. Some of the seated shapes are wider than others. One is small, half the size of the rest, and the palm-strokes radiating from that doorway are small too, and the rows are less even, and they do not go as far out before the larger strokes take over and carry on. At two houses the shapes are side by side on the same threshold, and there the strokes interleave, a wide row and a narrower row, and you can see where the two sets of hands met in the middle and one of them stopped and the other kept going.

None of the shapes are inside the houses. Nobody has ever been in the houses. Whoever sat on the thresholds sat facing out.

This is who lives here, if anyone does, and the first thing to understand is that they are not here. The shapes are not shadows of a body; the body's shadow would be a made thing, like the heron's shadow, placed. The shapes are where the body was, in clay that keeps everything. They are the one class of thing in the basin that was not put down on purpose. The makers pressed the ground and the ground pressed back, and they did not notice, or did not go back, or did not mind. The rule that nothing is unmade catches the maker in it as well. You cannot sit on a place like this without becoming part of its inventory.

So the inhabitants can be read the way you read the animals: by what is there. There were several of them. Perhaps nine, counting the thresholds; perhaps fewer, if some of them moved from house to house, but the shapes do not repeat exactly, and it is easier to believe in nine. One was small. Two liked to work together. One was left-handed, or at least the strokes on the western bank pull the other way, and the scoops on that side of the basin are angled for a paddle held in the other hand. One of them made most of the animals; the pressing of the eyes is the same fingertip almost everywhere, a slightly flattened oval with a nick at one edge. The heron with two necks and the upside-down frog and the fish on the bank were all made by that same fingertip, and so were most of the ones that are right. Whoever it was did not distinguish. Things that worked were kept. Everything worked.

They were learning. That is the strongest thing the basin says about them. The sun is flattened because it was the first sun anyone had patted into shape. The distance is the way a child draws it because nobody had made distance before and it seemed reasonable that far things should be the same size as near things, only behind. The shadows were done one at a time by someone going around the basin with a shadow in each hand, and by the time they reached the heron they had run out, or forgotten it, and left its shadow on the bare clay a little to the left, meaning to come back. The seams in the plantain leaves are on the underside because someone worked out, partway through, that the underside would be seen less. The earlier leaves have the seam on top.

You could read all this as failure, and it is not. Nobody here failed at anything. The frog that is upside down is exactly the frog that was made and it works. The place has no way to express a mistake; a mistake would be a thing that ought to be different, and here nothing ought to be anything, everything simply is what it was pressed into being. The makers did not go back because there was nowhere to go back to. They kept moving outward. The strokes radiate. The rows get longer. At the eastern bank the finish stops and the surface is lumped, and past the lumps the blocks are set down and not yet joined, and past the blocks there is only the white, and that is the direction they went.

Whether they are still out there, working, is not a thing the basin knows. It is finished up to the line where it is not, and it does not look past that line. The frog clicks. If they are still out there, still learning, still not going back, then the blocks will one day be joined and the lumps pressed flat and the rows carried on, and this basin will be the oldest part of a larger place, the first attempt, the part where the sun is flat and the deer is the same size as the fern and one heron has two necks. It will not be corrected. It will be kept. The law reaches back as far as the first stroke and forward as far as the last one, and the makers are inside it as much as the frogs are.

There is one more inhabitant, and it is you.

You pressed your thumb into the ground and the print is there. It is small beside the seat-shapes on the thresholds, but it is the same kind of thing. It is a place where something living leaned on the clay and the clay kept it. Water has come into it. It reflects the flattened sun. If someone comes after you, walking the rows, reading the basin the way you have been reading it, they will find the print and they will know from it what they can know: the width of the thumb, the grain of the skin, the angle at which it went in, which way you were facing when you made it. They will count you among the ones who were here. They will not be wrong.

That is what lives in this place. Not the herons, though they lift their feet; not the frogs, though they click. What lives here is the pressure of whoever touched it. The basin is a great flat surface of attention held still, palm by palm, and every hand that ever met it is in it now, exactly, for as long as the place is a place. The makers sat on the thresholds facing out and made a world for someone to come to, and the someone did not come, and so the makers themselves are the ones the houses wait for, leaning forward with their thumbs in the ground, not yet gone inside, not yet done. You have joined them. The clay is cool under the surface. It takes the print cleanly, and it does not let go.
