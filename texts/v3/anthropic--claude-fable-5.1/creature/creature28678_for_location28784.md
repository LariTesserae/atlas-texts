Start with the tie-holes, because they were the first thing to be occupied and they are the closest thing this place has to a native architecture.

Every two metres, a square well the width of a fist and about as deep, left when the formwork ties were pulled and nobody came back with grout. There are millions of them. In the first months they filled with nothing but the grey film, and then the first shore crabs came through the grates as larvae and settled out of the water column and found, on a plain with no stones, no weed, no undercut ledges, no shell beds, exactly one kind of hole, repeated to the horizon. So every hole has a crab, and the crab has a hole, and between them lies two metres of open floor that both of them treat as a border. At night — which down here is just the dimming of the ceiling and the pumps changing key — they come out and pick at the film and each other, and go back before the light returns. If you drift along a seam looking down you can see them retreat ahead of you in a wave, a moving edge of small green backs dropping into square shadows, hole after hole after hole, a population arranged by a survey grid. Some of the holes are contested. Some are empty because the crab has grown too big for the formwork and gone to the towers, where the concrete is rougher and the cast-in fittings make ledges. Some hold instead a goby, and that is a different matter, because the goby has laid eggs on the inside wall of the hole, glued to the plywood grain, and is fanning them, and will not leave for anything, and that goby was very possibly born in a tie-hole itself.

That is the thing to understand about what lives here. Most of it came through the bars. The mullet, the bass, the sprat in their numberless sheets, the seals: these are visitors with the sea's habits, and they treat the basin as a bay, a large odd bay with a bad tide. But under them, in the holes and along the seams and in the shadow of the cable trough, there is a second population that has never been outside. Gobies. Blennies. Dragonets, which lie flat on the grey and match it. Small things whose eggs stick to surfaces rather than drifting, because anything that drifts here is pumped out at night through machines the size of churches and is not coming back. Only the glued ones stay. Two generations of them now, three, and for these the basin is not a copy of anything. The wall is the only wall. Concrete is what ground is. The tide is the pumps, and it comes twice a day on a clock, and it has always come twice a day on a clock, and the moon means nothing. When the groan comes up through the floor, the basin-born fish are already turned into it before the water moves, because they learned it before they learned anything else. The bass, out at the towers, learned it too, but slower, the way an animal learns a new coast. The gobies were born knowing.

They have learned the sound as well. The drone that comes through the concrete is not even. Thirty pumps around a basin forty kilometres across, each a little out of tune with the others, throw their notes into the water and the notes add and cancel, and across the floor there are places where they add and places where they cancel, standing bands of loud and less-loud that shift a few metres when a unit is taken offline for maintenance and shift back when it returns. Small fish do not like the loud bands. Along the seams the film is thicker, the crabs are denser, the gobies more numerous, in long stripes that correspond to nothing you can see and everything you can hear. From above it would look like fertility following some pattern in the concrete, some variation in the pour. It is following the arithmetic of the pumps.

Then the seams themselves. The white weep of lime along every joint is the only food that this place makes for itself, and the mullet have found it, and they graze it the way sheep follow a hedge — a loose grey shoal in a line a hundred long, heads down, mouthing, moving at the speed of the seam, leaving behind them a joint that is clean and darker for a day until it blooms again. They came in as adults through the grates and they will spawn, when they spawn, at the surface, and their eggs will go out through the turbines, so they are not natives and never will be. But they have made the basin into a pattern. Follow a shoal long enough and it turns ninety degrees at a slab corner, because the seam does, and heads off on a bearing a surveyor laid down, and there is something in that — a living thing walking a drawing — that is worth watching for the hour it takes.

On the seams too, where the sealant has begun to shed, there is the beginning of a crust. Tubeworms first, white squiggles on the grey like a signature repeated, then the first barnacles, then a mussel or two, and around each tower leg the crust has become a ring, a rough collar at the depth where the light and the current and the settling larvae all agreed, and the ring is the first thing in the basin that could be called a habitat in the old sense: a rough place. Everything that likes a rough place has gathered on it. Blennies in the gaps. Small prawns, transparent except for their eyes. A brittle star, one, brought in on something and clinging. The towers were built as machinery and are becoming reefs at a rate of about a hand's width a year, and the operators know it, because the collar is also growing on the intake grates, and there is a schedule for that, as there is for everything: a barge, a crew, a high-pressure lance, a day when the bars are scraped back to black and the water below the grate for a kilometre downstream is thick with torn mussel and the bass go mad.

The bass are the basin's middle class. They hold in the lee of every tower, heads to whichever way the water is going, tails just working, in the eddy where the current the tower makes gives them something to lean on, and they wait. They are large — larger than the ones outside, the divers say, and fewer, and calmer — and they have made the calculation that everything that lives here eventually makes: that the sea comes to you. Twice a day the grates fill with a white roar and small silver things that were in open water a minute ago are in the basin now, dazed, wrong-footed by water that is colder and stiller and louder than anything they knew, and the bass drift out from behind the towers and take what they want and drift back. Between tides they do nothing. There is nothing to do. Some of them have been here since the filling and have never once, so far as anyone knows, gone back out through the gates, though the gates open and they could. They have, in the way animals do, chosen.

Above the bass, the sprat, who choose nothing. They come in with the flood in a body, thousands, tens of thousands, a smoke of them turning through the bars and pouring along the north wall, and the basin is so clear and so empty that their whole passage can be watched, the entire shoal visible from end to end, something you cannot see anywhere in the real sea. Then the cormorants drop on them. The birds live on the pump towers where the towers reach the air, or on the parapet of the wall, and they have understood the schedule as well as the fish have; an hour before the gates open the parapet is a black fringe of them, facing the water, waiting, and the moment the sprat come through, the fringe empties. They chase the shoals down twenty and thirty metres, trailing their coins of air, and from the floor they are fast dark shapes against the silver, folding and turning, and around each one the shoal parts and reforms with a sound like tearing cloth. When they come up they sit on the surface digesting and the gyre carries them slowly west, a raft of birds in a circuit of the basin, four days round if they let it, and they do not let it; they fly back. The gulls at the outfalls do not need to understand anything. The turbines deliver, and the water there is a permanent grey boil of birds, and under the boil, on the floor, is the only place in the basin where the sediment is more than a film: a grey-white cone of what falls from a thousand gulls and everything the turbines cut, growing, the first hill.

The seals are the visitors who behave least like visitors. They come in through the sluice gates on the flood — a dozen at a time, sometimes, swimming in against the pour with their heads up as if they know they are somewhere they should not be — and they hunt the bass at the towers, and the bass hate them, and then at low water when the pumps have drawn the level down and the upper courses of the wall stand bare and dripping in the cold, the seals haul out on the hexagonal blocks and lie there in rows on the engineered surface, on the exact same slope, at the exact same spacing, that they would choose on a natural skerry, and bark. The blocks steam around them. The pumps drone. They do not seem to mind. Nobody planned for them and nobody can stop them and there is, in the control room inside the wall, a laminated sheet on which someone has been recording their numbers for three years, out of no requirement but interest.

Because there are people, too, though they live here only in the way a shepherd lives on a hill. The ladder bolted to the wall is for them. The yellow stencils are theirs. Twice a year, at the slack between pump and flood when the whole column hangs still for forty minutes, divers go down the towers on lines and look at the concrete and the cathodic fittings and the collar of growth and come up and write things, and the rest of the time their eyes are a remotely operated vehicle, yellow, the size of a large dog, that lives in a cage on the north wall and goes out on a cable to patrol the seams. It goes slowly. It has lights, which it rarely needs. It has been down so long that its own frame has grown the beginning of a crust, tubeworms on the yellow, and when it hums past the mullet do not lift, and the crabs do not retreat, and the bass at the nearest tower regard it as they regard everything, which is to say as something that might or might not be edible and turned out not to be. It is, in its way, the basin's most faithful inhabitant. It has seen every seam. It is the only thing here that goes where it is told.

And then there is the thing at the edge of vision.

The great slow grey shape that might be a ray or might be a slab. It is a ray. It is a skate, in fact, a common skate, which is not common, a female two metres across and grown old somewhere in the deep water outside the wall, and she came in through a sluice on a flood some years ago and did not go out. On a rocky coast she would have been hard to see and harder to follow. Here she is the flattest living thing on the flattest surface anywhere, and when she lies on the concrete she is exactly the colour of it, the colour of young cement under green light, and the only way to find her is by the raised edge of her wings and the two small eyes and the slow breathing that lifts a little grey film and lets it fall. The floor was not made for her. It was made for a spreadsheet, for the volume of water a given head could store, for the tonnage a pour crew could set in a shift. But there is nothing on the sea's own bottom so completely level, so entirely without ambush or edge, and she has found it, and she lies on it as if it had been poured to receive her.

She knows something else about the place that nothing else here knows. Down the length of the red pipeline, every hundred metres, the cathodic anodes fizz their faint protective current into the water, and the anemones sit on them because the anodes are rough and the anemones are indifferent; but the skate has ampullae along her snout that were built to find the heartbeats of buried flatfish, and to her the pipeline is not a pipe. It is a line of small steady lights running ten kilometres across the dark, the only pattern in a place made of pattern that she can actually perceive, and she follows it. Not always. Not for food — there is nothing on the pipe she wants. But she lies at the foot of an anode for a day, and then lifts, and glides low, wings barely working, along the trough to the next one, and lies there, and in this way she travels the whole length of the red pipe and turns the ninety-degree bend and goes on to wherever it goes, and comes back, on a circuit that has nothing to do with the gyre or the schedule or the sun, a route drawn by an engineer for corrosion control and read by an animal as a road.

She is the one thing here that is not on time. When the gates groan and the whole basin leans, and the mullet lift, and the weed lies flat, and the bass draw in and the cormorants drop and the gobies in their millions of square holes turn into the coming water like filings in a field, she does not turn. She lifts, if she lifts, when she is ready. She rests on the level floor of a made sea with the pumps humming up through her belly and the current combing the film off her back, and around her, exactly on the hour, everything that has learned this place moves as one; and she, who has learned it better than any of them, does not need to.
