The largest thing that lives here is the hardest to see, because there is nothing to see it against.

The moss is one organism. That has been said, but it takes some time to understand what it means when it is true of a thing the size of a continent. It has no edge except the edge of the world. It has no near side and no far side. It has no age, because it grows at the base and dies at the base at the same rate in the same place and so has always been exactly as old as it is. It cannot be counted and it cannot count; there is no place inside it from which anything looks like a second thing. The people say it has never been plural, but that is not quite right. It was plural once, so long ago the willows were seeds, and it stopped. It stopped resisting. It is what the rule looks like when the rule wins completely and the loser is not destroyed but filled.

It is not warm. But in the deep hollows where it lies to the depth of a standing man, the lower layers are rotting very slowly into themselves, and that rot gives off a degree or two, and on the worst mornings you can push a bare hand down to the wrist and feel it. Every cistern on the plateau holds less water than the moss holds within a day's walk of it. The channels the people cut are only ways of asking the moss to let go of a thread.

It can be cut. It is cut constantly; the roofs are made of it. The cut does not heal in any ordinary sense. The two edges are alike, and they close. A turf lifted and laid on a slab roof is not a piece of moss; it is the moss, occupying a roof. The people live inside it the way a fish lives inside the sea, which is a comparison they cannot make, having no fish and no sea. Their whole city is a pattern of holes in one living thing, and the living thing does not mind, because minding requires a self to be intruded upon, and the moss gave that up along with everything else.

Does it think? The people do not believe so, and they have watched it longer than anyone. But it changes with the fog. It darkens when the cloud settles and pales when the cloud lifts, and the change moves across the plateau faster than the cloud does, so that a watcher on a shoulder will see the sward go dark a mile off, then half a mile, and know to bring the cord in and cover the hearth. It is not thought. It is the plateau having one skin and the skin having one temperature. The people say: the moss is the day. They do not mean anything mystical by it. They mean that if you want to know what the day is doing, you look at the moss.

The willows are the second oldest thing here and the first that can be counted.

Each one is an individual and each one knows it, in whatever way a plant knows anything: by shape. They grow a fingernail's width in a century and they grow crooked, because crooked is safe. A straight stem is a shape a thousand stems can share. So the willows have spent four thousand years each finding a private way to be wrong — a stem that doubles back on itself, a stem that goes flat along the rock for an arm's length before it thinks better of it, a stem that has split and rejoined and split again so that the silver bark encloses a hole you could put a fist through. The people map these. They count the rings as a devotion, but they draw the twists as a duty, and the drawings are kept far apart from one another because a collection of drawings is a collection of like things.

At the foot of every willow of any size there is a name-stone with a mark cut in it. When the wind lays two neighbors toward each other over a season, the people prune, or they dig one up whole, roots and moss and all, and carry it on a bark sledge a day's walk to a hollow chosen for it, and pack it in. These transplantings are the plateau's great public works. Half a dozen people, more than are ever otherwise seen together, each walking at their own pace and their own distance from the next, taking a tree to where it can go on being itself. A willow moved is a willow saved. A willow lost is written down, and the mark on its stone is struck through, and the stone is left.
