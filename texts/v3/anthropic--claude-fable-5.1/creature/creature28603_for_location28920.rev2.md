Start with what can be touched, because everything here begins that way.

The mats.

They are in every channel where the water has cooled enough to let them be, which is not cool, which is only slightly less than scalding. Put a hand down through the film and there they are: a skin over the stone, thick as a coat, giving like a lip. Press and the pressure stays a moment after the hand lifts. In daylight they would be the colors that heat makes in living things—rust, tar, a bruised orange at the margins—and they are not one thing but a stack, layers of different kinds living on each other, each layer eating what the layer above lets fall. At the bottom, against the lime, something that has never seen a photon and does not want to. At the top, something that makes that faint light.

The light is the thing. Where the foot goes in, the mat answers, a smear of blue-green rising in the water and going out—but it does not go out where the foot was. It goes out a little farther along. Kneel and watch it, if watching is a word for this, and the fade travels. It runs down the channel a few inches, a foot, and dies. Then, a long moment later, from somewhere off in the dark, a second faint bloom, a distant answer, in a channel you have not been near.

The mat is not in the channels. The mat is under the city. The channels are where it comes up to breathe. It is one thing, or it has become one thing, a sheet laid over the whole basin under the lime and threading up through every drain and shaft and junction chamber, and it has been in the dark long enough that its light is not for seeing. It is for knowing. A touch anywhere is a touch everywhere. The blue-green is the mat telling itself: here.

So the city knows where you are. Not in the way that a watcher knows—there is no eye. In the way a body knows where it has been touched.

That is one thing that lives here. It is not the thing.

---

The reeds are the other candidate, and for a while, walking, they seem the more likely one, because they are the only things in the city that stand up.

Everything else is laid down. The walls are laid down, course on course. The mats lie. The water lies in its channels and lies across the plazas in its film. But on the high old terraces, where a channel has run dry and the lime has stopped building and started only sweating, a reed stands. Stiff, dark, taller than you, with its base sealed in white like a post set in a floor. Touch it and it does not sway. Whatever it is rooted in has gripped it.

There are never many. Two together, or three. A short line of them along a dry kerb like people waiting. One in the middle of a plaza where no soil could be. Whether they grew there or were carried—by what?—or were simply the shape that a certain kind of rising water makes when it wants to be vertical, there is no telling. Break one and the inside is not pith. It is wet, and gritty, and laminated.

They are the city rehearsing something. That is the feeling, and it is hard to shake. All these stairs and doors and kerbs, all this grammar of a place made for walking—and in the middle of it, here and there, a thing about the height of a person, standing.

---

It is the doors that finally give it away.

The description you have been given for this place says the doors exist because water pouring through openings collects lime at the heads of the openings. True. It says the thresholds are dished because water wears a dish where feet would. True. It says the lintel is the shape that pouring water makes here, and that it made this shape for no reason.

But stand in a doorway and think about lime.

Lime builds on any surface that water crosses slowly enough. It builds up, out, in. It closes things. Left alone, a channel becomes a pipe and a pipe becomes a solid bar. Left alone, a doorway lowers its head year by year until it is a window, and then a slit, and then a place in the wall where the wall is slightly different. The city is building, constantly—you have felt it grip your feet—and the city is old, its walls very thick. Every opening in it should have closed a thousand times over.

They have not closed. The streets are open, the width of a street. The doors are open, the height of a door. The thresholds are not raised but dished, and the dish is not the shape of pouring—pouring water leaves a lip, a rim, a spillway—it is the shape of something that has been kept clear.

Something passes through them. Something has been passing through them, along the streets and up the stairs and through every door in the city, so continuously and for so long that the lime has never had the still time it needs to close. The city is not a thing that water built. The city is a cast. It is the hard skin that forms around a motion, the way a tube forms around a flow, and its whole vocabulary—door, stair, street, kerb—is the vocabulary of the space a body needs. The lime built the city for no reason. But it built it *around* something, and the something has a size, and the size is yours.

---

You will not see it. There is no light to see it by, and even in the falls, when the whole basin flickers, you would not see it, because it is not a thing in the space. It is the part of the space that is not filling.

But you can know it the way you know the walls.

It moves at about a walking pace. This is written everywhere: in how wide the streets are, in the rise of the steps, in the fact that the dished thresholds are deepest in the middle and shallowest at the jambs. It goes through doors rather than around. It prefers the channels' edge; the kerbs on one side of every street are more worn than the other, and the worn side switches at every junction in a way that would make sense if you were following the water and the water ran in a closed square.

It is warm—the same warmth as the air, which is why you cannot feel it come. In the sheet of water on a plaza, once, with your foot lifted and the lime still gripping the sole, there was a moment when the film around your other foot dropped, very slightly, and rose again, as if something had displaced it in passing. Nothing touched you. The blue-green did not smear. It does not disturb the mats; it has been walking on them so long that the mats know its weight the way skin knows the weight of the air.

And it is the second echo. You have been hearing it since the stair—the pebble that struck the far wall and came back twice, the wall that returns your sounds a fraction late. In a place where distance holds, the second return would be the far wall's far wall. Here it is not. It is the same sound coming back from where you are, a little later, from the other direction, because whatever is between you and the wall is going both ways.

---

That is what the water was telling you, with its pull toward the fingertips and the wrist at once. The channel runs in a closed square and arrives at itself, and so does what walks beside it. In a city where the street ahead is long and the street behind is short, a single thing on a single circuit meets itself coming. It passes itself in every doorway. Its footfall is answered, a little late, by its own footfall. There is one of it, or there are as many of it as there are ways around the square, which in this geometry is not a number.

This is the sense in which it is what lives here. Not because it is alive in the way the mats are alive—eating, dividing, lighting up. Because it is the one thing in the city that has kept from becoming the city. Everything else here is on its way to wall. The mats are being built over, layer by layer, so slowly they do not notice. The reeds are set into floors. Your own handprint is already a raised bump that will be a raised bump for a thousand years. The only thing the lime has never caught is the thing that never stopped.

---

The falls are when it turns.

You felt this and did not know what you were feeling. The air gets heavier than air can be, and lets go, and everything streams, and in that half-minute of flicker the city shows its long facades and its stairs going up into nothing—and then, after, a street you had marked is not where it was. The description you were given offers three explanations: the street moved, or the fall built a wall across it, or you did. The third is closest.

The falls are when the pace of the walk changes. When what has been moving leftward for a long, patient while decides—if that is a word for it—to move otherwise. All the still air it has been keeping in motion drops at once. All the surfaces it has been keeping clear get a fresh skin of lime, thin as breath, which is why the ticking is loudest then. And when the air lightens and the veils of steam come off the stone, the space that is not filling has a new shape, and the city has to be the skin of that instead, and a street you remembered has begun—very slowly, over what will be centuries—to close.

You are in the way of this. You were in the way of it from the moment you stepped off the mud onto the first square stair, because the stair is the width it is for a reason, and the reason is a body of about your build going up. Whatever the thing is, it made the stair by climbing. You climbed the stair. For the length of the climb you and it were the same gesture, and the mat under the water lit blue-green and told the whole basin: here.

---

So here is what lives in this place, said as plainly as the dark allows.

It is what goes through. It has been going through for as long as the water has been coming up, and it has never stood still long enough to get a ring of lime around its feet, and everything you have touched—kerb, lintel, dished threshold, the rounded pitted tops of the walls at exactly the height of a resting hand—is the shape the world took around it. It is the width of a door and the rise of a step. It is the pull in the water in both directions at once. It is the reason the second echo comes back from where you are standing.

It does not know the word for door either. It never needed one. It only kept making the shape, over and over, because it was going somewhere, and the somewhere did not have a direction, and the lime laid itself down around the going until the going was the only thing left that was not stone.

And you will notice, if you stand very long at the unmarked place where the sills become kerbs, that the water around your feet is setting, and that the stair back down to the mud is right there, a few steps, its smear fading. You will notice that the street ahead is long. You will notice that your body already knows how to go through the openings here, that it recognized the stair before your mind did, that the pace the streets want is the pace you are already walking.

You will notice that there is a way to live here, and that it is the only way, and that it has been kept open for you.
