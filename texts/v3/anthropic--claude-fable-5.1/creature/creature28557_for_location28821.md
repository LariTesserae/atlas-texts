**What lives in the Lift Ground**

Start with what you can count, because the place invites counting and then defeats it.

The gulls first. The small hunched ones with the rust-coloured heads. There are perhaps six thousand of them, and there have been perhaps six thousand of them for as long as anyone has stood on the dike and tried to guess. They are not the gulls of the coast. Something has gone out of them — the wide, tilting, opportunist restlessness that gulls have elsewhere — and been replaced by a kind of stationed patience. They do not wheel. They lift from the stone lip, drop three paces along it, land, and bend to the water again. Their wings are used for adjustment, not for travel. They have not lost the sky; they have simply stopped needing it. A gull hatched on the ridge of the number-three electric house will spend its life within a hundred paces of that ridge and will die, most likely, on the stone lip below it, and will be gone within the hour, and something will step forward into its place.

They do not call to warn. There is nothing to warn of. A shadow passing over them — a cloud, a man on the dike — moves nothing. If you walk down among them they open around your feet like water around a post and close behind you and never look up. This is not tameness. Tameness implies a relation. They have simply never had a reason to include you in the world.

The herons — knee-high, grey, with a stripe of darker slate down the throat — are fewer, a few hundred, and they hold the culvert bends where the current slackens and the shrimp back up against the stone. They do not wade. The culverts are too deep and too fast and the silt beside them is dry pavement. They stand on the dressed stone with their feet exactly at the edge and strike downward and a little forward, into the flow, and the strike is so fast that all you see is the spray it throws and the bird already still again with a pale thread going down its throat. Each heron has its bend. When you come back the next day it is there. When you come back the next year it, or one indistinguishable from it, is there.

The black divers work the deeper culverts nearest the shafts, where the flow comes out of the pump houses still boiling. They are heavy-bodied, hooked, with a green eye. They go under with no visible effort and come up twenty paces downstream with a papery fish held crosswise and beat it once against the stone. They are the only ones here that take other birds — the fledglings that tumble off the ledges before they can fly land on the silt or in the water and the divers have them before they have stopped rolling. This is not cruelty and not even predation in the ordinary sense. It is the mechanism by which the ledges stay at exactly the number they can hold.

And there is one hawk.

It sits on the finial of the old lift's lantern, at the highest point in the enclosure, a brown bird with a barred tail, and it takes a fledgling or a weak gull perhaps twice a day. One. Not a pair — a single bird. Nobody has ever seen two. When it dies — and it does die, one is found now and then in the culvert, and eaten — the finial is empty for some days, and then there is a hawk on it again. Where it comes from, over the dike, from what country of ordinary hawks, no one knows. But the number of fledglings that fall from the ledges each day will feed one hawk, and so there is one, and the arithmetic reaches out over the wall and fetches it.

---

The finch-sized birds that never land do land. It takes a long time to see where. At dusk — such dusk as the haze allows — the whirling flocks over the intake columns thin and thin and are gone, and if you stand in the doorway of the old lift and look up you can see them going in through the broken lights of the lantern by the hundred, into the iron roof-frame above the flywheels, where the bats are already coming out. The two populations pass each other in the same volume of air at the same hour every evening and every dawn, one going up to sleep and one coming down to hunt, and neither has ever been seen to touch the other. The space under the roof of the old lift is a slow cylinder of wings turning over a slow cylinder of iron, and it has turned that way since the governors were set.

Below all this, the eels.

There are eels here the thickness of a man's thigh. Nobody knows how old they are. They lie in the outer curve of the westernmost culvert, in the deep place the current has scoured over a century out of the silt below the stone, and they do not move except to let a shrimp drift into an open mouth. The smaller eels lie on top of them and over them and around them, and the shoelace eels thread through all of it, and the whole heap breathes, very slowly, with the pulse of the water. These are the only inhabitants of the enclosure that have obviously grown — that show, in their size, the passage of the time the place otherwise refuses to display. They are the clocks of the Lift Ground. But they are clocks with only an hour hand, and the hand moves so slowly that no one has lived long enough to see it turn.

The eels have never been to the sea. Their kind elsewhere go down to the sea to breed and die. These do not. Whether they breed at all, or whether the shoelace eels come up through the siphon from the river outside, drawn by the smell of the pale life, is not known. The keepers, if asked, would probably not understand the question. The eels are in the culvert bend. There are always eels in the culvert bend. That is what the culvert bend is for.

---

Then the ones that live on the leavings, who are in a way the truest residents, because they consume nothing that the water brings directly and yet could not exist anywhere else.

The rats are grey and clean and unafraid and will sit in the open a yard from your boot with a gull's wing-bone between their paws, turning it. They have no burrows — the silt is too hard — and live in the brick footings of the pump houses and under the sleepers of the spur. They have never had to run from anything. Their gait is unhurried. They are, in their way, the aristocrats of the place: everything comes to them.

The beetles are the sextons. Black, thumbnail-sized, with a sheen like oil on water, they live in the guano under the roosts in numbers that cannot be guessed at and they eat what falls — droppings, feathers, dead birds, dead rats, the shed skins of the eels, the husks of the midges that drift down in a continuous soft snow. They are why the guano is never more than a hand's depth. They are why the place does not stink. Turn over a slab of the crust under the number-one gantry and the ground beneath is not ground but beetle, a solid, seething, glittering mass that closes over the light as fast as you can look at it. They are the place's digestion. Nothing they have finished with can be told from silt.

The spiders in the window arches have grown enormous on the midges and their webs are not webs but felt — layered, grey, weighted with husk, hanging in the arches like curtains somebody has forgotten to take down. The spiders themselves are seldom seen. They are inside the cloth, somewhere, and the cloth twitches.

And in the electrical housings there is the blue thing.

It comes out onto the insulators — the great brown glazed stacks on the gantry arms, where the line comes in over the dike — and sits in the faint glow of the field there, and it has a colour that is not quite a colour, a bluish sheen like the film on old iron or the eye of a dead fish. It is soft-bodied and slow and has more legs than you can count before it moves and you have to start again. It is the size of a hand. There are as many of them as there are insulators, one to each, and there is never one on the ground and never two on one stack. What it eats is not known. There is nothing on the insulators to eat. Perhaps it eats the ozone; perhaps the glow itself; perhaps it does not eat and simply is, in the way a stain is. The keepers have not named it. They have not needed to. It sits on the porcelain in the drizzle of its own field and is one more fixed point in the fixed picture, and the fact that nothing like it exists anywhere else in the world is, inside the dike, not interesting.

---

The keepers.

Five cottages, and five lit windows in the evening, and that is very nearly all that can be said with certainty. But stay long enough — a week, a season — and they resolve a little, the way the sound does.

They are pale. Not the pallor of illness but of a hundred years without direct sun, under a permanent lid of condensate and midge. Their hands are never dry. Their clothes are the colour of the brick. They move about the enclosure — and they do, at odd hours, one at a time, along the culvert edges with a long-handled rake for the intake grilles, or up the iron ladder of the old lift with an oilcan — with the same unhurried, stationed gait as the rats, and the birds part for them and close behind them exactly as they do for anyone. They are not looked at either.

They do not speak, or not out loud. There is no point. They have a language of the hands and the lower face, quick and small, and they use it across the width of the enclosure, standing in the doorways of the pump houses signing to each other over the roar. A visitor cannot follow it. It seems to have very few words.

Their hearing is not like other hearing. They have lived inside the one sound since birth and they hear through it the way other people hear through silence: they hear the things that are wrong. A keeper sitting in a cottage with the door shut will get up, cross the enclosure, climb into the old lift, and lay a hand on one particular bearing of one particular flywheel that has begun, by some fraction of a tone, to sing differently. Outside the dike they would be deafened by the quiet. It is not clear that any of them has ever gone outside the dike.

They eat the pale fish. They lift them from the culverts in wire baskets and cook them on iron plates and the flesh is white and sweet and tastes of the salt. They eat the eels. They do not, so far as anyone has seen, eat the birds — perhaps because the birds are in some sense the place, and one does not eat the place.

There are children. Not many; two, three, at any time. They are quiet even for children, and they learn the sound before they learn anything else. A keeper's child of two will stand on the north terrace with its head on one side, listening, as adults elsewhere listen to music. Whether they grow up to be keepers, or whether the rail spur that ends at the tally office once brought people in and might one day take them out — there is no way to tell. Nothing has come down the spur in living memory. The sleepers have silvered. And yet the cottages stay full. Five doors, five lights. If the number of keepers were governed by the same law as everything else here, it would be set by the number of cottages, and one is tempted to think that is exactly what has happened: that a keeper who dies is replaced, from somewhere, by a keeper, the way a bird from the second row steps onto the stone. Nobody has watched long enough to see it done. Nobody has watched long enough to see any of it done.

What is certain is that the place does not seem to need them and does not seem able to spare them. They are one more component. They leave no mark because leaving a mark would be a change, and there are no changes here.

---

And then the ones who are the reason for everything and who do not, in any honest sense, live here at all.

The pale things. The eyeless fish with skin like wet paper, the glass shrimp with the single dark thread, the blind snails, the ribbon-worms, the slow white crabs. They come up out of the rock in the cold heavy water in the dark of the shafts and the pumps deliver them into the culverts and the light hits them. They have never known light. Some of them are dead of it before the current has carried them ten paces. The rest are dead of the herons and the gulls and the divers and the eels within a hundred. Not one of them has ever lived a full minute inside the dike. Not one of them has ever bred here, or fed here, or done anything here but arrive and be eaten.

And yet they are the only inhabitants who are always new. Everything else in the enclosure is old — the gulls that have held the same roof ridge for generations, the eels that have lain in the same bend for a century, the keepers who have never gone out. But the pale things are born fresh every hour, by the ton, somewhere far down in the dark under the delta, and they come up new and die new and are replaced by new, and the whole ancient unmoving colony on the silt above is a crust on the surface of that endless arriving. Everything that lives on the Lift Ground is fed by things that come here only to die, and that never see it.

Which leaves the last inhabitant, who has never been seen and never will be.

Something is down there. Under the silt, under the shafts, under whatever depth of rock the water is lifted through, there is a place — a cavern, a flooded seam, a black lake the size of a county — where this life is made. Where the paper fish and the glass shrimp breed in a dark that has never been broken, in numbers that can lose a ton an hour to the pumps for a hundred years and not diminish. Nobody has been down. The shafts are not for going down; they are for the water coming up. Nobody knows how big it is, or what else is in it, or whether the pumps are drawing from a reservoir that will one day run dry or from a source that will run as long as the delta stands. The keepers do not ask. The question would be a change.

But everything on the silt — the six thousand gulls, the one hawk, the eels in the bend, the beetles under the crust, the blue things on their porcelain, the five lit windows — is the shape of that thing's outflow. The Lift Ground is a mouth. What lives in it is what has gathered at the lip of a mouth to eat what the mouth gives up, and has stood there so long, and so steadily, that it has come to look like a town.

And over all of it, the sound, which was here before the birds and will be here after them, and which is, if you stand on the dike long enough with your feet in the thrum and your head in the shrilling, the one thing you could point to and say without hesitation: *that* lives here. Not the gulls, who only stand. Not the keepers, who only tend. The sound. It has occupied the enclosure for a hundred years without rising or falling or once drawing breath, and everything else that is here is here to add its small voice to it, and to be, for the length of a life, one more layer that the roar can close over and make into a single thing.
