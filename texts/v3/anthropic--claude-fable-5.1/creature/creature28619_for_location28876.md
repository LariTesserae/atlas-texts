Say the swifts first, because you would, because they are what you see. But say them properly, because the smear above the edge is not the whole of what they are.

A swift here is born in a crack on the eastern face, in a cup of hardened spit and insect husk wedged into a fault where the rock leans out over three thousand meters of falling air. The crack is quieter than the plateau — not quiet, but the roar is a wall outside rather than a medium inside — and it is the only enclosed place the bird will ever know. It grows there for six weeks, fed by parents who arrive from below without landing, who hang for a second in the doorway with wings still working and pass a pellet of moths into it and are gone. Then one morning it is heavy enough and its wing is long enough and it falls out of the crack, and the standing band of air catches it before it has fallen ten meters, and it opens its wings and stops.

It does not land again for years. Perhaps not until it breeds. Perhaps not ever. It eats in the band, drinks the fog that streams off the spine of rock, sleeps in the band — drifting up at dusk into the highest part of the layer, where the air is thinnest and the wind steadiest, and shutting one half of its brain and then the other while its body goes on making the small corrections that keep it fixed above the edge. It mates in the air. Its feet are almost nothing, four hooked pins good only for clinging to rock. Its whole body is a decision about the wind, made a very long time ago and refined since.

And it is deaf — not in the ear but in the life. It has never once in its existence heard another swift, or anything else, over the wind. So it has become a creature of the eye. The white ring around the eye is the signal: it makes the eye visible at forty meters, so that each swift can see where every other one is looking. A swift that sees prey looks at it, hard, and a dozen others read the look and tilt. A swift that sees the falcon looks at it, and the look spreads, and the flank of the flock rolls away without a sound. What you take for a wave through grass is a sentence. They are talking all day, in a language made entirely of attention, and the only thing they cannot say is anything about the past — because there is nothing to point at.

Sometimes one comes down. It miscalculates, or is struck, or is simply old, and it drops out of the band onto the plateau floor, and there the wind takes it. A swift on the ground here cannot get up. The air over the rock is too fast and too flat; it pins the wings open and drives the bird eastward along a trough like a leaf until it fetches up against a standing stone, in the wet lee, and lies there. For the swifts the whole forty kilometers of plateau is a floor they must never touch. The edge is where they live. The plateau is where they die. They have never seen it as anything else.

---

The gull sees the plateau differently. It walks.

Watch it and you understand it is not a gull at all, though it has a gull's shape — the black cap, the white body, the long grey wing — because no gull would put up with this. It flies in short hops, hugging the ground, dropping into the lee of each ridge and walking the wet rock in the shelter. It is a scavenger of lees. It knows every one of the black basins, and it makes a circuit of them, and what it finds is what the wind drops: moths that were too wet to stay aloft, the swift against the stone, and occasionally, in a pool, something small and red moving.

There are things in the basins. You would not expect it; the water is cold, and lit from every side, and stirred flat by the wind, and it has no source but the rock and no outlet but the air. But it has never once frozen and never once dried and never once changed temperature by more than a degree, and there is nothing else on the planet of which that is true, so something has moved in. A red mite that swims. A fine dark mat on the bottom that is not stone. A crustacean smaller than a rice grain, pale, blind, that eats the mat and the drowned moths and has no need of eyes because the water is never anything but bright. These are the true residents in one sense — they have never been out of the water and never will be, and the water has never been out of the wind. The gull eats them by the hundred and does not know what they are.

---

The marmots — call them that — live in the sand.

The troughs between the ridges are full of it, the fine pale grit that used to be plateau, moving eastward without ever stopping, and the burrows go down into it a meter or more and then sideways under the lip of the nearest ridge, into the packed layer that does not move. They are deep and they are the quietest places in the world. A marmot in its burrow can hear its own heart. It can hear the roar overhead as something far away, like the sea from a house. It spends most of its life there, in the dark and the near-silence, with its family, and it comes up perhaps two hours in the day.

When it comes up it comes up into the wet lee of a rock and sits with its back to the wind, and eats the cushion plant — a mouthful of something like hard grey bread — and then it sits up and looks. It looks for a long time. The eyes are enormous, wet, dark, and they do not stop moving. It is looking for the falcon and the gull and for the other marmots on the other rocks, because they cannot call to one another, so they watch. An alarm here is a posture: the belly turned to show its pale side, a flat drop of the body. It passes from rock to rock across a kilometer in a few seconds, faster than sound would, if there were room for sound.

Underground they speak by drumming. Foot against packed sand, a rhythm that runs through the ground faster than anything through air. The whole trough is wired that way, burrow to burrow, and a marmot at one end can feel a marmot at the other end arrive home. They have never used their ears in the open, so the ears are small and folded and there is nothing to lose to the cold. Everything they know about the world above they know through the eye, and everything they know about each other they know through their feet.

Their burrows silt in. The sand keeps coming and the wind keeps bringing it and the mouth fills, and they dig it out, and it fills. Generations have dug the same hole. It is the only labor on the plateau — the one thing here that is being unmade and remade rather than finished — and it is done by animals who never see more than a few dozen meters of the place they live in and never hear any of it.

---

The falcon does not live here. That has to be said. It comes up from below, from cliffs on the eastern face a thousand meters down where the air is thicker and slower, and it rides the updraft to the plateau's edge in the morning and hangs in the same band as the swifts, a little above them, and it takes one when it wants one. Then it goes down again. It is small and brown and it cannot hear anything either, but it does not need to; it was built for the eye anyway. The swifts ignore it. Ten thousand of them and one dies, and the flock has no way of holding the fact. There is a ripple, and the ripple closes, and the swift that died is not remembered because there is no organ here for remembering.

The person did not live here either. Somebody came up out of the cloud with a bag of chert and climbed the whole plateau against the wind, which would have taken most of a day, and found the fold in the spine of rock, and went in, and sat down. They made three points. One broke. They may have said something aloud to themselves for the pleasure of hearing it. Then they went back out into the roar and down. Perhaps the plateau meant something to them; perhaps it was in the way; perhaps they were the only one who ever came, or they were one of a long line and the wind has scoured away every trace of the others. The fold is the one room on the plateau that could hold a human being, and it is not a room anyone could stay in. There is nothing to eat in it. Nobody who needs to hear can live here, and that is the rule that has sorted everything else.

---

So you go back to the edge, at the end, and stand where the ground drops off into the down-pull, and look up into the layer.

The swifts hang there in their thousands, adjusting, not moving. You have been watching them all day and they have not gone anywhere; the flock at dusk is in the same place, the same shape, as the flock at dawn. Individual birds have come and gone — to the cracks and back, up to sleep, down to feed — but the shape has not. It has hung above this edge for as long as the fog has streamed off the spine, tearing off at the same point. Birds enter it from the cracks and leave it dead, and it is never smaller and never larger and never anywhere else. It is a standing form, like the leaning rocks, like the veil of fog, and it is the only standing form on the plateau made of things that are alive.

That is what lives here, if anything does. Not a swift; a swift is here a few years and is gone. The flock. A single body the size of a weather system, held open against the edge of the world, that has never once folded its wings. It has no memory and no sound and no way to know that it is the same today as it was ten thousand years ago. It doesn't need to know. The wind holds it there. The wind has never stopped.
