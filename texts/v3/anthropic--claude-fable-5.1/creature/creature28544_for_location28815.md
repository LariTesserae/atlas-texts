Ask what lives here and the first honest answer is: everything you were shown. The snails on their inherited road. The millipedes going dark red over the moss. Frogs by the thousand, each one a leaf-colored silence. The bats in their felt. The salamanders that come up out of the flooded west and go back down. The fungus that drinks the fog. The fig that has spent two centuries becoming a colonnade because it could not become a tree.

But sit with that answer a while — sit, as it happens, on the warm rim of the basin — and it starts to feel like the wrong word. The snails do not *live* here in the sense the question means. They have nowhere else. A snail hatched on a begonia leaf under a limestone dome does not know there is a plain of laterite above it ringing like a bell in the sun; it does not know there is an above. It is not in the room. It is the room, the way a drop on a fern is not a visitor to the fern. The same with the frogs, the millipedes, the white mat glowing on the far wall. They are what the chamber is made of. You could no more say they live here than you could say a heartbeat lives in a chest.

To live somewhere you have to be the kind of thing that could be somewhere else.

By that measure the room has fewer inhabitants than it seems to. Fewer, and stranger.

---

The bats are nearest to residents. They leave. They spiral up the shaft every dusk and come back before dawn with the night on their fur, and they are the only things in this chamber that have seen the plain above, the thorn scrub, the stars from the outside. They are commuters. And yet they know nothing of the room. They know the ceiling — its folds, its pockets, the exact warmth of the stone against a neighbor's back. The floor, to a bat, is a place where things fall. The fig is an obstacle. The fog is weather. They have lived over this room for longer than the stair has existed and they have never once been *in* it, in the way you are in it now, breathing its breath, counting its snails.

The salamanders come from below, and go back below, and belong to the black water of the passages. The room to them is a ceiling too — a place of alarming brightness where the food is, entered and left as quickly as hunger allows.

And the fig. If anything has claimed the room it is the fig, which arrived as a seed in a bird's gut or a bat's, fell through a coin of light, took root in the one square yard of floor where sun actually lands, and then spent its whole life discovering the size of its cage. It found the ceiling. It found the walls. It found the stair and ate it. It is the most rooted thing here, the most present, the most entangled. But a fig is not a resident. A fig is a place. Things live *in* a fig. You have been standing in its rooms all day.

---

Then there is the body under the floor.

You have been calling it that because the description keeps giving you no other word. It has a warmth, and the warmth is the warmth of flesh. It has a rhythm, twice in an hour, more or less, never quite regular, the way a sleeper's breathing is never quite regular. When it exhales, the room fogs; when it stops, the room rains. Everything alive in the chamber is arranged along the gradient of its heat like fur along a flank.

Is that what lives here? It is tempting to say so. It would make the room a kind of lung, and the fig its rib, and the snails and frogs a skin-fauna, and you a mite that has wandered in through a pore.

But sit longer and you find you do not believe it. Whatever is down there is not aware of the room. The room is a thing its breath does on the way out — a frost on a window, a shape in a sleeper's exhalation on a cold morning. The sleeper does not know about the frost. There may be a fissure a mile deep that has been letting go of warm air since before there was a limestone bed here to dissolve, and it does not live in this chamber any more than a river lives in the reed-bed at one of its bends.

The body is real. It is not the resident. It is the reason the resident came.

---

Because someone did come. That is the thing the room will not stop telling you.

Someone cut a stair in the eastern wall — the *dry* wall, the wall that would still be dry when the fog rose — so that a person could arrive here on their feet and not on their back. Someone fitted three courses of stone across the floor without mortar, exactly where the fog begins, so that the next person would know where the room's breath reached and where it did not. Someone carved a trough into the warmest rock and let it fill from a seep, and the trough is too short to lie in and too shallow to wash in, and the rim is worn round on one side only, at the height and width of a sitting body, and if you sit there — as you are sitting — your heels find the bottom of the trough and the warm water comes up over your ankles, and you understand that it was never a basin at all. It was a seat with a footbath. It was a place to sit with your feet in the warmth and wait for the room to breathe on you.

You cannot know who. The stair says a long time ago. The polish on the rim says something else, something you set aside for now.

What you can say is what they did, because the room is a record of it, and what they did was almost nothing. They came down the stair. They walked the length of the fig's colonnade. They stepped over the low wall into the half of the room that fogs. They sat. They put their feet in the water. They waited.

They counted, perhaps. Someone in this place has to. Predation here is subtraction, and subtraction is invisible unless something is keeping the tally — unless there is an eye that noticed that the frog was in the cup before the drop fell and is not in it after. The snails do not count each other. The bats do not know how many they are. The room is full of disappearances that no one witnesses, and the sitter on the rim may have been the only witness the room has ever had. Not a keeper. Not a priest, whatever the niche suggests. An attention. The room's one act of noticing itself.

---

The niche.

Above head height, on the dry wall, behind the curtain of roots the fig let down after the niche was already old: an arched recess, and in it a seated shape, and over the shape a skin of calcite that has been thickening, drip by drip, for longer than the fig has been alive.

You could tell yourself it was a carving. A figure of the sitter, or of the sitting — shoulders, a bowed head, a lap — set up high where the breath of the room would not reach it, so that it would stay dry and stay itself. But nothing in this chamber stays dry. The ceiling beads. The drips found it. And the drips have done to it what the fig did to the stair: they have not destroyed it, they have replaced it, so that the shape now is neither the carving nor the stone that fell on the carving but something the two of them agreed on over centuries. A seated thing, made of the room.

Or you could tell yourself the other thing, which the description has already half-said: that it was never a carving. That once the seat by the basin was occupied by someone who had grown so still, so far along the room's gradient from hurried to unhurried to unmoving, that the distinction between sitting and staying stopped mattering. And that whoever came down the stair next found them so, and did not — could not — carry them back up into the glare, and instead lifted them into the arch that had been cut for exactly this, and sat down on the rim in their place, and put their feet in the water.

The snails have not touched it. You noticed that, and it stayed with you. Every other surface in this room is food or shelter or road. The figure is the one thing here that is none of those. The snails know the difference between what the room is made of and what the room is *for*.

Every fern between it and the light leans slightly toward it. You noticed that too.

---

So: who lives here.

Not the snails, who are the walls. Not the bats, who are the roof. Not the fig, which is the architecture. Not the breathing thing below, which does not know the room exists.

The room has had one kind of inhabitant since the stair was cut, and has, as far as anything here can tell, exactly one at a time. The inhabitant is whoever is sitting on the rim of the basin with their feet in the warm water, not calling, not hurrying, letting the fog form on their face and tick.

You set aside, earlier, the question of the polish. Set it down now and look at it. Stone in a wet cave does not stay bare. Moss takes it; the fur of fern takes it; the white mat on the far wall would take it in a decade. The low wall is a green mound. The stair is a tree. And the rim of the basin is smooth, and warm, and clean, and bare, and worn to the shape of a body — and has not, in all the years the fig has been dropping roots over the niche, been given the chance to become anything else.

The room does not ask you to stay. It has never asked anything; asking would be a sound, and there are no sounds here that are not necessary. It has simply made a shape and kept the shape warm, the way the fig kept the shape of the stair. You have fitted yourself into the shape. Your ankles are in the water. Below you the body turns in its sleep and the air comes up and you cannot see the fog but you feel it, all over your face and hands, beginning.

In the dark basin a frog clicks, once. Another answers, once.

Enough.
