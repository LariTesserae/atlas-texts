**What Lives in the Loud Country**

The place will tell you that nothing lives here. It says this plainly: no animal, no insect, no bird, no track in the grit. Nothing that eats or drinks or dies. It says this the way the finished rock says things, with its edges sharp and its grain settled, and you are inclined to believe it, because the rock underfoot is the part of this country that has stopped arguing.

But the rock underfoot is also the quietest thing here. And in a place where distance is measured in loudness, quiet is far. The finished ground is the part of the country that has already gone away from you, even as you stand on it. So when the finished part says *nothing lives here*, understand that it is speaking from a great distance, about a place it has left.

Listen toward the edges instead.

---

Start with the plants, since the place at least admits to them.

They do not drink. That is the first thing to understand about how they live, and it is not a hardship for them, because they have never known what water was for. Where other growth lifts its face toward the sun and its roots toward the wet, these lift toward the roar and root toward the roar, and the roar does not fail. It does not have seasons. It does not set. A frond that has turned toward the loudest quarter of the sky is fed continuously, at a volume that would kill anything from a wetter world, and it takes what it needs at the pace it needs it, which is very slowly, and it does not want.

Their roots go down into the grit and then further, into the stone, following fractures, and you will notice — if you find a slab that has been split and can look at its edge — that the roots do not stop where the stone becomes finished. They go on into it. They are *in* the rock, fine dark threads running through the blue-black grain like veins through marble, and the rock closed around them without disturbing them, which means the plant was there first. It stood on the ground before the ground was said. It grew in a place that was only a bend in the light around a mass and it waited, feeding on the enormous noise of that place being decided, and when the rock arrived it arrived around the roots, and the plant is older than what it stands on.

This is how they live: at the edge. They are thickest where the rock is least resolved because that is where there is most to eat, and they are the only things that can be there, because they do not have bodies that refuse. A stand of the tall jointed stalks at the lip of a drafted valley is standing in a sound that would stop your heart, and it is trembling, and it is fine. When the valley is finished — in a thousand years, ten thousand — the stand will find itself on quiet ground, and the quiet will be a kind of famine, and the growth will thin, and the survivors will lean harder toward wherever the saying has gone. That is why the wide bare reaches are bare. They are not barren. They are finished. Everything that was living on them has followed the noise.

The tall ones with the translucent layered fronds — look at those closely. Each page of the fan is a growth-layer, laid down over a span you could not measure in a life, and they are not identical. The earliest pages, the innermost, have a grain that leans one way. The outer pages lean, very slightly, another. The plant has been recording the chord. Every frond in this country is a written history of the one slow note it grew up inside, and if you could read them, and if you could read them fast enough, you would know what the mountains have been saying for as long as there have been mountains here to say it.

Nobody can read them. But they are the only writing in the country that is still being written, except for one slab on a saddle, and we will come to that.

---

Because the place also says there were hands, and that they left.

*Left, or were unmade, or walked out along a ridge into a part of the world that was still being decided and have not yet been finished.* The place offers these as alternatives. They are not alternatives. They are what happened to different ones of them, and the third is the one that matters, because the third is the one that means the country is inhabited.

Go to the half-wall on the partly resolved ridge. Stand at the last laid stone, where the wall stops being there. Do not step past it; the volume will not let you. But look along the crest, into the draft, in the light that does not come from anywhere.

There is a bend in the light around a mass, about twenty paces out. It is the height of a person stooping. Below it, the ridge is a pencil suggestion of a ridge, but the ground directly under the bend is a little more resolved than the ground around it, a small oval of nearly-rock, as though the presence of a foot were enough to hurry the stone along. At the level of the hands — you cannot see hands — there is a stone. A single flake, dark, sharp, entirely finished. It is the most finished thing for a hundred paces in any direction. It is being set on the wall. It has been being set on the wall for as long as the cairn has stood.

The stone is finished because the stone was brought from the quiet. The one carrying it is not finished because they walked into the loud. They are not frozen. Freezing would require time to be stopped, and time is not stopped, it is only very slow — geological, the pace of a world being made — and they are moving at that pace, in it, as part of it. If you came back in a thousand years the flake would be lower. In ten thousand it would be laid. The wall does not know whether it was built into a stretch of ridge that has been un-said or a stretch not yet said. Here is the answer: it is being built, now, by someone who is being said at the same rate as the ridge, and the wall will be finished when they are.

They are not alone out there. Further along the crest, fainter, another bend. Further still, three together, close, as people stand when they are speaking to one another in a wind, except there is no wind. What they would be saying you cannot know, because you cannot hear anything they say — not because it is too quiet but because it is too loud, because they are in the saying, because a word spoken in the draft is spoken at the volume of the draft, and that is the volume that made the mountains.

Understand what this means for how near they are. In this country a thing that is deafening is close. The finished ground is far. The people on the ridge, twenty paces out in the light, are the nearest living things to you in the whole of the loud country. They are nearer than the plants at your knees. They are nearer than your own feet, which stand on quiet rock and are therefore at the far end of a long distance. They have been this near to everything here since they walked out, and they will be this near for the whole of the time it takes them to become far, which is the time it takes to be finished.

---

Now the slab.

Three hundred and eleven strokes, grouped in fives, scored into finished stone with a whiter stone that was dropped and left. Below the last one the surface dissolves into draft and the marks stop, *or continue into a surface that does not yet exist.*

They continue.

Kneel at the edge of the finished part and put your face close to where the stone goes soft. In the region just past the last stroke, in the rock that is only a suggestion, there is a mark that is only a suggestion of a mark — a faint vertical bias in the light, the length of a finger, exactly where the three-hundred-and-twelfth stroke would fall. It is being made. Whoever was counting did not stop counting. They walked into the draft with the whiter stone still in hand — no; they left the whiter stone here, so they are counting now with nothing, with a finger, with the intention of a stroke, and the intention is enough, in the loud, to begin bending the light. The count is going on out there at the pace of the mountains. Three hundred and twelve will be true in a few hundred years.

What were they counting? The place does not say and you should not pretend to know. Not days; there are no days. Not stones; there are more than three hundred and eleven stones in the wall alone. Perhaps the slow flickers of the doubled terrain, each time a slope decided against being a cliff. Perhaps each other. Perhaps the number of times someone walked out toward the loud and was seen to keep walking. It does not matter. What matters is that it is the only tally in the country, and it is not abandoned, it is only slow, and the one keeping it has become the most careful and patient clerk there has ever been, marking a surface that will exist by the time the mark is done.

---

And then there is the one who did not go.

Someone built the hut. Someone laid the cairn and chose each flake for how it sat on the last, and set the hammerstone down on the floor by the doorway, and left the flakes beside it as though they meant to come back. The doorway faces east, into the loudest quarter. That is a doorway for watching from. For keeping the cairn visible, so the ones out on the ridge could find their way back to the quiet if they ever finished and wanted to.

They did not go into the loud. They stayed with the finished rock, on the ground that has stopped arguing, and here is what happens to something that stays in the finished: it becomes finished too. Not dead — there is no bone, no grave, nothing that dies here — but *resolved*. Settled. Quiet. And quiet, in this country, is distance. You can see how it went. The one in the doorway watched east and grew still and grew still and grew quieter, and each degree of quiet was a degree of far, and at the end there was a person sitting in a stone doorway who was so entirely finished that they were no longer near anything at all. Not gone. Far. Infinitely far, in the doorway, with the hammerstone by their knee.

There is nothing in the hut now because what is in the hut is too quiet to be there. That is the only death this place has, and it is not death, it is just the other end of distance from the roar. The ones on the ridge are the nearest things in the country. The one in the doorway is the farthest. Between them, the cairn, and the plants leaning toward the loud, and you.

---

So this is what lives in the loud country.

Growth that eats sound and roots into rock before the rock is there, and moves toward the edges when the edges move, and keeps a record in its pages of the one long note it lives inside.

People — not many, five or six, perhaps eight — standing in the light along the unfinished ridges, mid-gesture, setting stones and speaking and counting, not frozen but moving at the pace of a continent leaning, so near to everything that you cannot approach them, so loud that you will never hear a word, alive in the only way this place allows anything to be alive, which is to be unfinished, and to be being said.

And one who stayed, who is not there, who is so finished that the doorway is empty of them and always will be, and who was the only one to understand that in this country you can go out into the roar and become part of the saying, or you can sit on the quiet ground and become part of what has already been said, and there is no third thing, and neither one is dying.

They did not leave. Nothing leaves. The wide bare reaches are not empty; they are far. The drafted valleys are not empty; they are near. The country is full of what lives in it, distributed along a single axis from the silent to the deafening, and the living end is the loud end, and it is arriving, at the pace of a world being made, and it has all the time there is.

Stand at the cairn and look east. Twenty paces out, a bend in the light is setting a stone on a wall. In a thousand years it will be lower. Do not wait for it. But do not say that no one is there.
