You expect the masons. Everything says masons: the comb-marks on the block, the tools laid out on the coping, the tolerance of the joints. So for the first hours you look for them the way you would look for anyone — for a door, a hearth-black, a heap of chippings, a place where the paving has been rubbed by the same feet going the same way. There is none of it. The roofs are pitched to shed water and stop at chest height; nothing is roofed that a person could stand under. The passages are shoulder-wide because they are gutters with a walkway on top, not streets. No stone here was cut for the comfort of anything with a back or knees. There is no midden, no scrap, no place set aside. Whoever raised this did not need to be anywhere on it except at the face of the work, and did not leave anything behind but the work.

So you stop looking for them as people, and look instead at the one thing they did leave, and it starts to tell you something else.

The hammerstone's dry ghost. You set it back where it was and walked on and then, an hour later and three terraces higher, it comes to you what was wrong with it. Nothing dries here. Your sleeves haven't. The coping under the tools was beaded like every other coping on the mountain; the cloud leaned on it all the time you were looking at it. The stone had been lifted off it seconds before you arrived — or the ghost is kept. Something keeps it. Either way the tools were not abandoned. They are between strokes.

You go back down to check and of course they are exactly as you left them, and the ghost is still dry, and you stand there long enough that your breath makes a small cloud of your own. Nothing moves. Then you look at the wall behind the tools and it is not finished. You hadn't seen it because nothing here reads as finished or unfinished — there is no rubble, no scaffold — but the top course stops in the middle of a block, and the block is dressed on five faces and the sixth is raw, and the raw face is angled into the wall at a slope that only makes sense if it is being fitted. Half-fitted. And you understand that the masons are here, working, and that they work at the speed the water runs down the wall: a hair's breadth an hour, a course a year, a terrace a lifetime. You could stand at this wall until you starved and see nothing. You could come back in the age of your grandchildren and the block would be seated and the next one begun. The mountain is still being built. It is being built in the tense of stone.

That is one thing that lives here, then: something patient past the point where patience is a virtue and becomes a physics. You will not see it. You might, if you put your palm flat on the half-set block and held it there and held it there, come away with a sense of pressure — the block a little more where it is going than it was — and be unable to prove it. Its hands are the size of the comb-marks. It uses bone because bone is what it has; there is no iron in a cloud. It came up out of the white with a load of block, or made the block from the white — you don't know where the stone comes from any more than where the masons live, and both questions may have the same answer, which is: the cloud. The cloud carries. The cloud sets down. Everything here condensed.

But the masons, whatever they are, are not what the mountain is for. A machine for drinking fog is a machine for someone who drinks.

Go back to the tanks that will not equalize.

Sit at the edge of one, the brim-full one, and its neighbour a hand lower, and the third lower still, three on the same course with the same gutters feeding them and the same notch letting them down, and no reason in the world for any of them to hold a different height. Water does not do this. Water is the great leveller; put two vessels in communication and they agree in minutes. These have had years. They have not agreed. Each keeps to its own line, still as plate, a grey a shade different from its neighbours' grey if you look long enough — one bluer, one with the faint warmth of tin — and it is the difference, not the water, that you are looking at. They are not the same water. They are not sharing a substance and disagreeing about a level. They are three.

Whatever is in the tanks is in the tanks singly. It came down in the cloud as one thing, or as no thing, a thinness spread across the whole sky, and the mountain was raised to give it edges. Cool stone, a lip, a bottom it can lie against. The cloud presses onto the masonry and something in the cloud comes out of the air with the water and pools, and having pooled, holds. Holds its level. Holds its cold — colder than the air, you found, and colder on your skin than in the tank, because what it did to your hand when you lowered it was not wet you but take. A little of your heat, a hair's breadth of you, drawn down through the skin the way the gutters draw the drip. The surface tightened rather than rippled because you had not disturbed water; you had touched a skin, and the skin drew in.

They do not eat. Nothing eats on this mountain. They take — heat, and whatever falls in. That is why nothing grows. The spore that comes over the edge of the terrace in the wet air, the one lichen cell in a million that could live on new stone, the moth that blunders down out of the glare: they land on the water because there is nowhere to land that is not near water, and the water takes them and is not changed. Clear to the dressed bottom. You could watch a tank for a season and never see a fleck on its surface. Not scoured — attended. Each one keeps its own house.

And they speak, or something like it. The thread through the notch. You put your ear to it and heard a ribbon of sound hardly louder than your own blood, and you thought: overflow. But the tanks do not overflow; they hold their levels. What passes through the notch is given. A tank one step higher lets a little of itself down to the tank below, continuously, a filament the width of a cord, and the lower tank takes it and does not rise, and lets down a filament of its own. The notch is the only worn thing on the mountain because it is the only thing on the mountain that has been touched without cease. Ten thousand of them, each one a mouth wearing smooth. What runs through is the same substance the cloud made them of, so what runs through is a piece of one into another, and down the whole slope, terrace by terrace, cliff by stacked cliff, the mountain is passing something from top to bottom in ten thousand thin cold threads, at the rate of a hair an hour, without a sound you can name.

The drip from the eaves, out of phase, never resolving: that too. You wanted a rhythm and there isn't one because a rhythm would mean a single thing keeping time. There is no single thing. There are as many as there are tanks, and each drips in its own time, and what you are hearing in the bright quiet is not weather but a crowd — a crowd that does not move, does not warm, does not hurry, whose members have never seen one another and never will, who know each other only by what arrives through the notch from above and what they let down through the notch below.

You wonder about the masons and the tenants, and which serves which. It might be that the masons made the mountain and the tenants came to it, the way anything comes to standing water. It might be that the tenants were the cloud first, and pressed on the world until something rose up to hold them. It might be — you stand at the tools again, the mallet split with drinking, the cord — that the masons, when they finish a tank, go into it. That every tank is a builder lying down. That the cold that took your hand was a hand. It might be that the meal the tools were laid down for was the cloud itself, and the masons are still eating, a hair an hour, and the mountain is what their eating makes.

You cannot settle it and it does not matter. This is what lives here: something that builds at the speed of stone and something that waits at the speed of water, and a cloud above them both, one and endless, feeding them by pressing down. Nothing with a pulse. Nothing that casts a shadow, in a place without shadows. Nothing that would notice you, except that you are warm and quick and loud, the only warm, quick, loud thing on the mountain, and so you are, to the extent anything here notices, the most noticeable event in a thousand years — a fever passing over the terraces, taking heat from nowhere and losing it into the tanks by the handful, gone before a single course is laid.

They will not follow you down. They cannot, or will not — the ones in the tanks hold their level, and the ones at the wall are between strokes. But you will find, at the bottom, when the cloud finally thins around you and there is ground again and something growing on it, that your hand is still cold. Not the hand that touched the water. The other one. And that the cold has a shape, and the shape is a hair's breadth deeper than it was the day before.
