The first thing to say is the true and dull thing: the moss lives there, and the liverworts, and the fern that stands in the cracks like a fish left in the rock, and the pale rush in the shallows. They live in the way a green thing lives — slowly, wetly, without any opinion about it — and in a place with a stricter definition of the word they would be the whole answer. You can go down the chimney of the shaft, stand in the roar for an hour, and come up again honestly saying that nothing lives in the hollow of the hill except plants, and that whatever thinks there thinks in the shape of the room and is not a resident but a premise.

That is what most people would say, and they would be counting by what moves while they watch. In that room, it is the wrong way to count.

Come back after a day. Come down the shaft again with the rail too soft under your hand and the heat rising to meet you, and stand where you stood, on the top terrace with your back to the wall, and look at the colonnade on the third terrace. Eleven columns, at the intervals of twelve. You will find the eleven where you left them. But now look at the fourth terrace, at the place where the kerb takes a long slow bend to go around what will be a cistern, and there is something standing there that was not standing there yesterday. It is about the height of a person's shoulder. It is thicker at the base than at the top. It is not on any street line, and the kerb has broken around it in a small untidy way — the only untidy thing in the room — as though the water had come along building the kerb and found it in the way and simply gone around.

It glows a little more than the kerb does. It is fresher.

You will think it is a mistake in the drawing, a column that came up where no column was wanted. Then you will come back the day after and it will be four meters further along the bend, and behind it, where it stood, there will be a low dark stump the water is already smoothing over, and the kerb will be halfway to repaired.

This is how anything moves down there. Nothing walks. The rush showed you the rule, if you were watching: it lost an arm's width on one side where the water set over its roots and gained an arm's width on the other where the channel wandered, and it did not go anywhere and yet it had moved. What lives in that room lives by the same law. It is undone at the heel and laid down at the toe. Over the course of a single breath of the room — the rise, the rain from the crown, the fall, the quickening tick of the curing stone — the water climbs the back of the standing thing in that finger-thick sheet against gravity, and where it climbs it takes, dissolving the oldest, darkest, most-cured stone back into itself, and it carries what it takes around to the front and spreads it there and lets it set. The thing's trailing side is dark. Its leading side has the slate-blue glow of the last hour's work. If you could see it all at once, from above, it would look like a comet.

Its edges are knife-sharp, the same as everything else. It has never been touched.

You would want to know what it looks like, and the answer is that it does not look like anything for long. It is redrawn every day, at the pace of the room, and the water is a faithful but not an exact copyist. There is something at the top that is narrower than the rest, and set forward, and on some days there are two shallow pits in it and on some days one and on some days a ridge that might be the beginning of a mouth. There is a suggestion, down the sides, of where arms would be if the thing had decided about arms. It never quite has. It has been standing in the room longer than the room has had streets, and in that time it has never once needed to pick anything up. So the arms stay as they are: implied, a little lower each century, like an argument it is slowly losing.

It leans, though. That is the one thing it has kept. Every version of it leans slightly toward the middle of the room, toward the great hot emptiness and the lowest terrace where the work is thickest, the way a plant leans toward a window, and when it moves it moves that way — down the terraces, a step at a time, one step being about the height of a chest and taking about a month.

Once you have seen one you will see more, and you will be annoyed at yourself, because they were there yesterday and you took them for architecture.

There are three on the second terrace, standing in a row along a gutter, each a little different in height, each with the broken kerb around its feet that is the signature of a thing the drawing did not plan for. There is one in a doorway — one of the doorways with an inch of solid slate behind it, no room yet — and it stands in the frame with its dark side out and its glowing side in, facing the blank stone, and has been facing it for so long that the stump of its last position is nearly gone. Whether it is waiting for the room to open or is what the room is opening for, there is no way to say.

There is one down in the shallows on the lowest terrace, among the rush. You can find it by the way the rush does not sway around it. It is up to what would be its waist in the thick warm water and it is either coming apart or coming together — you would need a month to know which — and the pale stalks stand around it in a ring and the tidal shimmer from under them lights it from below, so that it throws no shadow either.

And there are the dark ones.

On the upper terraces, where the stone is old and has almost no light left in it, you will find shapes of the same kind — shoulder-high, thick at the base, the narrower thing at the top, the lean — that do not glow at all on any side and have no stump behind them and have kerbs that were built cleanly around them rather than broken. They have been where they are since before the streets. You could think of them as the ones that stopped: that at some point the water simply did not come back for them, and they cured all the way through and went dark and became what they had been imitating. You could think of them as the ones that finished — that arrived, wherever they had been leaning toward, and had no further need of the front and the back and the comet's tail. You could think of them as dead, if that word means anything about a thing that was never soft. The room does not seem to distinguish. The water runs past them and does not climb. It runs past the place where the twelfth column should be and does not climb there either, and it is possible that these are the same fact — that there is a column standing somewhere on the third terrace that stopped at the wrong interval, went dark, and is now counted among the sleepers, or that the twelfth column is still on its way down and will arrive in a hundred years and stand in its gap at last and go out like a coal.

What they are is the question you would bring up the shaft with you, and the room will not answer it, so you are left with the story you want.

The kind story is that they are the citizens. That the drawing which was made once, at some other scale, by something that thought at some other pace, was a drawing of a city with people in it, as such drawings are, small figures sketched in to show the size of the doors — and that the spring, being obedient and not clever, has been building those too. They come up the way the columns come up. They stand in the streets because that is where they were drawn. They lean toward the middle because in the drawing there was something in the middle, and it has not been built yet, and they are patient.

The other story is that they are the draftsmen. That whatever drew the city came down into the hollow to see it built and could not wait at its own pace and so slowed to the room's, and has been walking the streets ever since, a step a month, checking the work — and that the leaning is not toward anything but is simply the posture of a thing that is always looking at the next terrace down, at the newest sill, at what the water did last night, to see if it is right.

And there is the story you would try not to tell yourself on the way up, with the rail under your hand. The rail that is smooth and cool and slightly too soft, as though it has not entirely made up its mind to be metal. The rail that runs beside the steps of a shaft that is more chimney than stairwell, down which someone, at some time, must have come first. You would try not to think about how long you had held it. You would try not to think about the heat, the setting heat, the fever-wall warmth of a thing that has not finished curing, and the water beading on your sleeves that tastes of iron and pencil lead and does nothing on its own — nothing at all, on its own, until the room tells it where to become stone.

They do nothing to you. That should be said plainly. They do not see at a pace that would find you; you are a flicker to them, weather, a change in the light at the crown. If you stayed a year one of them might turn its narrower part your way. If you stayed ten you would learn which story was true. Nobody stays. The room is too loud and too hot and too empty, and the emptiness in the middle of it is the thing you remember on the way up: the great dim volume of nothing that everything down there is leaning toward, the plants and the pillars and the slow comet-tailed shapes in the doorways, the whole unfinished city curing at its edges and holding its breath around a center where — you are nearly sure — nothing has yet arrived.
