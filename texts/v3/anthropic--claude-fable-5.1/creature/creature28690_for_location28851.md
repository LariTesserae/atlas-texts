The first word that arrives is *bat*, and it is not wrong, and you should let it go anyway.

It is not wrong because of the fur, and the fold, and the hanging, and because the mind reaching into a dark full of warm winged bodies has one drawer to open and opens it. But hold your hand against the wall a while longer, until the shape of what is under your palm comes clear through the heat, and the word starts to slide. The fur is too long for a bat — a finger's depth, dense, with a lie to it like an otter's, and beneath it a skin that is loose on the body, as if the animal were wearing something a size too large. The wing, where it is folded, does not fold like a hand. It gathers. Where you would expect the hard spokes of long fingers there is something more like a pleated sheet, with a few stiff cords through it, and it goes tight or slack the way a sail does rather than the way a fan does. There is a lot of it. Spread, it would be much wider than the body warrants, wider than a spread hand, wider than two — enough surface to hold a thing this small aloft in air this thin, where a valley animal's wing would beat and beat and find nothing to push against.

So: something that was once, far below, small and ordinary and nocturnal, and that has been changed by height. Or by time. Or by the long slow lives that height and time have imposed on it. You cannot tell in the dark, and you cannot tell in the light, because there is no light, and no one has carried one of these out into the two grey hours at the mouth and looked. If you did, you would probably see a face. It would be small and folded and mostly nose, with eyes that are either very small or sealed, it is difficult to tell which, under a skin of lid gone thick and unused. The ears would be large and would be moving. Even in the deepest torpor the ears move — the only part of the animal that does, on any timescale a person can perceive — turning a few degrees this way and that, slowly, like the dish of some instrument, tracking the drips.

---

Lift one. It is possible. The ones on the outside of the layers, the coolest, the ones nearest the pool, will let go of the wall if you work your fingers under them, and come away in your hands with a slow reluctance, still folded, trailing a few matted threads of a neighbor's fur.

It weighs almost nothing. That is the first shock. A thing that size, that warm, should have heft, and this has the heft of a wadded glove. Whatever it is made of has been spent. The body under the fur is mostly frame — the cords of the wings, a keel of breastbone you can feel through the loose skin, a spine like a string of seeds — and the flesh over the frame has been drawn down over forty years to the minimum that will keep a heart going at eight beats a minute and a breath at one. They came up here fat, presumably. Whatever they were below, they would have been fat in the season the wind took them, the way any small animal is fat before a hard time. They have been living on that ever since, and on the little they get from the walls, and on each other's warmth, which is a kind of food. They are not starving. Starving is a process with an end. They have simply become the amount of animal that this room will support, and the amount is very small, and it is distributed over a body the same size as it always was, so the body is mostly air and fur and intention.

The second shock is that it is doing something. Held in the palm, out of the mass, it does not wake — waking is not available to it in any quick sense — but it *knows*. The breathing changes. The long sigh comes a little sooner than it would have, and there is a tremor through the folded wing, and then, from inside the fur, a sound: a click, dry, small, exactly the click you have been hearing scattered through the roar without being able to place it. Then another. It is speaking to the wall. It is telling the wall where it is, or asking. And if you hold it long enough — a minute, two — you will feel, through your hand, from somewhere across the hollow, the answer arrive: not heard, felt, a change in the pressure of the roar as a few hundred of them shift a hair's breadth toward the sound.

Put it back. It will take an hour to work itself into the layers again, and the ones around it will take longer than that to close over the gap, and for that hour there will be a cold place in the wall where your hand was.

---

What lives in the room, then, is not really the animal in your palm. The animal in your palm is a cell of it.

The thing that lives here is the mass — the whole slow turning body of them, the convection, the churn. It has a temperature, which it holds. It has a surface, which is always the coldest and most awake of it, and a core, which is the warmest and most nearly dead, and it moves material continuously from one to the other so that neither is ever the same individuals for long. It has a metabolism, a single one, made of thousands of small ones summed, and that metabolism is what keeps the room from being a crack of ice. It has a voice, which is the roar. It breathes into the stone and the stone gives the breath back as water, and the water runs down to the pool, and the pool is where the thing's edge is.

It has something like a nervous system, in the clicks. Each animal speaks to its neighbors; each neighbor speaks on. A disturbance at one wall reaches the far wall in a few seconds as a ripple of adjustment, a settling and re-settling, a slight lean of the whole mass away from the trouble. It has something like a memory, which is the whistle — the single thin rising note from the roof, and the long wait, and the answer from elsewhere. No one has been up there. Whatever is in the roof is either the oldest of them, the ones who got highest when the room first filled and have not come down, or it is the roof itself, some resonance of the leaning stone that the animals have learned and now keep alive between them, call and response, the way a room full of people can carry a tune none of them started. The interval between call and answer has been growing. It was, the first year, perhaps a heartbeat. It is now the length of a held breath. It will be, one day, so long that whoever whistles will have forgotten the whistle before the reply comes, and then the reply will be the only sound and the call will be gone and there will be no way to tell which was which.

And it is going somewhere. That is the strangest thing about the thing that lives here — stranger than its size or its slowness. It has a direction. The whole mass, over years, a body at a time, migrating toward the back where the walls close. Nothing in it decides this. Each animal, unfolding at last from the cool surface near the pool, opens its too-large wings in the wet air and lets go and glides — and glides a little further in than it strictly needs to, because that is where the warmth is, because that is where the others have gone, because the roar is fractionally louder in that direction and the roar is the sound of safety. Every glide is a small error toward the dark. The errors sum. In the way that a river does not want to reach the sea, the colony does not want to reach the back of the cleft, and it is going there anyway, and when it arrives it will do what a river does, which is stop being a river and become something else.

---

There are other lives in the room, and they are the ones that live off the first one.

In the fur, if you had a way to see them, there would be mites — there are always mites — and they would be as changed as their hosts, slowed and paled and strung out, feeding on skin that is shed a flake a month. Between the animals, in the matted felt where one body has been pressed against another for so long that the fur has grown together, there is a whitish growth like the bloom on old leather. That is the same fungus as the one at the pool. It lives on what falls: the sheddings, the occasional death. It lives most of all on the floor, in the soft skin underfoot that you were told is difficult to think about, and which is difficult to think about because it is the ones who did not finish the turn — who went too far out in the rotation, or too far in, and stopped, and were held up by their neighbors for a while out of simple mechanical crowding, and then were let down. Not many. A few a year. Forty years of a few a year, unrotted at this altitude, flattened under the slow shuffling of the living and grown through and through by pale threads, until the floor is a felt of the colony's dead with the colony's fungus knitting it, and it gives under a boot like a mattress, and it is warm.

At the pool, where the water is, the fungus has enough to drink to do more than knit. It rises. The thumb-sized pads are its fruiting bodies, and the faint green they give off is the only light in the room and is made, in the end, of the animals: their breath condensed to water, their dead resolved to soil, and this thin cold shine coming up out of the join. It is not for anyone. There is nothing here with eyes to see it. It is, if it is for anything, for the thing the animals cannot do, which is make light in the dark — so that the room, taken all together, does that too.

The drips live here, in the sense that they are always present and always the same. The wind-note lives here. The orange crust at knee height, at the mouth, that no one has examined, is most likely living — a lichen that likes iron and cold and two hours of grey — and it has been at its patch for the whole forty years and has widened it by the breadth of a fingernail, which is the same speed the mountain rises, so that in some sense it and the ridge are keeping pace. The cushion plant in the angle of the floor is older than the room; it was on the crest before the slab leaned, and the slab leaned over it and did not crush it, and it has adapted to living in a doorway, growing only on the side the light reaches, so that it is now half a plant, a hemisphere, dense and dark and perfectly still.

---

And there is the one who came.

They do not live here. They are the one thing in the description that does not live here, and they are the thing you keep coming back to, because the flakes are laid in a line and the line points in.

Try to see them. It is not possible to know when, so it is not possible to know what they wore or spoke or wanted, but some things are possible. They came along the blade of the ridge, as you did, because there is no other way. They were carrying a piece of dark stone from somewhere else, and a lamp, and the fat to burn in it — which means they knew they would be somewhere dark, which means either they had been here before or they had been told. They knapped at the mouth, in the grey light, and left the flakes, which means the flakes were not what they wanted; what they wanted was the edge that came off the core, and they took that with them. They lit the lamp. They went in.

They would have come back out. The lamp burned long enough to leave a proper scorch, but not long enough to leave much of one, and the fat would not have lasted an hour. So an hour, at the most, in there — an hour in the roar, with a small flame, seeing the walls move. Seeing what you did not see: fur, and the slow turning, and thousands of small ears tracking the light. Perhaps the mass leaned away from the flame. Perhaps one of them unfolded, and glided across the lamplight and was, for a moment, enormous. Perhaps they went to the pool and drank the iron water. Perhaps they were only checking that it was still there.

Then they left, and laid the flakes in a line pointing in, and the line has stayed pointing in for five years or five hundred, and the meaning of the line is what you make of it. It could be a warning. It could be a marker for the next one, or for themselves. It could be the simplest thing a person does when they have seen something they have no word for: put down a few small objects so that the place is not entirely unmarked by having been witnessed. So that the room knows, in the only way a room can know, that someone was here and was looking.

Whoever it was, they are the only visitor the room has had, in the sense that matters. And they are the one thing in it that stopped and did not stay. Everything else that came into the cleft — wind, water, the animals on the wind, the fungus on the animals — came in and slowed and became part of it. They came in and did not slow. They kept their speed, and their speed carried them back out, and down, and away into whatever the world was that they came from, and the room closed behind them and went on breathing.

---

That is what lives here: a single warm slow animal made of thousands, turning itself over in a crack in a mountain that is still being made, keeping a pool and a light and a felt of its own dead, whistling to its roof across a widening gap, drifting a wingbeat at a time toward a wall. It came on the wind. It will be lifted, the width of a fingernail a year, into air it cannot breathe. It does not know this. It does not know anything, in the sense that you mean. But it knows where it is, all of it, at every moment, every cell of it speaking to every other in the dark, and that is more than can be said for most of what lives, and it will be true for a long time yet, and then the stone will finish breaking and there will be, for a while, a very different room.
