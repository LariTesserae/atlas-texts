The first answer is the one you have already been given, so you distrust it. A fish. A thing with legs. A shape that comes in from the dark and goes out again. Three lives on a floor too poor to feed them, each keeping to its lane — the pump house, the base of the wall, the streets — as if they had divided the town among themselves long ago and saw no reason to renegotiate. You could stop there. Many would.

But the needles move, and you were told that whatever they measure is being measured. That is a strange way to put it. A gauge measures by itself; a needle rises because pressure rises. The sentence implies a reader. And a valve, however faithful, does not open twice a day on its own for longer than a generation. Something has a hand on it.

---

For a while you think the answer is the works.

It is not a foolish thought. Stand against the boiler house wall long enough and the hum stops being a sound in your bones and becomes a pulse. The flywheel turns at the pace of a sleeping heart. The chimney breathes out, warm, and the snow comes down to be breathed in. The kelp grows from the top of it like hair from a scalp. The warm quarter has the shape of a body — a compact, hunched thing with its back to the drop-off, its arms drawn in, holding the ice off with nothing but its own temperature. The fur that coats every surface is thinnest on the boiler house not because it is scoured but because it is *warm*, and cold-water growth does not like warmth; the building keeps itself clean the way skin does.

So: the works is alive, and the fish and the legged thing are its small tenants, and the great shape is its only predator, and that is the ecology, and you have your answer.

You almost keep it. Then you notice the paths.

---

The fur, which has rounded the bricks, has a grain. On the flat tops of the pipes it lies in a nap, and where the current runs the nap is combed one way. But between the pump house and the boiler house — the two buildings whose doors face each other across a strip of paving no wider than a cart — the nap is worn. Not combed. Worn, the way a stair is worn, down the center, where the weight goes. The ribbon-weed there does not grow at all.

Inside the boiler house, beside the turning wheel, at the height of a hand held out from a stooped body, there is a patch of wall where the fur has gone entirely and the brick beneath is dark and slick. Something has rested there, leaning, watching the needles. The glass on the gauges is clear. Everything else in this town is filmed, blurred, softened; the gauge faces are clear. Someone wipes them.

And the valve — you find it, in the lattice overhead, a great wheel-handle on a stem — has fur on its rim like everything else, except for two places opposite each other where the metal shows through, worn to a dull silver. Two hands. A turn to open. A turn to shut.

The works is not alive. The works is *kept*.

---

You see it, finally, in the way you see anything down here, which is by having already looked at it for a long time without understanding.

The round window of the pump house. The pale unhurried shape passing behind it like a face at a door. You had that wrong the first time. It was never like a face at a door. It was a face at a door.

It comes out — not often, and you would not call what it does emerging so much as the water in the doorway becoming slightly more occupied — and it is not a fish, though it moves the way a fish moves, because in water this cold everything that moves at all moves that way: slow, with the whole body, without any wasted stroke. It is roughly the height of a person who has stopped standing up straight. It is furred, as the buildings are furred. The growth on it is thinner than on the dead sheds and thicker than on the boiler house, which tells you something about its temperature — it is warm, but not very. Where the fur is thinnest you can see that the surface beneath is pale, and that it is not skin, quite, and not scale, quite, but a thing skin becomes when it has been held in near-freezing water for longer than skin was meant for and has decided, rather than dying, to change the terms.

Its edges are rounded. That is the detail you keep returning to. The bricks have been rounded by growth; so has this. Elbows, knuckles, the line of the jaw if it is a jaw — all softened, all slightly accreted, as though the same slow secretion that made the town look grown rather than built has been working on the keeper as well, and has nearly finished.

It has two arms. It has hands. The hands are the least changed part of it, and the most worn.

---

It does not see you, or it does and you do not register as a thing that requires response. It crosses the worn path to the boiler house. It leans on the dark patch of wall. It watches the needles rise a little, fall a little, rise.

This is most of what it does.

The rest is the round. It goes up into the lattice — it climbs the pipes with a slowness that would be agonizing if there were anyone here to be agonized — and puts its two hands on the two bare places on the valve, and turns. The knock travels out through the water. It waits there, holding the wheel, for a length of time you would measure in hours if you were still measuring, and then turns it back. The second knock. Then down.

It goes to the border. Not to the wall, not to the drop-off — to the line between the warm quarter and the dead one, where the brinicles hang unfinished a yard from the boiler house and drip cold upward. It stands there. It does not touch them. It does not appear to be able to do anything about them. It looks at how far they have come since the last time, the way you might look at a water stain on a ceiling, knowing it means something about the roof, knowing you cannot reach the roof.

It goes back to the pump house. The face passes behind the window and is gone.

---

What it eats, you do not learn. It is possible that it eats the snow, as everything here does. It is possible that it eats nothing and lives on the hum, on the small heat leaking through the boiler house wall into the hand that leans there, the way the kelp lives on the warmth through the hatch. Its metabolism, if it has one, runs at the speed of the flywheel: too slow to accomplish anything, and never stopping.

What it remembers is easier to guess, because you have been told what you yourself cannot remember. The surface. It does not remember the surface. It does not remember the sky the pipes have replaced, or the river before it was a valley before it was this. What it remembers is the numbers — where each needle should sit, how far it may drift before the valve must be turned, which knock comes first. Procedure outlasts biography. Whoever it was, it was someone who understood that a river becomes something else when the valley floods and did not see why the machinery should stop, and it did not stop, and neither did they, and here is what that decision looks like after enough time.

The script on the gauges is half-eaten. It can still read it. Or it no longer needs to.

---

The legged thing at the base of the wall — you wonder about that. It never crosses open ground. It keeps to the boundary. It moves along the dressed stone the way the keeper moves along the worn path, purposefully, with a route. You wonder if there were two of them once. Two who stayed. One took the works and one took the wall, and the one who took the wall has changed further, because the wall is colder, and has more legs now than it started with, or fewer, and no longer comes in to the warm quarter because it has forgotten why it would. They do not meet. You never see them meet. But the wall is unbroken on three sides, sagging but unbroken, and someone has been walking it.

And the shape from the drop-off. Once in a season. When it comes, the keeper is behind the round window and stays there, and does not do the round, and the valve does not knock, and the needles drift a little further than they should. The shape moves through the streets like a thickening of the water. It does not touch anything. It passes the boiler house and slows — you think it slows — near the warmth, and then goes on, and out, and down. The keeper waits until it has been gone for a long time. Then the face passes the window, and the path is crossed, and the valve knocks twice, catching up.

You do not think the shape is a predator. You think it is something that once had a right to this valley and comes back to look at what has been done with it. The keeper does not argue. It waits, and then resumes.

---

The rope on the bollard. The keeper knows it is there. You have watched it come to the edge of the wall, near the drop-off, and stand where the rope goes up, and not look at it. Not look at it with great precision — the way you do not look at a door you have decided not to open. Its face is turned toward the dark below the drop-off instead, where the shape comes from, where there is nothing to see. Then it goes back.

It could climb. It has hands. The rope goes up toward a surface that may or may not exist, and if it exists, someone there tied this end to a boat and meant to come back, and the keeper is the last thing in this town that could still be called a person, and it has never gone to find out. You understand this without being able to say whether it is fear, or duty, or simply that the rope is not on the round, and the round is what there is.

---

Here is what it is, then, as near as you can come.

Not the fish; there was never a fish. Not the works; the works are a thing it tends. Not a ghost — a ghost is something that has stopped and does not know it, and this has not stopped, and knows exactly. It is what remains when a keeper outlasts the reason for keeping and does not notice, because the noticing would have required looking up. It is the hand on the valve. It is the reason the gauges are clear. It is warm enough to hold the ice off a small building and no warmer. It has been rounded by the same slow growth that rounded the bricks, so that from the drop-off, in the snow, you could not say where the keeper ended and the works began, and it has been that way for so long that this no longer looks like a failure of the eye. It looks like the truth.

The tree settles around the nail. You had assumed the works were the tree. Watching the pale shape lean on the dark patch of wall, watching the needles, its rounded shoulder against the brick, its heat leaking into the hum and the hum leaking back — you are no longer certain which of them is the nail.
