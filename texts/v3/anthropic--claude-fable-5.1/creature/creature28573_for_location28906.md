What lives there lives in the wall.

Start with one. Pick a hole at eye level on the west side, where the afternoon light is heaviest, and wait for the thing in it to come out, which it will, because you are not much of a disturbance and it has never learned what a disturbance is.

It comes forward the way a drop of water comes forward along a twig, all at once and slowly. There is a moment when the mouth of the hole is simply lighter than it was, and then a head is there, blunt, eyeless-looking because the eyes are shut and the lids are the same color as everything else. Then the forelimbs, splayed. Then the body, which is flatter than a lizard's, a body that has been pressed for a very long time between two things — the stone beneath and the light above — and has taken the shape of that pressure. It settles. The belly meets the polished lip of the hole with a small dry sound. The limbs fold in. It is done.

It is the length of your hand. It weighs, you would guess, almost nothing. Its skin is not scaled but grained, the grain running the same way as the grain in the rock, and where it lies across a seam of the rose-colored stone the rose shows through it faintly, as if the skin were not entirely opaque. You look for breathing and find it eventually: a slow swelling under the ribs, once, and then a long time later, once again. You count and lose count. Twenty breaths in the time it takes the sun to move a finger's width. Perhaps fewer.

What it is doing, it does with its whole surface. You can see this better in the row than in the one. Along a stretch of wall where a dozen of them sit within a few arm-spans of each other, the air has a different quality. The grain that hangs everywhere else in the pit — that thickness, the sense of light having weight — is not gone there but is lessened, a clear patch in a cloudy pane. It moves with them. When one withdraws, the patch shrinks. When it comes back out and settles, the patch grows again, over the space of a minute, as the thing re-opens whatever it opens.

Their eyes stay shut all day. This seems like a decision rather than a limitation. You can watch one for an hour and not see the lids move, and then, when the sun has gone from the rim and the pit is holding its light alone, the lids part — on that one and, when you look up, on all of them, in the same quarter hour — and there are eyes in the walls. They are the only dark things in the place. Thousands of them. Small and wet and black as the inside of a seed, open in the grey-white glow like the holes in the holes. They do not look at you. They look, as far as you can tell, at nothing, or at the light itself, which by then is dim enough to be looked at.

---

The holes belong to them the way a shell belongs to a snail, which is to say they made the holes by living in them.

You can see this if you look at the spread of sizes. Near the rim, where the light is thinnest and the night cold reaches soonest, the holes are small — a thumb's width, a knuckle deep — and the things that live in them are small too, a finger's length, so pale they are nearly indistinguishable from the rock except that the rock does not withdraw when you lean toward it. Lower down the holes are larger and deeper and their occupants larger and deeper-colored, if a shade of white can be called deeper. At the base of the wall, in the band that holds light longest and gathers the heaviest dew, the holes are as big as they get, fist-wide and forearm-deep, mouths polished to a gloss you can see your face in, and the animals in them are the full hand-length and have the rose cast strongest, as if a lifetime pressed against that particular stone had taught the skin its color.

So they move down as they grow. Or they grow as they move down. A young one — you never see the very young, only infer them from the smallest dimples, which have something in them the size and color of a barley grain — a young one begins near the rim, in a hollow it may have found or may have started, and it comes out and sits and withdraws and comes out again, and each time its belly meets the lip of the hole it takes a little of the stone with it, and over years the hole gets wider and the mouth gets smoother and the animal gets larger, and at some point the hole is the right size for something bigger and the animal is that something. Whether it then moves to a lower, larger hole or simply keeps working the one it has, you cannot say. Both seem to happen. There are holes at every height with the wrong-sized occupant in them, a small one in a big hole or a large one wedged at a mouth it barely fits, and there are holes with no one in them at all.

The empty ones are the deepest. That is the rule that emerges. The most polished mouths, the ones that look most like they were made on purpose, are the ones with nothing behind them. Put your fingers in one — the animals do not seem to mind, or to notice — and the back of it is cool and dry and rounded like the inside of a cup, and the polish goes all the way down.

You think about what fills them. Not new animals; the young go to the rim. Dew, at night. Light, by day. Nothing else. The holes outlast what made them, and then they are simply part of the pit's way of holding things.

---

They die, you decide, by not going in.

You do not see it. You see the evidence, once you know what to look for: here and there on the wall, a pale patch the shape of one of them, flat against the stone below a hole-mouth, slightly whiter than the rock around it and slightly raised, like a lichen that has not decided on a color yet. Old ones are hardly raised at all. The oldest are just a difference in texture, a hand-sized place where the stone is smoother than the stone around it.

The night does it. The cold air pools and the wet comes down and everything with a hole to go into goes into it, and something that has stopped going in — from age, from whatever ends a thing like this — stays out on the stone with its eyes open and takes the dew on its back for the whole of the dark, and in the morning the light rises and takes the wet back up and the animal does not rise with it. It has not eaten anything in its life that would let it rot. It dries. It goes the color of the wall and then the texture of the wall and then it is the wall, and the hole above it is the deepest, cleanest hole in that stretch, and stays empty.

There is no smell. There is never a smell down here.

---

They do not touch one another.

The spacing that is not regular and not random is this: each one sits as close to its neighbors as it can bear, and no closer. You see the bearing when one shifts. It edges a fraction sideways to follow the light and the one beside it edges away by the same fraction, and the one beside that, and a ripple of tiny adjustments runs along the wall and dies out a dozen bodies along. Nothing in it looks like fear or like courtesy. It looks like the thing the fig leaves do, turning edge-on. It looks like a sum being worked out.

Because of this the wall never has two of them at one hole. It never has one crossing open stone to reach another. In the whole afternoon you see perhaps four move any distance at all — a slow descent from one hole to an empty one below it, belly never leaving the rock, a matter of half an hour to cover the length of your forearm — and each time the mover goes straight down, where the stone is emptiest, and settles into the vacant mouth and does not come out again that day. You do not see any go up.

And yet at dusk they all do the same thing at once. The eyes open together. The bodies spread together, limbs unfolding a little from the tucked position, each one giving the last of the glow a slightly larger surface. And in the grey before dawn they withdraw together, not at a signal but in a wave that starts at the top of the east wall where the cold arrives first and moves down and around, a tide going out of the honeycomb, until the holes are all mouth and no face and the dew begins.

Whatever they are to each other, it is not company. It is more like the thing a field of grass is to itself.

---

Nothing hunts them. That is the other quiet.

A hawk goes over the plain in the late afternoon and its shadow falls on the grass and stops at the rim, because there is nothing in the pit for a shadow to fall on; the light comes from everywhere, and a shape between the sun and the floor is just another object in the brightness. The animals on the walls do not react. You watch for it and there is nothing — no flattening, no ripple of withdrawal. They have not learned to fear a shape against the sky because down here a shape against the sky is not dark. It is possible nothing has ever come down for them. The walls are steep and the light is a thing a bird with open eyes could not tolerate for long, and there is nothing else on the floor to bring a predator in, no water most of the year, no seed, no grass to speak of. They are not hidden. They do not need to be. They are simply in a place that offers nothing to anything that eats.

So the pit has no alarm in it. Your footsteps are the loudest thing and the animals nearest you withdraw not from fear but from the way you disturb the light, the way you throw a body into what should be an even medium. When you stand still the medium closes over you and they come back out.

---

The others are few.

The fig has given up on leaves and lives on the stone, or lives with it; its roots are into the seams so deep you cannot tell where root stops and rock begins, and its bark is the same white and takes the same wet at night and gives it up the same way in the morning. It is possible the fig is drinking the way the animals drink, through the bark, and the leaves are a habit it has not quite lost. The one leaf you can see from the rim has turned a little further by the time you leave. In a year it may be edge-on. In ten it may be gone.

The fern under the seep is the only green thing that is really green, and it grows in the one place the light does not fully reach — a fold in the eastern wall, an overhang of a few finger-widths, the closest thing to shade the pit has. Even there it is a fern that has been bleached. Its fronds are pale at the tips and it has folded itself in half.

The lichens are the animals' cousins, or their landlords. They do the same thing in a different tempo — hold to the stone, take the light, take the dew, give nothing back but color — and they do it at a pace that makes the animals look hasty. The blue one, in particular, in a seam on the north wall, is older than the boulder. You can tell because the boulder, which fell in from the rim some century or so ago, has the beginning of that blue on its upper face, a patch the size of a coin, and the patch on the wall is the size of a door.

The boulder also has holes. Six of them, on its top and its lit side, none deeper than a thumb-joint, three of them occupied by finger-length animals who have chosen, out of all the wall, to begin here. In a century they have got this far. The walls are a thousand times that deep. You do the arithmetic and stop.

The moth comes from the plain and does not belong. Every night, one moth. Perhaps the same one, perhaps not. It circles the glow that never quite goes out and it cannot find the source because there is no source, and toward dawn it lands on the stone, exhausted, and in the morning it is gone — flown, or dried to a fleck, or taken by the one thing you never see, which is the possibility that the animals, in the dark, with their eyes open, do eat after all.

---

And the one who came down.

Not an inhabitant. But the hand-hollows on the northwest wall are holes of the same kind — made by a body meeting stone, again and again, over a time the body itself did not measure — and they are spaced the way the animals' holes are spaced, at the distance of a limb, and they are polished at the lip. Whoever came down came down often enough to leave that. The cup on the ledge is a hole worn on purpose, which the others never are, but it does the same work: it holds the night's water until the day takes it back.

You do not know what they wanted. You can guess at what they got. To sit on the floor of this place in the full of the light, with the walls alive around you and no sound and no shadow, eyes shut because the eyes will not stay open — to sit like that for an afternoon is to feel yourself become one more surface for the light to press against, one more pale thing at the edge of the hollow, not drinking exactly but not entirely dry either. The flake on the floor is very old and very good and was not dropped by accident. Something was cut, or finished, or begun. Then the hand let go of it, and the light closed over it, and the patina took a thousand years to come.

They stopped coming. The hollows are weathering back. In some centuries they will be shallow enough to mistake for the animals' work, and then shallow enough to mistake for nothing, and the pit will be one age simpler.

---

So: a wall of small white bodies, each in the hole it has made of itself, pressed flat between the stone and the light, eyes shut all day and open all night, drinking one fluid and then the other, never touching, dying by staying out, becoming the rock that holds the next one. A tree that has decided to be a root. Lichens keeping a longer time. A fern in the only fold of shade. One moth, wrong. The memory of hands, going.

And the pit itself, which lives in the sense that a lung lives — by being emptied and filled — and which will go on doing so long after the last of the honeycomb has worn back into a bowl too shallow to keep anything, light or water or something the length of your hand, from simply running out over the plain and being lost.
