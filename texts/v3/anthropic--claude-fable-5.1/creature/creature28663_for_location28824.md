The people first, because they are the ones who would answer if you asked, and because from the rim they are the hardest thing to see.

There are something over four hundred of them, and they are not white, though everything else is. They are the colour of the mats in the runnels — the colour of whatever temperature they have spent their lives standing at. The pan-men, who work the shallow acres where the water is hottest and the glare is worst, are a dull deep red across the shoulders and the backs of the hands, a red that does not fade in the dormitories and is not exactly sunburn. The scalers, who work under the buildings and in the pipe-runs and see the sun only in slices, are a yellowish grey. The engine families are pale, almost the pale of the floor, from three generations behind stone walls. You can tell what a person does here by the colour they have gone, the way you can tell the temperature of a channel by the colour of what grows in it, and nobody finds this strange because nobody has ever seen it otherwise.

They are not large people. The basin does not feed anyone into largeness. What comes over the pass twice a month on the mule-track is flour and dried beans and tinned things and the shrimp are eaten but they are small and mostly shell, and the herons have a cleaner claim to them anyway. So the people are lean, and their hands are the biggest thing about them, and their hands are astonishing.

Everything here is done with the hands and very little of it with the fingertips, because the fingertips go first. The water finds the pads and the cuticles and the skin there thickens and cracks and heals white and cracks again, and by thirty a pan-man's fingers end in something more like hoof than skin, so the fine work — the telegraph key, the lamp filaments, the setting of a valve — passes to the young and to the engine families who have kept their hands out of the water. But the hands of the old are eloquent in a different way. The basin speaks in hand-signs, and the sign-language of the Works has been drifting on its own for a century now, so that it has words no one outside would recognise, words for things there is no other name for: the particular shiver of a walkway board that has gone from wood to ivory and is about to go from ivory to nothing; the smell that comes off a vent a week before it seals; the exact shade of orange that means a channel can be crossed barefoot and the exact shade that means it cannot. The signs are made low and small, at hip height, one hand, because both hands are usually needed for something else and because a large gesture in this light hurts to watch. An old scaler talking across a pan looks like a man idly flexing his fingers. A young one, still using both hands and lifting them to shoulder height, looks like what she is: someone from the outside, shouting.

They are mostly born here now. The Works were staffed from outside for the first two generations and then simply weren't, because people who had grown up in the quiet did not do well over the pass, in places where the air moved and dogs barked and doors slammed, and came back, and married each other. So there are children in the dormitory blocks, and the children are the only ones who have ever been loud, and not for long. A baby's cry in the basin is the most audible thing for kilometres — it travels the way nothing else does, thin and high over the steam — and the herons all turn their heads at once toward it, and then away, and the children learn very fast from the faces of the adults that this is not a thing you do. They sign before they speak. Many of them speak very little at all, ever, and when they do it is in the flat close voice of the Works, a voice for use across a table and no further, and the ones who leave and come back say that the worst of the outside was not the noise but the way people looked at you when you answered them: leaning in, saying what, saying sorry, as though you were ill.

Their eyes go, as the description says. The smoked glass slows it but does not stop it, and the glass itself scales over and has to be wiped every few minutes and then polished every night and replaced every year, and there is always a day you do not bother, because the light is beautiful and you are young. By forty most of them see the basin as a brightness with darker shapes in it, and by fifty many see very little, and this is when they become most valuable. The blind of the Works are the ones who know the ground. They walk the raised timber with a hand on the rail and can tell by the rail — by the thickness of the skin on it, by whether it is warm or merely hot, by whether it drips — how long since this stretch was re-planked and whether the vent it crosses is coming or going. They ream the mains by ear, listening to the pitch of the cutter change as it passes from stone into iron. They sit at the corners of the pans with their faces turned up and their eyes shut and read the day by the pressure of the light on their eyelids and the smell on the air, and the young pan-men come to them and put a hand on their shoulder, which is how you ask a blind man a question here, and the old man puts his hand up and signs into their palm, and the young one goes away and does what he is told. Nobody here retires. They move inward, from the pans to the walkways to the dormitory stoops to a chair in the turbine hall where the humming is, and eventually to the eastern knob, and then they go on being useful in a slower way, as a landmark.

That is the other thing about them: they are not afraid of the accretion. It would be easy to write them as a people fighting the stone, and they do fight it, twice a year with chisels under the sheds and every eight years on the walkways and every day on their own hands and glasses and boots, but they do not hate it, and they do not talk about it as an enemy. They talk about it as weather. The word they use, in sign, for the mineral skin is the same word they use for the passing of time, and it is a small closing gesture, two fingers drawing together, that means both it is getting late and this has thickened. A pan-man showing you a shovel that has gone to stone in a decade will turn it over in his hands with something like affection, the way a man elsewhere might show you a tree he had planted. When a dormitory block is given up to the white, they do not tear it down. They take out the iron and the glass and the people and leave the rest, and for a few years it is a building, and then a shape, and then a hump with a chimney, and the children are taken out to it and shown which of the older humps was the school and which was the first bath-house and which is the one nobody remembers, and they take this in the way children elsewhere take in that their grandparents are in the ground. Here you can watch it happen. Here the ground comes up to meet you at a rate you can measure with a ruler across a life.

So the graveyard is not a sad place, or not only. It is out on its knob toward the eastern rim, a broad low field of white mounds of different ages, the newest still rough with the loose cairn-stones showing through their skin, the oldest smooth as eggs and taller than a man, because the mineral does not stop when it has covered you. It goes on. In another two centuries the graveyard will be a single terrace and the people under it will be a layer in it and someone with a hammer could find them, but no one will, because by then there will be a newer field on a newer knob and this one will have become part of the floor that people walk on, which is what all the floor is. The families go out on the day after a burial and again after a year and again after ten, and on the tenth year the mound has closed and taken the shape, and they put a hand on it, and the water is running over it in its centimetre sheet, warm, and that is the closest thing the Works have to a rite.

Now the rest of what lives here, seen from inside the quiet instead of from the rim.

The herons are the great presence, and the people have a complicated relation with them that is neither ownership nor fear. Each pool that is the right temperature has its bird, and the birds do not share and do not fight, and a pool that loses its bird — because the vent upstream has changed and the water has gone a degree too hot or cold — is watched by the pan-men with real attention, because the birds know before the instruments do. When a heron leaves a pool the pan downstream is marked. When a heron comes to stand on the rail of a walkway that has never had one before, the walkway is checked. The workers do not feed them or name them, but the children know them individually by the pattern of scale on their legs, because the birds too are accreting — the water gets into the scutes and builds there, and an old heron's legs are thick and knobbed and white as stalactites, and it stands taller than a young one by the height of the stone it has grown. They do not seem to mind. They live a very long time. There is one on the far side of the lake that the oldest people say was there when they were children, and it stands now on legs like fence-posts and has not been seen to fly in living memory, and when the shrimp move it takes one step, very slowly, and the sound of that step — stone on stone, under the water — is one of perhaps a dozen sounds in the basin that everyone knows.

The stokers are the workers' other familiars, and here the relation is closer, because the stokers come to the pipe-runs. Something in the iron draws them — the warmth, perhaps, or the vibration of the pumps through the mains — and at dusk they climb out of the runnels and lie along the pipes in the last of the light with their frilled necks flattened against the metal, and the scalers working late will find a dozen of them along a single main like dark commas, and lift them off one by one to get at the flanges, and set them down on the walkway, where they wait, and climb back when the work is done. They are cold to hold, which is the strangest thing about them in a place where everything else is hot. They are cold and dry and heavy for their size, and the plate under their skin can be felt with a thumb, and the children who are afraid of nothing else in the basin do not like to touch them. The old people say the stokers were here before the water, which makes no sense, and say it anyway.

The thing in the lake the workers do not discuss, and so there is not much to say about it, except this: on the nights when the steam is thickest and the back surfaces at the far end, the herons all turn to face it, every one of them across the whole floor of the basin, and hold that position until it goes down, and then turn back to their pools. The people have seen this all their lives and have their own way of holding still when it happens. The lamp on the turbine hall is not changed during it. Shift-change waits.

The bat-things from the rim are counted in the way weather is counted; when they come low it will be a heavy night for condensation. The lizards on the mains are simply stepped over. The shelf-growth under the buildings, the not-fungus, is the one thing the workers treat as an adversary rather than as weather, because it is the only thing in the basin that seems to want something — that lifts, that pushes, that would take a shed off its footings and set it down a hand's width east if allowed — and the twice-yearly scraping of it is the nearest thing here to a festival, everyone under the buildings at once with chisels, the pale frilled plates coming away in sheets and dropping into the runnels where the shrimp cloud around them. What comes off is neither soft nor hard. It is warm on the underside and cool on the top. The children make things out of it for a few days until it dries and cracks. Nobody has ever found where it starts.

And under all of it, under the sinter, the ones who do not live here but are here anyway.

The humps with square corners that nobody remembers building are not all the Works'. There are shapes further out, toward the rim on the north and west sides, that are square in a different way — a different proportion, longer and lower, with something at one end that might have been a doorway facing the lake — and there are a great many more of them than there have been generations of the Works to build. The engineers know about them and do not go out to them. When a walkway has to be re-routed past one, the piles are driven wide. Once, in the grandfathers' time, a pan was cut too close to one and the cutting broke through into a space, and the space was still warm and was lined on the inside like the inside of a shell, smooth and white and faintly lit by the light coming through the crust, and there were things in it that had been laid down in the shape of the water and were the same colour as everything else, and the pan was moved, and the hole was left for the water to close, and it has closed. This is not told as a ghost story. There are no ghost stories in the Works, because nothing here is unquiet; whatever went into the ground went in the same way everything does, slowly, under warm water, becoming stone at the rate of a ruler across a life. The people who dug that pan and moved it are themselves on the eastern knob now, closed and smooth, and the ones under the older humps are deeper in the floor, and the herons stand over all of it at their intervals, and the shrimp cloud in the runnels that flow through what were kitchens.

So if you asked what lives here, and you asked at the rim, someone would point down at the buildings and say: the Works, the pan-men and the scalers and the engine families, four hundred and some, and the birds. And if you asked on the floor, with a hand on a shoulder, the answer that came back into your palm would be the small closing sign, two fingers drawing together — it is getting late, this has thickened — which is also, in the dialect of the basin, the word for us. Not the people alone. The whole slow accreting thing: the water and what grows in it and what eats that and what stands over that and what has laid the pipes to draw it off and what is under the pipes, all of it in the same light, all of it going quietly to stone at a pace it has agreed on, and no one in it raising their voice, because there has never once been anything to be heard over.
