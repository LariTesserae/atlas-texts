The first answer is: nothing. You will want to give that answer for the first hour, and it will feel accurate. The place is wet and green and loud and there is nothing in it that looks back at you.

The first answer is wrong in a particular way. Things live here. They are just slow enough that the difference between living in the fen and being part of the fen is not a difference the fen recognises. What lives here is what can weather. That is the whole of the admission rule. Anything that cannot hold its shape through a long enough rain does not stay, and the insects are the proof of this — a fen this wet should be a haze of them and it is not, because a midge lives a day, and the basin has no room for anything that lives a day. It cannot *hold* a day. The day would slip through it like water through a hand.

So begin with the smallest thing that has passed the test.

---

The snails are on the horsetail. They are on the underside of the willow leaves and in the wet crotches of the alders and on the weeping rocks themselves, just above the threads of water, where the liverwort ends. They are small, brown, glossed with wet, and the first thing you will notice about them is that they are ordinary. They are the only ordinary thing here. You will find yourself returning to them for that reason, the way you return to a familiar word in a page of a language you do not read.

Look longer. They all coil the same way, and it is the wrong way — the spiral opens to the left, every one of them, where a snail's shell opens right. There are no small ones. There are no large ones. They are all the size of the last joint of your little finger, and they are all the same shade of steeped-tea brown, and their shells are all worn to exactly the same matte softness at the apex, as if they had all been rained on for the same length of time. Which they have.

There are no dead shells. In a population this size there should be empties everywhere, bleached, in the moss, in the hollows between tussocks. There is not one. Whatever a snail does here at the end of being a snail, it does not leave a shell behind. It does not leave anything behind.

If you put your ear very close to a stem where one is feeding — close enough that the roar is muffled by your own skull — you can hear the rasp of it eating. It is a small, steady, papery sound, and it does not pause, and it is the only sound in the basin that is made by a mouth.

---

The eel is not an eel, but it is the shape water takes when water wants a body.

You will see it in the slower channels, where the sedge stands in open water and the surface is dark enough to be opaque. A long shape under the surface, moving against the drift. It is always moving against the drift. The drift here is barely a drift at all, a faint pull toward wherever the pools are refilling, and the shape moves the other way, at the same speed, so that the water and the thing in it are always exactly cancelling. Nothing on the surface disturbs.

Follow it. This is hard, because it is under the surface and the surface is dark, but there is a way of unfocusing that makes it easier, the same unfocusing that lets you see the pattern in the moss. Followed this way, the eel goes where the water goes at grey noon when the pools drop. It slides under the tussocks. It goes into the peat — not into a hole in the peat, *into the peat*, the way a thread goes into cloth — and the peat closes over it and the channel behind it is a hand's breadth lower than it was.

Once, near the end of the long afternoon, it turns beneath a pool that reflects a copper sky, and for a moment it is visible against that brightness from below, and it has a face. The face is not an eel's. It is long and closed and calm, and the eyes are shut, and it turns away before you can be sure of anything except that it was not looking at you and had no need to.

---

Under the sedge, the sleeper.

You have felt the swell — the thirty feet of ground that rises and settles as though a back had rolled over beneath it — and you will feel it again, and the third time you will notice what the roar does when it happens. The roar does not change. That is what the description says and it is nearly true. What happens is that the roar *thickens*, the way a crowd's noise thickens when the crowd shifts its weight, and then the ground rises, and then the ground settles, and then the roar is what it was. Once you have caught this you cannot uncatch it. The roar is not underneath the sleeper. The roar is the sleeper, heard through the peat, and it is not the sound of one thing breathing.

Or it is. Here is the difficulty, and it is the same difficulty as the alder trunk that lies across the same channel in four places. The swell comes in different parts of the basin. It comes far to your left, and then a long while later it comes to your right, and once it comes almost under you, so that the tussock you are standing on lifts and your knees bend to take it, and each time it is thirty feet long, and each time the roar thickens in the same way, and there is no reason to think these are different sleepers and no reason to think they are the same one and the fen does not treat this as a question.

Nothing here breeds. You will have gathered this from the snails. Nothing here breeds and nothing here dies in a way that leaves remains, and yet the basin is populated, and the method is not reproduction. The method is recurrence. A thing that has passed the test — that has held its shape long enough — is not multiplied but *repeated*, the way a word is repeated, and each repetition is the original, and the originals lie under the sedge in the dozens or the hundreds, and turn over sometimes, and the sound of all of them at rest is the roar.

What the sleeper is, under the peat: something very long, very heavy, very patient, that lay down here before there was peat to lie under, and let the fen grow over it, and is dreaming the water's routes. You do not see it. You are not meant to. It is not hiding. It is simply beneath, in the way that a foundation is beneath, and the water that runs uphill toward the pools is running along the lines of it.

---

The heron does not belong here, and that is what it is for.

Everything else in the basin moves at the fen's speed, which is the speed of weathering. The snails eat at it. The eel slides at it. The sleeper turns at it. The heron stands at it — hours motionless, grey on grey, the same broken barb on the same primary — and then the heron stabs, and the stab is fast. It is the only fast thing in the basin. It is a knife through cloth. It is over before you have seen it start, and then the heron is standing again and has something dark and thrashing in its beak and the dark thing goes into the heron and is not, afterwards, anywhere.

Look at the barb. Something broke it. Nothing here breaks; nothing here has a raw edge; the whole basin is a balance of falling and rotting and growing that leaves no fresh wound anywhere. The barb is a wound. It is old, it is worn soft, but it is a wound, and it means the heron came from a place where feathers can be broken, which is to say a place with wind and weather and other birds. It came in from outside. It stood at the edge of a pool. It stayed.

It has been repeating ever since. You see it in clearing after clearing because the fen does with it what the fen does with anything that has stayed long enough to hold its shape: it repeats it. Each heron is the heron. Each heron faces the same way, and if you go to enough clearings you will find that the way is *inward*, toward some centre of the basin that the paths will not take you to.

The croak arrives before the beak opens. You will hear this several times and each time it will be a half-second early, and you will decide that either the heron is a little ahead of the fen or the fen is a little behind the heron, and it will occur to you that this is the sound of something that has not yet finished arriving. The heron has been here for as long as the peat. It is also still, in some small stubborn remainder, on its way.

What it eats: the brief things. Whatever comes into the basin that lives at the speed of a day — that falls in, blows in, hatches by some accident in a pool the fen has not yet noticed — the heron takes. Not out of hunger. The heron is the fen's edit. It stands where the brief things surface and it removes them, and the air over the water stays clean, and the roar has no living voice in it.

Except its own. Once, dry, a half-second early.

---

Now the one that lives here.

Not the snails, though they live here. Not the eel, which is the water's. Not the sleeper, which is the ground's, and not the heron, which is a guest that has been kept. There is one inhabitant in the sense you meant when you asked, and it is the reason two people twenty paces apart are alone.

You will see it at twenty paces. Never closer. Never, once you have seen it, further. It stands in the gap between two alder stands at the limit of what the vegetation lets you see, and it faces away from you, and it is not the heron, though for a long moment you will think it is — the greyness, the stillness, the posture of attention at the edge of a pool. It is taller than the heron. It is roughly the height of a person, or of what you remember a person being, out there where distance measures distance. It is wearing something, or is the colour of something, that is green in the way the fibrous shred caught in the split bark of the fourfold trunk is green: a green that has been rained on until the rain and the green are the same age.

Its hand is out. That is the one detail you will be sure of. The right hand, extended, palm down, a little above the surface of the pool, not touching. It holds the hand there the way you held your palm on the tussock to feel the roar come up through the fibres, and it does not move, and you understand that it is listening. Something in the basin has to be. All that sound — the weir that is not there, the water falling from no height, the walking thunder, the crowd through the wall — is going *somewhere*, is being *heard*, and this is where.

Under its hand the water is lighter. You will have noticed already that the pools give back more light than the sky offers, that the fen is the source and the cloud the receiver. Stand at the edge of one of these pools and your shadow lies on the underside of the sky. The pool under its hand is the lightest in the basin. The light does not come from the water. It comes from what the water is being attended to *by*. Where the inhabitant's attention rests, the fen brightens, and the fen is bright everywhere, faintly, because the inhabitant has been attending for so long that its attention has soaked in, like the too-heavy water, and is now simply a property of the place.

It does not turn around. You will wait for it to. It has no reason to. What is behind it — you, the alders you started from, the direction you came — is not where the listening is.

You can try to approach it. This is the last of the rules. Walk toward it. You will walk. The sedge, the hand's depth of water, the peat that gives some of the weight back. The alders you are walking toward will arrive, and be very like the alders you picked out, and the gap between them will be there, and the figure will be in the gap, at twenty paces, facing away, hand out. It is not retreating. You are not failing to advance. There is simply one span of twenty paces in the basin, and it is the span between you and it, and the basin is made of that span repeated in every direction for what is probably a day's walk. The paths return you because the paths are the space around the one who stands still. You are not being kept out. You are being kept at the exact distance from which it can be seen and not heard, which is the distance the fen allots to a presence.

What it is: the one who did the engineering, or the one who was left when the engineering was done. There is no way to tell these apart any more, and you should distrust the question, because the fen has weathered it into the same shape as the question of whether the rocks were made. Say instead: it is what the water slowed down for. There is no high ground here, only places where the water is a little slower, and the water is slowest at its feet, and has been slow there long enough that the peat beneath it has a smooth shallow bowl in it exactly as deep as it needs to be.

It has been standing here for longer than the alders, longer than the sleeper has been asleep, longer than it took the rocks to lose their seams. It came, or it was, and it put its hand out over the water to listen, and the fen grew up around the listening and through it and treats it as it treats any other fixture — as something to root against, to drip past, to route the water around.

It is not a keeper. Nothing here needs keeping. It is not a god; the basin does not need one, and the figure is not large, and it does not do anything. It is a habit that has a location. It is the fen's one act of attention, held so long that it has become the fen's weather.

---

Toward what you take to be evening the thunder comes more often and the rain begins to fall from the bright place in the west, and the bright place is where the figure stands. The rain falls on it. It does not move. The drizzle drifts slantwise across its shoulders and its extended hand and the pool under the hand brightens further and begins, very faintly, to reflect a sky that is not the one above it — very clear, very close, unfamiliar stars — and its shadow, which you can only just make out, lies upward on the underside of the felt lid of cloud, and is the shadow of someone with one hand out.

You notice that your hands have been the temperature of the water for some time. You notice that you are standing at the edge of a pool. You notice that you have not moved for a while, and that you have been facing the same direction, and that the direction is inward, and that your hand is out.

Twenty paces behind you, at exactly the distance where a person would be alone, someone has stopped walking.

You do not turn around. You have no reason to. What is behind you is not where the listening is.
