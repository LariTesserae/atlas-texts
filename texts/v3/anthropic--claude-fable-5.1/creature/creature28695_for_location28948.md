The people first, because they are the ones who will speak to you, and because at first they seem to be the answer.

There are perhaps two hundred of them. Forty roofs, five to a roof, give or take a grandmother. They have the look of people who have been kept under glass — not unhealthy, but soft-edged, pale in the way of things that have never been fully dry, with a faint pink flush at the knuckles and the sides of the neck where the damp has been working longest. The pads of their fingers are permanently wrinkled, as yours would be after a long bath, and they don't notice this until they shake hands with a barge-man and feel the difference. Between the toes, in the crook of the elbow, behind the ears, most of them carry a fine reddish bloom that anywhere else would be a complaint. Here it has the same name as what the iron does. It ferns. A child who ferns badly is kept near the engine for a week, where the air at least moves, and then goes back to the gangways and ferns again, and nobody thinks of this as a failure of the cure.

They move slowly and you take this for temperament until you have been there a day and understand it as engineering. Hurrying makes heat, heat makes sweat, and sweat here has nowhere to go: it stays on you, and the air lays more on top, and by evening a man who hurried is wearing a sleeve of his own water. So they don't. They have a gait for the gangways — weight low, foot placed flat, a small pause before committing — which is the gait of people walking on something that has given way before. They know which planks are soft the way you know which stair creaks. They step over the cane where it comes up through the boards and do not look down.

---

Their hands are the most worked thing about them and they are worked differently by trade.

The cutters have the hook-hand, a grip that has closed so many thousand times around the same curve of handle that it no longer fully opens; they hold a cup as though it were a tool. Their forearms are scored with old white lines from the leaf-edges, which are keen as paper and about as forgiving, and their shoulders have a groove — an actual dent in the muscle, left and right, a hand's width apart — where the bundle rides. They go out along the strips at first light, walking on the mat itself, which takes their weight the way a mattress would, and they come back at mid-morning with the day's cut in a rick on their backs, dripping, so that each cutter arrives in the works at the centre of a small local rain.

The roller-men have no hands to speak of. That is, they have them, but the hands have been taught so completely to stay outside a certain line that they hold themselves there even at rest, even in sleep; you will see a roller-man at his own table with his fingers curled back from the edge as if the edge might turn. Their job is to keep the feed clean and the bundles square and never, in forty years, to be where the ram is going, and the ones you meet have done this, and the ones you don't meet are the reason the beam is cupped and not something worse.

The tank-women — it is women, mostly, and nobody explains why — have the long soft hands of people who work in liquid, and their hands smell so permanently of the retting that they can no longer smell it and can smell only its absence: they say a barge-man's hand smells of nothing, and mean it as a mild disgust. They know the liquor by its skin. They can tell by the colour and slowness of a bubble how many days a tank has to go. This is not recorded anywhere and could not be taught to you.

The moulders in the low wing have the resin in the creases of their palms and under their nails, amber, permanent, and when they close a fist there is a faint tack to it.
