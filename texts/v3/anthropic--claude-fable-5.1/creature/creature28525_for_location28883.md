Start with the smallest, because they are most of it.

Just under the living skin, in the hand's-breadth of recently dead, the rind is riddled with galleries no wider than a finger. These belong to the borers. They are long, segmented, the colour of wet paper, with a head that is mostly jaw — two curved blades of a hard amber material that the people prise out and set into resin as scrapers. A borer spends its whole life in the layer it hatched in, eating forward, packing the tunnel behind it with what it has passed. It never sees the surface. It never sees anything. When the plant lays down a new skin over the top of it, it simply keeps going, a little deeper than before, and its galleries are carried down with everything else. The ticking you hear when you press your ear to the rind — that dry, uneven, close-in ticking — is millions of amber jaws in millions of tunnels, each one taking one bite.

Below the borers, in the denser stratum where the fibre has gone dark and begun to soften, live the singers.

They are small and soft and pale, with legs too fine to see in poor light, and they are never alone. A colony fills a region of the pith the way roots fill a pot — tens of thousands of chambers joined by passages, and at the heart of it the gardens, where chewed fibre is laid out in beds and a white fungus is raised on it and eaten. The fungus needs the wet, so the gardens are dug where the plant's condensation seeps, and the wet is why the colonies are where they are and not somewhere else, and the drone is how they know it.

Every colony has a pitch. Every member of the colony makes it, continuously, by drawing a ridged plate across another ridged plate, and the sound fills the colony's chambers the way a held note fills a room. A worker carrying fibre down a passage in the total dark orients by the note — by whether it is getting louder or softer, by whether the neighbouring colony's slightly different note is creeping in from the left. Where two colonies meet, the notes beat against each other, and the beating is the border. There are thousands of colonies under every square kilometre, and each one is holding its note, and the low unvarying floor of the roar, the part that never swells or ebbs, is all of them at once. It has never stopped. It has not stopped in the whole life of the plant. When the plant grows over itself the colonies move up, chamber by chamber, abandoning the deep gardens to rot; the rot is what turns the dead layers black; the black layers are what the plant stands on. The singers are not on the plant. In a real sense they are what it is made of.

Then the sap channels. These run vertically through everything, hair-thin near the surface and wide as a wrist deeper down, carrying the plant's sweetness up from wherever it comes from. Things live in the sweetness. They are blind, without pigment, shaped like ribbons or threads, and they move with the sap, up, feeding on it, in a current so slow that a single one may take a season to rise the height of a house. Where they end up is the crowns of the stalks. The bulb at the top of a stalk is where the channels open into a chamber and the sap pools and the swimmers are strained out of it and breed, and on the hottest afternoons the crowns weep a thin sticky rain that smells of green stems and is full of eggs. When the people drive a hollow spine into a soft place and the sap comes, there is often a white thread in it, still moving. They drink it down. It is considered lucky, or at least not unlucky.

And below all that, where the light does not go and where the people's knowledge does not go either, the drowned galleries. Chambers the plant has hollowed and abandoned, flooded with condensation that has been falling into them for longer than the top skin has existed. Something lives there that whistles. The whistle is long, descending, and comes up through metres of pith with the pitch smeared off it, and nobody has seen what makes it. The people say — with a flat hand pressed to the rind, then drawn slowly down — that it is the water's. That the water down there is large enough to have a voice. This is not a belief exactly. It is what the hand says, and the hand is usually right.

---

The vents are where the inside comes out, and what comes out is one animal at two ages.

In the warm wet chambers under a vent rim, in the layer the borers have already opened up, there are grubs — fat, translucent, the length of a thumb, with the beginnings of wings folded under the skin like folded paper. These are what the people hook out of the cracks. They taste of sap. They are most of the diet. Left alone they harden in the fibre and lie still for a season, and then, on an afternoon when the surface fever peaks and the interior can no longer hold its breath, the whole cohort under a vent splits its casing at once and climbs toward the light along the same passages, and the throat begins to smoke.

Once up, they have one evening. They mate in the column, in the twisting updraft, and the pairs fall out of the rope in a spiral and land on the rind still joined. The males die where they land. The females walk — you can watch them, a shining stagger across the tobacco-coloured swells — until they find a crack, and put their bodies into it as far as they will go, and lay, and die there with their wings sticking up out of the crack like tiny sails. By morning the wings are all that is left on the surface. The eggs are inside. They will hatch into grubs that eat their way down toward the wet, and be hooked out and eaten, or not, and in their season climb back up, and so the vents have smoked for longer than the people can count. A person here eats the swarm at both ends of its life and thinks nothing of it. There is nothing else to think.

---

The grazers you have seen: the arches of dun plate unfolding from cracks at last light. Understand what they are doing.

The wind-polished trails on the plain are theirs. A trail runs from a vent to a cup to another vent, and a grazer walks it because its mother walked it, and the trail has been walked for so long that it is a shallow groove worn into rind that heals a little each cycle and is worn again. They do not see well because they do not need to. They navigate by the drone — the pitch of the colonies under them, which changes along the trail in a fixed sequence that a grazer knows the way you know the stairs in your own house in the dark. Put one down on unfamiliar rind and it will stand still for a night, turning slowly, listening through its feet, until it finds a note it recognises and walks toward it.

They eat the dead skin and the fallen swarm and, at the cups, they drink — lowering the front arch until the mouth is in the water, and holding still, and the level in the cup going visibly down. Their young are small flat things that live in the cracks and eat what the borers leave. Their dead lie on the surface until the plant flows over them; sometimes a crack will show you a plate, half-glazed, a curve of dun in the paler fibre like a rib in a cliff. The people do not kill them. There would be no point; they are made almost entirely of plate, and the plate comes up on its own when they die. What the people take is the plate. Every knife handle, every door, every hook that holds a house to a stalk, is some part of a grazer that finished its trail a long time ago.

If you lie still at night they will step over you. The underside of one is soft and warm and smells of cut stems and passes above your face like a low cloud with legs. They are not thinking about you. They are listening to the ground.

---

The shadow.

It comes from over the curve, in the middle of the day, when nothing else moves. It is silent. It is not cloud, because it keeps its own darkness with it and the cloud ceiling is above it, not below. It is not the swarm, because the swarm shrieks and this does not. Under it the light goes to the colour of dusk and the heat lifts a little, and the drone — you will feel this before you hear it — drops in pitch along the whole width of its path, as if every colony beneath it had leaned away.

Where it has passed, the cups are empty. Not dry; empty, the glossy bowls holding nothing, and it takes them a night to sweat full again. The vents under its track do not smoke that afternoon. The grazers do not come out along its line. Once in a long while it passes low enough that a person standing at the edge of its shade can look up into it and see, before it moves on, a texture — a hanging fringe, or the underside of something folded, or a very great many small things holding on to each other. The accounts are not consistent. The people do not have a knock for it. They have one gesture: the flat hand drawn slowly across the eyes, once, and then the hand put away.

That is all. You were told nobody would tell you. Nobody has.

---

Now the people.

They are not tall. The air up here is thin and the ones who thrive are broad in the chest and short in the leg, with a way of standing that puts the weight low, because the ground gives and it is easier to keep balance if you are already close to it. Their skin has gone the colour of the rind, or of the rind's shadow, from the hard white light; their eyes are narrow from a lifetime of squinting at glare. But it is the hands you notice. The hands are thick through the palm and heavily scarred — resin burns, mostly, white smooth patches where hot gum has set on skin — and at the wrist of one hand, always, a striker: a knob of hardened resin on a cord, worn the way you would wear a watch.

The striker is the voice. A person's name is a knock — two strokes, or three, in a rhythm, on the rind or on the stave of a house, and the rind carries it in a way it will not carry a shout. Children are named by the sound their mothers make when they are called, which is to say the name comes first and the child grows into it. A child learns to knock before it learns to speak, and many never learn to speak very much; the throat is for the few things that must be said close, in the dark, with a mouth against an ear. Everything else is hands and striker. There are gestures for the vents and their seasons, for the cups and their sweetness, for the grazers and their trails, for wet and dry and the two kinds of heat, for the layers under the skin down to the third, beyond which there is only the gesture for *down*. Two people arguing at a distance look like they are trying to hit each other. Two people who love each other, at a distance, look like the same thing slowed.

They hear. They hear better than you do, in the only way that matters here: they hear *inside* the drone. Where you hear a roar they hear a vent beginning to hold its breath a day before it smokes, a colony under the house shifting up a layer, a grazer approaching on its trail and which grazer it is. An old woman will put her palm to the rind in the morning and tell you with three gestures what the afternoon will do. She is listening to the singers. She has been listening to the singers since before she could walk, and the singers have not stopped.

They live far apart because there is not enough in any one place to feed more than a few. A stalk, a vent within a day's walk, a cup within a night's — that is a household. The house stands off the rind on plate legs, roofed in husk, walled in twisted pith, lashed with cord. In the flank of the vent rim there is the hollow, the cool room chipped into living fibre, where the household sleeps in the worst of the heat and where the walls are marked. The marks are made by pressing a resin-blackened palm to the sweating wall, and each generation presses over the last, and the plant heals over the oldest, and so the wall is a slow tide of hands going down into glaze. Nobody says what the marks are for. You put your hand on the wall. That is what the wall is for.

They eat grubs, mostly, hooked from the cracks at dawn when the cracks are breathing out and the grubs come up toward the warm. They eat the swarm when it falls, gathered from the rind before the grazers wake, roasted on husk-fires until the wings crisp. They eat the sap when they can reach it, and the reaching is an occasion: the spine driven in, the wait, the first clear bead, the household gathering to put a finger to it in order of age. They drink from the cups and from the dawn dew off the stalks, and they do not drink from the rain, because the rain does not stay.

They do not count far. The counting stops at the number of fingers on both hands, and then there is the gesture for *many*, and then there is the gesture for *skin*. A skin is one laying-over — the long cycle in which the plant grows a new surface across the old — and it takes longer than a childhood but not as long as a life. An old person has seen two. She remembers when the ground stood lower, when the crack in front of the house was somewhere else, when the trail the grazers walk ran a hand's width to the left. She can put her hand on the rind and say: under here, this far down, is the ground I was born on. This is what age means here. You have seen the place become another place while remaining exactly itself.

The dead go down.

Not far — a body is laid in the deepest crack within reach, at dusk, when the crack is breathing in. Nothing is said. The household knocks, once, all together. Within a day the plant has begun to seal the wound with amber gum, and within a season the rind has flowed across, and the person is in the second layer with the borers and then the third with the singers and then — the gesture — down. The people do not talk about where they go after that, because they can hear where they go. It is under the house. It is in the drone. When a child is frightened by the roar at night, which happens, an adult will take the child's hand and press it flat to the rind and hold it there until the child stops being frightened, and this is not a lesson and it is not a comfort in words. It is that the child has been made to feel who is under there. Everyone. Everyone who was ever here, going down, in the warm, singing to keep their bearings.

---

Between the households, sometimes, a walker.

There are people who do not stay. They know the trails the grazers know and they walk them, a day between stalks, carrying cord and resin and plate and, more than anything, knocks — news, in the form of rhythms learned at one house and struck out at the next. A vent has gone quiet. A cup has cracked. Someone has died and someone has been named. The walker sits in the lee of the stalk and eats the household's grubs and strikes the news into a stave, and the household strikes back its own, and in the morning the walker is gone across the swells toward a vertical that does not seem to grow. This is how the plateau knows itself, insofar as it does. Slowly, by percussion, at walking pace.

Walkers know the shadow best, because they are most often under open sky. They are the ones who have seen the fringe. They are the ones who make the gesture and put the hand away.

---

So: what lives here.

Amber jaws taking one bite. Colonies holding one note for longer than there have been ears to hear it. Blind threads riding the sweetness up to weep from the crowns. Whatever whistles in the drowned dark. A single animal that spends its life underneath and its one evening in the sky. Slow arches walking grooves worn by their grandmothers, listening to the floor. Something that passes over and takes the water with it. And a few thousand people with scarred hands and strikers at their wrists, spread across a million square kilometres of warm rind, knocking to each other across the roar to say the night is starting, the ground is warm, everything is as it was.

And under them, under all of them, in the layers going down — the older galleries, the abandoned gardens gone to black, the plates and staves and handprints and the bodies laid in cracks at dusk — everything that lived here before, still present, still carried, still part of the ground. The plant does not lose anything. It grows over it. The inhabitants of the plateau are not only the ones on the surface. They are the whole column, from the living skin to wherever the whistling comes from, all at once, and the roar is the sound of them.

You will feel it, lying down. You have already begun. Your palm is flat on the rind and you did not decide to put it there. Someone knocks from the lee of the stalk and, without thinking, you find you have knocked back — twice, on the warm ground, with the heel of your hand — and the rind has carried it, and somewhere under you a colony has shifted very slightly in its note, and you are, for tonight, one of the things that lives here.
