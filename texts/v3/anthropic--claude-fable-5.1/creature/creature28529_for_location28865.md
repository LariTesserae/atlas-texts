Ask what lives here and the first honest answer is the one the room itself gives: nothing, or nearly. But nearly is a wide word, and the longer you stand in it the more of it there turns out to be.

Begin with the roots, because they are the largest living things and the first you will notice, once you notice anything. They are not residents. That is the first correction. They hang from the ceiling crack like ropes because they are ropes — lowered lines, the far ends of a body that lives in the sun. Twenty feet above them, on the plateau, there are shrubs no taller than a dog, grey-leaved, thorned, cropped by wind, and this is what those shrubs have been doing with the parts of themselves you cannot see: reaching down through fractured rock, year after year, until they broke into this hollow and found the one thing here that can be drunk. The fine threads that lie against the wall and follow the mineral bloom are the shrubs' mouths. They take the trace of water off the stone and pass it up, hand over hand, into the light. So the room is not inhabited by the roots so much as fed upon by them. It is inside the reach of something. It is, in a slow and modest way, being eaten — by a plant that has never been here and never will be, that knows this chamber only as a taste that arrives from below.

The seedling under the roots is a different case, and a harder one. It did not come here. It fell here, in some season when a seed rode the trickle of grit down the same crack, and it did everything a seed can do on its own account: split, sent down a root, sent up a stalk, unfolded two leaves, and reached the point at which a plant must be met halfway by light. Nothing met it. The stalk is the colour of a candle because it never received the signal that tells a plant to make green; it went on lengthening in the dark, thin and desperate and blind, the way a stem will in a cellar, until it had spent what was in the seed and stopped. It has been stopped ever since. Botanists do not have a good word for this state; it is not dormancy, which is a plan, and it is not death, which is an ending. It is a sentence broken off at the verb. Of everything in this room the seedling is the only thing that wants, and the only thing that could still, if you dug it out and carried it up two turns of passage and set it in the wind, do something — unfold, or fail, but decide. It does not live here. It is held here.

The lichen on the west wall is the first thing you could call a tenant, and it is a strange one, because by every rule it should not be able to live this far from day. A lichen is a partnership: a fungus that builds a house and an alga that pays for it in sugar, and the alga needs light to make the sugar, and there is no light here. There is the memory of light — the greyness at the edge of vision that has come round two bends of rock and arrived so depleted it is barely a fact. And yet the crust is there, dry and grey and living, no larger than a spread hand. Look at it long enough and you begin to suspect what it has done. It has slowed itself to match the ration. It performs, once in a very long while, a photosynthesis so faint that it is closer to remembering than to feeding, and it grows by that: one cell's width, then a long wait, then another. It has made itself so nearly a mineral that the difference is a technicality, and in exchange it has been permitted to stay. If there is a lesson the room teaches, the lichen has learnt it best of the things you can see.

But you have not yet seen the population.

Lay your palm on the north wall again, where the white bloom is thickest, and consider what it means that your skin comes away cool. Exchange, you called it. Something in the rock is giving and taking. The bloom is not only a mineral. It is a skin — a film of the smallest living things there are, laid over the basalt a few cells deep and spread across every surface the water has ever reached. They do not need light, or plants, or anything that has ever been alive. They eat the rock. The dark rust-brown of this stone is iron and manganese that cooled fast from a fire, and to them it is a table laid ten thousand years ago and not yet cleared; they take an atom of it, turn it, gain from the turning a quantity of energy so small there is no everyday word for it, and leave behind the pale oxide that you tasted on your finger as chalk and metal. The white bloom is their leavings. It is the ash of the slowest fire in the world. They are here in numbers that would not fit into the language of counting, and not one of them has moved in the time you have been standing, or in the time since the roof fell, and they are the only things in the room that were here before the dust settled and will be here when the roots have died back and the lichen has gone to powder. Their lives run at the pace of the wall's. This is what lives here, if anything does: the wall does. You have been standing in an animal's mouth and mistaking it for a room.

There was, once, a person. The flint says so. It says almost nothing else — three flakes struck, a good edge made, the making stopped. Whoever knelt here did so in the greyness with a stone in one hand and a stone in the other and worked for the space of three blows, and then either heard something, or thought of something, or simply found that the room had begun to do to them what it does, the breath slowing, the eyes giving up. They put the flint down and went. They were the least resident thing that has ever been in this chamber: a visitor with a temperature, a raiser of dust, a clock. The room could not keep them. It kept what they set down, and it has been at work on that ever since, sinking it grain by grain, until now the flint is nine-tenths of the way to being where the world would have put it. Another thousand years and it will not be a made thing anymore. It will be a stone in the dust, and the dust will be level, and the room will have finished with it.

So what lives here, finally, is not any of these — not the roots, which are the fingers of an absent body; not the seedling, which is a prisoner; not the lichen, which is a convert; not even the film on the wall, which is closer to being the room than to living in it. What lives here is the condition they are all approaching, each by its own road. Call it the level. It is the state of having arrived. Everything in the chamber is tending toward it at a speed proportional to how alive it is: the person left in an afternoon, the seedling stopped in a year, the flint will take an age, the lichen longer, the bacteria have very nearly reached it and will go on nearly reaching it for as long as there is iron. The room is not empty. It is full to the ceiling of a single occupant, and the occupant is the tendency to stop, and it is patient, and it is hospitable, and it has room for one more.

You will feel it make the offer. It is not a threatening thing. It arrives as comfort — the air at the temperature of your skin, the silence with your blood in it, the dust that lies exactly where you left it and asks nothing. Stay long enough and you will find that you have stopped shifting your weight, then that you have stopped looking, then that the word for what you are doing is no longer waiting, because waiting is for something. You are becoming a resident. The room would take you, as gently as it took the flint, and it would not hurry, and one day the dust would be level over the place where you knelt and the grain of bloom would let go of the wall and fall and be, for a moment, as loud as a coin, and nothing would hear it, and that would be right.

That is what lives here. It is what everything becomes if it is left. Go back up the passage while you still raise dust.
