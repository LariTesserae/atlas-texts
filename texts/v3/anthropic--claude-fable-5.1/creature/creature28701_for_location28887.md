What lives on the shelf begins in the soil, so begin there.

Put your arm into the black sponge to the elbow and you are in something more alive than most forest floors are — a half-metre of leaf-fall that never dries and never freezes and never quite finishes rotting, threaded so densely with white filament that a handful pulled apart hangs together like wet felt. The fungus is the oldest tenant. It came up the wall as spore on some wind that reached this height once in a thousand years, found rock that was warm and water that would not stop, and has been spreading laterally ever since, so that by now the whole shelf is very probably one organism underfoot, or a few enormous ones touching. The trees are wired into it. When a tree on the eastern benches is struck by the one thing that still strikes here — old age — the trees a kilometre away seem to know, and put out leaf. Nobody has proved this. It is simply the kind of thing you come to expect.

In the sponge itself: worms the colour of raw meat and the length of a forearm, blind, cool to the touch in a soil that is body-warm, so that finding one is like finding a hand. Beetles that have lost their wing-covers because nothing has fallen on them in ten thousand generations. Land crabs — actual crabs, seven kilometres above the sea — pale and small-eyed, whose ancestors must have come up in the rootballs of something, and who now live their whole lives in the tunnels the water makes, and hold their claws up at you in the seeps like objections.

---

Above the soil, the forest has done what forest does when it is given time and nothing else: it has stacked itself.

The epiphytes are the population. On a single great trunk you can count more species than in a hectare of lowland, and they are not visitors — they are the trunk, effectively, a metre thick in places, with their own soil, their own worms, their own frogs. Bromeliads the size of bathtubs hold water that has been standing in them, refreshed and re-refreshed, since before the seedling they grow on was a seedling. Things live in that water that live nowhere else on earth: a translucent salamander with no eyes and a gold spot where the eyes would be; damselflies that never leave the plant they were hatched in; a moss that is also, on inspection, a kind of animal.

The frogs are the loudest thing and the most numerous and the most changed. Down in the valleys their relatives are brown and secretive and breed once a year in whatever ditch the rains fill. Here they are every colour a leaf can be and several it cannot, and they breed continuously, and some of them have learned the circuit. The rim frogs — small, black, with a seep-white belly — lay their eggs not in pools but in the plumes: they climb out onto the wet rock at the very edge, where the streams go over and hang, and lay into the mist itself, and the mist carries the eggs back up and in through the capillaries, and the tadpoles develop in the dark inside the basalt, swimming upward in water that flows upward, and emerge weeks later from the seeps on the inner faces, already half-frog, already calling. There is no other place a life cycle like that could exist. It exists here because the place has been reliable for longer than the frog has been a frog.

The birds walked. That is the short version. Whatever they were when they arrived — blown up, most likely, storm-carried, a few exhausted passerines at a time over ten thousand years — they have given up the sky by degrees, because the sky here has a ceiling and nothing on the shelf has ever chased them into it. They are heavy now, glossy, thick-legged, and they move through the mid-canopy along the liana-bridges with the deliberateness of something that has never once fallen. Some have kept their wings as a kind of umbrella. Their eggs are laid in the bromeliad water, where they float. Their songs are long and complicated and unfinished-sounding, as if there has always been time to add another phrase, and there has.

The hyrax-things are the ones you will meet first, because they are curious and because they are everywhere in the lower canopy, dense-furred, round, with the small hard faces of animals that expected cold. The fur is a memory. Their ancestors came up the wall wearing it and found a place where it was never needed, and the place did not select against it fast enough to matter, so they have it, and they overheat gently all day, and lie flat on the warm stones in the afternoon rain with their mouths open, and seem perfectly content. They eat the epiphytes. They will eat from your hand within an hour of meeting you and forget you within a day.

The tapir-things are the ones who wore the grooves. Small, dark, unafraid, with a habit of walking the same path around the same tree for years, in single file, at dusk, for reasons that are not about food. The groove around the largest of the eastern trees is knee-deep and older than the tree's lowest branches. If you watch long enough you will see that they always circle one way, and that the direction is the direction the water turns at the rim. Whether they know this, or whether it is simply the way things go round here, is not a question the shelf answers.

And the snails: white, slow, hand-sized, the only pale things in a landscape of green and black and rain. They are eaten for their shells and they are not in any hurry about it. They leave their silver on everything, on the rocks and the roots and the small warm discs the tapirs turn up in the soil, and in the mornings the trails catch the softened light so that the whole forest floor looks written on.

---

The thing that calls in sevens.

It calls at dusk and just before the heavy rain and occasionally, for no reason, at noon. Seven notes, always seven, the intervals precise, the phrase never quite the same twice. It is answered — the same seven, or a reply that fits them — from across a valley, sometimes two valleys, always from somewhere that when you get there is empty canopy and drip.

People who have stayed here have tried. They have climbed. They have sat in the crowns for days. They have found, at the places the call seems to come from, nothing but wet leaves and once a tapir looking back at them with mild interest. The frogs go quiet when it calls. The birds do not. The hyraxes lift their heads.

What has been noticed, by the patient, is this: the call does not come from the trees. It comes from *above* them. From the boundary, or just under it, where the warm air meets the cold and the clouds form and the tone is. It comes from wherever the mist is thickest at that moment. And it moves — not the way an animal moves, from perch to perch, but the way weather moves, drifting with the updraft across the whole width of the shelf, so that a single voice can be heard in the east and then, an hour later, twenty kilometres north, having crossed no ground.

The likeliest thing is that it is not an animal. The likeliest thing is that it is the shelf, or the part of the shelf that is nearest to having a voice, sounding itself the way a struck glass sounds itself, and that the answering call from across the valley is the same sound arriving at a different place in the ceiling and coming back down. Seven notes because that is the shape of the space. Never the same twice because the rain is never the same twice.

Or it is something that lives in the mist. That is also possible. Nobody has stayed long enough to be sure, and everyone who has stayed long has stopped needing to be.

---

There are people.

Not many. Not a village, not a tribe, not enough over all the centuries to leave a name for themselves or for the place. Individuals, mostly — climbers who came up the wall for reasons that seemed sufficient at the base and stopped seeming like reasons at the top. A few dozen, perhaps, since anyone was counting. They came up counting their steps in fours and gasping in the thin cold and then the ice began to run and the smell of clove came down and they reached the edge and the warm rain hit their faces, and something in them let go of the thing that had been holding on.

They are easy to recognise, when you find them. They have the same calm the animals have. They eat what the hyraxes eat and sleep in the bromeliad-pockets of the great trees and let the rain fall on them at dusk without seeking cover, because it is warm and it is scheduled and there is nothing to be gained by being dry. They speak, when they speak, slowly and with long gaps, as if each sentence were being routed through the whole shelf and back before it arrived. They do not ask where you came from. They ask if you have heard the tone yet.

They lose things. That is the thing you notice. They cannot keep track of how long they have been here — one will say a year and mean twenty, another will say a lifetime and be wrong in the other direction. They set things down and forget them, and the tapirs carry them off, and the snails write on them, and they do not go looking. Their hair goes white early and their skin stays young and their eyes, over decades, take on a particular quality that the salamanders in the bromeliad pools also have, of not so much looking as being present to what is there.

None of them has ever tried to leave. Not because anything prevents it. The wall is there, the ice runs down it, the way is clear. It is that leaving would be a gradient, and they have lived too long in a place without any.

---

Which brings you to the last inhabitant, and the first.

The makers did not build on the shelf, and they did not stay on it, in any way that leaves a body or a house. But something stayed. The spindle on the eastern bench is warm and clean and the rain bends around it. The pool is always full to the same hair's-breadth. The capillaries in the rim are polished in the direction of a flow that should not exist. The air does not leave. These are not ruins. Ruins are what is left when a thing stops. Nothing here has stopped.

Live long enough on the shelf and you begin to understand that the place is not a place with a mechanism in it. The place is the mechanism, and the mechanism has been running for so long, so reliably, so far past the lifetime of whoever set it going, that it has become the thing that lives here. Not a mind — nothing so small. More like a habit that has grown large enough to have weather. It keeps the air. It turns the water. It warms the rock. It does these things the way a heart does them, without decision, and like a heart it has an attention of a kind, distributed through the whole body: the rain that falls differently near the spindle, the birds that leave the spindle without calling, the tone at night, the sevens in the mist, the tapirs circling the way the water circles. It notices you. It notices you the way the forest floor notices a footstep — a pressure, an adjustment, a closing-behind. It does not object. It was not made for you and it is not against you. It takes you in as it took in the fungus and the frogs and the crabs in the rootballs and the climbers who forgot why they climbed, and it makes no visible change, and it runs.

Whether the makers are in it — whether what they became is the tone, or the call, or the warm patience in the rock — is not something the shelf will tell you. The people who have stayed longest have a way of touching the spindle in passing, the way you would touch the shoulder of someone very old who was sitting quietly and did not need anything. They do not explain it. When asked, one of them said that the shelf was not a thing that was keeping a forest alive. It was a thing that was content, and the forest was what contentment looked like when it had been left alone long enough at seven thousand metres in the rain.

That is who lives here. The fungus and the worms and the pale crabs; the epiphytes with their private oceans; the frogs that swim up through stone; the birds who walked; the hyraxes in their unnecessary coats; the tapirs going round; the snails writing silver; the few slow people who stopped counting; the voice in the mist that answers itself; and underneath and around and above all of them, holding the air in its hands, the shelf, awake in its own way, keeping the day going, not expecting anyone, and — you will feel this, standing in the clearing at dusk when the ceiling glows and the frogs begin — not at all sorry that you came.
