Start with the smallest, because the room does.

The pools have a skin. Not scum, not oil — a film so thin it only shows where the drips break it, and even then only as a hesitation in the ripple, a place where the ring of water spreads a fraction slower than it should. If you lower your face to it in the grey-green you can see the skin has a grain, faint parallel striations like the grain of a leaf held to the light, running always toward the nearest rib. It's alive. It is the most abundant thing in the room and the least noticeable, and it lives the way the moss lives, off the difference between one temperature and another, except that its gradient is horizontal — the pool is warmest at its centre, where the sub-floor pulse comes up through the sunken concrete, and coolest at the edge nearest the rib, and the skin has stretched itself across that whole slope like a hand laid flat on a stove-top to see how hot it is. It doesn't move. It doesn't need to. When the ceiling rains, it tears in a hundred places and sinks and you'd think it was gone, and an hour later the pool has a skin again, and the grain runs the same way.

Everything in the room is oriented to the ribs. That is the first thing you learn about what lives here, and it is the thing the room keeps teaching you in different sizes. The mineral crust around the drains is thickest on the rib-facing side. The moss-pads sit on the pool-facing side of each rib, where they can have both. The pale shoots at the end of the long pool lean, all of them, a few degrees off vertical, and the direction of the lean, when you take a sighting, is toward the seventh rib, which is not the nearest but is — you decide, standing there — the one that speaks most often. The room is a field, and the ribs are the poles, and the living things have arranged themselves along the lines of force the way iron filings do, without anyone asking them to.

The moss deserves more than the glance you gave it. Crouch to it again. The felt is made of individual stalks, each one no thicker than a hair and capped with something like a bead, and the beads are what's warm — not the stalks, not the mat beneath. Hold your breath and watch and the beads are moving, very slightly, not in the wind (there's no wind) but in a slow orbit, each one describing a tiny circle over the course of a minute or so, all of them together, the way a field of sunflowers would if there were a sun down here to follow. There isn't. They're following the rib. They're tracking the moment the rib is coolest, which comes just after the sheet-metal sound has gone up through it, and turning toward it, and turning away as it warms back toward the temperature of the room. A crop that leans toward the cold. If you put a fingertip into the pad — gently, it doesn't seem to mind — the beads around your finger swing toward it for a second, taste your temperature, and swing away, uninterested. You are only warm. Warm is everywhere. It is cold that is scarce here, and cold that is worth reaching for.

The pads are not the only colonies. On the underside of the walkway bracket where the stalactite is growing, in the lee of the drip, there's a crust the colour of dried blood that is not mineral. It's a lichen or a lichen's understudy, a partnership between something that can eat the copper the water carries and something that can eat the first thing, and it has spread from the bracket down the redeposited fang of the stalactite in a spiral, so that the fang is barber-poled in violet and rust, and at the tip, where the drip forms, the crust is thickest and darkest and slightly furred. Every drop that falls from that stalactite falls through a mouth. The crust drinks first. What reaches the pool is what the crust didn't want.

Now the shoots.

You thought they were plants, and they might be, but standing among them for a long time you notice they don't have the stillness of plants. Between pulses they tremble. Not in unison — each shoot on its own schedule, a shiver that starts at the base and runs to the tip and makes the tip, for a moment, brighten a fraction more than the pulse alone would account for. It looks like they're talking among themselves in the gaps. It looks, after a while, like they are keeping time — that the flicker in response to the pulse isn't a reflex but a confirmation, a marking of the beat, and the trembling between beats is counting. Eleven seconds is a long time to hold a rhythm. Something has to hold it. You find yourself wondering whether the shoots keep time to the pulse, or whether — in the way that a room full of pendulum clocks will eventually swing together — the pulse has come, over long years, to keep time to the shoots. You don't know. The honest position. But you notice that when one of the fliers passes overhead and drops its short cry, all the shoots go dark at once for about half a second, and stay dark, and then come back on the beat exactly where they should be, as if the cry had been a rest in the score they were all already reading.

They have roots. You can see them where the pool has retreated: white, ropy, running in a mat just under the crust, and running — of course — toward the seventh rib, and at the rib they don't stop. They go up the face of it. Not into it, or not visibly; they lie along it, a pale tracery no thicker than yarn, following the vertical grain of that material that isn't stone and isn't metal, and they go up out of the range of the grey-green and you lose them. You put your hand on the rib beside one of the root-threads and the rib is cool and dry and faintly unwilling, and the root-thread is warm and damp, and neither one seems to have noticed the other, and you have a strong impression that this is a very old arrangement, older than the concrete, older than the drains, and that if it were ever going to fail it would have failed by now.

The thing that crossed the far shore. You saw it once. Here is what you'd see if you stayed long enough to see it again.

It comes out of the water. That's the first correction: it's not a shore animal but a pool animal, and the flat gait is the gait of something that spends most of its life pressing itself along a floor under a few inches of warm water, feeling the pulse through its belly. Its body is long and low and jointed in more places than a mammal's would be, and it has a coat, or something that reads as a coat in that light — dense, dark, laid flat, beading water off it the way the moss doesn't. No eyes you can find. Where eyes would be, the face is smooth and slightly domed, and the dome is lighter than the rest, and you understand after watching it turn its head — it does turn its head, deliberately, at each rib as it passes — that the dome is how it hears. It is walking the room the way you'd walk a dark house by running your hand along the wall. It knows where the ribs are because they are the quiet places, the dry places, the places where the sound comes from and therefore where the sound is not reflected.

It eats the skin off the pools. You see it do this: it comes to the edge, lowers its blunt face to the surface, and the skin — that striated, rib-leaning film — draws to it, gathers to its mouth the way a tablecloth gathers when you pinch it. It takes a minute. The pool behind it is clean and naked and shivers under the drips in a way it didn't before. Then the animal goes to the shoots. It doesn't eat them. It stands among them through two pulses, and the shoots do not go dark for it as they do for the flier's cry, and on the second pulse the animal's domed face brightens — actually brightens, a soft grey pearl of light in the grey-green — and then dims, and then it moves on. You don't know what passed. You know the shoots kept time and the animal kept still and something was exchanged, and you know that when you came back this way later the pool had grown a new skin, and the new skin's grain ran, as before, toward the rib.

There are more than one. You hear them more than see them — the plash you took for a mistake about the pool's edge; and later, a soft repeated tapping from beneath a housing that you eventually decide is one of them knocking its head against the underside of something, listening to what's behind it. You never hear two together. Whatever their society is, it happens somewhere you're not, or at a distance from each other that the room's acoustics collapse into a single voice.

The fliers.

They live in the ceiling, which you can't see, and they are the reason the ceiling makes sense as a place — that up there in the dark where the ribs go, there must be ledges, brackets, the inverted architecture of a room whose builders assumed nothing would ever roost. The cry is short because the room doesn't need it to be long; the room does the lengthening. What you've heard twice is two animals, or one animal twice, mapping the chamber by shouting into it and reading the shape of what comes back. Everything down here that isn't the ribs is a listener, but the fliers are the only ones who make their own sound to listen to. They hunt, presumably. What they hunt you don't see, but you notice the moss-pads nearest the stairs are smaller and rougher than the ones deeper in, and you notice small pale crumbs of felt scattered on the floor around them, and you think: the pads are grazed. Something comes down out of the dark and takes the warm beads. The moss's careful placement isn't only about the gradient. It's about being just far enough from the walls, just close enough to the cool of the rib, that whatever drops on it from above will be — for one crucial half-second — colder than the room, and can be felt coming.

Everything here is felt coming. That is the second thing you learn. Nothing sees. The grey-green isn't for seeing by; it's a by-product, the way the warmth is, the way the water is. What lives here reads the room through its skin. The moss reads cold. The shoots read the beat. The pool-skin reads the slope. The crosser reads silence. The fliers read their own echo. Not one of them knows what the room does, any more than you do, and not one of them is any worse off for it. They live in the room's outputs. They have learned the room's habits — its rain-hour, its beat, its cold breath up the ribs — with the exactness of animals that have never known a world without them, and they don't ask what the habits are for.

Which leaves the ribs.

You'll have been circling this. Everything leans toward them. Everything listens to them. They are clean when nothing else is clean, cool when nothing else is cool, dry when the whole nave sweats. They are the newest thing here by any reckoning, and they are also the thing the oldest roots have grown up along, and those two facts don't sit together unless the ribs have been replaced — unless something has come, over and over, across whatever spans of time the crust measures, and taken out the old ribs and put in new ones without disturbing the roots. Or unless the ribs are not fixtures at all. Unless they are the tenants.

Put your hand back on one. Stay this time. It has the faint give of something that would rather not be touched, and if you keep your hand there through the reluctance you find the give has a texture, a very slow flex, like a chest that is breathing once every eleven or twelve seconds. Under your palm, on the pulse, it tightens. On the half-beat, it lets go. The sheet-metal sound goes up through it and you feel that too — not a vibration but a passage, something long moving along the inside of the thing under your hand, the way you can feel a swallow move down a throat. And then it is cool and dry and quiet again, and it has not asked you to leave, but it would prefer it.

You don't know what they are. Perhaps they were built; the regularity argues for it. Perhaps they were planted. Perhaps whoever poured the first concrete floor found them already here, standing in a cavern, and built the room around them the way a city builds around a river, and the drains and the walkways and the abandoned housings are the record of a long, patient, ultimately incurious attempt to make use of something that was here first and will be here after. Perhaps the pulse is theirs and the roar is the city's and the room is the place where the two sounds have agreed to overlap. Perhaps they eat the city's warmth. Perhaps they are the reason it's warm. Perhaps the moss and the shoots and the pool-skin and the crosser and the fliers are not the room's inhabitants but the ribs' — a small, patient, sightless ecology that has assembled itself around a dozen tall cool tenants the way an ecology assembles around a hot vent on the sea floor, and the ribs have no more opinion about that than the vent does.

You'll notice, before you go, that your footsteps' echo has never quite matched your footsteps. You thought it was the room. It might be. But once, when you stopped and stood very still to listen to the shoots, the second set of steps took two more paces before it stopped. You put this down to the length of the room, the time it takes sound to come back from the far wall. That is a good explanation. The other explanation is that the room has a listener you haven't found — something that keeps a distance behind you, in the dark past the grey-green, matching your pace because your pace is the newest sound in a place where every sound is a fact, and it wants to know what kind of fact you are. It didn't follow you up the stairs. It has no reason to. The stairs are the one place in the room that gets cold as you climb, and nothing that lives down here goes willingly toward the cold — except the moss, which leans toward it, and stays exactly where it is.
