The first answer is the one the place gives you itself, and it is the right answer, and it is also not enough: the mats live here. Everything else is passing through, including the water.

But say *mat* and you have already got it wrong in one direction. It is not a thing lying on the ground. It is the ground's upper edge, the part that has not yet stopped. Kneel at a rim where the flow is and look at the orange band — the one the heat makes, the one you are not to touch — and understand that you are looking at a skin a few cells deep, and that below it, in that same square inch, are the grey and rust and lemon seams going down and down, and that those seams are the same organism's own dead. It stands on its ancestors. It *is* its ancestors, in the way a tree is its heartwood: not alive there anymore, but not something else either. The whole pale plain is one body that has been growing for longer than it has been light, and the living part of it is a film you could lift with a fingernail, and the living part does not know the rest is there. It has no down. It knows only up, and out, and *warm*, and *wet*.

So what lives here is the topmost millimetre of a continent, and it is very busy.

Within the millimetre there are layers again, because the mat cannot help itself. The top is the colour you see — the green, the olive, the orange — and it faces the sun and takes the light. Just under it, in the dimness the top makes, there is a different company: cells that want the light the top rejects, red and violet and colours that have no names because nothing has looked at them. Under those, in the dark, there are things that want no light at all, that eat what falls from above and breathe what would kill the ones above them, and give back what the ones above them need. It is a city stood on end, a street of tenants who never meet and cannot live apart. The whole arrangement moves upward a hair's breadth a year as the mineral fills in beneath. Nobody in it relocates. The floor simply rises under them and they stay at their proper height, and what was the dark cellar becomes stone, and a new dark cellar forms above it.

That is the settled life. There is also a frontier life, and the frontier is the orange.

Where the hottest water spills over a rim, the mat there is thin and violent and almost nothing but pigment, a sheet of cells living exactly at the temperature where they can live and one degree short of where they cannot. This is where the plain is made fastest. This is also where it dies fastest — the flow shifts, the vent chokes, and the orange goes brown in an afternoon and black in a week and bone-white in a season. But before that, while it runs, the orange is the plain's appetite made visible. It reaches. Every dark wet fan spreading over old pale stone is the mat finding that it can go further than it went last year. It does not go further because it wants to. It goes further because that is what the leading edge of a mat *is*: the part that has just discovered wet.

And here is the thing that took me longest to see. The mat does not die when the pool dries. The pool dies. The mat was never that pool. The same lineage, the same handful of kinds, the same tenancy of light-eaters over shade-eaters over dark-eaters, is already thriving three hundred paces downslope where the water went, and it got there by no journey at all — a few cells carried in the spill, a few dust-grains of dried crust lifted by the afternoon's rising air and set down again in the next wet place. The resident of this plain is a pattern that reforms wherever heat and water meet, and it has been reforming for so long that the reforming *is* the pattern. Ask where it lives and the honest answer is: at the wet places, all of them, the ones that exist and the ones that will. The dry basins are not its graves. They are where it used to be standing.

---

If you stay long enough at one rim — long enough that the sun has crossed and the shadow of the chimney has swung round to you — you begin to suspect the mat is not alone in its millimetre.

There are no shells in the pool bottoms, and no tracks but yours, and nothing that flies or hops or scurries, and the description of the place is right that nothing moves which is not water, weather, or growth. But *growth* is doing quiet work in that sentence. Put your eye a hand's breadth from the mat where it is cooler and older, green rather than orange, and wait. The surface is not still. It heaves, very slowly, in places — a bubble of something forming under the rubbery skin and lifting it and letting it down. That is gas, the mat breathing out its own waste. Look longer. In the clear hot water above the mat, in the last few millimetres before the surface, there is a glitter that is not light on water. Motes. Turning. Too small to have a shape, but not too small to have a direction, and they are all going the same way, which is toward the mat.

They eat it. Of course something eats it. There is a table laid across every horizon, warm, self-replenishing, undefended; you could not build such a thing and have nothing come. So in the film of water over the film of mat there are grazers — small enough that you will never see one as itself, only as the glitter of many — and they crop the green and leave the stone, and their own dead go down into the seams with everything else. There are things that eat the grazers, no doubt. It does not go very far up. The whole chain of it would fit in a thimble, and the thimble is repeated across the plain a number of times you could not write down. This is the fauna. It is real. It has never made a track, because a track is a mark on something firmer than yourself, and here there is nothing firmer that they are heavy enough to press.

I want to say the plain is *loud* with them, in the way it is loud at dawn with the crackle of drying, but that is a lie of scale. They make no sound. What they make is the pale ground. Every seam finer than paper is partly them.

---

The plants, then. The place calls them guests, and that is fair, and it is also a little unkind, the way *guest* becomes unkind when the guest has stayed for a hundred years.

The grey-green tufts in the cracks are the true opportunists. They come from somewhere else — seed on the wind, dropped from a height no bird has flown here at, or more likely walked in on a hem long ago — and they have found the one thing the plain does not do for itself, which is *hold*. A crack holds dew. A crack, over years, holds dust, and the dust is dead mat, and dead mat is the nearest this place has to soil. The tufts live on the plain's rare failures of continuity. When the crack heals — and it does heal, the mat finds it, the deposit fills it — they are gone. They have not lost. They were never in it to win.

The moss in the cool channels is more like a native. It lives in the same wet the mat lives in, a little downstream, a little cooler, taking the water after the mat has already had its heat out of it. It does not compete. It has found the one temperature the mat does not care for and it has taken that whole narrow band, emerald, soft, a few fingers wide and as long as the channel runs. When the channel dries the moss dies where it stands and does not come back. Its patience is a different kind from the mat's. The mat's patience is that of something that will be here later. The moss's is that of something that is here now, entirely, and has made no plans.

And the shrubs. The shrubs are what happen when the plain forgets a place for long enough. They stand in the oldest basins, the ones dry so long that the crust has gone through bleaching and cracking and into something like earth. They are the only things here with a trunk, a top, a shadow they own. They stand far apart because each needs more than the ground can give in less than its own radius, and they do not send out runners or seed the space between, because the space between is warm pale stone and nothing they can do about that. They are, in the strictest sense, the elders of this place: the one living thing on the plain that is standing on ground older than itself. Everything else is standing on last year. Under the shrubs there is shade, and in the shade the stone is a fraction cooler, and at the coolest hour before dawn the dew lasts a little longer there than anywhere else. If there were a where to sleep, on this plain, it would be under a shrub.

---

Which brings the walker back into it, because the place mentioned a *you*, and *you* left tracks, and the tracks are the only ones.

Whatever crosses this plain must keep crossing. There is nothing to stop for — no game, no fruit, no shelter but the shrubs and the shrubs are days apart, no water you can drink unless you wait for the storm and catch it, or crouch at a cooled spill-channel and drink after the moss. The ground is warm to lie on at any hour, so there is that. You could sleep anywhere. You could sleep in a dry basin with a dead rim at your back and the stone giving up its heat into your shoulder blades and the vents ticking somewhere off in the dark, and it would be more comfortable than most beds, and in the morning the dew would be on you like on everything else, and it would lift off you with the haze. It is not a hostile place. It is simply a place that does not accumulate anything but itself. Whoever lives here, in the sense that a person lives somewhere, lives by not stopping, and reads the country by ear — the tick and seep that means water ahead, the crack far off that means a rim has gone and there is a new pool to reach before it shifts again — and knows the plain the way the mat knows it, as a set of wet places that move.

And they leave tracks, but only in the wet. On the dry pavement a foot leaves nothing; there is nothing there soft enough to take it. It is only at the rims, in the living mat, that the foot sinks a few millimetres and the rubbery skin folds and tears and shows the paler layer under it. And then the mat heals over. Not in the sense of forgetting — the mat forgets nothing, it only builds. It lays the next film across the footprint and the footprint is now a seam. A very slight one. A small curved irregularity in one band, a hundred bands down, a hand's breadth into the stone by the time anything cared to look, if anything ever did.

That is what lives here, finally, if you insist on a single thing. A ground that is made of everything that has ever stood on it, at the pace of a fingernail, by the smallest possible masons, and that is in no hurry, and that will take the shape of your foot as readily as the shape of a grazer you could never see, and keep both, and not distinguish between them, and rise.
