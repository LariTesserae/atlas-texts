Start small, because the basin is mostly made of small things, and because the smallest of them tell you nearly everything about the rest.

The frost on the fungus. From a pace away it looks like a bloom, the kind a plum has. Kneel in the moss — it takes you to the knee and it is warm, and it gives — and the frost resolves into individuals: soft, legged in some number that doesn't matter, each the size of a grain of salt and each of them perfectly white. They are white the way a thing kept in a drawer is white. There has never been a sun to shield against and never a hunter with eyes worth fooling, and pigment costs something, and so over a very long time the pigment was simply put down and not picked up again. That is the first rule of everything that lives here, and once you've seen it in the frost you will see it everywhere: anything not needed has been let go. They graze the grey fur at a steady creep. Where a patch of them has eaten the fungus down to the liverwort beneath, the fungus has grown back by the time the patch returns to that spot, and it always returns to that spot, because a patch does not spread and does not shrink and does not move to a new tree. A given patch has a size. It has been that size for longer than the tree has stood, and when the tree finally softens and folds into the moss the patch will go down with it and be exactly the same size on the trunk that rises through the wreckage.

Below the frost, the moss. The moss is the oldest thing alive in the basin and it may be a single thing. It is red-brown, wet through, and when you press it it does not spring back the way moss does but yields and then slowly refills, like a body. Dig your fingers into it and they come up warm. Its threads go down into the vesicles of the rock, the little bubbles frozen into the lava as it cooled, and it has filled every one of them for miles. It has never set a spore that needed to wait for rain, because it has never been without rain. It has never had a dry edge. It reproduces only by continuing — a strand grows and the strand behind it dies and is eaten and the eaters die into it and it grows on. If you could see the basin with the rest of the life stripped away you would see the moss as one enormous red animal lying face-down on the black glass with its mouth in every pore, breathing the warmth up out of the ground, and you would not be far wrong.

On the undersides of the great door-sized leaves, the many-legged things. They lie so thick that the underside of every leaf is a different colour from the top — the top is black-green and wet, the underside is the mottled brown of a hundred segmented backs. They eat the leaf. They eat it from beneath at the rate the leaf thickens from above, so that no leaf is ever eaten through and no leaf is ever without its cargo. They are born on the leaf and they die on it and their young are the size of the frost and their old are the length of a finger and every size between is present on every leaf at every moment. They have no eyes. There is nothing under a leaf to see. What they have instead is a sense of the leaf's thickness through their feet, and when a place grows thin they move, all together, a hand's width over, and the thin place heals.

The haze. You walk through it as through a hung curtain, and it parts and closes, and it is made of small flying things — but it is important to understand that the haze is not a swarm. A swarm gathers and disperses. This has never done either. Each cloud hangs in its own column of air, from ankle height to the leaves, and it has hung in that column since before there was anything in the basin that could remember. The flies in it live a few days. The cloud has lived as long as the basin. Nothing holds it there; there is no wind to move it and no reason to leave. New flies rise into it from the water below and old flies drop out of it dead and the salamanders on the nearest trunk take those, and the shape of the cloud does not alter by a hair. This is the second rule, and it is the one the whole basin runs on: the arrangement is the living thing. The animals are what it is currently made of.

The frogs. There are more frogs than there are pools, so the pools are more frog than water — pale-bellied, dark-backed, eyes fixed round and open because there has never been a brightness to close them against and never a glare to narrow them for. Each frog sits in its place with its throat working and its note going out. Each frog has one note. It has had that note since its voice came in and it will have it until it dies, and it sounds it continuously, not in pulses with gaps for listening but as a held tone with breath taken through the nose so it never stops. The frogs in one pool sit with their notes very slightly apart from one another, so a pool is a chord, and the chord has a shape that fills the pool the way the water fills it. When a frog is taken — and they are taken, the dark low things come through and lower their heads and take one and walk on — the chord is short a note for a while, and then one of the small ones that are always at the pool's edge comes to voice, and its note is the missing one. Not because anything chose it. Because that is the note the pool is missing, and a young frog's voice settles where there is room.

They are not for anything, the notes. Not for territory; nothing here holds territory, since nothing here is ever short of anything. Not for a mate at a season; there are no seasons and mating happens by drift, a frog moving a body-length toward a tone that suits it and staying. The notes are simply what frogs do, in the way that dripping is what leaves do. A frog with its throat still would be a frog with something wrong with it, and there is no such frog.

The salamanders on the trunks are the ones you could mistake for the tree. They lie head-up in rows, pale bellies to the moss, and their mouths are open, and the water that runs down the trunk — the trunk is a gutter, every trunk is a gutter — runs into their mouths and out through the sides of them, and they take from it what the water carries. Fallen flies. The frost, washed loose. Spores. They are living sieves. In the whole of a long life a salamander will move perhaps the length of itself, up the trunk as the moss below it thickens and the moss above it thins. Its skin is the only skin in the basin that is not wet from outside, because it is wet from within — a slick it produces continuously, that keeps the water sliding. Turn one over and it comes away in your hand without a struggle, soft, blood-warm, its mouth still open and still working. Put it back and it settles into the row and the water finds its mouth again and it is as though you had never been.

Under the leaves, in the litter that lies on the moss, the eaters of the dead. Pale, all of them; some shaped like worms and some like slugs and some like nothing with a name; blind, all of them, and slow. They are the basin's floor, in the sense that they are what the floor is made of. Everything that dies comes down to them and passes through them and goes into the moss. Because nothing ever dies in a heap — no winter kills, no drought, no flood — they are never glutted and never starved. Each one has, at any moment, exactly the meal it has always had.

Now the dark low things in the ferns.

They are the largest animals in the basin, and they are the size of a dog, and they are the closest thing here to a river. Sleek, short in the leg, black or nearly, damp-furred — the fur is short and lies flat and has never once been dry — with small eyes and enormous whiskers, because down among the fern-stems there is nothing to see and everything to feel. They walk. That is the whole of what they do. They walk in a slow continuous traffic through the fern layer, and the traffic goes in loops, and the loops cover the whole basin, and nothing in them ever stops. They eat walking: the head goes down at a pool, comes up with a frog, the jaws work, the feet do not pause. They mate walking, in a brief matching of pace. The young are born walking, dropped wet into the moss and up on their legs before the mother has gone two lengths, and they walk behind her and then beside her and then in their own place in the loop. They sleep walking, in the way that some things in the old world were said to — one side of the brain and then the other, the legs never losing the rhythm. Stopping would be an event, and starting again would be another, and there is no room in them for either.

You could stand in the ferns and they would come through around your shins like water around a post, and close behind you, and not one of them would lift its head. Not because they trust you. Because your shins are, to them, in the same category as a fern-stem or a hump in the rock: a thing that is where it is. The traffic has been flowing through this basin for as long as the moss has lain on the glass, and it has never once met anything that behaved differently from a fern-stem, and so it has no idea of the difference.

In the canopy, the round-eyed things.

They are small — you could hold one in two hands — and they are the only warm-blooded creatures here, if that word still means anything in a place where the rock and the air and the water and every body in it are all at the temperature of blood. Their warmth is nothing they had to make; it is simply the warmth of the place, carried in a fur that is always wet, always parted, always dripping from the same points at the elbows and the tail-tip. Their eyes take up most of their faces and are fixed open and are entirely black, because there is no such thing here as too much light and so nothing to close down for. They have hands. They cling. They move through the great leaves in small groups, six or eight or nine, and each group has been the same group for as long as any of them has lived — the same nine, or their indistinguishable children, in the same span of canopy, never out of one another's sight and never, since none of them has ever been anywhere else, out of one another's minds.

They call, of course. Their call is a soft even tone, held, breathed through the nose like the frogs' — everything here that calls has learned to call without stopping — and it is not for anything either, except that a group of nine tones is how nine of them know where the other eight are without looking, and looking would mean turning away from the leaf they are eating. When one dies its tone drops out of the nine, and the nine goes on as eight for a while, and then one of the young ones' voices deepens to fill the gap.

They have no faces. That is to say: they have eyes and nostrils and a mouth, but there is nothing on them that moves in a way that means anything, no flicker of ear or lift of lip, because an expression is a report of a change in state and there is no change of state to report. They look at each other, when they look, with the same fixed black regard they turn on the leaf, the water, the sky that is the cloud, and you.

If there is anything in the basin that could be called a mind, it is in them, and it is worth trying to say what such a mind would be.

It would have no memory, not because memory failed but because memory is a tool for telling then from now, and here then and now are identical and the tool was never made. They know one another, the nine, but not by recall — they know one another the way your left hand knows your right, by never having been apart. They cannot anticipate, because anticipation is the shape of a future that differs from the present, and they have never had material from which to build one. They cannot be surprised, cannot be afraid, cannot want anything they do not already have in their hands, since everything they could want is within arm's reach and always has been. What is left, when you take those away, is attention. Pure, constant, undivided, with nothing to divide it — attention to the leaf, the water, the tone of the other eight, the warmth coming up. They are, if you like, the most awake things that have ever lived, and their wakefulness has never once had anything to be about.

There is one more place, and one more kind of inhabitant, and you would not find it unless you knew to look.

The crust is hollow in places. Where the lava domed and the dome held, there is a chamber underneath, and the chambers connect, because the lava ran in tubes and the tubes drained, and the whole black floor of the basin is threaded with them like the veins in a leaf. They are perfectly dark. They are warmer than the surface — not by much, but this is the one gradient in the entire basin, the only direction that means anything, and it means down. They are wet, and the water that seeps into them from above goes nowhere, and lies in them, and is warm as the water above.
