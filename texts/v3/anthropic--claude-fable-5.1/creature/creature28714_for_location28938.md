The first ones you meet are the scrapers, because they are the ones who move. You see them before you see anyone else — a figure on a gantry overhead, walking slowly with a paddle held out ahead like a blind man's stick, tapping. Tap and the tap becomes a soft crush and a fall of white into the trough. Tap again. They walk the whole length of the ward on the upper runs and come back on the lower and by the time they are back the place where they started has begun to fur again. It is not work that finishes. They do not seem to expect it to.

They wear very little, and what they wear is wet. A wrap at the waist, a cloth over the head that is less a hat than a sponge, wooden sandals with a high sole because the brick is hot and slick both. Their skin is the first thing that tells you the ward has residents and not just workers. It is pale — not the pallor of a cellar-dweller, which is grey, but a scoured look, a skin that is always damp and never dirty, because nothing here holds dirt, everything is rinsed by the drizzle and dried by the rock and rinsed again. The knuckles and the outer edge of the forearm carry small pale scars in rows, the way a fisherman's hands carry hook marks. Those are from the crystal. You cannot scrape it for a year and not be cut by it, and you cannot be cut in the ward and have it fester, so the cuts close fast and clean and leave these little white ticks, and the oldest scrapers have forearms that look frosted.

---

Then, once you have adjusted to the dimness, the ones who do not move much.

There is the woman at the pans on the eastern wall, and there are several of her, at intervals, each with her stride-wide ceramic dish and her rake. The rake is wood because iron would grow salt and ceramic would break. She sits on a stool with one leg tucked up and she does not watch the pan; she watches the fog on the lamps. When it thins she lifts the rake. She knows to the quarter hour how long the brine takes under this or that sky and she is never wrong and she has never said so. When the crust is gathered she tips it into a lidded pot and ladles new brine and sits again. She does not look at the red glaze on the far wall. Nobody at the pans looks west.

There is the kiln-tender, who is the only person in the ward whose skin is dry, because he spends his hours with his arms inside vaults that would take the water out of a river. He has a face like a walnut. He is not old. He puts the green clay in and takes the biscuit out and the whole art of it is knowing which vault is which today — because the rock behind them shifts its heat by a hair from month to month, and the vault that was gentle in the spring is hard by autumn, and a pot left in the wrong one comes out cracked. He tells them apart with the back of his hand held near the mouth, and he is talking to the rock when he does it, not in words, but you can see it is a conversation.

There is the boiler-keeper. He lives closer to the glaze than anyone, in the vault at the eastern end, with the four pumps and the great iron belly sunk in the floor. He does not have a clock. Nobody in the ward has a clock, or needs one, because the boiler sighs every eleven minutes and has done for longer than the youngest have been alive, and time here is counted in sighs. *Three sighs*, someone will say, meaning half an hour, near enough. The keeper's whole life is the interval. He listens to the sigh and it tells him the pressure and the seep and the state of the rock beneath, and if it comes at ten minutes he wakes, and if it comes at twelve he wakes, and he has not slept through a change in it in thirty years. He is the one who looks the most like the place. The red not-light has been on his skin so long it has left something there, a faint ruddiness under the pallor like a bruise that never quite forms, and his eyes water constantly and he does not notice.

---

The builders you may not meet at all, because they are waiting.

There are five or six of them, and their trade is the ward's slow descent. They have a store of anchors and elbows and pipe and fired brick stacked in a dry vault against the day a floor lets go. When it does — when a section of brick sags and cracks and drops into a new black pocket that breathes heat like an open oven — they rope it off and they wait. Months. A year. They go down the stair from the city each day and sit at the edge of it and drop a bit of wax on a string and watch how fast it runs. When the wax comes back a bead and not a smear, they go in with the anchors. Between pockets they patch and reroute and shore. They are the patient ones, and there is a way they have of touching the walls — flat palm, a moment's pause, move on — that everyone in the ward does but they do most, and if you asked them what they were feeling for they would not be able to say. They are feeling for whether it has changed since yesterday. It usually has, a little.

---

Most of the ward goes up the stair at the end of the shift. You see them at dusk, or what the grates make of dusk, climbing with their heads down, and by the fifth landing they have begun to shiver — not from cold, from the absence of the medium, the way a swimmer shivers on the ladder. The city above calls them steamers, or pan-hands, or something worse, and takes their salt and their water and their biscuit and their seasoned timber and pays them what it pays them.

But some stay. Perhaps forty. They have the mid-level vaults, behind screens of fired clay pierced in patterns to let the air through, and they sleep on cots of slatted wood with no bedding, because bedding would be wet in an hour and would never in this place go dry in the way a bed should. They sleep through the day, when the city draws its water and the ward dries and the pans run hard and the brick pales; they wake at the wet hour, when the seep comes on and the fog thickens and the drizzle steadies, and they work the night that is not cool but is less. They eat what comes down the stair — bread that has gone to rusk before it is unwrapped, dried fish that was already dry, fruit that must be eaten the day it arrives or not at all. Nothing is cooked. Nothing has ever been cooked here. There is a story about a man who tried to boil a pot on the hot floor and the pot was fine and the water was fine and he was fine but the whole ward stood around him watching as though he were doing something obscene, and he did not do it again.

They speak little, and low, because the air carries. When they speak of the ward they do not say *the Warm Ward*, which is the city's joke. They say *the seam*, or simply *down*, as in *born down*, which a handful of them were. Those ones have the pallor from the start and cannot bear the grates. On the rare days they climb they go up wrapped to the eyes and come back sunburned in a way that is not from the sun.

---

The children — there are three, at present — are the only creatures in the ward who run. Everyone else moves at the pace of the drizzle. The children run the lower gantries in their high sandals with a slap and clatter that sounds, in that quiet, like a thing being broken, and no one stops them, because the ward's people have some feeling that the seam should hear something young in it. The children collect the fig-stones. They have small pierced bags of them at their belts and they trade them and grade them by heft and by how far they fell, and the best one, the heaviest and darkest, is held by a girl of about nine who will not show it to you. She is the one who will tell you, if you ask her what lives here, that the rock does. She says it the way she would say the boiler lives, or the pumps. She is not being fanciful. She has heard it crack.

---

The ward marks the people the way it marks the pipe.

There is the salt. Anyone who has slept a month down here has a faint rime at the hairline and the rims of the ears and in the creases of the elbow, where sweat sat and was taken back by the heat and left its mineral behind — the same white edge that grows on every horizontal surface in the seam. They rinse it off each morning in the cistern water and it is back by night. The oldest of them have it in the folds of their eyelids. When one of them laughs, which is rare and quiet, you see the salt crack at the corners of the mouth.

There is the pallor, and beside it the ruddiness of the west-wall workers, and there is a thing the eyes do — the pupils sit wide in the dim and do not close fast in the grate-light, so that the ward's people squint upward like moles and see very well in the brown between the lamps. They see you before you see them. They always have.

And there are the lungs. The air is water and salt and heat and everyone who lives in it has a soft catch at the top of each breath, a wet little hitch, not a cough, that you stop hearing after a week because everyone has it, and that the city above hears the moment one of them opens their mouth up there. *Steamer*, they say. It is the sound of the ward speaking through the person.

---

Nobody has told you what happens when one of them dies, and you do not ask, but you work it out.

Nothing rots here. Nothing softens. The kiln-tender's dry face is the answer. The ward's dead are not carried up the stair — the city would not thank them for it — and they are not put in the ground, because the ground is the thing they have spent their lives learning not to touch. They are laid in one of the middle vaults, the ones neither hot nor wet, on a slatted cot like the ones they slept on, and the air does what the air does. In a fortnight they are light. In a year they are the colour of the biscuit that comes out of the kilns, and they weigh what a child weighs, and they can be moved. There is a vault at the far pinched end of the fissure, beyond the last lamp, where the salt glaze grows over the black facets like ice on a broken window, and they are set there, sitting, against the wall, and the glaze grows over them too. Slowly. A translucent skin, a little thicker each year, that catches what light there is and holds it. The oldest of them are almost gone into the wall now, a shape under a pane. The ward's people go back there sometimes with a lamp and sit for a sigh or two and come out again and go back to work. They do not scrape them.

---

The moss you have seen. It lives in the drizzle-stripe and on the coolest gantry-undersides and at the lip of the northern cistern, and it is more alive than anything else here by a wide margin, so alive that it seems to be doing something, and in a way it is: it has found the one set of conditions in this cathedral of wrong conditions that suits it, and it is not moving from them by an inch. The ward's people step around it. They have never discussed this. If a gantry needs reworking where the moss is, the builders take the moss up on the brick it grew on and set it in the trough until the work is done and set it back, and it does not seem to mind, and nobody has said why they do it, and if you ask they look at you as if you had asked why they step around the boiler.

And the fig hangs in the ceiling with its roots in the old city and its leaves in the seam, drinking from both and belonging to neither, and drops its stones. The ward's people speak of it the way you might speak of a very old relation who lives on the top floor and does not come down. *The tree had a good year*, they say, meaning the children's bags are heavy. Once, some years ago, a branch fell. It was the size of an arm. It lay on the highest gantry and dried there in a day to something like a bone, and it is still there, on a ledge in the boiler vault, and the keeper touches it when he passes, flat palm, the way they touch the walls.

---

You will spend a while, in the seam, trying to decide what it is that lives here. The scrapers, the pan-women, the walnut-faced tender, the keeper with his eleven-minute heart, the patient builders, the forty sleepers, the three loud children, the sitting dead going slowly into glass; the moss; the fig. That is the list. That is who came down the stair on their own feet, or hung down through the ceiling, or was already here.

But stand on the lowest western gantry long enough, through six or seven sighs, and something else comes clear. Watch how the scraper stops when the rock cracks and does not move until it is done. Watch how the keeper wakes to a sigh a minute early. Watch how the pan-woman reads the fog and the builders drop wax on a string and everyone, everyone, touches the wall in passing without knowing they do it. Watch how the salt grows on the pipes and on the eyelids alike, how the drizzle rinses brick and skin alike, how the hitch at the top of every breath is the ward's own wet air announcing itself through a throat.

They have not tamed the seam. It has not tamed them either. What has happened is slower and stranger than either: they have grown into each other's habits until it is not obvious, looking at a scraper furred white at the hairline standing very still under a stalactite of the same white, waiting for the rock to finish speaking, which of the two is the inhabitant and which the inhabited.

The girl with the heaviest fig-stone was right, in the plain way she meant it. The rock lives here. It is new and it is hot and it is still deciding, a hair's breadth at a time, whether to be a scab or a wound, and it has these people on it the way a body has its flora — small, quick, salt-marked, breathing wet, keeping its time, going down with it as it opens, and in no hurry at all to be finished.
