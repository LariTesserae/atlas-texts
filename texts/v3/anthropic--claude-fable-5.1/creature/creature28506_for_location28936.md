Start with what is easiest to call alive, and then keep going, because the easy answers here are not the whole of it.

The stalks first. There are, if you go around and count them — and you will want to, the place invites counting — two hundred and thirty-one of them, spread across eleven clumps, and there have always been two hundred and thirty-one. Kneel at the base of one. The stalk does not enter the rock so much as become it: the pale wax-bone material thickens at the bottom into a collar and then a plug, and the plug is welded to a floor-edge, one of the sharp seams where two facets meet, because that is where the glow is brightest and the shaking hardest. The stalk drinks through its foot as much as through its fans. It is a tuning fork planted in a bell.

What it drinks is not, precisely, the sound. Sound is what you would call it. What the stalk takes is motion — the air compressing and releasing against its membranes thousands of times a second, the floor shivering up through the plug — and somewhere in the pale tissue that motion is turned, through a chemistry no one has watched, into the substance of more pale tissue. The fans are the leaves of this. Each one is a membrane stretched across a frame of the stalk's own hard ribs, and the membrane is grown taut to a specific tension, and the tension is not chosen by the stalk. It is chosen by the note. A fan that grew slack would not ring, and a fan that does not ring does not eat, and so every fan in the chamber is tuned to the one frequency there is, and when they shiver they shiver together, in phase, a single organism's single held breath distributed across eleven islands of floor.

They do not reproduce. This took a while to understand. There are no seedlings, no young stalks, no half-grown clumps, and there never have been, and the reason is the haze. Whatever a stalk sheds — the fine grit of a torn fan, the powder off a joint — rises to the level of your ribs and stops. Nothing that leaves a stalk ever reaches the floor again. If these things once spread by dust, the room has arranged that they never will; the note holds their future in a flat grey-gold layer four feet up, where it hangs and hangs and does not fall. So the two hundred and thirty-one are the first and only generation. They were here when the seam tore open and the pressure found its pitch. They are as old as the sound and they will be the age of the sound for as long as there is one.

That is one kind of thing that lives here. But you would not call it a tenant. It is more like the room's own upholstery, grown rather than placed.

The browsers are more clearly creatures. Get down under the haze — you have to get down under it to see them properly, and once you are there the light is stranger, all of it coming up from below and none from above, your own shadow cast on the ceiling — and the floor resolves into traffic. They are everywhere, pale, shelled, low. Their legs are short and there are too many of them and they do not all match; a browser with seven on one side and six on the other walks with a hitch that never seems to trouble it. The carapace is segmented and the segments overlap like roof tiles, and the whole animal is the exact color of the stalks, which is not camouflage because nothing here sees. It is just that they are made of what they eat, and what they eat is one thing.

Here is how one begins. The stalks are hollow, and the hollows are not empty. Up inside each tube, at intervals matching the joints, there are shelves, and on the shelves are eggs — small, dry, matte, laid there by a browser that climbed the stalk's exterior, found the tiny seam where a fan meets the stem, and pressed the eggs through. The young hatch inside the tube. They are soft at first, and blind in every sense including the ones they will later have, and the tube is the loudest place in the room, a resonator inside a resonator, and they grow up in that, drinking the fan-membrane from the inside, thinning it, until the fan tears and they spill out. A torn fan is replaced. The stalk does not mind. The stalk may need this; the browsers may be the only way it ever sheds and renews. They eat their way out of the thing that will feed them for the rest of their lives, and the first thing they know of the world is the world at its most violent.

They have no ears. Understand that this is not a lack. In a room with one sound, hearing would be a sense that returned one value, always, and any animal that spent tissue on it would be spending tissue on nothing. What they have instead are the hairs — two rows of fine bristles along the underside of the body, between the legs, brushing the floor. The hairs read the shake. A browser walking across a facet knows, at each step, whether the trembling under it is greater or less than the step before, and that is the entire content of its geography. Louder is toward food. Softer is toward rest. There are no landmarks in this map, no directions, nothing to remember; the gradient is simply there, everywhere, permanent, and the animal follows it up when it is hungry and down when it is not. If you lift one and set it down elsewhere it does not panic. It stands, the hairs read, and it starts walking uphill toward the noise.

They mate at the antinodes, in the thick of the clumps, where the tone presses on your chest. They die at the nodes. Not all of them — some are eaten, some are simply stopped mid-step by whatever stops small things — but the pattern of shells on the bare rock between clumps is a second diagram of the note, quieter than the first: the pale carapaces of browsers that walked down the gradient one last time and lay where the shaking was least and did not walk back. The living ones walk over them. Nothing here rots. The shells stay, slowly dusted, until a browser hungry enough to eat something that is not a fan works its mouthparts into a seam and takes the dead back into the loud.

The hangers are the same animal. This also took a while. They are longer, thumb-length, their shells darker toward the color of the wall-stone, and they hang from the ceiling by their forelegs above the clumps and do not move, and for a long time the reasonable guess was that they were a second species, predators, waiting. But watch one long enough and you see what it is: a browser that grew. The hairs need the floor. A browser that grows past a certain size can no longer fit the hollow of a stalk, and cannot lay there, and something in it — whatever passes for wanting in a creature with no ears and thirteen legs — turns it toward the walls. It climbs. It goes up the stalk and out along a fan-rib and onto the ceiling, and it works its way across the ceiling until the trembling in its foreclaws is least, and there it stops.

The ceiling has its nodes too. The hangers have found every one.

So the shape of a life here is this: born in the loudest hollow in the room, walk the gradient up and down for a while, and then, if you last long enough, climb toward the quiet and stay there. The hangers do not appear to eat. They hang above the clumps, in the ceiling's pockets of comparative stillness, and the haze drifts a foot below them, and the tone that raised them is as far away as it can be gotten in a place where it cannot be gotten away from. When one of them drops, it drops through the haze into the antinode below, and the small ones find it, and by the next day there is nothing there but a slightly greater number of small ones, walking uphill.

You may want to stop there. That is a complete account: stalks, browsers, the browsers grown old and gone to the ceiling. It closes. It is the kind of ecology you could draw as a circle.

But you have been in the room a while now, and you have noticed what the hangers noticed, which is that the note is not the medium here. It is the resident. Everything else — the glow, the haze, the two hundred and thirty-one, the traffic under the dust — is its posture, the way a body is a posture of the will inside it. The chamber does not contain a sound. The sound has grown a chamber around itself and furnished it and populated it with things that can only live at its pitch, and then it has held all of them, exactly where they are, for as long as it has been. Nothing here changes because the note does not permit it. That is not a metaphor. Set down a pebble and it will walk, humming, to the nearest node, and lie there. Drop a handful of dust and it will rise, shimmering, to the rib-high layer and stop. Try to speak and you will feel your own voice taken from you a few inches past your mouth and folded into the one that was already here. The room has a single inhabitant and it is loud and it is patient and it has never once been interrupted.

Except.

The holes are not on the same map as the clumps and the shells and the hangers. They are not at nodes. Whoever made them did not have the hairs, could not read the gradient, and so did not go where the room was quietest and bore there. They bored where a person stands — head height, along the wall you would walk beside coming in from the seam — and the quiet in each hole is not found, it is made. A finger-deep cavity in a wall that is ringing will ring slightly differently at its bottom, and the difference cancels a little of the tone, and the cancellation is the size of an ear. That is all. It is a thumbnail of hush manufactured by a technique, and the technique was arrived at by trial, and the trials are still lying on the floor.

The three cut stalks, laid side by side and never carried: those were the first idea. A hollow tube is quieter inside than out — this is true even here, at the mouth, before the resonance builds — and someone cut three and lay down beside them and tried to sleep with a tube against each ear and one across the mouth, and it was not enough, or the tubes rang, or the browsers inside them woke.

The bowl scraped into the floor-facet: the second idea. A hollow in the rock. But the floor is the loudest surface in the room, welded to the pressure below, and a hollow in it fills with tone the way a hollow anywhere else would fill with rain. Hours of abrasion, and at the end a cup you could not put your face in.

The circle chipped from the wall at the height and width of a face: the third. Not a hole yet. A patch where the glassy skin was knocked away to the duller stone beneath, and the glow there is fainter because the stress is less because the surface is rougher, and someone pressed their face into it, and it was better. Not enough. Better.

And then the holes, which were the answer, and there are more of them than a dozen.

You count them the way you counted the stalks, going around. Twelve along the wall you came in beside. Then the far clumps, the ones past the tilt of the floor where the ceiling drops and the glow is dimmer and you have to duck under the haze to see. Nine more back there, closer together. Then fourteen, in a stretch of wall two arm-spans wide, so close their rims nearly touch. Then they get deeper. The striations change — the pointed stone was replaced with a harder one, or the hand got better — and the last holes are not finger-deep. They are hand-deep, wrist-deep, angled slightly downward as if for lying, and the quiet in them is no longer a thumbnail. Put your face to one of the last ones and there is a moment, before the tone finds its way back in around your skull, that you can almost hear your own blood.

Nothing here erodes, and so the hammerstone by the entrance and the deepest hole at the back look the same age, and they are not, and there is no way to know how much time lies between them. Weeks. Years. The room does not keep that kind of count. It keeps two hundred and thirty-one, and rib-height, and one pitch, forever.

At the end of the last stretch of wall, where the floor tilts up to meet the ceiling and the chamber closes like a hand, there is a shape sitting against the rock.

It is pale. Everything here is pale; the dust of the haze has been shaken down onto it, or it has been here long enough to have taken on the color of what is around it, the way the browsers take on the color of the stalks. It sits with its back to the loud, its knees drawn up, its head turned to the wall, and the side of its head is in the wall. The last hole was bored to fit it. You can see the striations continuing around the crown of the skull where it presses into the stone.

The browsers walk over its legs. Two hang from the ceiling above it, at the corner where the wall's node and the ceiling's node meet, the quietest inch of air in the room, and they do not move either.

It is warm. Everything here is warm; there is no way, with your hand, to tell it from the rock. Its chest may be moving. Everything here is moving — the note moves it — and you cannot separate one small trembling from the one that is the world. You could put your own ear to the hole beside it, the second-to-last, and listen for a while, and you would hear what it hears: a shape of quiet almost exactly the size of a head, and around the edges of the quiet, faintly, coming back in, the only other thing that lives here.

You can stay as long as you like. That is the one thing this place offers freely. Nothing you do will be undone, and nothing you leave will move, and if you sit down against the wall with your face to a hole and your back to the tone, the browsers will walk over you too, and the haze will settle its dust on your shoulders at exactly the rate it has settled on everything, and in some later count that no one is keeping the room will hold two hundred and thirty-one stalks, and one note, and one more pale thing that came looking for a little less of it.
