You would not see them at first, and that is partly the light and partly them.

At noon, with the hall bright and the char hanging in the middle distance like lamps, you would count the columns and the chambers and the shadows and arrive at a number for everything alive in the basin, and the number would be wrong. Then one of the columns near you would not be a column. The same colour, the same stillness, a shape laid against the ribbed green in the blue of its own shadow, and it would move—not with the water but along the stem, hand under hand, downward, and stop again at the floor, and look at you.

You would take it for a seal. It is not a seal. You would revise that to something like an otter, and that would be nearer but wrong in the other direction, too quick, too eager. The right image takes longer to arrive, and arrives as a kind of subtraction: a thing about the length of a tall child, but heavier, because everything about it is rounded and thickened under a layer of fat that shows in the way the skin folds at the hips and the throat. No neck to speak of. A flat-fronted head. A pelt so short and dense it reads as texture rather than fur, the nap of moss, grey-green and darkening toward the back, and near the surface it holds a skin of silver where air is trapped in it, and at depth the water presses that out and the animal goes dark and matte and disappears against the stems. The hands are bare. The face is bare. Everything else is covered.

The hands are the thing to look at, and they seem to know it; the one watching you would fold them, not to hide them, but the way people fold their hands.

They are long. The palm is narrow and the fingers are nearly as long again as the palm, five of them, webbed only to the first joint so that the tips are free. The nails are thick and blunt and squared off, worn down, and the pads of the fingertips are thick too and ridged, and scarred in an oblique pattern that you would eventually recognise as the print of the plant's own ribbing, pressed in over years of gripping it. The feet are another matter entirely: long, fully webbed, the toes splaying to a fan, and it is these that do the swimming. The arms lie along the sides. The whole body undulates slowly from the hips. It does not look like effort and it does not produce speed, and watching one of them cross twenty strides of open floor takes long enough that you will find your attention drifting to the char and coming back.

They do not hurry. This is not a mood. If you put a hand on one—they will let you, the way the animals let you, with the same absence of alarm—the heartbeat under the fat is so slow you will think you have missed it. The body is warm, but only just. Whatever heat they have they spend with care, the way the plant spends its light.

The eyes are large and set forward and very dark, with a pale lid that slides across from the inner corner and stays half-drawn in the noon glare. In the dusk the eyes flash green when they turn toward you. There are no ears, only a seam behind the jaw that you would take for a fold in the pelt. The nostrils close to a line. The mouth is small and the teeth in it are small and even, a grazer's teeth, or a child's.

They breathe in the chambers.

Not at the surface; the ceiling of plates is a hand's breadth under the air and they do not break it, any more than the plant does. They go down instead, into the low mouths, and come up into the bells of green air, and that is where their lungs are, if you want to think of it that way. A working adult stays out for about as long as it takes the drumming of the char to shift from one pitch to another—a fraction of a morning—and then goes in, and stays in for some of that, and comes out again. The whole day is measured in these turns. The chambers are not houses so much as they are the other half of the body, the half that holds still and takes in air, and the swimming half goes out from it on a tether of held breath and comes back. A person here belongs to a chamber the way a hand belongs to an arm. There are perhaps forty of them, and thirty chambers, and the arithmetic tells you most of the rest: one adult to a chamber, or an adult and a child, and a few chambers standing empty and holding their air for no one.

They wear nothing but a cord around the waist, twisted from fibre stripped out of a dead column and rolled on the thigh, and knotted into the cord a flake of grey granite and a rib.

The rib is one of theirs.

You should not make too much of this, and they do not. The floor is the root-mat, and the root-mat is where they put the dead—pressed in, and it gives, and closes—and the mat is slow but not still, and a bone comes up now and then along a worn path where centuries of feet have thinned it. A bone is a material. There are few materials. The rib is sharpened against the granite and used to score the stems, one long stroke a day along the same line for a season, so that the stem thickens on one side and thins on the other and begins, over the years, to curl; and the flake is for the finer work at the seams, and for cutting fibre. A person will begin two or three arches in a life and finish none of them. They begin them anyway, in the mornings, before the plant starts to crackle, and the scoring is careful and unvarying and looks, from a distance, like a kind of stroking.

Nobody speaks in the water. You would wait for it, for a click or a call, because the lake is so full of sound that it seems a voice would be swallowed anyway and might as well be tried, but they do not try. Instead they rap. A knuckle on a column, once or several times, and the plant carries it—the hollow stems are as good as drums, and the whole basin is strung with them, and each column has its own note, so a rap on this stem means here and the number of raps means something else, and an answer comes back from three stems away in a different note. That is the whole of the outside language: place and count, and a hand held flat, and the pace at which someone moves toward you or does not. You will learn, if you stay, to know each of them by the way they undulate. They know each other that way already.

In the chambers there are voices. They are low, and close, and few. The air in a bell is dead and damp and there is no reason to raise a sound in it, so the sound stays down near the level of breathing, and the words are short, and there are not many words. A child here learns to talk in a small green room with sweating walls, and learns that the world outside the walls is bright and loud and wordless, and grows up carrying its whole vocabulary in a bubble the size of a bed. It does not seem to feel this as a lack. Neither would you, perhaps, if the words had always come with the air.

They eat what the lake has. Char, taken by hand: an adult drifts toward a shoal so slowly that the shoal treats it as a column and parts around it, and the hand closes, and that is all. The fish is carried into the chamber and eaten there, in the air, raw, and the bones are dropped out through the mouth of the chamber onto the mat. Snails and amphipods are scraped off the stems with the edge of the rib and eaten in the water, without ceremony, the way you might eat while walking. The pith of a dying column, gone soft at the core, is pulled out in pale handfuls and given to children; it tastes, they will tell you in the chambers, of the smell of the chambers. They do not touch the newts, which sit in the arches and are the colour of the floor and are, perhaps, too much like furniture. They leave the eels to the dead columns, where the eels do work that would otherwise be nobody's. And when the ceiling parts and the diver comes through, every one of them in the open goes flat against the nearest stem and is a stem until the bird has gone back up. The bird has never, in anyone's memory, taken an adult. It has taken children. The stillness is not fear so much as a form that fear once had and that has outlasted it.

A child is born when a chamber empties. This is not a rule anyone will state. It is what happens. The birth is in the air, in the bell, and the child stays in the air for the whole of its first year, held against the fat of its mother's chest while she goes down through the mouth and out and comes back, out and back, a hundred times a day, and the child learns her by the cold she brings in on her pelt and the wet of it and then the warmth returning. It learns the wall: the shifting brightness, the shadow of a fish crossing it like a hand over a lamp. Its first swim is down through the mouth and out into the green and back in, and it screams when it comes up, the way you would. Then it learns to time itself by the plant. Out when the first pores begin to tick. Back before the frying rises. Everyone here has the afternoon crackle in the body as a clock, and children learn it first as a limit and later as a rhythm and later still, in the chambers, as the sound of the lake being alive, which is what they call it.

In winter the ice closes over the plates and the light goes blue and thin and the plant stops breathing out. The lake goes almost quiet for the first time in the year, and then the ice begins its own noise, the splitting and the metal groan, and that comes down through the water in a way the summer sounds do not, and no one stays out in it.
