You will see them before you know you are seeing them, and you will know you are seeing them before you can say what they are.

The first difficulty is color. They are the color of the floor. Not by any effort — there is no dye here, nothing to be dyed, and the hides they wear are scraped to the same gray-white as everything else — but because the salt gets into them. It crusts in the creases of the knuckles and the folds of the neck and the corners of the eyes. It stiffens the hair, which is worn long and matted and hangs in pale ropes, or is cropped to the scalp with flint and grows back white at the roots, no one seems to know whether from age or from the salt or from the light. A figure standing still against a hemisphere forty paces off is very hard to separate from the hemisphere. A figure lying down on the open floor is invisible. So the first way you will see them is as motion: something in the glare that moves when the floor does not, and keeps moving, and resolves, when it comes closer, into a person bent under a block.

The second difficulty is the eyes. The narrator was right that they squint. That does not convey it. The lids are thick and heavy and permanently half-closed; the brows are shelved forward; there are deep radiating creases from the outer corners that have been cut into the skin over a lifetime of narrowing against a light that has no direction to narrow against. The opening that remains is a dark horizontal seam, wet, and you will rarely see the eye itself unless someone turns to you inside a hemisphere at the end of the day, when the light has gone gold and the lids ease, and then the iris is pale — not the pallor of a blind thing, but bleached, gray-blue, gray-green, gray. Children's eyes are darker and their lids are not yet shelved and they are, as a consequence, in visible pain for most of the day. They walk with a hand held flat above the brows. They are given, when they are small, a sliver of the herd-animals' back-plate with a slit rubbed through it, worn on a cord, and they look at the world through the slit until their own faces have grown the slit for them.

The third difficulty — and by now you have stopped calling them difficulties and started calling them what they are — is that they are not looking where you expect. When they stop and stand and look at something, they are looking up.

---

Understand what the roof is to them.

The floor moves. The buildings move with it and are cut down and re-raised forty paces to the left. The pans fill and drain and shift. The herds go where they go. Nothing on the floor is where it was, and nothing on the floor can be a landmark, because a landmark that migrates is only a companion. But the roof does not move. Gypsum does not creep. The great fractured sheet with its bands and lozenges, its thick amber stretches and thin white blazes, its sagging places and its seams — that is fixed. That is the only fixed thing in the world.

So the roof is where the names are.

They have names for perhaps two hundred features of the ceiling within the range that any one cluster habitually travels: this fracture, that healed crack with its ladder of bright bars, the place where three lozenges overlap and make a brighter fourth, the long thick amber tongue that runs toward the seam and under which the light is always cool. A crew does not say *we will rebuild over there*. A crew points with the chin at a place on the roof and means *we will rebuild under that*. A child lost on the open salt is not told to find a building — the building will be gone — but to walk until a certain shape stands overhead, and wait. The narrator was told that nobody knows which way the city moves because there is nothing to measure against. That is true of the visitor. It is not true of them. They know precisely: this cluster stood under the ladder-crack when the oldest of them was small, and now the ladder-crack is far behind and they stand under the tongue, and they have a word for the direction that is, roughly, *toward where the roof gets thinner*. They do not think of it as the city moving. They think of it as the roof staying.

And the roof gives water.

This is the whole hinge of their lives and the visitor did not ask about it. The brine in the pans cannot be drunk. The walls cannot be licked — that is what the herds do, and it is why the herds are always at the edge of dying, shedding their dead for the others to eat. The only fresh water in the cavity is the condensation that gathers on the underside of the ceiling and drips, and the drip-points are fixed, because the roof is fixed, and every one of them is known, named, and belongs to someone. Where the ceiling sags low enough to reach — near the seams, over the thick amber stretches, where it comes down to within a body's height of the floor and then a body-and-a-half and then nothing — they cut catch-basins into the salt beneath the drips, and line them with the plates of dead herd-animals so the water does not sink and salt itself, and they drink from those. Farther out, where the roof is two hundred feet up, the drips fall too far and too scattered to catch and the water is lost into the floor. So the settled parts of the city hug the low roof, and the far, high-ceilinged reaches are worked but not slept in, and the entire loose gigantic pattern of hemispheres is in fact a pattern of thirst: denser where the drips are, thinning where they are not, following the ceiling's sag the way a coastline follows the sea.

A cluster's basins are the one thing it will fight for. Not with any noise. Two crews who both believe a drip is theirs will stand under it, twenty paces apart, and wait, and the one that outwaits the other has it. This can take days. Water is brought to them by children. They do not speak.

---

They do speak, close to.

The language you will hear inside a hemisphere at nightfall is low, breathy, mostly made in the front of the mouth, with a great many stops and hisses and very few open vowels — the sounds of dragged salt and poured brine, a language that has grown to fit the only acoustics it has ever known. It does not carry. It was never meant to. Across any distance greater than an arm's length they use the other language, which is made with the hands and the head and the whole tilted body, and which the children learn first, because a mother forty paces off cannot be heard and the whole floor between is glare. There are perhaps three hundred signs. Most of them concern salt: *the floor is swelling here*, *this block is too fresh*, *pour more*, *pour less*, *this wall is finishing*, *leave it for the herds*. There is a sign for *look up* and it is the commonest gesture you will see, and it is also how they say *yes*, and *I understand*, and *we are agreed*, and *there* — a small lift of the chin — because everything true is written on the roof.

Their names are roof-names. A child is named for what stood overhead when it was born, and since the cluster has moved since then, the name is also a distance, an age, a record of travel. To say a person's name is to say how far the floor has carried them. The old have names for places on the ceiling so far behind that no one living has stood beneath them, and this is a kind of honor and also a kind of sadness, and they do not distinguish the two.

---

They live in crews, not in houses. A house is eleven days old. A crew is the six or ten or fifteen who cut, carry, pour, and sleep together, who own between them a set of tools and a claim on a drip, and who will, when the floor splits the hemisphere they raised, dismantle it together and raise the next one. Children belong to the crew. Pairing is a matter of two people who pour brine on the same seam — that is the phrase, and the sign for it is two hands tilting a bag together — and it is neither permanent nor expected to be, because nothing is. A person may leave one crew and walk to another cluster under a different piece of roof and be taken on, if they have hands, and everyone has hands.

The hands. You have to look at the hands. They are the worn part of a worn people: broad, split, white to the wrist with salt that has crystallized inside the cracks and healed over and cracked again, the nails thick and ridged and gray, the pads of the fingers so calloused they seem to have grown a plate of their own. A hand that has poured brine for thirty years has a permanent curl to it, the shape of the bag's neck. A hand that has quarried has a flint-scar along the inside of the first finger, the same scar on everyone, in the same place. They rub fat into their hands and feet every night — fat rendered from the herds' dead, gray and rank — and it is the only thing that keeps them from splitting to the bone, and the smell of it, close and animal, is the smell of the inside of every hemisphere at dusk.

They are not tall. Nobody who spends a life bent under blocks under a low roof is tall. They are heavy through the shoulder and back and thin in the leg, and they move slowly, and you will think at first this is laziness and then you will understand it is the same slowness as the floor's: an unwillingness to hurry that has become a way of standing in the world. To move fast on the salt is to fall, and to fall on the salt is to open the skin, and to open the skin here is to be salted into a wound that does not close. They move like people carrying water. They move like the walls settle.

---

What do they eat? You will wonder, watching a herd cross a work-site and nobody so much as lift a hand toward it.

They do not eat the herds. It is not that the herds are sacred. They have no word that means that. It is closer to say that the herds are colleagues — the other builders, the ones who take down what the people put up. A wall left too long is licked away; a body left in the open is eaten; a fold that has split a hemisphere is cleaned of everything that could rot by the herds within two nights, and then the people come and take the blocks. The herds finish things so the people never have to. Killing one would be a kind of confusion, a mistake of category, like eating a tool. When a herd-animal dies of its own accord the people do take the plates, and the fat, and the long tough gut for cord, and leave the rest to the others of its kind, and this is understood on both sides as correct.

What they eat is the scum. The pink and orange films at the pan edges are scraped off with a bone edge, wrung of brine, pressed into cakes, and eaten raw or laid on a hemisphere's outer wall to dry in the glare, where they go brown and leathery and last. They taste, if you must know, of iron and salt and faintly of something sweet, and they are most of what the people are made of. They eat the thumb-length joint-things too, the translucent ones — gathered by the handful when the brine is poured and they come out to drink, cracked between the teeth, more shell than flesh. They eat the eyeless fish when quarrying breaks into a void, and this is a feast and a scramble, the crew and the herds converging on the same sudden pool, the people scooping with their hands and the herds gliding in around them, and nobody strikes anybody, and the fish are divided by speed. And where the roof comes low, under the thick amber, they harvest the hanging gray colonies — the soft fringed dripping things — on long poles of lashed antler, and boil them in brine over a fire of rendered fat, and the smell of that is the one smell in the cavity that is not salt.

Fire is rare. There is nothing to burn but fat. They keep it small and low and inside, in a bowl cut into the floor of a hemisphere, and it gives more warmth than light, and the walls take the warmth and hold it and give it back through the night as the glow fades.

---

Now the dead.

The narrator's rule — *nothing finished, nothing new* — is a rule about buildings, and they would not put it that way, but it is also the rule of their own bodies and they know it. A child is too new. It is loved, but it is not yet a person in the full sense; it has no worn edges, it makes noise, it hurts in the light. It is hurried toward wear. It is given a block to carry as soon as it can lift one, and its first block is cut for it by the crew and set in a wall and wetted, and after that it has a place in the wall the way it has a place in the crew, which is to say a place that will move. The old, on the other hand, are the most worn and the most honored. They have the deepest lids and the whitest hands and the longest names, and they are the ones who walk the floor with bone pegs and read where it is beginning to swell, because reading the floor is a matter of forty years of feeling it lift under the soles. An old floor-reader stopped on the open salt, pressing a heel down, tilting the head, is the nearest thing this place has to a sacred figure. Nobody interrupts them.

And when they die they are laid on the open salt, in the glare, for one day and one night. The herds come. What the herds leave — and they leave the long bones, the skull, the plates of callus off the hands, anything too hard — is gathered on the second morning and set into the next wall the crew raises. Set in, and wetted, and the brine poured over it, and the salt closes and the bone is inside. Every hemisphere has bones in it. Every hemisphere is eleven days old and has ancestors in its walls. And when the floor splits that hemisphere the crew takes the blocks apart and finds the bones and carries them to the next wall, forty paces to the left, and sets them in again, so that the dead migrate with the living, are rebuilt with the living, are never finished because the walls never are. The oldest bones in a cluster have been through hundreds of walls. They are worn to nubs and pale as the salt and no one remembers whose they were, and their name is the crew's name, and the sign for *our crew* and the sign for *the bones in the wall* are the same sign, a hand laid flat against a curved surface.

Sometimes a quarrying crew breaks into an old void and finds a body the floor has taken — one that fell into a fold and was carried under and preserved, salted white, the face still there — and this is not a horror. It is a stranger. It is brought up and laid out for the herds and its bones are taken into the wall, and the crew's name is, from then on, one stranger larger.

---

They know there is somewhere else. Not because they have been there, but because flint comes from it.

Flint arrives along the seam, where the roof meets the floor. It comes in the hands of people who look like them and squint like them and speak the close language with a different hiss to it, and who have walked a long way along the seam from a part of the city under a part of the roof that nobody here has a name for. They trade it for scum-cakes, for cord, for rendered fat, for a night's water. They do not say where they got it and probably do not know; probably they traded it in turn from a crew still farther along the seam, and that crew from another, and somewhere very far along the line of the seam there is a place where flint is quarried from the floor or falls from a hole in the roof or lies in the open, and somewhere past that, perhaps, the seam ends, or does not. There is a word that means *farther along the seam than anyone has come back from*, and it is the closest thing they have to a word for the world outside, and it is not a word for a place. It is a word for a direction.

Of the plain above the roof they have no idea at all. The roof is the roof. The light comes from it because that is what the roof does. When a thin stretch of gypsum somewhere far off gives way — this happens, once in a long while, a crack and a fall you feel through the floor more than hear — and a shaft of true unfiltered sun comes down white and vertical and full of drifting dust, they do not go toward it. The light there is too much even for their lids. They move their clusters away from it, under thicker roof, and in a generation the new hole is a named feature of the ceiling, the place where the light is wrong, and children are told not to walk under it.

---

At the end of the day, when the roof goes gold and the shadows arrive and the city has edges for an hour, they go inside.

This is when you will see them as people most plainly, if you have been taken in by a crew and given a place against the wall. The hemisphere is close and warm and smells of fat and salt and the sour tang of drying scum. The walls hold a dull amber that fades as you watch. The floor-fire is a red coin in its bowl. The lids ease. The hands, greased, rest in laps or on knees, curled to the shape of the bag-neck. Somebody speaks — the low, front-of-the-mouth speech — and somebody answers, and it is very quiet, and what they talk about, mostly, is the floor: where it is swelling, which wall is finishing, where the next one goes.
