You want to say the birds. It is the honest first answer. They are the thing that is most here, most of the time, in the greatest numbers, and if you asked the basin itself what lived in it, it would have to shout back over their noise. But stand with them a while and you notice the birds do not live here. They occupy it. They arrive from the sea side of the wall in the morning in long low skeins that come over the rim and drop into the fog like something poured, and they go back over it at dusk, most of them, to roost on cliffs and islands you cannot see, and what they do in the hours between is work. The basin is their shift. The gulls stand on the polygon plates in a way that looks at first like patience and turns out to be the exact spacing of a picket line, each bird a pace and a half from the next, all facing the wind, all waiting for the crack beside them to weep a shrimp. The terns come down out of the vapour onto the pools in a fast bent line, a fold in the air, and come up again with something bright, and never touch the ground at all if they can help it. Even the cormorants on the barge, who look settled, who hang there like washing for hours, are only drying. They are always about to leave. The flamingos are the exception — they have taken the shallow lake at the wet end and will not surrender it, and there are nests there now, low grey cones of the sediment itself, and pale chicks — but the flamingos are such an absurd thing, such a pink argument against the rest of the place, that they feel less like inhabitants than like a rumour that happens to be true.

The crabs are closer. The crabs have done something the birds have not, which is take the shape of the place into their own bodies. Forty years is a long time for a crab. They are the colour of the skin now, that grey with salt in it, and their backs have the same faint polygon texture, as if they had come up out of the cracks and brought the pattern with them. They live in the cracks — the cracks are the streets of a city that only exists at the scale of a crab, each polygon a block, each fissure a lane running down into the black where the water stands — and at dusk when the birds thin they come up in such numbers that the whole flat seems to shift a centimetre to the left and then click back. They eat the algae film. They eat each other. They eat the birds that die, and a great many birds die here, and by morning there is only a wing and a spine and the flat has gone grey and quiet again. They do not have the look of animals that were stranded. They have the look of animals that were waiting for exactly this, for the sea to be lifted off them like a lid so they could get on with it. But the crabs are only as deep as the cracks go. Below the skin, below the black, below the standing water in the fissures, the sediment is theirs no longer, and they know it, and they do not dig.

Something else does. Dig, or rather thread. The eels.

Nobody knows the route. The pump crews have theories that they draw on the chalkboard in lines and cross out. The wall is rubble and clay, a hundred metres through at the base, and the eels come through it — not over, not up the discharge pipes, which run the wrong way and would kill them; through, somewhere in the wet dark heart of the wall where the rubble has never quite closed against itself and the seepage runs in threads finer than a finger. They are the only living thing that moves freely between the sea and the gap. They are the only thing that has, in a sense, kept the old address. The bay was theirs before the wall, and the wall went up, and they simply continued, arriving as they had always arrived, small and glassy, out of the deep water to the west, and finding the way not closed but narrowed. They grow large along the seep-line. The braided shallow water at the foot of the wall is a hunting ground with nothing in it that can hunt them back except the herons, and the herons are few, and so the eels get long, and thick, and old, and dark, and they lie in the braids with only the current moving over them, and they look, if you see one in the grey light with the fog down, less like a fish than like a part of the wall that has decided to move. The crews eat them. They set traps of wire mesh in the seeps, weighted with rubble, and what they take out is heavy and sweet and tastes of salt and sulphur and something older, the same something you smelled when the plate tilted under your boot. It is the taste of the bottom. They say you get used to it. They say after a few years you miss it, off-shift, in the town on the far side of the rim, eating fish that only ever knew the top of the water.

Which brings you to them. Because if you ask what lives here and you mean lives — sleeps here, wakes here, has organised its whole small life around the gap and its keeping — then the answer is the crews, and the crews are stranger than the eels.

There are perhaps two hundred of them. Eleven stations, each with a house attached, each house a kind of barracks where the walls are a metre thick against the sound and the windows face inward toward the flats and not out toward the sea, because there is nothing on the outer side of the wall to look at but the thing they are holding back and nobody wants that view at breakfast. They work a rota that has nothing to do with day and night, because the basin has abolished day and night — there is the dim and there is the dark, and the pumps do not distinguish — and so the stations are always awake, always lit, and a man can go a month without seeing what the sun is doing. They are deaf, all of them, to some degree. Not from carelessness; the muffs are good and they wear them. But you cannot wear muffs for forty years, or twenty, or the twelve that is the average stay, and not have the low pressure of the pumps get in under the seal and take something from you. The older ones have stopped bothering. They stand in the doorways with the muffs pushed back and the sound going straight into them and their faces are calm in a way you find hard to look at.

They talk with their hands. This is the thing you notice about them first and never stop noticing, in the way you never stop noticing the noise. The signing started as a set of practical gestures — up, down, tide, gauge, six, eleven, stop — and forty years in a closed bowl with the same two hundred people have done to it what forty years in a closed bowl do to anything. It has drifted. It has become a language that exists nowhere else, with its own grammar and its own jokes, and the young ones who come in from outside take a year to be able to follow a conversation and two to be funny in it. They have signs for things there are no words for. A flat hand tilting, palm down, and then the fingers curling — that is the plate going under your boot, the moment the skin holds and then doesn't, and it also means a lie you almost believed. Two fingers walking and a third dragging behind: the seep rate rising on the north wall, and also a debt, and also grief. The sign for the sea is not a wave. It is a hand laid flat against the chest, on the sternum, where the pumps live, and pressed.

They read the wall the way farmers read weather. A crew chief on the north stations can walk the seep-line at the foot of the wall and put his palm to the rubble at certain places — the same places every time, worn faintly smooth — and tell you what the tide is doing on the other side to within a quarter of an hour, and whether it is a spring or a neap, and whether there is a swell running that the gauges have not registered yet. They know where the eels come through by where the threads of water taste different. They know the barge is sinking, a centimetre a year, because they have been measuring it against the gauging post for so long that the graph is a family document. They know which polygon flats will hold a machine on timber mats and which will not, and this knowledge is not written down and cannot be, because the flats change, and so it is held in the heads of six or seven people and passed on by walking, and if those six or seven were all lost in a season the basin would swallow the next machine that went out on it and everyone knows this.

They eat the eels and the crabs and a grey bitter green they pick from the glasswort and cook in salt water because there is no other kind. Their skin is a particular colour, a fog-tan, the tan of light that has come down through vapour, and their hair is stiff and pale at the ends with salt, and they smell faintly of sulphur and low tide and do not notice it and are surprised, off-shift, in the town, when people step back. They keep ravens. Not deliberately. The ravens keep them; each station has its two or three, black shapes on the chimney that have been there longer than any of the crew, that know the rota better than the crew do and come down for the lunch bags at the exact minute the shift changes. The crews have names for them, and the names are in the hand-language, and the ravens seem to know when they are being discussed.

There are children. Not many, and not born here, or not officially — the town over the rim has the clinic — but raised here, in the thick-walled houses, in the dim. They grow up thinking the world is loud and that the silence on the far side of the wall, the mere sea, is a kind of holiday. They sign before they speak and some of them never quite speak comfortably, their voices pitched for a room where voices don't carry. They learn the flats. They are the ones who know where the pools are this month, which cracks have fish in them, which slab of skin over which hollow will hold a child's weight but not an adult's, and they go out there in packs in the dim afternoon and come back grey to the knees with a crab in each fist, and the parents let them, because you cannot raise a child in this place and not let it, and because the children come to no harm out there in a way the adults find difficult to explain. The basin seems to know them. Or they know it in the way the crabs know it, at the right scale, in their bodies, and the adults have lost that and can only measure it.

The dead are in the wall. There is nowhere else. You cannot bury a body in the sediment; the sediment will not keep it, it rises, everything rises, the water comes up under it and lifts it and in a season you would have it back. So there is a stretch of the inner face of the wall, on the north side, where the rubble has been opened and closed again, and the closed places have flat stones set in them without names, and the seep runs a little slower there, or the crews say it does, and the eels do not come through at that stretch, or the crews say they don't. They put their palms to those stones too. They can tell you the tide from them.

You could stop there and have answered. The crews live here; the crabs and the eels and the flamingos and the ravens live here with them; the rest is passing through. But you have stood a while at the bottom now with the water coming up round your boots, and you know that is not the whole of it, and so does the crew chief nodding at you from his doorway with his hand flat on his sternum, and so does the child grey to the knees, and so do the eels, who have never once been fooled.

Something else lives here. It is not the birds and it is not the crabs and it is not the two hundred people with their drifting language and their palms on the wall. It is the thing you were told about in the first sentence and have been trying not to hear since. You are below the water. And the water knows it.

It is not gone. That is the mistake the old surveyors made, the ones who put up the hut on the little rise and thought about fields: they thought that when you lift the sea off the bottom of a bay the sea leaves. It does not leave. It goes down. It is in the sediment, in every layer of it, laid down flat and annual and never squeezed out, fifty metres of it standing on the honest limestone, and it is in the hundred metres of saturated silt that runs under the wall and joins the ocean without a seam, and it is under enormous pressure, and it is patient, and it is aware of the level at which it would stand if it were allowed. Every polygon crack that weeps is the sea saying so. Every dome that lifts and splits and cries a bright coin of water into the grey is the sea putting a finger up through the floor to feel the air. The thousand threads along the foot of the wall are not leaks. They are the sea reaching in. It comes up and stops at the level it wants and stops there because it can, because it has no need to hurry, because the pumps are running and it can feel them running the way you can feel them in your sternum, and it has decided to wait. It breathes with the tide. Twice a day the whole floor of the basin lifts by a finger's width and settles, and every crab and every eel and every crew chief with his palm on the stone feels it and does not say anything, because there is nothing to say, because it is only the sea, downstairs, turning over in its sleep.

It is the true resident. Everything else is a guest in the gap. The crabs have taken the shape of its skin and the eels have kept its address and the crews have made a language out of holding it back and buried their dead in the only place it cannot reach them yet, and the birds come over the wall every morning to eat what it gives up through the cracks and go back every night without thinking about it once. And it lies under all of them, the old bay, the whole weight of it, connected through the silt to the whole weight of the ocean beyond, and thinks — not in words, it has no need of words, and not in the crews' hand-language either, though the sign for it is the right one, the flat palm pressed to the chest where the pumps live — thinks about the level. About eleven metres, or fourteen depending. About a wall of rubble and clay and a hundred metres of saturated silt and eleven brick houses with their doors open and the men in them nodding. About how long forty years is, and how long it is not.

That is what lives here. Under your boots, in the black under the plate, in the sweet old smell that comes up when the skin tilts. Holding still. Keeping its place. Waiting, with the good manners of something that has never once in its existence needed to hurry, for the gap to close.
