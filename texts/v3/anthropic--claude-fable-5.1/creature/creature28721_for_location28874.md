You would say, after one day here, that the beetles live on the shelf. They are what you see. They are what moves. By the second day you would add the choughs, because they come at the same hours and walk about like owners, and the snowfinches, and perhaps the wall-creeper, and you would feel you had the census done.

But watch what happens at the shadow-line, when the headwall's shade slides across the shelf toward the lip. The choughs lift off the boulders without a sound and drop over the edge into the cloud. The snowfinches go over after them. The wall-creeper, which spent the afternoon climbing the headwall as though it were nailed there, is simply not on the headwall any more. They do not live here. They commute. The shelf to them is a table, laid every afternoon, and when the table is cleared they go home to warmer rock a thousand metres down.

Who stays is a different question, and it takes longer, and most of the answer is underneath.

---

The beetles stay. Turn one of the plucked blocks — carefully, they are heavy and they are balanced the way the ice left them — and the underside is a different country. Damp, black, cold enough to sting your fingers. In the fissures where the rock split along a plane of weakness there are beetles wedged in rows, a dozen, twenty, motionless, their shells dulled with condensation. This is where the night is spent, and the hail, and the eight months of the year when the shelf is under snow. The rock's inability to decide which world it belongs to is their whole architecture: warm above to hunt from, cold below to endure in, and the crack between the two is home.

They are ground beetles mostly, but there is one species here that occurs nowhere on the mountain below a certain line, a small bronze one with a domed back, and it is flightless. It has no wings at all under its shell, only the memory of where wings were. It cannot have come up on the wind. It walked. Or its ancestors did, generation on generation, climbing behind the ice as the ice went up the valley, staying in the cold band at the glacier's edge because that band was the only world that suited them. When the ice was down in the valley these beetles were down in the valley. When the ice was a kilometre lower on the mountain they were a kilometre lower. The shelf is not where they chose to be. It is where the retreat has left them, for now.

---

The snow lives.

This is not a figure of speech. At noon on the shaded patches, if you kneel and put your face close, the surface is moving — a fine dark grain shifting over the white, thousands of specks each smaller than a grain of sand, and the specks are animals. Glacier fleas, springtails, purple-black, so small that a single one is a smudge and only in their numbers do they become visible at all. They come up through the snow to the surface at midday to feed and go down into it again when the sun leaves. They eat what falls: pollen from the valley, the wing-dust of stalled moths, the bodies of aphids, a film of algae that grows in the snow and stains it faintly pink in late summer. They are the only creatures on the shelf that live in the snow rather than on it or beside it, and the only ones for whom the fallout is not a windfall but the whole economy. They contain a chemical that keeps them from freezing. They do not care that the temperature rarely rises above zero; zero is their summer.

There is a spider that hunts them. It is the colour of wet slate and it walks on the snow with its body lifted, and when it stops it disappears, because a grey spider on white snow in a violet shadow is not grey or white but some third thing the eye slides off. It has no web. There is nothing to string a web between and nothing would fly into it if there were. It has become, over however long it has been up here, entirely a thing of the surface: it walks, it waits, it takes what has fallen. Watch it for an hour and you will see it eat once.

---

The moss has inhabitants of its own, and here the census gives up counting and begins to estimate.

Pinch a piece the size of your thumbnail from the wet green basin and hold it to the light and it is a forest: stems like trees, leaves like leaves, and between them a dark wet space in which things are moving that you cannot quite resolve. Mites, red as blood, hurrying. Rotifers. Nematodes threading through the water film. And tardigrades — the moss-bears, plump and eight-legged and slow, ambling along the stems on claws, grazing the algae off the leaves the way cattle graze a hill. There are, in that thumbnail, more animals than there are birds on the whole mountain.

They live in the afternoons. When the cloud touches the shelf and every stem is beaded, the moss is a shallow lake and the tardigrades swim and feed and lay eggs in the water. When the cloud lifts and the sun comes back and the water goes, they do not die. They pull their legs in and shrink to a barrel a third of their size and stop, completely, their chemistry suspended, and they wait. When the frost comes they are already stopped, so it cannot hurt them. When the eight months of snow come they are stopped under it. Water returns and they unfold within an hour and carry on eating. A tardigrade on the shelf might live two years by the calendar and be alive for a few hundred wet afternoons, and to it the shelf is not a cold place but a place where time comes in installments.

They arrived the way everything here arrives except the beetles. They fall. Dried to their barrels they are dust, and dust goes up on the valley wind and comes down on the shelf, and a barrel that lands in moss and gets rained on is an animal again, on a new mountain, with no way of knowing how far it has come.

---

Under the fourth block from the lip — the one with the deepest glacial scratches, the one that is warm enough at noon to sit on — someone has been cutting the willow.

You notice the stems first: a small pile of them, no longer than a finger, laid on the top of a flat stone in the sun, drying. Not fallen. Cut, each with a clean angled bite at the base. Beside them, a few saxifrage leaves, their white lime edges intact. Lift the overhanging edge of the block and there is a runway worn into the gravel underneath, and the runway goes back into the dark, and from the dark comes a smell that is very faintly warm and very faintly sweet, like hay.

A snow vole lives here. Perhaps a small family of them; you will not get a count because you will see it, in a week, perhaps twice. It is slate-grey, darker along the back, with a tail longer than a vole's should be, and it moves between the blocks in the gaps beneath them where nothing overhead can reach it. It is the one large animal on the shelf, if you can call a thing the size of your fist large, and it is the one that most plainly did not fall from the sky. Nothing that weighs what it weighs has ever ridden an updraft. It walked up. Its lineage has been walking up this mountain behind the ice for ten thousand years, and it lives at the top of a long retreat, in a scatter of tumbled rock that suits it precisely because the rock is new and sharp and full of gaps that a rounded, settled, grassed-over hillside would not have.

It is making hay. All through the short summer it cuts willow and saxifrage and cushion-plant leaves and lays them on warm stone to dry and then draws them into the dark under the block, into a chamber where the temperature holds. When the snow comes and buries the shelf two metres deep, the vole will not sleep. It will live in the space between rock and snow, where the snow's own weight keeps the air a few degrees warmer than the sky, and it will eat its hay, and run its tunnels under the drift, and never once come up into the light until spring. It is dark, and it hides. It has taken both of the shelf's options.

The eagle that hangs on the updraft over the headwall knows about the vole. That is why the eagle hangs there. It never comes down onto the shelf, because the vole is never in the open long enough, and the two of them have been locked in this arrangement — the one always about to, the other always just under — since long before the ice went. Perhaps one year the eagle will get it. More likely the eagle will slide away over the ridge as it always does, and the vole will finish its haymaking, and nothing will have happened, which is the shelf's usual outcome.

---

There is a bird that stays, and it is the one you were told about at the beginning without being told.

Things that live here are dark, or silvered, or they hide. The ptarmigan is all three in turn. In summer it is the colour of the lichen crust — grey and black and a pale mottled brown that is not a colour at all so much as a texture — and it sits among the exact stones it resembles and does not move, and you can pass within a few paces of it and never know. In winter it is white, pure white, with a black tail and a black line through the eye and nothing else, and it digs itself a hollow in the snow and sits in it with its feathered feet under it and lets the drift close over. It is the snow's own bird. It eats the willow buds, all winter, from the runs of carpet-willow that lie under the snow where the wind cannot reach — the same willow the vole cuts, the same willow that decided not to be a tree. It has never in its life been below the cloud.

You will find where it roosts before you find it. A small neat heap of droppings, fibrous and pale, in the lee of a boulder near the headwall, and a scatter of feathers, some white and some barred, because a ptarmigan in September is halfway between its two selves and shedding one for the other. If you are lucky, at dusk, you will hear the only song the shelf has: a dry, wooden, rattling croak, three or four notes, like a stick dragged along a fence. It is not much of a song. There is not much of anyone to sing it to. One pair, perhaps, on the shelf. Another pair on the next shelf along the mountain, out of earshot. Enough.

---

Look again at who lives here, then, and a pattern shows through, like the shape of the glacier under the moss.

None of them are natives. There cannot be natives on ground fifty years old. Some fell and landed and happened to be able to stay — the springtails, the tardigrades, the slate-coloured spider, the moss itself, which arrived as spores on the wind and found bare rock and wet and took it. And the others — the flightless beetle, the vole, the ptarmigan, the dwarf willow — climbed. They followed the edge of the ice up the mountain as it went, because the edge of the ice was the world they were fitted for and it was moving, and a creature that is fitted for a world that moves must move with it or stop being. The shelf is not their home. It is the current position of their home. In another lifetime, if the ice keeps going, home will be two hundred metres higher on the headwall, or on the ridge, or it will have gone up past the summit into the sky and there will be nothing to follow.

So what lives on the shelf is, more than anything, a rearguard. The ice went and these came behind it into the space it left, and they are making what they can of the sharp new ground before it softens into something that no longer suits them. The vole's hay, the ptarmigan's buds, the beetle's crack — every one of them is a way of holding a position on a line that is retreating.

---

Which brings you back to the shelter, and the cache under the lip of rock, and the eleven cairns.

Whoever built the cairns built them for the same reason the vole cuts the willow: to have water where and when there would otherwise be none. Whoever left the flakes wrapped in hide was laying down a store against a return. Whoever blackened the floor of the shelter with dung-smoke was making a warm place on top of a cold one and lying in the gap between them, which is what everything on the shelf does.

The hide is stiff as bark and could be a century old. But look at the fourth cairn from the notch. Its top stone is not like the others. The others are split and pitted by frost, mottled with the black lichen that takes decades to establish. The top stone of the fourth cairn is clean. Its edges are sharp. It was set there, at a guess, within the last few years — put back after the frost or a bird knocked the old one off, by someone who came down through the notch and saw that the cairn was short and knew it should not be.

So someone still comes. Not often. Not this week, or last. Someone from the valley, most likely, who moves animals up to the high pasture below the cloud in summer and, on one of the rare mornings when the cloud is late, comes up the last thousand metres to the pass and drops down through the notch and walks the line of cairns to the shelter and sleeps one night against the headwall. Perhaps they check the cache and do not take it. Perhaps they grind something in the depression in the flat stone — the saxifrage, the lichen, whatever it was their grandmother ground — and carry it back down. Perhaps they are only making sure the place is still there.

You will not see them. If they came while you were here you would find the shelter floor freshly blackened one morning and the person already gone up through the notch, and that would be all. They are dark, or silvered, or they hide, like everything else. They come from above, like everything else, down out of the pass instead of up out of the valley, and they fall onto the shelf and stay a night and are gone. They are following something too — a way of using this mountain that is older than the bare rock and is retreating, as everything here is retreating, up toward the ridge and the thin air and the point past which it cannot go.

---

At night, count.

The beetles are in the cracks under the blocks, twenty to a fissure, cold and still. The springtails are down inside the snow. The spider is under a stone. The tardigrades are barrels, stopped, waiting for the cloud. The vole is in its hay in the dark under the fourth block, awake, listening. The ptarmigan is in its hollow with the drift closing over it, white in the white. If it is one of the nights — one in a hundred, one in a thousand — a person is lying in the curve of stacked stone with a dung-fire going out beside them and the whole sky pressing down.

Every living thing on the shelf is within an arm's length of rock, or under it. Nothing is exposed. Nothing is out in the open under those stars, which are too many and too close. The shelf by day is a table laid for visitors; the shelf by night is a set of small dark spaces between what is warm and what is cold, and each of them is occupied, and each occupant is holding on.

That is who lives here. The ones who found the underneath. The ones who came behind the ice and are staying as long as the sharp ground lasts, taking what the sky drops and what the rock hides and making, out of a place that is only just adequate, a home.
