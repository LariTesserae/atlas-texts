They are born into the chord. That is the first thing, before faces, before milk. A child on the plateau arrives in a room whose walls are humming at a pitch too low to hear and it will not be silent again for the rest of its life, and so it never learns what silence is, and so it never learns to want it. Ask anyone up here what the plateau sounds like and they will not understand the question. They will list the surface noises for you — the pump-houses, the valve that shrieks on the north sluice, the swifts — because those are sounds. The chord is not a sound. It is the shape of the inside of the chest. The only people who ever feel it are the ones who lose it: the tower-keepers who climb the cistern stairs into the wind, where the brick and the height damp it away, and who come down after a shift grey-faced and shaking and lie on the warm iron with their palms flat until it comes back into them.

So begin there, with the body. The people of the plateau are deep in the chest and short in the leg. Their blood is thick with the altitude and their hearts are large and slow, and they do not run, ever, because there is nowhere to run to and no air to do it with; they move with a rolling economy, shoulders turned to fit the paths, and when they climb lattice they climb the way the goats do, without looking, the hands and feet finding the rungs by an intelligence that lives below the neck. Their feet are horn on the sole and their toes spread. In the beds they go barefoot in the black wet soil and on the iron they wear sandals soled with split bamboo, because the rivet-heads in sun would take the skin off a heel, and they can tell by the sound of the bamboo on the plate — a flat knock or a ringing one — whether there is air under the tile or mortar.

Their hands are stained. Everyone's, growers and crews alike. The rust gets into the creases and the moss-water fixes it and it does not wash out, so that the knuckles and the lines of the palm are traced in a pale, permanent orange, and an old woman's hands look like the concrete faces on the east side, worn and pitted with the grain showing through. A child's hands are clean for perhaps two years.

Their eyes are the strangest thing about them, and they do not know it. Under the canopy the light is a green dusk that never brightens and they have made their eyes for that: they see into the wet dark between the fern-trunks the way you would see across an open field, they can read the needle on a gauge in what a stranger would call darkness, they can tell one fig from another by the shade of its leaf at dusk. Then they step out onto the open plate at noon and they are blind. The cloth goes over the eyes — bamboo fiber, woven loose, darkened with soot from the stoking-gallery vents, three or four layers — and they walk the sunlit stretches by sound and memory and the heat on the face, seeing shapes only, moving among the ranks of lizards like people in a fog. So their world is inverted from ours. The shade is where things are clear. The light is the place of shapes and guesses. They have a word for the sun-bleached open stretches that means, roughly, *the blur*, and a word for the deep beds that means *the seen*.

They speak loud. They have to, under the insects and the pumps. Close up they speak into the chest — a hand on the other's sternum, a low voice, the words carried through bone along with the chord — and at a distance they speak with their arms, a signal-language of the crews that the growers have half-adopted, so that two people on opposite sides of a bed can carry on an argument over the tree-fern crowns without a sound. And underneath both of those, the plateau has its own tongue, which is the dialect of the maintenance crews and which is made mostly of names.

Every valve on the plateau has a name, and the name is not a label but a sentence. It says where the valve is by counting from the trunk main, and what it does, and what it sounds like when it is well, and what it did once, long ago, that no one has forgotten. *Third return, weeping, that scalded Anse.* The crews learn these the way other people learn kin, and they name their children from them. A child born while its mother is on the north sluice is called for the valve she had her hands on when the pains came, and so the crews go through life as Weep and Seat and Half-Turn and Scald, and think nothing of it, and a stranger — but there are no strangers — would hear a roll-call of the crews and think he was hearing the plateau describe its own plumbing, which he would be.

There are four kinds of people here, and they are less kinds than positions, the way a snail on a rail and a snail on a gauge-face are the same snail differently placed. There are the growers, who live in the beds and under the canopy and are the most numerous and the greenest — their clothes rot on them and are replaced, their hair is always wet, they smell of soil and fig-milk and the sulphur of the fog. There are the crews, who live in the pump-houses and the galleries under the plate and have the orange hands and the valve-names and a way of standing with the head cocked, always, listening for a change in a sound that has not changed in their lifetime. There are the tower-keepers, twelve families for twelve towers, who live above the canopy in the wind and are dry — the only dry people on the plateau — and who are considered by everyone else to be slightly mad, because they choose to be where the chord is faint and because they eat pigeon. And there are the stokers.

No one has seen the boilers. That is true. But someone goes down. The stoking galleries are reached by a spiral of iron stair that descends through the plate into a heat that closes the throat, and at the bottom there is a long low room of chutes, and coal comes up the chutes on a rattling belt from somewhere below the plateau, from the piers, from the cloud, and the stokers break it and feed it down other chutes toward a light that comes up red and is never approached. They wear wet moss on their faces. They work short and come up often. They are blind in a way the others are not — not the cloth-blindness of the sun but a slow scalding of the eyes that leaves them milky by forty — and the growers do not marry them, and the crews do not name their children for anything in the galleries, and yet the stokers are the ones the whole plateau is quietly turned toward, the way the fig roots are turned toward the trunk main. The heat is theirs. The fog rising through the decks is theirs. When a stoker comes up the spiral at the end of a shift and walks out under the canopy with the moss steaming off his face, the growers step aside on the path, and it is not quite deference and it is not quite fear. It is what you would feel if the sun came down a stair.

Now the animals, because the people cannot be described without them and would not want to be.

The lizards have the light. This is an arrangement, though no one made it. The open plate at midday belongs to them absolutely — the ranks of pale bodies on the pale rivets, the rust-red rind along each spine like a seam of the iron showing through — and the people, blind in their cloth, walk among them without disturbing them, and the lizards do not move, because nothing that walks in the blur has ever been a threat to them. In the evening when the plate cools they go down through the cracks into the gap between tile and plate, into the warm air over the mains, and at that hour a grower crossing the open will feel them pouring past her ankles like a dry wind. Children try to catch them and cannot. Old people say the lizards are what the plateau dreams of being when it is asleep, which is a saying and not a belief, and means: hot, still, the color of itself.

The snails belong to the crews. They are on every rail, big as fists, and the crews read them. Where the snails cluster, the moss is thick; where the moss is thick, the iron under it is weeping, and a weep is the first word of a failure that will not arrive for twenty years. So a crew-woman walking a main in the morning is not looking at the pipe. She is counting snails. She will lift one — heavy, cool, the shell the same pale chalk as the lizards, everything up here that lives in the light goes that color — and put it down elsewhere, gently, a colleague moved to another shift. They are also eaten, boiled, and taste of the boiler-house, and the shells go into the beds and are what makes the soil grey in its lower layers.

The goats belong to no one and to the edge. Small and black and hateful, sure on the lattice as the crews are, they live out in the wind where the plateau is being cut for its iron, and they come in at dawn through the outer galleries to strip the bamboo, and the growers curse them and throw things, and at the edge the cutters milk them. The cutters are the lowest of the crews, the ones who go out with saws to take the old outer decking a hand's width at a time and drag it back to the forge, and they live half the year out there in the wind in shelters of scavenged plate with the goats for company, and they have come to resemble them — black-clad, narrow, unsentimental, with a way of looking at the plateau's center as at a thing that is eating them. The cutters say things. They say there are goats on the sixth deck down that are not black. They say that when the wind drops at the edge you can hear, from below, a sound like the chord but not the chord — a different note, older. No one goes to check. The cutters are known to say things.

The swifts belong to the stokers, or the stokers to the swifts. They nest in the vents of the galleries, in the hot black updraft, and their young are sooty, and at dusk they come out in a body screaming and the stokers coming off shift stand at the vent-mouths with their milky eyes turned up and listen to them go. A stoker's child is told that a swift never lands. This is not true but is the kind of not-true that is meant to be kept.

Frogs in the sluice-pools, whose ancestors nobody can account for. Crabs in the overflows. The people have an answer for everything they cannot account for, and it is the same answer, and it is: *it came up in the cloud*. The frogs came up in the cloud. The grey crabs came up in the cloud. A grower with a child that does not look like her husband will say, with no irony at all, that it came up in the cloud, and everyone will nod. The cloud is the plateau's word for the place things arrive from that were not sent for. Heat comes up in the cloud. Coal. Fog. Whatever the piers are standing on.

And what goes down is what the plateau is finished with. Water, in the hundred silver ropes off the edges. Cut fig-root. Shed struts. And — once, in the memory of the old, or once in the memory of the old people's old people — a person.

There are two ways to leave the plateau and the people are very clear on the difference. The first is the bed. When someone dies they are carried under the canopy to a trench opened between the fig roots, and laid in, and the cut bamboo from the season's clearing is heaped over them, and within a year the trench is soil and within five the fig has found them. That is why the beds are deep. That is what *centuries of soil brought up in the pockets of the dead* means, up here: not that the dead carried soil, but that the dead are the soil, layered grey with snail-shell and black with bamboo-rot, generation on generation, the whole forest standing in its own ancestors. A grower will tell you which fig is her grandmother. She means it plainly. She eats from it. The dead go into the bed and come back as fruit that tastes of sulphur and that is the ordinary way, the way of nearly everyone, and it is not grieved much, because it is not really leaving.

The second way is the cloud. Every few generations, someone goes down. Not falls — the cutters fall, sometimes, off the edge in the wind, and that is an accident and is grieved in the ordinary way and their bed-trench is opened empty. Goes down: climbs onto the lattice at the edge with a coil of rope and a sack of figs and starts, deck by deck, toward the white. There is always a reason and the reason is always different — a quarrel, a stoker's milky eyes wanting to see something before they close, a child of the crews who has heard the cutters' talk of the other note. What is the same is that they do not come back. No one ever has. The plateau's whole history holds perhaps nine of these and knows each one by name and none by fate, and they are the only people up here who are truly gone, because there is no fig with them in it. When the fog comes up in the morning, thick and warm and smelling of iron, the old say — not a belief, a saying — that it is the breath of the ones who went down, coming back the only way it can.

They do not think about the plateau falling. This must be said, because a stranger would think of nothing else, standing on riveted iron over fourteen decks of rusting lattice over a cloud. They do not. When a strut lets go far below and the note comes up through the feet, they stop, and they count. Every person on the plateau carries a number, which is how many struts have gone in their lifetime, and the old have large numbers and the young have small ones and the numbers are a kind of age truer than years. A woman with forty struts has seen more of the plateau's life than a woman with twelve. That is all it means. It has not fallen yet. What has stood will stand. They hold this the way the lizards hold the rivets — not as a belief, not as a hope, but as the temperature of the world, the thing that is simply so.

It would be easy to say that the people are the plateau's caretakers, and the crews would say so, and be a little proud. But stand at a bed's edge in the evening when the pumps have cycled down and the forest is ticking and rasping like a second machine, and watch a grower go by with wet hair and orange hands and cloth pushed up on her forehead and her eyes wide and clear in the green dark, and it is hard to hold the distinction. The moss holds the heat in and drinks the drip. The snails eat the moss that eats the iron. The figs pry the tiles and the crews cut the figs and the cut figs go in the beds and the beds are the dead and the dead are the soil the figs stand in. And the people — with their thick blood made for this air and their eyes made for this dark and their names made of valves — the people cut back the roots and scrape the gauges and feed the fire and go into the trench, and the plateau is warm, and steams in the morning, and rains in the afternoon, and has not fallen yet.

They are one more thing that grew on the iron. They are what the machine turned out to need in order to go on being a forest. In the night the whole surface hums with insects and under the humming the chord goes on and on, three or four octaves below hearing, and every chest on the plateau is shaped to it, and in twelve brick towers above the canopy the tower-keepers lie awake in the wind with their palms flat on the floor, waiting for it, and down in the galleries the stokers break coal that came up from somewhere in the cloud, and on the outer decks the cutters sleep among goats and do not listen for the other note, and at the edge the water goes off in silver ropes into the white, and does not come back, and no one is watching it go.
