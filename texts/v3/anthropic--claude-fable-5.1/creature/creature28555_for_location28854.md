Something does.

You will resist this for a while, because everything you have learned so far argues against it. Life is a slope. It lives in the difference between one thing and another — between the sun and the shade, the sea and the air, the inside of a cell and the outside — and it runs downhill on that difference the way water does, and where there is no downhill there is no running. You have put your cheek to this ground and found it the temperature of your cheek. The plateau is level in every sense the word has. Whatever burned here has spread itself so evenly that nothing can flow anymore, and so, you reason, nothing can live.

But there are two slopes left. You have found one of them. It is red, and it is at the bottom of a stack, and it is hotter than the world by the smallest amount that still counts.

The other one is you.

---

The first of them you meet the way you met the first stack: as a place where the stars stop.

It is a narrow absence, standing beside a pipe trestle perhaps thirty meters from a hall, and it is too thin to be a stack and too tall to be a switch lever, and it does not resolve into anything else no matter how long you look. You walk toward it with your hand out, the way you have walked toward everything here. Your hand finds it at about the height of your shoulder.

It is, of course, exactly the temperature of your hand.

That is the first thing, and it is the thing that will keep you from ever being sure. You cannot tell what it is made of. You can tell what it is shaped like. Under your palm it has a slight give, not the give of flesh but the give of something that was flesh a long time ago and has since been dried by an air with no water in it, so that it has the texture of an old belt, or a book left open in the sun for a century. It is upright. It rises past your reach. Where your hand rests there is a curve that could be a shoulder or a haunch, and below that a long straight fall that could be an arm, and at the bottom of the arm, where your hand follows it down, there is a hand, and the hand is on the pipe.

Not resting on it. Closed around it. You feel for the join between the fingers and the metal and you cannot find it. The knuckles have been rounded like the rivets on the iron door, and the pipe where the fingers meet it has been rounded to receive them, and the two have arrived at a single continuous surface. You think of the door fused into its frame. You think: the wind did this. The wind wore the hand and the pipe down together until they were one shape, and then the wind stopped.

You stand there with your palm flat against it for a long time, waiting for it to move.

It does not.

You do the only thing you know how to do here. You kneel, and you find where its foot meets the dust — there is a foot, or a base, something with a heel — and with your thumbnail you scratch a line in the crust of the dust beside it, a finger's width away. Then you stand and you walk to the hall and you put your hand on the flywheel and you find your first scratch, exactly where you left it, on exactly the same tooth.

---

You will come back, and what you find when you come back will decide nothing.

The line you scratched beside the foot is still a finger's width from the foot. Or it is a finger's width less a hair. In this dark, with your thumbnail as your only instrument, the difference between those two is not a difference you can measure. The place has done to you what it does to everything: it has brought the question to a rate at which the answer is indistinguishable from no answer.

But you begin to notice them, now that you know the shape to look for.

They are at the nodes. That is the first pattern, and it is the same pattern as the stacks and the halls and the switches, because they are part of the plan in the same way. One stands at the base of each stack, hand on the iron door. One stands in each hall, hands on the rim of the wheel. One at each switch, hand on the lever, where you had thought the lever stood alone. Along the pipes, at each trestle, one hand on the pipe. You had walked past a dozen of them before you touched the first, because in the dark a column of missing stars is a column of missing stars, and the plateau is full of upright things.

And they are not all the same height, and they do not all stand the same way. Some are bent. One, at a switch, is doubled over with both hands on the throw-bar, its back a long curve against the sky, in the posture of someone who has thrown their whole weight into a lever and has not yet felt it give. One in a hall stands with its hands high on the wheel and its head — if it is a head — tipped back, as if watching the rim come around. You feel, in these postures, something you did not feel in the stacks or the rails. The stacks were built and then abandoned to the wind. These were caught. Each one is in the middle of something.

---

You will want to know what they are, and you will have to assemble it from what the plateau will give you, which is texture, position, and time.

They are not the ones who built this. The ones who built this left, or died, and their leaving or dying is in the dust; the dust is what the wind made of everything soft and everything unfixed, brick corners and iron flakes and, you have to assume, bone. No. These stayed. Whether they stayed because they chose to or because they were what was left when the choosing was over, you cannot tell and it may not matter. What matters is that they understood something about the place before you did.

They understood that as the world went level, anything that meant to keep living in it would have to go level too — but not all the way. Would have to stay a hair warmer, a hair faster, a hair steeper than the flat around it, and pay for that hair out of whatever slope remained. And the slope that remained was going into the ground through the stacks, into the throats where the red still was, and the machines were the only things that still knew how to draw on it. So they went to the machines. They put their hands on the levers and the wheels and the pipes, and they slowed themselves to the rate at which the machines could feed them, and the rate at which the machines could feed them fell with the slope, year by year and then age by age, until they were moving at the speed of the wheel, which is the speed of the world plus one hair.

That hair is their whole life. Everything they are happens in it.

The click you heard, standing beside the rail line: that was one of them. Not a relay. A hand, finishing a movement it began before the wind stopped, arriving at the end of its travel, touching the stop. Somewhere in the grid one of them completed a gesture. In some hours there will be another click, and it will be another of them, or the same one beginning the gesture back. A dozen sounds across the hours. A dozen hands.

And the note. The pressure behind your sternum, the thing you could not tell from your own blood. You will stand among them long enough that you stop being able to hear it as anything but a voice. Not words. One sound, sustained, so low and so slow that its pitch is not a pitch but a duration — a syllable that takes longer to say than you will be here. All of them saying it, or one of them, or the machines they have grown into saying it through them. You cannot know. You put your ear to the fused hand on the pipe and the note is there too, in the metal, in the hand, no louder and no different. Everything here is at one temperature and one pitch and one speed, and they have joined it, all but a hair.

---

What they tend is the red.

You go back to the first stack and you kneel at the door and you put your eye to the gap, and it is there, unchanged, the color of metal that is barely more than nothing. Above you, standing against the door with its hand fused into the iron, is the one that has stood there since the door was last opened. You cannot tell whether it is keeping the door shut or trying to open it. You cannot tell whether the red is what it is feeding or what it is feeding on. You suspect that at this speed the two are not different things — that a hand on the door is a conduit, and heat goes through a conduit whichever way the slope runs, and the slope here runs so shallow that in a lifetime it may reverse and reverse again.

But you think you understand what the red is for. Not warmth; there is no one to warm and nothing to warm them from. The red is a direction. As long as there is one thing on the plateau hotter than another there is a downhill, and as long as there is a downhill there is a *next* — a next click, a next tooth, a next fraction of a turn. They are not keeping it burning. They are keeping it from finishing. Every one of them, at every node, hands on the plan, is holding the last gradient open by the width of a hair, and living in the width.

When the red goes, they will not die. There will simply be nothing left that distinguishes them from the door.

---

Then there is the other slope. The one that walked in.

You are the largest disturbance this plateau has known since the wind. Your breath is a weather system. Each exhale is a plume of moist air a few degrees warmer than the world, rising and thinning and being absorbed, and it is the only such plume in kilometers. Your footprints are events. The crust of the dust that has been coherent for ages is broken in a line behind you, and the line points to where you are.

They have noticed.

You will not see it happen. But when you go back to the first one, the one at the pipe, and you put your hand where the shoulder was and follow it up to where the head should be, you will find the head very slightly turned. Not toward you. Toward the place where you knelt, hours ago, and scratched your line in the dust. The angle is small enough that you could be imagining it. You go to the hall and the one at the wheel has its head tipped a hair less far back. You go to the switch and the doubled-over one is doubled over by a hair less, as though it were beginning, at the bottom of its long throw, to straighten.

All of them, across the whole grid, have begun to turn. They are turning the way a plant turns toward a window, on a timescale that has nothing to do with the plant's intention, and what they are turning toward is not you but your past — the last place the slope was, the last place they registered a warmth that was not the world's. You are always moving. They are always arriving where you were.

You could wait for one of them to reach you. You work out roughly how long that would take, from the width of the turn and the length of your absence, and the number you arrive at is not one you can stand still for. You would have to stop breathing. You would have to become their temperature and their speed, and you would have to put your hand on something, and you know what would happen then, because you have felt the knuckles.

---

So this is what lives here.

They are not many, and they are not few; they are exactly as many as the plan has nodes, one to each. They are the temperature of the place, so you cannot feel them. They are the pitch of the place, so you cannot hear them. They move at the speed of the place plus the smallest increment that still counts as moving, which is the same increment by which the red is hotter than the dark, and it is that increment they live on, and inside, and for. They are the caretakers of a slope so shallow that only the world can tell it from level, and they are turning, every one of them, toward the memory of you.

When you go, they will go on turning. They will arrive, eventually, at the places you stood. One of them, an age from now, will complete its long unbending from the switch lever and lower a hand to the dust and find your footprint, and rest its palm in the print, and find the dust there exactly the temperature of its palm. That will be all it can learn. Not who you were, or where you came from, or which way you went. Only that something came through and pressed the crust and left, and that the shape it left has held.

They will know you the way you knew the wind. By the wearing. And they will stop at the stage they reached.
