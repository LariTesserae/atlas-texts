**Who Lives in the Cistern**

Begin with the plant, because it was here first and will be here last, and because for a long time it was the only tenant.

It is one thing. That is the simplest way to say it, and it is very nearly true. Whatever it was when it first took hold on the ground-flat ruin of the summit — a moss, a mat, some tough high-altitude cushion that learned to keep its dead — it stopped being a population of individuals so long ago that the question has no remaining edges. It is a single skin. From the outside, from the rim, it is a low dull green that a passing eye would take for lichen or turf, forty kilometers across, humped a little toward the center, pocked with the shallow pans where snowmelt gathers before it sinks. That green is the whole of the plant that is alive. It is perhaps three meters deep. Below it is everything else.

The living skin faces two ways. Its upper face takes the thin fierce sun and the knife air and does the slow work of turning both into more of itself. Its lower face is pale, soft, faintly furred, and hangs in four-degree water in the dark, and it is the only living surface the whole cistern has. Everything that happens underneath it happens because of it. What it grows, it grows downward: it thickens a little each year at the bottom, and the bottom gets eaten a little each year, and the bottom gets compressed a little each year by the weight of the top, and the net of the three is nothing. The plant is not aware of this. The plant is not aware of anything. But if it were, it would be aware chiefly of pressure — its own mass pressing it down onto its own dead, the sun pressing on its back, and the enormous cold body of water beneath it that it made a lid for and cannot feel.

The dead part is the rest of the mountain. Chamber walls smooth as bone. Pillars that were once the stems of the plant's own upward reaching, in the age when it was still building. Floors that were ceilings once. All of it laid down cell by cell and then abandoned, the way a shell is abandoned by the soft animal moving forward into the next whorl. The plant has no memory of building any of it. It does not know that it has rooms. But the rooms are its body, in the way that heartwood is a tree's body, and the water in them is inside it, and so, in a sense that becomes more difficult to dismiss the longer you stay, everything that lives in the cistern lives inside the plant. It is the house and the landlord and, distantly, the harvest.

---

Directly beneath the living skin, pressed against it, are the ribbon-things.

They are the closest any animal here comes to the light, and they are separated from it by three meters of matted growth, and they have never known it is there. They are flat — flatter than seems possible for a body that eats and moves and divides — and long, a hand's width and an arm's length, and they lie against the underside of the lid the way a leaf lies against a window, held by a film of their own mucus and by the fact that there is nowhere else they would go. They have no eyes and no head worth the name. They have a rasping mouth along one edge and a sense, spread across the whole upper surface, for the difference between growth that is soft and growth that has begun to set.

They eat the soft. They take it in a slow scraping pass, an edge at a time, and they move on when the surface under them hardens, and they are so numerous and so well distributed that the lower face of the lid is grazed to a uniform pale nap, like a lawn. They are the plant's only gardeners. Without them it would thicken; the rooms below would shrink over an age; the chord would rise. With them, nothing changes, and neither party has any notion that it is party to anything.

When they move they crackle. Their bodies are stiff and thin, and shifting on the ceiling flexes them, and the flex makes the dry small sound of a page being turned in a quiet room. In air you would not hear it from a step away. In the water of the cistern it goes everywhere. Down the shafts it is a constant faint rustle, thousands of pages, the sound of the ceiling reading itself.

They die and let go. This is the other thing they do for the cistern. A ribbon-thing at the end of its life loosens, peels, and begins to fall, flat and turning, through four hundred meters of dark water, and it takes a very long time to reach the bottom, and it does not always reach it whole. Everything below the lid is fed by that fall — by the ribbons and their dung and the shed cells of the plant and the smaller unnamed things that live in the film — a slow inverted snow, sinking from the only lit surface toward the dead heart of the mountain. It is not much. Spread across a volume this size it is almost nothing. But the cold keeps it, and the dwellers below are patient, and almost nothing, here, is enough.

---

Lower, in the middle rooms, the knockers.

Here the walls are old and hard and the shape of every chamber is fixed, and the knockers know the shapes. They are jointed, plated, many-legged, low-slung — the small ones could sit in a palm, the large ones would cover a table — and they walk the walls upside down or sideways with equal indifference, because down is only a direction the water is slightly heavier in, and it does not concern them. Their plates are smooth and colorless. Their legs end in hooks that find purchase on the bone-smooth surface in ways that the surface, being grown and not carved, was never designed to prevent.

One of the front pair of limbs is not a leg. It is a hammer — thick, blunt, held folded until it is used — and the knocker uses it by drawing it back and bringing it down against the wall, once, hard. The sound goes out. The sound comes back. From each wall of the room, from the pillars, from the floor and the ceiling and the arches of the doorways, a copy of the knock returns at its own moment and its own strength, and the knocker, holding still, receives them all through its plates and its legs and the water against its underside, and knows the room. Knows it entirely: its length and its ceiling and where the pillars stand and which doorway leads down. Knows also — this is the part that matters — whether the room has changed since the last knock. It has not. It has never. But the knocker checks, and waits, and checks again.

And knows the others. Every knocker in the room is a hard body among soft walls, and hard bodies return the sound differently, and so each knock is also a census and each answering knock is a reply. Two knockers in a chamber fall into an exchange — strike, wait, strike, wait — that is not a rhythm because neither is keeping time, only listening to the other's echo before making its own. Six in a room produce something that almost becomes music and never does. Across the whole cistern, in every middle chamber, the knocks tick without pattern and without pause, and they are the pulse of the place, the closest thing it has to a clock, and they measure nothing, because nothing is passing.

The knockers eat what they find on the walls: the rooted filterers, the crust of fallen ribbon, each other's dead. When they die their plates outlast them. There is very little in the cistern that is hard except the walls, and so the floors of the middle rooms are strewn with a shallow litter of empty knocker shells, generation on generation, that returns the living knockers' echoes with a faint dry clatter the walls themselves do not make. The knockers hear this. It is part of the shape of the room. They do not know it is their ancestors.

---

The shafts belong to the callers.

The shafts are the great vertical rooms, the ones where the water goes down farthest without interruption, some of them wide enough to lose a village in and deep enough that the lid and the floor are both a long way from the middle. It is in the middle that the callers hang. Long, soft, eyeless, limbless, tapering at both ends, they hold themselves at whatever depth they hold — each has its depth, as each has its note — by a slow undulation that costs them almost nothing, and they open their bodies to the fall from above and take what drifts into them, and they sing.

A caller's note is made in a chamber inside its body, a stiffened hollow that it tightens and floods and empties; the mechanism does not need air, only muscle and water and a wall to work against, and the sound it makes is low and round and pure. A caller holds the note for as long as it can — minutes, usually — and lets it go, and the silence where the note was is not silence, because every other caller in the shaft is sounding, and then the caller takes the note up again a very little higher or lower, feeling for the place. That is the whole of its behavior. Feed, hang, sound, release, sound.

The notes of the population lie so close together, across the whole of the cistern, that there are no gaps between them. The sum is a chord that is not a chord — there is no interval in it, only continuity, a solid slab of tone from the lowest caller in the deepest shaft to the highest under the lid. It is the loudest thing here by far. The knockers' ticking rides on top of it; the ribbons' rustle is a texture at its edge; the long groan of the strained walls is its floor. It has been sounding, unbroken, since before the lid closed. Whether there was a moment when it began, whether the first callers came into a shaft where there was no chord and sang into an emptiness, is not a thing the cistern preserves any trace of.

The callers do not choose their notes. They find them. A young caller is born small and unpitched into a shaft already full, and it drifts, and it listens, and somewhere in the slab of tone there is a place that is thinner than the rest — where a caller has recently died, where a note has gone out — and the young one moves toward the thin place the way a fish moves toward a current, and settles at that depth, and begins, tentatively, to sound, and adjusts, and adjusts, until the thinness is gone and the chord is whole again. Then it holds. It holds that note at that depth for the rest of its life. When it dies, the note goes out, and the chord is thin there, and something young is already drifting toward it.

So the chord does not change. Only the singers do. If you could hear it from outside — and you cannot; the lid stops everything — you would have no way of knowing that the wall of sound reaching you was not being made by the same throats that made it a thousand years ago. In every way that the cistern can measure, it is.

---

Everything else lives on what falls.

The filterers are the humblest of it. They are rooted to the dead walls by a stalk and open at the top like a hand, and they hold their palms into the slow drift of the currents and close them on whatever comes. They do not move. They do not sound. They are the nearest thing the animals here have to the plant's condition, and it is possible they are on their way there — that in another age they will have learned to keep their dead as the plant did and begun to lay down their own small architecture on the walls of the mountain's architecture. But nothing here is on its way anywhere. They open. They close. The knockers eat them; the small quick things eat them; the fall replaces them.

The small quick things are almost not there. Finger-sized, transparent — a guess, like every color here — with a mouth and a gut and a tail and very little else, they dart among the filterers and along the litter of the floors and up the flanks of the callers, taking crumbs, and they make a sound so slight the water barely bothers with it: a flicker at the edge of hearing, a wrongness in the chord for the length of a heartbeat, and gone. They are the only creatures in the cistern that are ever in a hurry. They live briefly. Nothing depends on them.

And below the small quick things, below the sound of anything, in the film on the floors and the water itself, are the smallest tenants: the ones that finish what falls. The bodies of ribbon-things that reach the bottom, the dung, the filterers' cast palms, the shells of knockers — all of it is worked by things too small to knock or sing or rustle, and returned to the water as the faint rich cold broth that the filterers filter and the callers take into their open bodies as the water moves through them. It is a short loop. It is very slow. The cistern has been running it for so long that the floors of the deepest rooms are soft to a depth no one has measured with a sediment that is nothing but the cistern's own inhabitants, resolved.

---

That is where the large thing is.

It is known only by the chord. Sound moves through the cistern from everywhere to everywhere, and the walls return it and the water carries it and the callers are its source, and all of that is fixed — and so the shape of the sound in any room is fixed, and any animal in the room that attends to sound, which is every animal, carries the shape of that room's sound the way you carry the shape of your own house in the dark. In the deepest rooms, sometimes, the shape bends. A region of the chord goes dull, as if something were absorbing it. The dullness moves, very slowly, across the room, or does not move for a long time and then is gone. The knockers of those rooms strike and receive back a floor that is closer than it should be, and then, hours later, strike and receive the floor they know.

Whatever it is, it is soft. Hard things return sound; this one swallows it. Whatever it is, it is large — the dullness spans a chamber. Whatever it is, it lives on the floor and does not rise, and it moves rarely, and when it moves it does so along the deepest galleries where the sediment is thickest, and the guess that seems least wrong is that it feeds there: that it is the one animal in the cistern that eats the cistern's own accumulated past, mouth to the soft floor, taking in the resolved bodies of a thousand generations of everything above it.

Whether there is one of it or several, whether it is old past reckoning or is replaced as the callers are replaced, whether it is an animal in any sense the others would share or something the plant has done in its lowest and oldest and least attended parts — none of this can be known from inside the sound, and there is no outside. It does not knock or sing. It is the only resident that makes no noise, and so it is the only one that is *heard* rather than heard from: a place where the chord is thinner than it should be, moving slowly through the dark at the bottom of the world.

---

They have nothing. That has to be said again, because it is not a lack. No eye has ever opened here, and so there is no such thing as a face, and no such thing as seeing another and being seen. No hand has ever closed on a tool. No animal has dug or built or gathered or hoarded; the plant did all the building there was ever going to be, and stopped, and the animals moved into the finished rooms and have kept them exactly as they found them, which requires nothing of them. There is no territory, because there is nothing to defend it against and nowhere else to go. There is no season, or there is only the faint tightening and loosening of the walls once a year as the thin sun outside comes and goes, a long groan that rises a little and falls a little and is part of the floor of the chord.

What they have is the sound. Every animal here knows where it is by sound and knows what else is there by sound and knows, if it can be said to know anything, that it is alive by the fact that it is making sound and the sound is coming back. The knock returns. The note fills the thin place. The rustle of the ceiling goes down the shafts. And because the water is a single body and the walls are a single body and the chord has no gaps, every animal in the cistern is, at every moment, hearing every other — softened by kilometers, folded into the mass, but present. Nothing here has ever been alone in the way a thing can be alone in the open. Nothing has ever been out of earshot.

This is probably what it is to live in a sentence that has ended. Not stillness — the water is never still, the chord never drops. Not silence. Only the condition of being one of the sounds in a room whose sound is already complete, so that everything you do is a thing the room has already heard, and everything you will do is a thing it will hear again, and the room does not change, and you are the room. The plant grows and is eaten. The knocker strikes and is told the same shape. The caller finds the gap and closes it and holds. Somewhere below, the dark thins and thickens and thins as the large thing feeds on what all of them have been. And the whole of it goes on sounding, forty kilometers wide and four hundred meters deep and four degrees cold, under a lid of living green that nothing beneath has ever touched, at the top of the world, where the stars do not twinkle and no one inside has ever heard of stars.
