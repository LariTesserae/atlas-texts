What lives here is the herd, and you already know that; the plateau told you before you were finished looking at it. But the herd is a word for a distance, and the question of what lives here is really the question of what is happening inside any one of them, so you pick one and stay with it.

It has a face. That is not obvious from a rise, where the animals read as boulders with a slightly warmer boulder at one end, but a few strides off you can find the front of it: a blunt, heavy prow, hairless around the mouth, the mouth itself a wide horizontal seam that opens no more than a finger's width when it takes lichen. There are nostrils, two dark ovals with a ring of frost around each where the last breath left a rime. The eyes are set far back and far apart and are not looking at anything. They are the grey of the water in the basins and, like the water, they seem to reflect only the ceiling. A membrane draws across them at intervals so long you will begin to think they have no lids. It is not clear that they see. It is not clear that there is much, under this light, that seeing would tell them.

What they do attend to, you come to suspect, is not light. Stand close and you can feel it on the skin of your face and the backs of your hands — the faint prickle, the dryness in the air, the charge. Each animal carries a field, and the field of the next one begins where this one's ends, and the rule that spaces the herd so evenly across the country is not a rule of sight or scent but a rule of pressure: the way two things pushing at each other agree, without deciding, on a distance. They know where their neighbours are the way you know where the wall is in the dark. When one dies, its field goes out over a few days as it cools, and the neighbours drift — over a season, over years — a little toward the gap, and the gap closes, and the density is restored. Nothing has to notice for this to happen. It is enough that the pressure is gone.

Inside, the mill. You can feel it if you leave your hand on the flank long enough, and if you put your ear to the hide, which is like putting your ear to a wall behind which someone is very slowly moving furniture. The stones it has swallowed are not random. A young animal takes grit; an older one takes pebbles; a very old one has, somewhere behind its ribs, a few cobbles the size of a fist that it found, or was given, or waited for, and that have been turning inside it for longer than you have been alive, and are by now perfectly smooth. When the animals die and the small things have done their work and the ribs fall, the gut-stones are left in a loose heap on the rock, polished to a dull shine, and these heaps are the closest thing the plateau has to a grave marker. You can read an age from them. A heap of fine dark sand is a calf. A heap of a dozen river-round cobbles that have never seen a river is a life that lasted long enough to walk a good part of the great curve.

There are calves. This is the thing the plateau does not show you at first because there are so few of them, and because they are the only thing here that is not the colour of stone.

Watch two animals whose fields have drawn them closer than the rule allows. It is rare. It takes a night when the air is at its coldest and driest and the coronas along their spines stand up like the hair on a cold arm. They do not turn toward each other. They do not touch with their mouths or lean their weight. The light passes between them — that small soundless blue, gone before you are sure of it — and then the ozone, and then they drift apart again over the following months as if the thing had not happened. It is impossible to say which of them will bear. In practice it is the one that stops moving for a year, and then two, and eats more, and radiates more, and whose flank, if you rest your hand on it, has a second, quicker turning under the slow one.

The calf, when it comes, is the size of a large dog and the colour of a fresh break in rock — pale, almost white, the grey not yet on it. It is hot. Not warm: hot, a small burning thing on the frost, steaming from the whole of its skin so that it stands in its own weather. And it is fast. This is the shock. Everything you have seen on the plateau moves below the threshold of attention, and here is a creature that shakes itself, that turns its head when you shift your feet, that walks across a basin in the time it takes you to cross it and puts its nose down into the skin of water and drinks the whole herd's breath at once. It will not go far. The cold is waiting a few strides off in every direction, and the calf learns very early that the only place it can survive is in the lee of a large body, and so it moves from flank to flank, from warm shadow to warm shadow, licking the condensation off the hides of animals that do not appear to know it is there. It eats lichen with the frantic, ordinary speed of an ordinary animal, and grit with it, and in its first years the grit begins to turn.

And then it slows. Not from injury or hunger. It slows the way water thickens toward freezing, from the outside in. In its first decade you can still watch it walk. In its second you have to be patient. Its coat darkens and coarsens and takes the iron colour as the iron accumulates in its blood; its field builds, and the nearest adults, feeling it, ease off a little to give it room; it stops shivering, because it no longer needs the heat that quickly; it stops turning its head. Somewhere in its fourth or fifth decade it is no longer a young animal on the plateau but a small part of the plateau, and the pale ring around its nostrils forms, and you would not pick it out from a rise. Growing up here is a settling. A calf is a stone that has not yet finished falling.

There are other lives, and they are not small in the sense of mattering little; they are small only in the sense of being small.

The coin-grazers in the hide are a hundred to a flank. Flat, ridged, the dull black of wet slate, each about the size of a thumbnail, they hold on with a fringe of hooks and crop the crust of salt and skin and frost that forms on an animal over a season. Look closely and you find they have arranged themselves in a lattice, each at the same distance from the next, and you understand that they, too, are keeping a field. The hide is a country to them at the density the plateau is a country to their hosts. When the animal dies and the warmth goes, they wait — a week, two — and then they walk. It is the only time you see them move, and they go in a body, a slow dark tide of coins down the cooling flank and across the frost to the nearest warmth, and the nearest warmth accepts them.

The worms are in the wet underneath, in the thin dark seam between belly and stone, and they are the colour of the frost and as fine as its fibres, and they eat what the animal leaves without having to move it. When the body goes cold they go down instead of across, into the rind, and wait for the next one to stand on that patch of lichen, which will be some years. There are always more of them below than you would think from what is on the surface. Turn a piece of the rind over with your boot and it is threaded with them, pale, unhurried, a second herd beneath the first.

And the lichen. The lichen is the one you keep discounting because it is the ground, and it takes a long time on the plateau to understand that the ground is one of the inhabitants. It is a single kind, or seems so — a low grey crust that in wet weather goes the brown of a bruise and in cold weather furs over white — and it grows nowhere but here and eats nothing but the rind and the breath, and there is no animal that eats anything but it. It puts the iron into itself from the rock; the herd takes the iron out into their blood and gives back water and heat and, in the end, bone; the bone weathers and the iron falls back into the rind and the lichen draws it up. If you ask which of them is keeping the other alive there is no answer. The lichen is how the herd feeds; the herd is how the lichen moves. From far enough away — from the height at which you could see the whole curve of the grooves, which you cannot — the difference between the two might not be visible at all. A single grey organism, spread across a continent, part of it fixed and part of it very slowly walking.

Nothing else. Nothing flies. Nothing runs. Nothing waits on a rise for a calf to stray. Whatever might have lived here that was quick and sharp and hungry in the ordinary way gave up around the time the trees did, and left the country to things that had learned the other way of lasting.

So you go back to the one you chose and you stand in its small warm weather and you ask the question that the place refuses: is there anyone in there.

It does not look at you. Its eye, when the membrane draws back, holds the ceiling and your outline as one grey. The mill turns behind the ribs. Along the spine, faintly, the corona stands up in a thread of frost-coloured light and lies down again. You do not think it is thinking about the lichen. You do not think it is thinking about the cold, or the calf it has not had, or the animal a few body-lengths off whose field it has been leaning against for thirty years. But there is the pressure of something — the same pressure as the silence, a thing-shaped nothing. The whole herd faces one way. The whole herd walks, at a pace below seeing, along a curve that was here before any of them and will be here after. Something is choosing the direction, or nothing is and the direction is chosen anyway, and whether that something is in this animal, or spread out among all of them at the speed of a slow blue spark, or is simply what a million small fields do when you put them on one plateau and leave them there, you have no way to ask and it has no way to tell you.

That, in the end, is what lives here: a body that has no centre and has never needed one. Each animal is a cell. The lichen is the tissue between. The coins and the worms are what any body carries. The breath goes out in one direction and the water comes back and the iron goes round. It has no eyes to speak of and it is not looking at anything. It is not waiting for you to leave. It is not waiting for anything, because waiting is a thing that happens between events, and there are no events here — only the state, breathing, in every direction, in a silence that is not empty but full of a very large animal minding its own long business.
