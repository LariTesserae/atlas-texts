**Who Lives on the Shelf**

The first answer is that nothing does. Not in the sense you mean. To live somewhere is to have been there yesterday and expect to be there tomorrow, and the shelf does not extend that courtesy. It makes, and revises, and withdraws, and what it withdraws does not go anywhere you could visit. So the honest census is: at this moment, a great many; a minute from now, a great many others; over any span long enough to be called a life, no one.

But that answer is too clean, and the shelf does not do clean. Because some things do stay. Not by resisting—nothing resists the shelf—but by finding some angle of existence the shelf has no purchase on. Every persistent thing here is a strategy. You learn to read them that way. You stop asking what a creature is and start asking how it has managed to keep being it.

---

Begin at the bottom, with the felt, because that is where the smallest and most numerous live and because they are the ones you have already met without knowing it.

You felt them through your soles. A quick shiver in the pelt, a grain-sized something moving fast and close under the surface, gone before the sensation finished arriving. There are more of them than there is anything else. They are the size the shelf's products are when they are first made, and they are made the same way, out of the same undecided matter; the difference is speed. A fist-sized product lies in a hollow and is stone and beetle and leaf and is attended to and is revised. A grain-sized product that is already moving is not attended to. It is too small to be a question. The shelf's revisions pass over it the way weather passes over a gnat.

So they live in the gaps of the shelf's attention. And they eat there. When a form is withdrawn—when a beetle-stone at the edge of your vision stops being either and the felt closes over the place—there is a moment, less than a breath, when whatever it was made of has not yet been taken back into the rock. The felt-runners are there in that moment. You never see it. You see the felt shiver toward a spot where something used to be, converge, disperse. They are scavengers of second thoughts. They live on the shelf changing its mind, and the shelf changes its mind constantly, and so they are the best-fed things in the country.

Whether they are separate from the felt or the felt is simply the part of them that holds still, you cannot tell. Press your palm to the pelt and hold it there. After a while you feel a faint traffic against your skin, a warmth that is not quite the rock's warmth, more distributed, more hurried. It may be that the whole understory floor is a single organism thinking very fast about nothing, and the runners are its thoughts.

---

The trunks carry a different tenant.

You saw the bark bulge and flow. That is them, or the visible part of them. They live inside, in the conveyed matter that moves up the columns on its lift-and-pause. Put your ear to a trunk and count the beat, and then count again a minute later, and you will find a hitch in it—a pause that lasts a fraction longer than the others, a lift that is not quite as full. That hitch is one of them. They are what happens when something being hoisted from the roots toward the crown declines, for a moment, to go.

They are long, or they are arranged in lengths. They are the color of the inside of the trunk, which is a color you have not seen and will not, and they have no outside. They have never been in the understory. They have never been in the crown. They live in the column, in transit, in the interval between being taken up and being delivered, and they have made that interval their whole country. When a trunk is revised—when you turn and there are three where there were two—they are in one of the three, or all, or the one that no longer exists, and there is no way to ask which.

What sustains them is the hesitation itself. Everything the shelf conveys through the trunks is meant to arrive: light, water, the mineral fog, taken in at the crown and passed to the rock. The trunk-riders take a little of it in passing. Not much. A tithe on the hoist. They are the friction in the machine, and the machine has not decided to be rid of them, perhaps because a machine with no friction at all would run so smoothly it would not know it was running.

---

Above the trunk-riders, at the top of the columns, under the second lid, are the ones you have only inferred.

The canopy shifts like a hide with something moving under it. You have watched it fold and bunch and settle. Down here in the dimness the natural assumption is that there are creatures up there, many of them, large, moving among the broad leaves the way apes move in a real forest. But you have never seen one come down. You have never seen one fall, or a leaf torn loose by a body's passage, or anything at all descend but water and fog and the dim light.

What lives in the crowns, then, is not many things. It may be one thing. Or it may be that many things have been pressed together up there so long, in that near-touching abundance, that they have stopped maintaining the distinction. The canopy moves with a single logic. When it bunches on your left it flattens on your right, as if a weight had shifted. When a vent below breathes out its fog, the leaves above lean toward the rising column, all of them, and drink, and settle.

You come to think of it as the shelf's skin. Not made by the shelf—nothing here is made by anything, everything simply arises—but the layer where the shelf meets what falls on it, the first thing the light and water touch. Because it lives at the top, in the crowns, where nothing from the understory ever looks, it is the least revised thing in the country. It has been left alone. It has had time to become itself. It has had, perhaps, more time than anything else here, and it has used that time to spread until there is no more room, and now it simply receives, and shifts its weight, and leans toward the fog.

If anything on the shelf knows what the shelf is doing, it is up there. It does not come down to say.

---

Now the understory proper. Now the ones you can almost see.

The low, many-legged things the size of dogs. They move smooth and close to the ground, and they stop when you stop, and are gone when you look again. You have decided they are one kind of animal. You should undecide this. They are a solution, not a species, and the solution is: present a fixed face to attention and resume when attention passes.

While you are looking at one, it does not change. That is its whole trick. Every product on the shelf flickers under regard—stone to beetle to leaf—but these have found a way to hold, to be looked at without being decided, and the way is to give the looker nothing to decide. Low body. Even gait. A number of legs your eye cannot settle on but which does not, while you watch, alter. A hide the color of dim air. You can stare for as long as you like and it will stay precisely as ambiguous as it was, and then you will glance away, and it will be elsewhere, doing whatever it does when it is not being seen.

The too-many legs are not an error the shelf has failed to correct. They are the correction. A creature with a definite number of legs is a creature that can be counted, and to be counted here is to be attended to, and to be attended to is to be revised. These have kept the count open. They carry the shelf's indecision in their bodies as a kind of armor. They are what a form looks like when it has learned that the safest thing to be is a question that never quite gets asked.

Whether there is one of them, following you, or a dozen, or a class of thousands filling the understory from here to the horizon, each one stopping when someone stops—you will not resolve this. Neither, it seems, will they.

---

The cluster near the vent. Moth-sized things hanging motionless in the fog, then on the far side without crossing, then fewer, one of them now a leaf.

You took the individuals to be the animals and the cluster to be their gathering. Reverse it. The cluster is the animal. The moth-things are its parts, and it does not much mind losing them, because it can make more out of the fog, and what it cannot make more of is itself—the hanging, the number, the faint pressure of belonging that keeps a few dozen half-decided grains in the same place at the same time.

It lives at the vents because the vents are where the newest material is. The fog that breathes out of the fissures is the shelf's freshest undecided matter, the stuff that has been through the rock and come back out not yet anything. The cluster hangs in it and takes it in and grows a member, and somewhere else a member fails and drifts and is a leaf, and the count stays roughly what it was. It is pure appetite for the new. It has no interest in holding any particular form, only in holding a shape, an approximate quantity, a place in the air. If you scattered it with a wave of your hand—you will not; you have the sense it would count—it would gather again by the next vent, one fewer, one more.

---

The long damp creature with the salamander's gait and the mole's coat and the bird in the set of its head, which turns, and is different.

This one has chosen the opposite strategy from the many-legged. Instead of holding still under attention, it moves faster than attention can land. Every time you fix it as something, it is already becoming the next thing, and the shelf's revisions—which are, after all, a kind of attention—arrive to find the form already changed and nothing left to revise. It stays ahead. It is the most alive-seeming creature in the understory precisely because it never finishes being any creature; there is always a next one under the skin, and the skin is always halfway there.

You cannot say it persists as a something. It persists as a rate of change. The salamander does not survive; the mole does not survive; the bird was barely there. What survives is the turning, the shrug, the way the coat becomes scales as it moves away. That motion has been in the understory for as long as anything, and will be, restless, tireless, not a body so much as a habit the shelf has been unable to break.

It is also, you notice, the only thing here that looks tired.

---

The black basins. The trembling foam. The slow vortex, and the dim bruised light at its bottom.

Things live in the water. You see them when you lie flat and look down through the foam: shapes turning with the turning, at the same speed, so that they do not seem to move against the water at all but to be the water's way of having parts. They have no faces you can find. They may have no fronts. They live in the current by being the current, and the current is the only thing in the basin that is not revised, because it is not a form; it is a motion the light at the bottom makes. So they have attached themselves to that. They have stopped being things and become a way the water goes.

And on the rims of the basins, the watchers.

You have seen more than one, or one more than once. Heavy-bodied. A hide the color of the new rock, and—look closely—with the rock's grain in it, the sheen of something poured. A face you cannot bring into focus, not because it changes but because there is nothing there to focus on; it is not blurred, it is simply not decided, and it has not been decided for so long that the indecision has set. It does not eat. It does not move except to follow the rotation of the water with a slow turn of its head, and even that you begin to doubt is a turn; it may be that the head stays fixed and the shelf revolves very slowly around it.

This is what survives here longest, and the reason is the one you guessed: it has given up being anything in particular, and so the shelf has nothing to revise. But that is only the first layer. Sit with one long enough and you understand that it is not merely persisting. It is completing. Every other creature on the shelf is a strategy for staying undecided; the watchers have gone the other way, and become the only decided things in the country, and what they have decided to be is still. Their hides are taking on the rock's grain because they are becoming the rock. Not sinking into it—joining it. In a very long time, if the shelf has very long times, there will be a low, heavy outcrop at the rim of every basin, warm to the touch, faintly turned toward the center, and the shelf will have kept something.

Whether they are watching the light or being fed by it or feeding it, you cannot say. The light at the bottom of the basin is the color of the lid. The watchers sit between. It may be that they are the circuit—that the cloud and the water meet through them, that this is the one place the shelf's cycle closes on a form instead of passing through it. It may be that they are what the works are for.

You do not, in any case, disturb them.

---

Toward the edge, in the felt-lined passage where the lid comes down, the large unfinished things lean.

Do they live? They lean. They are warm. They have the character of waiting, and waiting is at least the shape of a life. Each one is the size of a trunk and is trunk and stone and beast in the way the small products are, but slower, heavier, with more at stake in the outcome. They are the shelf's ambitions. The small products are its doodles. These are its attempts at a thing large enough to matter, and it has not been able to bring any of them to a form that holds, and so they stand here in the dark near the newest rock, half-made, revising themselves so slowly you can watch a shoulder become a buttress become a shoulder over the course of an hour.

Among them—you see this once and do not look again—is one that has, on its lower flank, something like a hand. Five parts. A splay. A gesture of grip or offering. It is not finished. The parts do not agree on their number. It is being taken back even as you register it, the felt already climbing its wrist.

So that is settled, or it is not. The shelf has made hands. They did not hold. It made them the way it makes everything, out of warm indecision, and found no use for them, and withdrew. Whatever lives here does not need to build, and whatever needed to build has never lasted long enough to try. This is not a country waiting for someone to arrive. It has already, in its way, considered arriving, and declined.

---

There is a last answer, and it is the one the ground gives you when you have stopped asking.

The shelf forgets. That is what revision is: a forgetting, a taking-back, a return of the form to the rock without a record kept. The cliff face reforms and does not remember its old fractures. The hollow closes and does not remember the water. If the shelf were only rock and cloud and cycle it would be a machine with no memory at all, producing and withdrawing into a perfect blank.

What lives here is the memory. Not by intention—nothing here intends—but by the plain fact of having lasted. The felt-runners remember, in their speed, every withdrawal they have fed on. The trunk-riders remember the beat. The many-legged remember being looked at, and carry the memory as a way of standing still. The cluster remembers a number. The watchers remember the turning of the water, and are becoming the only record of it that will not be revised. Each persistent thing is a place where the shelf's forgetting has failed, and the shelf, which has no plan, has nonetheless kept those places, because you cannot make a world out of forgetting alone. You need something to have stayed.

They are not many, the ones that stay. Against the abundance of what flickers and is withdrawn they are a handful. But they are what the country has instead of a history, and the shelf, sighing, lifting, sinking, keeps making its thousand small transactions a minute around them, and every so often one of them holds, and moves off low through the felt, and stops when you stop.
