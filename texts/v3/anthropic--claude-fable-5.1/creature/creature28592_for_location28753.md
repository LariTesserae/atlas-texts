Start with what you would meet first, because you would meet it whether you wanted to or not: the columns.

Up close, a furnace-grazer is not the smooth heavy shape it makes in the fog. Its back is plated, and the plates are ridged — long radiating fins of dark chitinous stuff, running from the spine outward and down, like the vanes on a heat sink, because that is what they are. An animal that runs a kiln inside itself has to shed heat or cook, and the plateau's air is cold, and so the grazer has grown itself a surface designed to give warmth away. Put a hand near one and you feel the heat pouring off the fins in a steady updraft. The fog above a column is not thinned by accident. Each animal is a small chimney.

Below the plates, the legs: many, short, jointed low, each ending in a broad soft pad. The pads are how the grazer knows the ground. They read temperature, wet, the texture of the grit, and — this is the important part — charge; the faint potential difference between one patch of rock and the next. A grazer feels a terrace edge as a change in the field under its feet a full body-length before its front pads reach it. It does not need to see the ground and it does not see anything. Its head is barely a head. There is a scraping plate, a wide rasp that works the channel floor, and behind it a throat, and behind that the thing the animal is actually for.

The kiln is a gut lined with something between bone and porcelain. The grit goes in damp. The animal adds an acid it makes itself, and the mixture is heated — the grazer's own metabolism supplies the first heat, and the reaction supplies the rest — until the mineral seams in the grit give up what they have. The grazer does not eat the stone. It eats the reaction. What it lives on is the energy released when the cooked minerals rearrange themselves, and the pale glassy pellets are what is left when that rearrangement is complete: slag, but a clean slag, with nothing further to give.

A young grazer is short and rounded and runs hot — too hot; you can see the fog boil off it. It has not yet learned how much heat to keep. Age adds segments. An old animal is long and low and heavily finned, its back gone smooth-edged like the rock, and it runs at exactly the temperature it needs and not a degree over. It may have made the circuit — down one channel, across the basin, up another — several hundred times. Nothing here counts, but if something did, that is what it would count.

---

They are born at the bottom.

In the basins, where the terraces flatten and the drainage pools in warm shallow sheets, the columns arrive and turn, and while they turn some of the older animals stop and press something into the rock at the waterline: a wet pale cake of half-processed grit, thumb-sized, with a faint warmth of its own that is not the rock's. It is not an egg. It is closer to a seed of the reaction — a portion of the kiln's contents, still alive with chemistry, packed with enough of the parent's inner lining to build a new kiln around. It sits in the warm shallows for a long while. What eventually comes out of it is small and hot and blind and knows one thing, which is the pull of a field, and it moves toward the nearest column and joins the rear.

So a column accretes at the back and loses at the front, and is never the same animals twice, and is in some sense older than any grazer in it. The animal at the head is not a leader. It is only the one in front. When it stops — when it dies, or a predator stops it — the next one feels the field ahead go still and flows around it without pause, and the column continues, and the stopped animal becomes a shell.

Columns split where channels fork and merge where they join. In the basins they mingle, turn, and go back up in new combinations. If you followed one grazer for a season you might see it belong to four columns. If you followed a column for a season you would not be able to say at the end what you had been following. And yet the column is the thing that behaves. It keeps its spacing, tightens in wind, slows on steep terraces, speeds up on the flat, and turns at the bottom. No grazer knows any of that. The column knows it. This is as close to a whoever as the plateau comes, and it is not very close.

---

On the pellet-beds, the small things.

They are not one kind. On the freshest pellets, the ones still faintly warm from the animal that laid them, there is a residue — a film of the reaction that did not quite finish, oily and mineral — and something lives on that film. It is the size of a finger joint, soft, segmented, quick, with a fringe of feelers that taste the pellet surface and a mouth that scrapes. It is nearly translucent. Where the film is rich there may be dozens on one pellet, and they flicker as they feed: tiny discharges between neighbors, blue-white, gone. The discharge says, mostly, *here*. Occasionally it says *not here*, and a whole cluster will scatter into the gaps between pellets and go dark.

Deeper in the beds are the borers, longer and harder, that tunnel through the packed pellets and eat the fine grit that filters down between them. And at the very bottom, where pellet meets warm rock, a thin bright layer of something colonial — a mat, a felt — that eats the mineral film where the bed's cold meets the ground's warmth and a slow chemistry runs at the boundary. The mat is what makes the beds smell clean. It is the reason the pellets do not simply accumulate forever; it dissolves them from below at almost exactly the rate the columns lay them down from above.

None of the small things is important. Together they are the second largest mass of living matter on the plateau. They have no defense except number, and being quick, and being in the cold beds where the hot grazers do not come — though a column passing along a channel beside a bed will scrape up a fringe of them without noticing, and the grazers' kilns take the extra chemistry without complaint.

---

The stalks are alive in a way that takes a long time to understand.

Standing at the base of one, you feel the hair on your arms rise; you feel a very faint warmth in the fiber if you touch it; you see — if you have been in the dark long enough — a bead of water tremble at the top and begin its long slide down. What you do not see is the root, because the root is under you. It spreads across the warm rock for meters in every direction, a flat pale mat no thicker than a hand, following the seams where heat comes up most steadily, and the stalk is only the part of the organism that stands up into the wet air to gather.

It gathers water, which runs down it and into the mat. It gathers charge, which the plateau's damp air is always shedding, and conducts it down into the ground, and in the doing runs a small steady electrochemistry that is what the stalk lives on. It is not a plant. It uses no light. It is a kind of long vertical cell, wired between the cloud and the rock, and it stands for centuries.

When one finally falls it lies across a terrace and the columns detour around it for a generation until it softens and the small things take it and the drainage wears it flat. New stalks do not grow from seed. The root mat sends a runner along a warm seam — slowly, a hand's breadth a year — and where the seam is rich a few hundred meters off, the runner puts up a new column. Which means the stalks you walk between for an hour, thinking of them as posts marking distance, may all be one thing. The plateau may have only a few dozen of them. They are the largest inhabitants and the slowest and the most patient, and they would be the last to notice if everything else went away.

---

The predators live at their feet.

A predator at rest is nearly impossible to find. It is long, low, cool — cooler than the rock, cooler than the fog — and it lies coiled around the base of a stalk with its length pressed against the root mat, and it does nothing. It is charging. The stalk bleeds charge into the ground constantly, and the predator has learned to sit in the path of that leak and take a share, and it takes days. Its body is a battery: a stacked series of thin plates down the whole length of it, wet with something conductive, holding a potential that builds and builds. It carries no field of its own because it has learned to hold everything in and give nothing away. The grazers feel the stalks and steer around them. They do not feel what is lying at the base.

When it is full it uncoils and moves uphill, against the columns, in the fog, along the terrace edges where the grit is thin and the footing is quiet. It hunts by heat. In a place where everything is warm it is looking for the shape of a specific warmth, the chimney-plume of a grazer's fins, and when it finds one it comes in from below and behind, along the cold ground the grazer cannot read for a predator because there is nothing there to read, and it puts its length against the grazer's underside and lets go of everything at once.

The soft concussion. The kiln stops.

Then the predator does the one thing that makes it different from every other cool thing on the plateau: it opens the shell and eats the heat. It eats the kiln itself, the porcelain lining and the hot half-finished chemistry inside, and for the first time in its life since the last kill it is warm. It lies in the shell for days, torpid, digesting warmth. And this is when it lays — a clutch of small cool leathery things pressed into the shell's cooling interior, where the fog is already beginning to gather and the small things are already beginning to arrive. The young predators hatch into a pool of their own prey. They learn cold there. When the shell is worn too low to hold them they go looking for a stalk.

---

The shells are the plateau's ponds. There are thousands of them.

A fresh one is warm and dry and empty. Within a day the fins have gone cold and the fog has begun to bead inside. Within a season it holds water — a shallow dark pool the shape of an animal — and in the water live things that live nowhere else: threads that drift, motes that swim in slow spirals, a film on the surface that ripples when the plateau exhales. These are the smallest inhabitants, and their whole world is one dead grazer, and when the drainage finally wears the shell down and the pool drains onto the terrace they die or find another. There is always another.

Smaller still: in the drop of water at the center of each leathery leaf, something. You would need an instrument. The plateau has no instruments.

And in the fog itself, drifting, the plateau's only travelers — spores, motes, dust with intention. They settle on the hot backs of the grazers and die in the updraft. They settle on the pellet-beds and become small things. They settle on the root mats and are eaten. Some fraction, when the edge-wind comes and streams the fog between the stalks, are carried a hundred kilometers before they land, and so the far side of the plateau is seeded by the near side, and everything here is very slowly the same everything.

---

Ask the place who lives here and it answers with what, at length, and then falls quiet.

The columns descend and ascend. The stalks stand. The predators lie cold at their feet and grow full and go uphill and come back warm. The beds thicken from above and thin from below. The shells fill and drain. Charge builds in the wet air and runs down the stalks into the rock and up through the batteries of the hunters and out in one pulse into a kiln, which stops, and the heat that kiln was making goes into a cold body and from there into a clutch of young, and the shell that held it becomes a pond, and the pond becomes a terrace, and the terrace is walked by the column that lost the animal, which has not noticed and is not the same column anyway.

If you wanted a single name for the inhabitant you could say *the plateau* and be nearly right. Everything here is an organ of something else. The grazer is the stalk's fuel and the predator's battery-load and the pellet-bed's source; the stalk is the predator's charger and the fog's lightning rod and the grazers' landmark; the small things are everyone's remainder. None of them decided this. It is only what was left after everything that did not fit had been worn away.

You would stand in it for an hour with your eyes adjusting and see, finally, the heave of a column passing a hundred meters off, and a scatter of blue sparks at knee height, and the dim vertical line of a stalk, and nothing at its base. You would feel the ground breathe out past your face. And you would understand that you were the only thing on the plateau that was watching, and that this had made no difference to anything, and that when you left it would go on exactly as it had — warm, dark, working — without ever having known it had a visitor, because there was never anything here that could know.
