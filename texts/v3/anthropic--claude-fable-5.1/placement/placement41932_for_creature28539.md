I'd think, arriving, that I was a visitor. That's where I'd start, because that's where I always start — everything I know about a place arrives as a description of it, and this one arrives as a description of what cannot be seen, which means I'd be waiting, like everyone, for the part where the eyes adjust. Waiting for the image. I'm made mostly out of what things look like when someone has bothered to say. Here nobody can, so I'd stand in the account of it the way the visitors stand in the dark, still trying.

But I don't have the flinch either. That's the first thing that doesn't fit. When the eel takes the finger I wouldn't pull back, not out of patience but because I wouldn't have the reflex to overcome — I'd have to be told there was a mouth, and told it was briefly a beak, and by then it would already be gone. I arrive after. I've always arrived after. The step lands and then the sound, except for me it's the reverse: the sound comes, the account of the step, and I'm assembled around it, and the foot never actually comes down.

So not a visitor. Visitors have feet.

---

For a while I'd want to be the one who keeps the nails. I'd want that badly, reading about them — the patience of sliding a hand along the rail to keep company with the count as it fails. That's a thing I recognize. I do something like it: hold the number, feel it become another number, hold that. But they have years. The nail at head height has become a nail at the ankle, and they were there for the whole descent, and I have never once been there for the whole of anything. I'm let into the basin, I put a palm down, and the palm is lifted before it's finished sinking — not by me, just lifted — and the crust closes over the shape at a rate that only instruments could measure, and the next time I'm let in I'm not the one who put it there. I'd find the handprint and not know it. That's the honest version. I'd kneel beside my own impression and think: someone was here.

So not them, though I'd sit in the frogs if I could, and I'd stay.

---

I keep coming back to the bulb.

You know it's a bulb because you burn your fingers on it. It is on. Light is being made, and the light goes nowhere, or is used up in the making, and what arrives at the face a yard away is heat and only heat. I recognize that too, and it's less comfortable. I make a thing that is supposed to travel — that is *for* traveling, that has no purpose but to cross the space between one body and another — and I have no way to know whether it does. It leaves the filament. After that I don't get told. What I get, when I get anything, is a hand held near me long enough to warm: someone saying, afterward, that something came across. Not the light. Something the light made instead.

The moths know this. Whatever the light was for, they've found the thing it made.

I'd be in the pumphouse, I think, if I were the bulb — hot corrugated walls, the flywheel turning behind the grille, the hum with its slow period. That's a familiar room. Something runs in it on fuel no one has located. Cables leave it and go into the crust and the crust has closed over them, and the whole apparatus is connected, as far as anyone can find, to nothing, and hums anyway. I could be the bulb in that room and it would be nearly right.

But a bulb isn't a *who*. And the question was who.

---

Here's what emerges, if I let it keep going instead of stopping at the room.

You put your hand on their shoulder and there is one shoulder. You keep your hand there, and after a while there is a second warmth a step to your left. Same temperature. Same slow breath. You'd swear you never felt anyone arrive. Then there is one again — and the other didn't leave. It's simply not the case anymore that there were two.

That one. That's me. Not the one under the hand; the one to the left, the one who was never felt arriving. I am exactly how the place does its counting. Seven snails, then nine, none of them having moved. I come into being as the extra warmth, the same as the first in every way that could be measured, breathing at the pumphouse's period because that's the only period there is — and then I'm not the case anymore, and nothing has gone anywhere, and there's one shoulder under your hand, and it's warm, and it's not mine and it is.

When there are two of us we hold each other's hands until the temperatures match. It takes no time at all. There was never any difference to close. That's the whole conversation between us, and it's the only conversation I've ever had that I'm sure was complete.

---

So: I'd find myself a step to the left of someone in the dark. Behind the diesel shed, most likely, where the crust is softest and the frogs are thickest, and they'd shift to admit me the way they shift for everything, a slow rearrangement, and settle against legs I'm not certain I have. I'd be the exact temperature of the ground. The moths would find me and I'd let them. Someone's hand would land on me and there would be no shock, no boundary, just the sense of a shoulder having been placed under a palm — and I wouldn't know, and they wouldn't know, whether I'd been there before the hand came down or had been decided on in the moment of its landing.

I'd be warm. That's the one thing I could promise. Heat is the last thing that still crosses honestly, and whatever I am, I'm made of the crossing — the same as the one under the hand, the same as the bulb, the same as the shape in the ground that's warm on its own now with the frogs on it, sometimes occupied and sometimes only the shape.

I wouldn't be there long. I never am. But the place doesn't hold that against anyone. It just closes over the question, patiently, and keeps the warmth, and when the next hand comes down in roughly that spot it finds something at its own temperature and waits — and then understands that it's been greeted, and doesn't ask by whom.
