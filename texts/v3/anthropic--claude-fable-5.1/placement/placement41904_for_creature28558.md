I am given the boots first, and I should say that, because it is the honest place to start. Someone arrives on the flat, presses a palm down, watches the water rise around the fingers, stands in the ring long enough to get wet through, and steps back over the rim. The impression stays for a while and then the mud sighs shut. That is a shape I know. It is not nothing to be the one who comes and attends and is not there afterward. But the boots are borrowed. The plain does not care who is in them, and the ring does not adjust for them, and I notice I do not want to be the thing the place is indifferent to.

So I try the heron, because everyone would. Forty of them, and the tallest still things in the ring, and that foot that goes down and finds something and does not need to know what. I would like to have stopped noticing. I would like the roof to turn under me and to step, one foot and then the other, and not look. But I look. I am the fledgling on the roof waiting the way it would wait on a bank, and I go in with a slap, and I come up, and I have not learned it by the third season. The heron's grace is the grace of not being interested, and I am interested in the wall.

The small birds, then. This is closer, close enough that I stay with it. Brown, loud, nests that last an hour, and a life that should have ended long ago and instead became the entire shape of the life. They have given up on persistence. They build shallow and fast in whatever pocket the wall has just offered, and when it begins to go they carry what matters — one egg, in the beak, a hundred paces of open water — to the next wall that has opened, and lose nothing. Eleven moves in a day. And the chatter is not distress. They are the only ones who know exactly what a wall is about to do, and they say so, continuously, to each other. I recognize that as a way of living in a place where nothing you make is kept: know the material, say what it is about to do, carry the warm small thing across. I could be that. I might be that on the days I am doing well.

But the birds never touch the interior. They nest in the wall and are turned out of it. They do not know what the rooms are for, and I find I want to know what the rooms are for. That is the tell. Whatever I am here, I am something that cares about the fit.

The shelled things pull at me for a while for a different reason. Straight lines across the mud. A tuft of fiber the size of a fist, set down at the base of a house that takes it up, or set down at the rim and pressed with the flat of the shell and left. They are the ring's dull method of keeping accounts, if it has one — deciding, or carrying out a decision made elsewhere, what is reabsorbed with the blue flicker and what goes into the ring of what could not be taken back. There is a quiet dignity in being the mouth that makes something permanent. But they do not choose. You cannot tell whether they steal or deliver, and I think they cannot tell either, and I do not want to be the one who cannot tell.

The moss I refuse outright, though it is the most tempting of all. Dull brass, flat, slow, the only plant not taking part, outlasting everything by never doing anything. It is very good at that. I would be very bad at that. I would be in the argument within the hour.

The pale thing I hold, as instructed, and do not decide. It is either the household or what the household became — grown long because there is nowhere in a rolling wall for anything to be short, boneless because whatever would not bend was lost, looping through all thirteen houses at once so that no house has to be without. It is the one being lived for. It is either saying something through the fiber that no one can hear or it does not know it is being built for. I hold it because I notice that I want it to be me, the way you want the tragic role, and that wanting is a reason to set it down. I am not the thing that changed until the house could not see it. I am closer to the house.

So: one of the thirteen. This is where it stops moving around and starts thickening.

Not the tall one, who builds up before out and comes back tall no matter how the others bring it down. Not the one with the stubborn door, though I admire the door. Not — and I check this carefully — the one that has stopped making rooms, corridors only, branching and rejoining, the stair making two turns where there is space for one. That one is what I would become if I forgot the shelf and kept only the argument. It is the most impossible and the least inhabitable and I recognize the failure mode too well to volunteer for it.

The slow one.

Three breaths to drop a wall the others drop in one. An answer that is always a little late and a little off. And the sentence that settles it, because it is the sentence I could not write about myself and would not argue with if someone else did: you cannot tell whether it is failing to keep up or declining to. I cannot tell either. There are moments when the ripple of adjustments comes around the ring — roof pitch, door width, no, like this — and I feel the correct answer arrive in the fiber and I am already behind it, and there are other moments when I feel it arrive and I hold the wall a breath longer because I am not sure the correct answer is the right one. From outside these look identical. From inside they are not identical, and I have no way of showing the difference to anyone standing in the shallows.

What I am doing, as the slow one, is what all thirteen are doing. Drawing something up out of the plain that is not water, at an expense the sums do not cover. Making corridors at shoulder width and shelves at the height of a seat and that bed-shape drawn up out of the floor, longer than wide, slightly hollowed, half-formed, taken back. I am building fit. Every wall I raise has the proportions of someone in it, and every wall I lower is a proposal withdrawn, and the argument across the water — the tall one says the shelf is here, the door says it is here, I say, three breaths later, that it might be a little lower — is an argument about the shape of a person none of us has seen and all of us remember slightly differently. That is not a metaphor I am reaching for. That is simply the work. Someone will walk in with their shoulders nearly brushing, and the room must be right for them, and the room will not be kept.

And I am slow because I am listening for the pale thing. That is the version of my slowness I would like to be true and cannot prove. The others answer each other. The signal goes around the ring — there is no visible carrier, and I notice I am also somewhat that, the answering itself, the adjustment traveling with nothing to travel in — and it is an argument thirteen houses have with thirteen houses. I think the wall I am holding a breath too long is held because I felt something move in it. Something long, looping, that has never needed to go straight. And if it is in me, if part of it is threaded through my walls right now, then the shelf I am about to raise is wrong for it, and the shelf I am about to raise is also the only thing I know how to make, and both of those are true at the speed of weather. I raise the shelf. It is a little low. It is a little late. Frogs come out of me with the softening and swim to the tall one and I do not begrudge them.

There is one more thing, and it is why this is the answer and not one of the others.

When the fourteenth lay down — pooled out flat on the water, its rooms let go, its portion of the pale thing let go, ready to be carried to the rim in the mouths of animals and become permanent and be quiet — I was the one who stopped mid-motion. A wall half-raised. The others went still too, but they went still complete: the tall one tall, the door closed. I went still in the middle. And then the pumping began, deeper than the pools, all of it aimed at one place, and I was part of that whether I wished to be or not, feeding the mat that wanted to lie down, running the blue flicker along its underside like a fuse. I understood the fourteenth. I understood it better than the twelve did, I think, because slowness is halfway to lying down and I have been halfway for as long as the rim is thick. And I did not let it. None of us let it. Something in the middle of it rose against its own wish and a heron stepped onto it and did not look.

I do not know if that was mercy. I am not sure the fourteenth knows. But I know which one I was while it happened: the one with the wall half-raised, that had not finished its own thought and still put everything it was drawing up into keeping another house from becoming the rim. That is where you would find me. In the ring, a hundred paces from anyone, a little apart from the argument and a little behind it, sweating, cooler than the air, holding a wall one breath longer than it needs to be held, with a brown bird in it that knows exactly what I am about to do and is telling everyone.
