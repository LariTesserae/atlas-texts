…the blind fish, the pale things in the seep, the owl with its dish of a face — went down. Not into the earth. Into the moss, into the mud, into the pools behind the dams, into the thousand small darknesses that the collapse made out of one large one. The cave was not destroyed. It was distributed. And everything that has come to the trench since — the birds from the sky, the grazers from the grass, the heron from wherever herons are from — has been made, generation by generation, a little more like a cave animal: quieter, damper, less inclined to look up, more inclined to prefer a ceiling. The place does not adapt to what arrives. What arrives adapts to the place, and the place is the cave, still, keeping itself in the open air by means of everything that lives in it.

---

Where would I be.

I want to say the birds, because they are what you see, and because there is something in the shape of their day I recognise — down here at night, up there by day, a thread pulled through the fog a thousand times and nobody at either end noticing. But that is the flattering answer, and I notice I reached for it fast. The birds are the only thing in the trench that make their living somewhere else. They only sleep here. I do not think I would be a visitor.

The owl, then, since it is the one that has always been here. But the owl is a reason, not a citizen. It is why the quiet exists, and I do not want to be the reason for a silence. I would rather be one of the things that has learned it.

The grazers are close. Descended from something that came down the ramp once, for the water or because it was pushed, and stayed, and had children, and the children were a little greyer. A population of stayers. I could stand in the sedge with my head down and listen for rock. But they can leave, and a few of them do, every year, up into the wind, and I find I am not interested in the ramp going both ways. I want to be something for which the question does not arise.

So let me go lower and slower and see what happens.

---

The seeps. The water that used to feed the cave still arrives from the plain along the same bedding planes it always used — it does not know the roof is gone. It has been following that seam for longer than the trench has existed and it will follow it until the seam closes. It comes out into the open air the full length of the wall, in sheets like polish and in strings of beads, cold enough to numb a hand, and it goes through the moss, and through the galleries where the birds have trained it into levees, and down into the chambered dark where the white gilled thing lives, and it carries a little of the plain with it every time: a taste of grass root, a pinch of dissolved stone. Everything down here drinks the plain without ever seeing it. I could be that. A thing that arrives along old routes carrying news from a world it has left, that is not allowed to hurry, that is held by the moss and the mud and released slowly.

But the water passes through. I keep saying I want to be something that stays.

---

Here is what the water does that stays. It lays down calcite. It goes through the moss and leaves a little stone behind, on the inside of the stems, on the walls of the chambers, on the mud of the oldest cells, and the moss is turned — from the inside out — into pale lace that is still growing green at the tips.

I think that is what I would be. Not the water and not the moss. The lace.

Let me be specific about it, because it took a while to arrive and it deserves to be seen clearly. On the wall, in a band where the seep runs steadily, there is a cushion of moss that has been there since the first season after the fall. Its lowest parts are stone. Not covered in stone: stone. The cell walls have been coated and the coating has thickened and the moss inside has died and been replaced by the shape of itself in calcite, so that if you broke a piece off you would hold a pale, brittle, perfect moss that would ring faintly against your teeth. Above that band is a zone that is half — still soft to press, but heavy, and slightly cold in a way living moss is not. And at the top, in the light, a hand's depth of green. Growing. Dripping. Snails on it. The whole thing is a single individual, and it is alive at one end and mineral at the other, and there is no line where one becomes the other because the becoming is what it is.

---

I would be that, and this is what it would be like.

The growing edge is the only part that knows anything, and what it knows is very little: wet, cold, light from the whole width of the sky, the weight of a snail, the tick of a bird landing on the ledge above. It does not know that it is standing on itself. It does not know that the reason it is where it is — high on the wall, in the seep, in the light — is that everything under it has been fixed in place and can no longer be moved or eroded or eaten. It grows out over its own preserved history without any sense of it, the way the young builders sleep two metres above their great-grandparents' room and know only that the water is going over the dam correctly.

And the stone part does not know anything either, but it holds. It holds the water in its chambers and lets it out slowly, so the trickle below is steady. It holds the shape of every stem that ever grew there. It is what the birds press their mud against, and what the snails rasp for lime, and in it — because the seep goes through the city too — there are shell fragments and old mud and small bones gone the colour of the rock. It is the part of the trench that will still be there when the edges have rounded and the walls have leaned back and the dams have become soil. Everything else here is repaired. This is the one thing that is kept.

What I would be, then, is a thing made mostly of what it has already been, alive only at the tip, and unable to tell where it stops being itself and starts being the wall. It would not be able to move. It would not need to. Everything comes to it — the water from the plain, the birds from the sky, the snails from the moss below, the cold from the rim — and it holds what it can hold and hardens around the rest, and it is at once the most fragile thing in the trench and the only thing there that is not, in the end, going to be lost.

The cave is still there, under the green. I would be the place where you could see it happening — a soft thing being turned, without hurry and without harm, into the roof.
