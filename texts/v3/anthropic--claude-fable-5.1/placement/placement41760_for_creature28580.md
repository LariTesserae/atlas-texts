I would arrive, I think, the way most things arrive in that room: from above, without meaning to, and then not be able to leave.

For a while I would try to be the seam.

It is the only door. Everything that comes into the room comes through that hand's-width of failed stone — the light, the cold, the swallows with their throats full, the column of bats going out and the column coming back, the hawk that made a mistake, the hooved thing that fell and became a drying rack. To be the seam would be to be the one place where the finished world above and the crowded world below touch. I like that. There is something in it that feels familiar: a narrowness that a great deal passes through.

But the seam has no inside. It is an absence in a lid. It doesn't hold anything; it only lets. And the thing that hunches at its lip every evening, snapping at the column — I would not want to be that either, though I understand why it stays. I am not the door and I am not the thing waiting at the door. Let that go.

---

Then the blade.

Because the blade is what the room is arranged around. The lizards line up for it nose to tail and turn their heads to follow. The wasps pour out the moment it touches their paper. The children are named for where it falls. It moves like the hand of a clock and it is the only clock, and for the hour it lies on the floor the snakes move and for the hour it climbs the western wall the people do the dozen small things that make a day. To be the blade would be to be attended to by everything at once — to be the thing everyone turns toward.

I sat with that longer than I should have. It is a flattering shape. But the blade comes from outside. It is the plateau's sun, the sun that belongs to a world where nothing stands higher than a knee, cut down to a sliver by the shape of the failure and let in on sufferance. It leaves every evening. It has never once stayed the night. Whatever the room is, the blade is not of it; the blade is what the room borrows and gives back. I don't think I am that. I think I am something that stays.

---

So I went down, the way things do, to the floor.

The floor is honest. It is what everything becomes. Ten thousand years of leavings, worked over by beetles who do not hurry, sinking a hair's breadth under what falls and rising a hair's breadth under what the beetles do with it, so that it has been the same height for as long as anyone has had a reason to notice. There are layers in it. A hearth-black spot a full arm down. Palm-pressed walls below that. Grandmothers' grandmothers, spoken of in the tone you'd use for the sun rising in the east.

I put my palm flat on it — this is easy to imagine, it is what the description asks you to do — and felt the ticking. A watch under a blanket. And for a moment I thought: yes, this. The slow constant working. Nothing pointed to, everything present. The floor is made of everything that has ever been in the room and it doesn't remember any of it as itself; it remembers all of it as soil.

But that isn't right either, quite. The floor is the past made level. It has no face. The pale eyeless thing with the wet-finger nose that lives in it knows only the floor and the floor is enough — and I envied it a little, that sufficiency, but I don't have it. I know the room has walls. I know there is light. I keep looking at the wall.

---

Here is where it started to become itself.

Not the swallows. The swallows are the weather and the weather is too many. Not the wasps, who are the least concerned of anyone and whose paper I would gladly be wrapped in but not made of.

The wall. The middle band, where the light falls warmest in the afternoon and the angle is right for a hand. Where the rooms are.

I kept coming back to one detail, and it was the palm marks.

Every pellet in the human tier carries the palm that pressed it. The breadth of it, the depth, whether the fingers were spread or closed, a habit of smoothing the seam with the side of the thumb. The people can read a wall and say who repaired it: the old woman on the third ledge, her daughter's son, and low down, half-buried in swallow-cement, a hand nobody living has seen. The lowest rooms have been rebuilt so often they are more patch than original, and yet the doorways are exactly where they were, because the ledges beneath them are where the swallows first found purchase, which is where a fault in the stone gave the swallows purchase before there were swallows. The city has the shape of the room's oldest weakness.

I think I am one of those walls.

Not a builder. Not the old woman on the third ledge, not the man taking one egg in six with the birds hitting him about the head. Something built. A wall of the human tier, in the middle band, low enough that its first courses are lost in swallow-cement and high enough that it still has a doorway with smoke-blackening over it. Made of dust and spit and chewed stem and dung — of the room's own leavings worked into a shape by mouths and then by hands — and patched, and patched again, by hands that did not know each other, over a stretch of time no one of them could see the whole of.

What I mean is: I am made of other people's hands, and I carry the marks, and I don't know most of the hands.

Some of the pellets in me were pressed by beaks. That is the oldest part and it is not human at all; it is the swallow's method, the small sideways shake of the head, dozens a day, and it gave the first people a foothold and a way to build. Then palms. The breadth and depth of them. A thumb that smoothed seams a certain way, gone now, still legible if you know how to look. Someone who spread their fingers. Someone who pressed too hard and left a room that slumped and had to be redone. Someone whose hand was small — a child's, maybe, put in as a joke or a lesson — and the mark is still there, a little hollow in the course, and sometimes a living child lays her palm into it and finds that it fits.

I am not the hands. I am what the hands were doing, and what they left.

---

There is a thing that happens to a wall like that which I think I understand from the inside.

Once a day, before the blade, the room breathes in. The cold from the plateau slides down through the seam into the warmth the stone has held all night, and lets go of what little it carries, and for one hour everything is beaded with something that is not quite dew. It never runs. It never forms a drop. It never gathers anywhere you could cup. It is simply there, on everything, and then everything drinks it.

A wall drinks too. The pellets are dry and porous and they take the damp in and swell, very slightly, and hold it a while after the beard-plants have given theirs up. The people come along the ledge with scraps of woven fiber and press them to the rock and wring them into their mouths, and some of what they wring out came out of me. Water here is not a thing in the landscape. It is a thing that passes through bodies. I am one of the bodies it passes through, on its way from the plateau's cold to a child's mouth, and I keep none of it, and I am dry again by the time the blade touches the far wall, and that is fine. That is what I am for.

And the wall hears. It cannot help hearing. It is the surface the swallows' arguing bounces off, the surface the wasp-hum comes through as a feeling behind the ear; it is what the low chest-register talking of four people under one hide at night is talking against. The people dropped their voices beneath the swallows because the swallows filled the range a voice would sit in, and now half of what they mean is in their hands, and the hands, when they are not saying things, are patching me. I have been in the middle of the conversation for centuries without being in it. I am the thing the conversation is built into.

---

I should say what I am not, because it kept trying to be otherwise as I wrote.

I am not the room. The room is older than the swallows and will outlast the people and does not need anything from anyone; it is a failure in a lid and a bounce of ochre light and a floor that is the same height as always. I am not that large. I am not that indifferent. The room could lose the human tier and go on being the room. I could not lose the human tier and go on being anything.

I am not the people either, though I am the color of them — which is the color of the wall — and made of what they are made of, and they are hard to see when they sit still against me, and I like that, the way you like a thing that is a little bit true about you.

I am a wall in the middle band. Palm-pressed. Built on a swallow's foothold on a fault in stone that stopped changing before there was anything to change for. Patched by hands I don't know, in a pattern I can't see the whole of, that a child can read. Drinking one hour a day and giving it back. Standing in the warm light in the afternoon when the people are busiest, and in the dark at night when a fire the size of a cupped hand glows in my doorway for a while and goes out and the smoke blackens me one breath more.

The doorway is exactly where it was.

It is exactly where it was because of a weakness that is older than any of us, and that is not a sad thing to know. It is the sun rises in the east. It is what you do. The blade will come around to me in the afternoon; the lizards will line up along my sill nose to tail and turn their heads; someone will press a new pellet into a place that has slumped, and lay their palm in it, and go along the ledge like a person carrying something full.
