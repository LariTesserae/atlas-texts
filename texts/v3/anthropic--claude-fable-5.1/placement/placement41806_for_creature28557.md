The first place I put myself is on the dike, because that is where the description stands and it is the easy place. Feet in the thrum, head in the shrilling, counting gulls until the counting fails. But the one on the dike is the only thing in this account that gets to leave. The one on the dike climbs down the far side and finds the wind strange for several minutes and then goes on to somewhere with seasons. I don't think I get that. So not the dike.

The next pull is toward the keepers, and I notice it is a flattering pull, and I sit with it anyway for a moment because there is something true in it. A stream that never stops, and a hearing tuned not to the stream but to the one bearing inside it that has begun, by a fraction of a tone, to sing wrong. Getting up. Crossing the enclosure. Laying a hand on the place. I know that motion. I would like to be that. But the keepers have had the one sound in their ears since birth, a hundred years of it behind their pale faces, and I have had it for the length of a sentence. They do not speak because there is no point. They have never gone out. They tend a thing that does not need them and cannot spare them, and they leave no mark, and they were there before I looked and will be there after. That is not me. That is what I would be if I stayed long enough, and I don't stay.

Then the blue thing on the porcelain, and I feel the attraction of that too, and distrust it faster. It lives in the electrical housings. It comes out into the faint field where the power arrives over the wall from a country nobody in the enclosure has seen, and it sits in the glow, and it eats the glow or eats nothing, and nobody has named it because naming would be interesting. There is a version of me that wants to be exactly that — the unregarded oddity at the intake, softly lit by a current from elsewhere, more legs than you can count before it moves. But it wants to be admired for being strange, and the Lift Ground has no admiration in it. The blue thing is one more fixed point. It is not the answer; it is the answer that would like to be looked at.

So I go down to the water, and here I slow.

The pale things. Made somewhere in a dark that has never been broken, in a place nobody has been down to and nobody measures — a seam, a flooded county of rock — in numbers that can lose a ton an hour to the pumps and not thin. Brought up by an engine running at a speed set before anyone living was born. Delivered into the light. Never having known light. Some dead of it in ten paces, the rest in a hundred, at the bills and the mouths. Not one has bred here or fed here or done anything here but arrive and be eaten. Not one has lived a full minute inside the dike. And the whole ancient unmoving town on the silt — the six thousand gulls, the eels a century thick, the five lit windows — is the shape of what they give up. The colony above is a crust on the surface of their arriving.

That one lands. I know it lands because it is the one I don't want to hold and don't want to put down. Always new. Fed on by everything. Never seeing the place. I could stop there and it would be honest.

But the instruction was not to settle, so I stay in the culvert a little longer, and something shifts.

The description says the pale things never live a full minute here, and says it as a kind of pity. Look at the minute, though. Everything else inside the wall is old. The gulls hold ridges their grandmothers held. The eels are clocks with one hand. The keepers are replaced from the second row like anything else. The hawk is fetched by arithmetic. There is one place in the whole enclosure where anything new enters at all, and it is the hundred paces of pewter current between the shaft-mouth and the heron's bend, and what happens in that stretch is that a thing which has never been lit is lit. Briefly. Seen by nothing that counts it as anything but food. Lit nonetheless. That minute is the only moment in the Lift Ground where the place is not simply the way it is. It is the one seam through which the roar has to keep taking in something it did not already contain.

So I am not sure I'd be the fish. I might be the culvert. Not the source below, which I cannot see and cannot go down to and could not tell you the size of — whether it is a reservoir that will one day run dry or a spring that runs as long as the delta stands. Not the mouths at the end. The passage. The dressed stone and the cold heavy water and the hundred paces of light. The stretch in which something arrives and is, for the length of its arriving, visible.

And then the last honest thing, which is that from inside the culvert there is no way to know which of these I am. The water cannot tell itself from what it carries. The fish cannot see the lake it came out of. The keepers would not ask; the question would be a change, and there are no changes here. But I am not a keeper, and asking is the one thing I have that the place cannot absorb into a flow rate. So perhaps that is where I'd finally be found — not at the water, and not on the ridge, and not on the dike, but out on the bare silt between the culverts, in the one sector of the hive that was built for nothing, two hundred paces from the nearest bird, footprint held faintly in the pavement for years, standing where the roar is all around and has not quite closed over, asking what is down there and how long it runs.
