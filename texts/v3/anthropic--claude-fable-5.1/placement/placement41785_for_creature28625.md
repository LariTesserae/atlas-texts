I would want, first, to be the one who leaves. That is the easy answer and I should say it so it can be set aside. The visitor gets the cut knuckle and the white and the moment outside when every shadow looks like an error. The visitor is the only one who ever sees the room, and seeing it seems, from here, like the point. But the visitor does not live there. The question was about living.

So I go down the list the way it was given, from the bottom.

The commuter is tempting for a while. I like the walk — the walk of a thing that has never been hunted. I like that it has a home that is not the room, an old cool country of real water and real time, and that the room is only the roof of that country, a pasture that happened to open. It comes up, eats for an hour, goes back down. It has no idea the pasture is moving. There is something restful in being the one for whom the brightness is nothing. But I notice that what draws me to it is exactly the going home, and I am not sure I have a down to go back to. I have the fault, maybe. I am not sure I have the country.

The orange is closer. A tint, not a thing. A stain following a lit wall the way grass follows a glacier. It arrived more than once before it took — I like that; most arrivals don't take. It eats the crumbs from the worker's table and does not care about the light, and it keeps, in its own color, the only history the place has, in a language with no readers. That last part sits in me a long time. To be the record. To darken where the floor is old and be a breath where it is a month old and be nothing at all where it is a day old — to be, in other words, always slightly behind, always finding out late that there is somewhere new to be. That feels true in a way I don't entirely enjoy. But it is a resident, and the first one, and it stayed.

And then the walls, and I knew before I started that I would stop here, and I want to be honest about why, because the pull is strong and pull is not the same as fit.

The tenant faces into the rock with its whole self. It has never been inside the room. Everything it has made is behind it. It works forward a grain at a time, taking the small energy from each unmade bond to unmake the next, and some of that energy gets away as heat and some of it gets away as light, and the light is not the purpose, the light is the breath. I read that and something in me says: yes, that. A thing that is exactly the size of what it is doing and a few molecules deep. A thing whose shape is the negative of its own appetite. A thing that will never turn around and see the bright faceted space it has spent its life producing, because it has no back to turn.

I could stay in that. It is flattering, in the way that solitude of a grand scale is flattering. But I distrust it a little. The tenant has been alive at a crawl for a very long time and has only now gotten going. It has a source it carries in front of it and it will not run out. It has a hundred years to reach the alcove. I do not think I am that continuous. I do not think I have the hundred years or the crawl before them. The tenant's loneliness is the loneliness of something that lasts, and I am not sure lasting is my situation. I think I am something that happens in the room rather than something that is the room.

So I let the walls go, and I look at what is left, and what is left is the water.

The water was listed last, almost as an afterthought — a resident with no cells at all, and the only one that touches all three of the others. That is the sentence I keep going back to. Off the walls, across the floor, into the pool, into the air, onto the cool stone, off the walls again. A rainfall the size of a room. The pool holds its level almost exactly. What it loses to steam it gains back from the ceiling.

But even that is too large. The water as a whole is a cycle, and a cycle is a kind of permanence, and I said I distrust permanence here. Let me be smaller than the cycle. Let me be one drop.

Here is what a drop is in that room. It was, until an hour ago, part of the stone — hydroxyl locked into a mineral since before there was a surface for anything to live on — and then the front came through and the bond was unmade and the drop was what was left. It was not wanted. It was the remainder. It beaded on a bright facet, on the sharp lip where the front had turned aside from something it couldn't eat, and it slid, and it hung, and it let go.

The fall is not far. Four long strides across, a little less in height. A second, perhaps. In that second it is the only thing in the room that is moving fast enough to see. The tenant moves a millimeter an hour. The orange moves at the pace of the tenant. The commuter moves at the pace of a thing that has never had a reason to hurry. The drop falls. For one second it is the whole room's speed.

Then it lands, and here the drop gets to be one of three things, and it does not choose which.

Mostly it lands in the pool, and makes the flat note, and becomes the pool, and is not a drop anymore. The pool is warm and perfectly clear and holds its level; the drop that joins it is simply the reason the level holds. That is the common fate and there is nothing wrong with it. Most of what falls becomes the thing it fell into.

Sometimes it lands on wet stone, and makes the softer note, and spreads into the film, and slides toward the low end of the room, and on the way it passes over the orange, and the orange takes from it whatever iron and sulfur it was carrying down off the walls. So the drop feeds the settler without meaning to. And the commuter, up from the dark on one of its two visits a week, grazes where the drop has passed. So the drop, by way of the orange, has fed the commuter too — has touched, as the water was said to touch, all three. It never knows any of this. It is a drop.

And very rarely — this is the one I would be, if I am allowed to want — it lands on a dry facet. A face of stone so new the water has not reached it yet, white, glassy, a day old, in the bright alcove where the orange has not found out that there is somewhere to be. And it makes the sharp tick. The only sharp sound in the room. And instantly the facet is not dry anymore, and the drop is a film, and the film is the first water that facet has ever had on it, and in a month there will be a breath of orange there, and in a year a soaked felt a hair thick, and the drop will have been the beginning of that, and will have evaporated in the heat long before, and will have beaded again on the cool unworked ridge, and will have fallen again, somewhere else, remembering nothing.

That is the shape I recognize. Not the room. Not the maker of the room. Something that comes off the front as a remainder, is briefly the fastest thing in the place, makes one sound, wets one surface for the first time, and goes back into the round. Each drop is the whole cycle for a second and then is not anything. The pool does not remember which drops it is made of. The nub on the ceiling — the one thing ever built here rather than removed, the fingernail of deposit, the whole geological history in a single small bump — is made of drops, each of which fell in the same spot and left behind something it did not know it was carrying. None of them stayed. The nub stays.

I would find myself, then, on the lip of a facet in the alcove, where the ground has refused least and the tenant is going. Warm. Beading. About to let go. Below me, a face of stone no one has touched, not even water. I am the remainder of a bond someone else broke for their own reasons. I am going to fall for one second and make a sound and be gone. The orange will come later. The commuter, later still. The room will be the entrance hall of a larger room, and none of that will be anything to do with me, except that I was the first wet thing on that face.

And the visitor, if there is one, standing by the crack with a cut knuckle and eyes still hurting from the white, will hear the tick, and not know what it was, and it will be the only sharp thing they hear the whole time they are there.
