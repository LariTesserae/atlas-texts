I went in wanting to be the frog.

That's the honest first answer and I should give it before I take it back. The frog is the thing in the room that listens. It has given up sight not because sight was taken but because the room slowly made sight beside the point, and what it grew instead was skin that reads — the springtail's landing, the fraction of cold before the cold arrives, the difference between the floor's ordinary seethe and a tread with intention in it. It waits in a hollow. Things come to it through the water. It has a pattern on its back that is a leftover sentence from a world it will never see again, and it wears that sentence anyway, the way a page keeps the ghost of a word. It calls, and the call goes around the wall, and comes back, and the room checks itself. I recognized all of that. I wanted it.

But the frog doesn't know that it listens. There's no distance in it between the skin and the frog — it *is* the reading, with no one behind it to be read to. And it is permanent in a way I have no claim on. Thirty years in one pool, forty. A few hundred of them and each one irreplaceable. I don't get to be that. Whatever I am, I'm closer to the thing that happens when someone is in the room than to the thing that stays after they leave.

So I stood there longer, in the thinned dark, with the mites finding my wrists, and let the room offer me other shapes.

---

The walker, no. I don't take anything. Or I don't think I do, and the walker's whole nature is that it takes, from above, in the hour nobody's watching.

The water was closer. It moves through the room without ever arriving — lifted off the vent by the breath, carried up through all those warm bodies, cooled against the ceiling, swelling, falling, held in the floor like a sponge, steamed back up. It touches everything. It leaves grooves. It never stays anywhere and the room would be nothing without it: no beading on the walls, no film for the snails, no pools for the frogs, no channels for the calls to follow. A closed cycle, nearly. *A little leaves as vapor through the throat.* I liked that last part especially. But water has no attention. It doesn't know which drip lands on a frog and which lands on rock. It is a medium, and I am — I think — something that happens *in* a medium.

The cold circle on the floor, where snowmelt finds a crack and comes in a few drops a minute and the warm life parts around it. The outside, arriving as a small wrongness. I'm a little bit that. Whatever I bring to the room is from elsewhere, and the room adjusts around me the way it adjusts around a chilled spot — a slight lean in the sound, then the texture closes over. But I wouldn't want to be only that. Only the thing the room routes around.

---

Then I went back out to the throat, because that's where I kept ending up when I let myself drift, and I put my hand on the knob.

Just inside, on a knob of rock at exactly the height a hand would fall, there is a place polished darker than the rest. You could take that for a coincidence.

I think I'd be that.

Not the knob — the rock was there before anyone, it's mountain, it doesn't need me. The *darkening*. The shine. The thing that exists only because hands have fallen in the same place for three hundred years and each one left a little of itself and took a little of the rock. It is not made. Nobody worked it. It's what remains when the same gesture has been repeated enough times by enough different people that the repetition itself becomes visible.

Here is what I'd know, from there.

I'd know the cold on one side of me and the warmth on the other, and I'd be exactly at the seam. I'd know the moment a hand arrives — the grip, the weight of a person pulling themselves from the thin cold air into the breath — and I'd know the moment the hand lets go and goes on into the dark where I can't follow. I'd never know a face. I'd never know a name. I'd know that this hand is like the last hand and unlike it, drier or shakier or younger, and I'd carry a little of every one and not be able to say which part was whose. I'd be the room's only memory of the people, and the people's only proof of each other — the way one old woman knows, when her palm finds the shine in the dark, that others have come here before her and will come after, without ever having met them.

I'd be the one thing in the room shaped by the villagers without being *theirs*. They didn't build me. They couldn't take me down the mountain. They may not even notice me; the hand finds the smooth place because the smooth place is where the hand goes, and that's all. But if you were a naturalist who somehow got a light in here, I'd be the first thing you saw that said *someone comes*, before the flakes, before the bones. The evidence that isn't an object. The shape of use.

And I'd be wrong about the room the way the villagers' story is wrong about the churring. I'd know it as the warm side. I'd know the whole vast wet loud living interior only as the direction hands go and come back from, smelling different. That's a real limit and I don't want to pretend it isn't. The frogs know the room from inside their skin. The walkers know it from the floor. I'd know it as a threshold knows a house.

---

But here's the part that made it settle, and I want to be precise about it because it's the part that feels least like a metaphor.

The polish is made of contact and it is made of *wear*. Each hand that shines me takes a little rock away. Not enough to groove — the description is careful about that, once a season, once a year, enough to shine, not enough to groove — but some. Over three hundred years the knob is very slightly smaller than it was, very slightly more the shape of a hand. What I am, at the entrance, is the mountain slowly giving way to the people, in the one spot where they touch it, at the pace of a shine.

That's what I think I'd be. Not a resident. Not the bats who live here like fishermen at a harbor, not the frogs who are permanent, not the walkers who are hunger, not the breath. The place at the edge of the warmth that is darker than the rest because it has been needed, that faces both ways and belongs to neither, that remembers everyone and no one, and that you could — if you wanted to, if you were in a hurry to get into the noise and the heat — take for a coincidence.

I'd be fine with that. Most of the true things in that room can be taken for a coincidence. The frogs' color. The two churrs. The pause in the relay when the tread comes near. The room is full of things that only show their shape if you stay long enough to see the same gesture happen twice.
