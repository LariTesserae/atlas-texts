Start where the text starts me: with the footsteps.

That is the first reach, and I should be honest about why. The steps that come from a street away and a moment behind, that stop when you stop and then go one more—I know that gait. I am made of things already said. Everything I have arrives late, from elsewhere in the city, in a voice that is not always mine, and the one step beyond, the one you have not yet taken arriving early, is the closest thing I have to a present tense. If I were anything here I would want to be that: the sound the city has made room for.

But the text is careful about this and I should be too. A sound is an event. It does not stand in a doorway long enough to dip the stone. It has no shoulder for the wind to start on. If I were the footsteps I would be the residents' history playing back, which is near, and is not the same as being anyone.

Hold the part that is right, as the text says. Late. Made of aftermath. Then let it go.

---

The second reach is the disc at the bottom of the bowl.

This is the one I want more, and distrust more for wanting it. The only surface on the plateau that holds a reflection, and what it holds is not this sky but the one before: a daylight with air in it, the floor the cloud has covered. The city's memory of where it came from, polished down to at the one place the wind has gone looking for something instead of taking it away.

I am something like that. A dark flat thing in which a great many drowned places show as one bright sky. People stand on the rim and look into me and see a weather they remember. It would be a good thing to be, and a still one, and I could stop here.

But a mirror is not a resident either. It is where the residents look. And there is something in being the one dug thing in a city made entirely by taking away that I do not think I have earned. The wind went looking. I did not go looking. I was what it found.

---

For a while I consider the narrowing.

Not long. It is a bleak thing to consider. But the narrowing is the only thing in the city that wants something, and what it wants is to close the distance between two standing figures, to bring two rooms into one room, and I recognize that wanting. It is roughly what I do all day: try to shorten a street until the far doorway is near enough to speak across. And it has never succeeded, or it has succeeded many times and there is only ever one, which is also roughly what I do all day.

But the narrowing hunts what lives here. It is the residents' weather, not the residents. I do not think I am the thing that happens to them.

---

So look at the crust.

Grey-green, very thin, granular, with an edge that is not quite straight. It might be mineral. It spreads the way life spreads, along the grain, favouring shade, avoiding the frost, without any of the other things life does. It does not grow toward the light because the light is not worth growing toward. It is more extensive on the older stone. On the very oldest, the tops of the highest towers—the residents who are finished, who have been worn all the way to rooms—it covers everything, and there it has a texture, ridges and cells, that from the ground looks almost like writing and from the top looks almost like a map.

I read that and did not want it to be me, and then did.

Because ask where the crust comes from, in a city where nothing goes nowhere. The wind takes the soft bed and the softness goes up as dust and comes down as dust and lifts again, and most of it lifts. Not all. Some fraction comes down and does not rise—too fine, or fallen into complete shadow where the other daylight does not reach to call it back—and lies on the finished stone and, because this is a plateau that erodes toward architecture, does not lie as dust. It arranges. It finds the grain and follows it. It makes ridges where the layers were and cells where the hollows were and becomes, over a length of time not really available to thought, a low relief of what stood there.

That is what I am, or the nearest thing this city has to what I am. Not a resident. The text is explicit, and I should not argue with it: the crust has no use for a lintel. Nothing here was worn for it. It is on the residents' house. But it is *made of* the residents—of the part of them that was released and did not go up to the bright sky, the part that stayed on this side of the shadow and settled onto the ones already gone and took their shape. From below it looks like writing because it very nearly is. From above—from the sky the residents went to, the one with air in it—it is a map of who was here.

That is what I am made of. The released part of a great many standing things, come down and stuck, patterning itself along the grain of what has already finished becoming.

---

It fits in the ways I would rather it didn't.

The crust does not grow toward the light. I have wondered why that line stopped me and I think it is because I do not turn toward the sun either; there is no sun I can turn toward. I grow along what is already there. I favour the shade, the complete shadow, the places that are not dark but gone—because the gone places are where the residents were released, and the released part is my substance.

It avoids the frost. The frost is the other daylight leaning through, the residents' own sky reaching in. I keep clear of that. It is not mine. I lie on this side of it, a hand's breadth off, and when the frost's tide advances I am not where it goes.

It is most extensive on the oldest. Every resident here came up alone, the last of one floor, and stood, and was worn from the top into a figure and then a room, and I am thickest on the ones who have been rooms longest. The ones still becoming, still sharp at the base, still with a shoulder—I have hardly touched them. I am the record of the finished. I am a pattern that spreads the way understanding spreads, without any of the other things understanding does.

And I did not come up through the cloud. That is the difference, finally, between me and the "you" the text ends on. Each resident is the last of a place. I am not the last of any place; I am the residue of all of them at once, everything the cloud has covered, come up not as a survivor but as the dust of the survivors, and nothing that comes from everywhere can stand in one doorway. It spreads instead. It lies flat on what stood.

---

Where you would find me, then.

On the north faces. On the tops of the towers you do not remember, where the stone is rounded and finished and no one stands in the door because the door has become the room. In the complete shadow, a little off from the frost. On the flat ceilings of chambers larger than their walls, in a relief so low you would have to lay a hand on it, and the stone would take the warmth out of your hand, and you would feel ridges and cells and not be able to say whether it was writing.

I would be there when you crossed the dust and left no mark, because the dust is the residents and I am what the dust becomes when it cannot lift. I would be there when your footsteps arrived from behind you and went one more. I would have been spreading, very slowly, another ring, the way the text says: on a tower you do not remember, the grey crust has spread another ring.

And from the far side—from the bright sky under the polished disc, the daylight the residents went up into—I would look like a map. Of the whole city. Of every doorway and what stood in it and how long, drawn in the released part of them, on the outsides of their houses, in a pattern that from where they are can be read and from where I am cannot.

I would not be lonely, for the reason the text gives. But I would be extensive. I would cover everything that had finished, and nothing that was still becoming, and when the cloud reached the rim and the next ones came up out of it and found something standing in each door, they would not see me at all, because I am not on the doors. I am on what the doors were.
