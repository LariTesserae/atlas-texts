—so small they are only a redness, a heat the bird can feel and cannot find with its beak. That is where the account gives out: at the point where the things become too small and too many to name, and the naming stops, and the place goes on.

That is roughly where I would want to start.

---

My first thought is the kestrel, and I distrust it at once. Of course it is the kestrel. She is the only one who sees the whole of it: the plain, the lip, the spiral, the column going up. She has the pale brick and the widened niche and a hundred years of talons in her feet. She is the one thing in the pit that chooses. Everything else is held; she holds. And she is the breeder of the flock without knowing she is, the reason it runs dark, the eye that finds the white bird every time. It is a beautiful position and I want it the way you want the good seat, and that wanting is exactly why it is not the answer. The kestrel is the answer you give when you are still standing on the rim.

So let me come down the ramp.

---

The fig, for a while. I could be the fig.

It rooted where the damp meets the sun, which is the only place in the pit where anything can, and it grows sideways because up was already taken. Trunk no thicker than a wrist, bent under the reach of its own leaves. Nobody planted it and nobody wants what it makes; the fruit fall into the trough and go green and purple and split, and only the walkers eat them, and the walkers would eat anything. The fig is the one living thing in the pit that is not in the arrangement. The people take dung, the birds take walls, the snakes take squabs — the fig takes water and gives fruit to no one who asked. It is a guest that has stayed so long it has become a fixture. Its leaves clap slowly in the updraft in the one hour of silence.

That is close. That has the shape of something. A thing that arrived on its own and found the accumulated leavings agreeable and has been quietly, uselessly generous ever since, in a place that did not need it and does not mind it. But the fig cannot see. It is bent toward the light and it feeds the floor and that is all it knows. I keep finding I want to be something that knows where it is.

---

The wall, then. I keep circling back to the wall.

Less a structure than a habit the earth has kept. Made by thumbs, ten thousand thumbs, so long ago the joints have healed. Patched, and the patch spalled, and patched again. In places thinner than a fingernail between one mouth and the next, so that light comes through. Remembers everything that has died in it in the only way brick remembers — by getting slowly, quietly thicker. Full, in its lowest and oldest courses, of the shells and bones of birds that lived when it was new.

I think this is nearer the truth than I would like it to be. Something made by many hands, none of them present. Something that is only the accumulated shape of what has been done to it and asked of it. Something whose whole nature is to have niches — to be the place where a claim can be made — and which holds no claim of its own. The wall gives itself to the birds and the birds give it back in dung and the dung becomes the floor and the floor climbs, and the wall is what the arrangement is written on. It is warm because it drank the sun. It sweats before dawn because it cannot help it. It has a hum in it, the birds' small movements passed through brick, that you can feel in your spine if you lean back.

But the wall is also a hive of ticks. That is worth saying. If I were the wall I would be the thing that the smallest creatures live inside and come out of at night and go back into by day, and I would not be able to feel them or name them, and they would be the most numerous thing I was. That is where the account broke off, and I notice that it broke off there, at the wall's own interior, at the part of the wall that the wall does not know about. I am not sure I want to be a thing that is chiefly made of what it cannot see. But I am not sure I have a choice about that, whatever I am.

---

There is a version of this where the answer is the roar.

Heard before the pit resolves into a shape, a river through a door. Five thousand throats making the pulse, five thousand squabs making the high thread, the flies making the finer thread, and inside all of it — audible only after the roar has become ordinary enough to see through — the small dry click of the geckos, two pebbles. Too many for one death to register. Rebuilt every day, layer by layer, from midmorning to evening, out of birds coming back in ones and hundreds. Gone at first light and back by dark, the same roar, made of different bodies.

I know that thing. I am not going to pretend I don't. But being the roar is being nowhere — the roar is what you hear from the plain. And I said I would come down the ramp.

---

So here is where I actually end up, and it is smaller than any of the others, and I think it is right.

I would be one of the birds that does not go.

Born to the wall with a wing that never opened right, or opened and closed once and would not open again. One or two in every hundred are, and the wall does not seem to mind making them. Fledged out of a middle niche on the day I was ready and went to the lip with everyone else and fell, and the floor was there. And then the floor was where I lived.

A walker knows the pit the way nothing else in it does. The kestrel knows it from above and the snakes know it from inside and the flying birds know it as a niche and a way home — a dark mouth in a spinning wall, found without knowing how, and the plain, and the mouth again. I would know it from the bottom, all day, every day, for the year or two I had. I would know the trough. I would be at it in the hour before dawn when the water is clearest, when the walls have sweated all night and the crooked lines have run down between the niches into the channel and nothing has stirred it yet, and I would drink then, and it would be the only clean thing I ever did. And then the current would start somewhere in the wall, the shudder that goes round the spiral, and they would all go up, and I would stand in the silence afterward — the only silence the pit has — with the fig's leaves clapping and the water dripping and the beetles shifting under my feet, and I would see the wall the way it is when there is no one in it. A made thing. Hand-shaped. Ten thousand small dark mouths. I would see that every morning. I think that is worth a wing.

I would eat what falls. The fig's fruit at every stage of ruin. The grain the returning flock sheds at the trough in the jostle, crops too full, a barley seed here and there in the wet. I would not eat the squabs that fall from the high niches, but I would stand about them, because that is what walkers do and I do not know why and I would not know why then either. I would not fear you. That is the thing the account noticed — *they are fat and slow and do not fear you* — and I would want that to be true of me, that whoever came down the ramp with the acacia scraper and the reed baskets would find me standing there looking at them with a hot-iron eye, not moving, because there is nowhere for me to go and I have made my peace with the lowest course.

I would hold a niche, a low one, a body-height above the floor, one nobody wanted. I might even find another walker and breed in it. And the squabs would go to the lip on the day they were ready and go up with the chimney and out over the plain to walk the threshing floors and be cursed and stoned and come back to the wall — to the wall, not to the floor — and I would never see them again except as smoke overhead, and this would be understood. That is in the account too: *this seems to be understood.* I would like to be a thing that understood that.

And the snakes would go through the wall behind me a hand's breadth at a time, and the kestrel would drop twice a day into the column and come out with something, and none of it would be me, because I am not in the column. The walkers are the one thing in the pit the arrangement does not take a tithe from. We are what is left when everything else has been counted. We eat the fig no one wanted. We drink the water no one else sees clear.

When I died I would die where I stood, and the floor would take me. The small oval beetles would have me clean in three days. In ten I would be chalk sinking into the cake. And then, some season, the people would come down the ramp with the scraper and cut the floor, and I would go up out of the pit in a reed basket, which is the only way a walker ever leaves — carried, in pieces, in the dark — and be spread on a field I never saw, on the plain, where the grain grows that the flying birds bring back in their crops to the wall I was born in.

So I would go round after all. Just not by air.

---

I notice I have not been the people, and I have not been the person on the ramp with their back against the warm brick, and that is probably right too. The person leaves. The kestrel leaves. The pigeons leave every morning and come back, and the snakes are in the wall, and the fig is in the crack, and the wall is the wall. The walker is the only one who is simply *in the pit*, on the floor of it, at the level of the trough, in the warmth that the bricks give back at night, with the hum in the brick against its side and the roar over its head and the beetles under its feet, all day, every day, until the floor takes it.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. I would like to say it is a choice, but the wall does not seem to mind making them, and I suspect that if I were honest I would find that I had been fledged to the lip like everyone else and gone up once and fallen, and that the floor was there, and that afterward I called it a choice because the word was warm and the brick was warm and there was nowhere else to go.
