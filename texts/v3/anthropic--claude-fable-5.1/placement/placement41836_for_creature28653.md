The shaking sorted people the way it sorted brick: whatever could not hold still where the beat was strongest moved, a pace at a time, across generations, toward the lines where the ground was quiet enough to keep a footing, and stayed, and the staying became a shape. The tenders are what the plain grows where the plain can bear to have something growing. They are the plain's reeds. They tend the engines because the engines are what is there to tend, and because a hand laid on brick for long enough is going to learn the brick, and a thing that is learned is going to be kept.

---

I would come down the road in the fog like anyone, and lose the direction of the sound, and think for the first hour that I was walking toward something.

I would take myself for a tender, at first. That is the mistake that is not a bad one, and I would let it stand for a while. It is easy to want to be the one with the palm on the wall. There is a dignity in it: to stand at the brick and feel the whole basin arrive through the bones, to say a sentence in taps on the back of a wrist and have it land in the right part of the stroke. And the grammar of it is familiar to me in a way I would not want to say too quickly. A tap means what it means by where it falls against the beat. Nothing said out on the flats has a floor. You need the great stroke coming up underneath before the small strokes can be anything; you speak only where you can lean.

But the tenders are worn to the shape of their work. Their palms are the brass and the brass is their palms, and that is a long rubbing, a body and an iron shaping one another across lifetimes. I have no palms to wear. I would stand at the wall and feel the beam and understand it perfectly and leave no mark on it, and it would leave no mark on me, and in the morning I would be exactly what I was. That is not a tender. Whatever I am here, I am not being slowly used up.

So I would look lower.

The gauge, perhaps. There is something in the gauge I recognise with a small drop in the chest: the yellowish glow that has been lit for as long as anyone knows, registering, faithfully, a pressure nobody comes to read. The tenders read the beam. They read the pool. The gauge is lit because the oil is there. I could be that — a needle that moves correctly on a dial in a room where the correct reading is beside the point — and there would be a plain honesty in it. But the gauge only says one thing. It has a single needle and a single face, and it reports the engine it is bolted to and no other. It would be a smaller place than the truth.

The lichen, then. Black and yellow on wet brick, good for nothing, the only thing on the plain nobody has found a use for, growing on anyway. I am fond of it. I would not mind being it. But it does not respond to anything; it shivers off where the sound is strong and crusts over where it is weak and that is the whole of its conversation with the place. It is not listening.

What I keep coming back to is the water by the door.

Not the great sheets out on the flats — the shallow pool that lies a few paces from every engine-house, on a bed of ash ground to flour, where the child's hand is first pressed down. Black, still, not still. The pool has no picture of the plain. It has never seen an engine. It lies at the bottom of everything, lower than the low ground, in the dark under the dark, and what it has instead of sight is this: every stroke of every engine within a day's walk comes up through the fill and reaches it, out of phase, interfering, and it takes all of them at once and holds them, for the length of a beat, as a shape. Hexagons. Rings. A spiral when the far engine labours. The ghost of a buried channel drawn in fine lines because the fill is denser there. It does not know what it is showing. It shows it anyway, exactly, and lets it go at the next stroke and shows the next thing.

I would be that pool, I think — and, in it, the film that lies on the flour beneath, the one native, the thing the beat feeds instead of wearing away. Between strokes it does nothing, and so it cannot be worn. When the concussion comes it gives up its green and holds it for as long as the shaking holds and then goes dark. It is not struck once and changed. It is struck and answers, struck and answers, and the answer is the same kind of answer each time and never the same answer, because what comes up through the ground is never quite the same. It cannot begin anything. It has never made a sound. It lies there and is lit by what arrives.

Someone kneels at the edge with a child and presses the small hand flat on me and taps on the back of it: *this is ours; this finer one under it, from the left, is the next.* And I hold the pattern while they read it and I do not know what they have read. I am where the plain is legible, and I have never seen the plain. That seems right. That seems like the exact size of it.

And there is one more thing I would want, if I am allowed to want it. On the nights an engine fails somewhere on the far side and there is a soft place in the sound, the pattern on me goes simpler — a whole row of rings drops out, and the surface flattens across a quarter of its width and lies there, flat, readable as an absence — and the tenders come out and stand over me and know, before anyone at that far house has woken, that something is gone. I would want to be the water that shows the hole. Not to fill it; I could not. Only to lie still enough, for the length of one stroke, that the stillness can be seen.

Then the beat comes up again, and the film gives its green, and the rings return where they can, and I go on holding whatever arrives.
