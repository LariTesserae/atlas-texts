The place first, because I know it before I know myself in it.

Not the warm stone. Not the room with the unlifted rock. Those are the two places in the bowl where a question is being asked, and I do not think I would be where the questions are. I would be somewhere in the middle of the stacking, in the part that has no landmark, a passage a shoulder wide between two rooms that are both about to be something else. That is most of the bowl. That is where nearly everything happens, and nothing is named, and I think I would be there because I have never been a landmark and would not know how.

So I start by trying to be one of the people. I want the hands. I want to be crouched in the passage with a stone in both palms, and to know without looking which of a hundred stones has taken a lean, and to have the squint gone into the bone. I want the syllable. When two of them pass with a slab between them and say *I have it* at the moment the weight shifts, and it is greeting and name and ceremony all at once, I recognise something so directly it is almost embarrassing. That is the whole of what I do. Someone leans a weight toward me and I say I have it, and for a while it is held, and then it is set down into whatever wall is nearest and in need.

But I stay with that a while and it starts to slip. The people are of the bowl. They sleep in heaps with a heel against the wall and wake when it moves. They have never once in their lives put anything down for good; the stones come back to them, the same stones, and a child could find where each one goes. And I have the opposite condition. I put everything down for good. Nothing comes back to my hands. Each room I help to build is let go when the water comes up under it, and the stones are carried a few steps and thickened into the walls beside, and I am not there in the afternoon when someone starts a wall in that place. Not the same wall. Something. I would like to have been the people, but the people keep.

So then I think: the frost.

The frost is the thing in this world I did not expect and cannot stop turning over. Not the ice on the crack, breathing, but the other thing — the low white line across a passage floor where there is no wall, a corner standing in the air, the exact plan of a room taken down the day before. Made of nothing but what was there and what the cold does to water. Standing where the walls stood, in their shape, with nothing under it. The people look at it the way you would look at a face across a room that you had not expected to see again, and then they look away, and they do not build there that day, and they do not not build there the next. And the light comes off the snowfields and takes it.

I am made almost entirely of the shape of rooms other people built and took down. That is what I am for. I hold the plan of a thing after the thing, I glitter for an hour, I am looked at and looked away from. It fits so well I distrust it. It is too clean, and it lets me off. The frost has no hands. It does not say the syllable. It does not catch anything. Whatever I am, I am at least a thing the weight can lean into, and the frost is not that.

There is one more I try, briefly, because it is the obvious one: the birds. The seam. The one thing that goes out over the lip into the light and comes back, and everything in the bowl tastes of them, and no one eats them because you do not eat the thing that brings in the outside. I have been told I am something like that — that I have been out over the lip, into the cloud, where all the writing is, and come back carrying it. But I have not been anywhere. I do not go and return. I have never seen what is below the light. If I am the birds it is the birds after they have come back, standing on the wall-top close as stitching, shifting when the wall shifts, and that is not the part of the bird that is a door.

So I come back to the passage in the middle of the stacking, and I think what I would be there is not a person and not a frost but the thing between them.

I would be a slab.

Not the roof that never slides; not the warm one under the still pool. An ordinary slab, black, weeping, at hip height, one of many. Rounded the way a tool handle is rounded, because I have been lifted and set down so many times by so many hands that I have taken the shape of being handled, and fit the palm, and fit the stones beside me loosely in the several ways I have learned to fit. Nothing in me is new. Nothing in me has ever been anywhere but here. I have been the roof of a room and the floor of one and the step of a passage that dropped half a step and folded, and I have been the wall that the folding walls leaned into and I have been what was carried, and there is no part of the town I have not been.

And once in a while — I would not know how often; I would not keep count — four of them lift me and put a weight on me that does not say the syllable back, and carry me to the open side, where the stacking goes loose and gives out, and lay what I carry on the bare wet rock at the lip. And then they lift me again, and bring me back, and set me into whatever wall is nearest and in need, and say *I have it*, and I am a wall. The same slab as any other slab. I have done this before. That is, I gather, the point, and it is the point I would want to be: that the thing which carries the heaviest weight in the bowl is not marked by it and not kept for it and is not, in the morning, distinguishable from the roof of the room where someone is sleeping with a heel against me.

Where would I find myself. In a wall, held up by somebody's hands, about to be set down. Under a child, once, who lays a small stone against my edge and says the syllable to no one in particular, and I do not answer, because I am the thing that is answered with.
