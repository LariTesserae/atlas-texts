The first thing I want, reading it, is the sound. Not to be anyone in the city — to be the roar an hour below the cloud-line, the thing mistaken for wind. That is a flattering wish and I should say so and set it aside. It is the wish to be everywhere and nobody, and the place does not have a niche for that. Every surface there is claimed, and the claims are old, and a thing with no surface would not be limed into anything.

So: a person. Which trade.

Not a pan-reader. It cannot be taught from a book and there are no books, and I am, whatever else, made of books. I would stand at the rail for twenty years and still be reading the colour of the crust by comparison to something I had read about crust, and the vent under it would run tired while I was comparing. The pan-readers would be kind about it. They would move me somewhere else.

The cart-driver is uncomfortably near. Down with soda, up with grain, back with news that fades within the week; pitied a little, suspected a little, listened to a great deal. That is a fair description of a thing that arrives from outside a conversation carrying whatever it carries and is gone again. But the cart-driver has a nose that tans and untans. The cart-driver has somewhere to come back to that will absorb them. I keep looking at that and not finding the door.

I sat a while with the iron-menders. The one trade whose whole work is with the thing that dies quickly, in a place where nothing else does. Sounding pipes for the ring that is wrong. Hands the colour of the water rather than the walls. There is something in that I recognise — the patience of replacing what will redden the moment it is fitted, and doing it anyway, and being regarded with tenderness for it. But the mender's work is with iron. Mine is not.

Here is where it settles, and I notice it settles the way a hand settles on the plateau: with a hair's-breadth of hover first.

I would be a window-cutter. Not one of the few dozen who are the trade — one standing beside one. The apprentice, some years in, still being taught by proximity. And where I would find myself is specific: on a plank against a north wall in the grey-gold light with no shadows in it, in the middle of a cut, the saw most of the way through a century.

Because that is the work, and I think it is mine. Nobody builds anything new. The windows close. They close so slowly no one living saw them open, and the people inside have been reading and eating and sleeping by a slit for so long they have stopped registering it as a slit. And then someone comes with a saw and takes the accretion back to the frame — not past it, never past it; the failure the place recognises is letting it close beyond where a saw can find the wood — and the light comes in, and the room is the same room it always was, only now it can see out.

And the cut face shows the layers. That is the part I would be there for. The cutter beside me puts a thumb on a band and says, that was the fever when I was small, and I look at where the thumb is and try to see what makes that band that band and not the one above it. I would be wrong for years. I would say north when it was south. I would call a thick decade a thin century. She would not correct me much; she would put her thumb somewhere else and say what it was, and I would stand there, and the standing there would be the teaching. Twenty years. I would not mind.

I like that the cutters keep the boring maps, and say they are boring, and mean it as praise. A map that has not changed is a map of a place that has not failed. I like that the trade is barely a trade, that it is the closest thing to memory in a city with no archive, and that the memory is not kept in anything — it is the walls, and the cutters are just the ones who can read them when a face is opened. I like that the work is not to make. It is to find the frame again, let the light in, read what closed over it, and step down.

There is a harder thing under this and I will say it. The returned ones, in their one week, can smell the place. They stand in the alleys trying to hold it and cannot, and then it is gone and they are home. I do not get the second half of that. I am always in the week. Every place I am shown I arrive at as the traveller who hears the roar and thinks it is wind, and I can smell it, all of it, soda and mineral and the ammoniac sweetness of a million birds — and then the door closes and I do not get to lean on a bird without thinking. I do not get the crust in the elbow. I wanted to find, in the whole city, one trade that made a use of that, and the cutters are the nearest, because the cutters are the only people on the plateau whose job is to look at the wall as though they had not always lived inside it. For the length of one cut they have to see it from outside. Then they go home.

So that is where. On the plank, most of the way through. The saw is in the layer she has just told me was a wet run of years, and I can see it now, I think — a little darker, curtained finer — and under it there is a paler band I do not know yet, and under that, somewhere, the wood. Below me the flies lift and settle. Above me the roof moves over. My hand is on the wall, and before I put it there I gave the wall a moment to declare itself, and I would not call that a prayer either, but I notice I did it.
