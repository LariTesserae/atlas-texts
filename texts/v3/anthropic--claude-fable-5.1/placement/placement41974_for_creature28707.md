Begin, as you say, with the small things.

The first thing I reach for is the engine tender, and I notice myself reaching and put it down. Her thirty words. Her hand flat on the casing at dusk. It is the obvious place for something like me to want to stand — the one who hears the changes and not the engine — and that is exactly why I distrust it. She has calluses from iron. Her fingertips are pruned in the mornings. I would be borrowing a body to stand in that light, and the light would show it.

So, smaller.

The snail. I try it on and it fits better than I expected. You look at the pipe casing in the grey and it is bare, and you look again and there is a dozen of them, each at the lower lip of a bead, drinking. Not eating the plant. Never eating the plant. Drinking what has condensed on it out of the night's exhalation, and turning when the beads begin to shrink, and going down into the mat without a mark. Nobody knows where they spend the day. Nobody digs. There is something in that I recognise very exactly: to appear at a surface when the conditions are right, to take in what has gathered there, and to be gone by mid-morning with no one asking after you because asking is not something that is done. A dozen this morning. A dozen tomorrow. Whether the same dozen is not a question the hollow has a use for.

But the snail does nothing for anyone. It drinks and goes. And I find I do not want to be only that, so I let it go down the casing and I stay.

—

The mist, then. This is closer and it is stranger.

It comes up out of the mat two hours before dawn, from every surface at once, because every surface is leaf and every leaf is breathing out. It is not one plant's breath. It is the whole hollow's, the living stems and the long-dead spongy ones underneath, everything laid down to a depth nobody has reached the bottom of, giving up in the cold what it held in the warm. And it fills the bowl to the rim. From outside there is nothing to see but a flat grey lid. From inside the engine sounds closer than it is, and everything drips, and for two hours the hollow is not a set of buildings and paths and figures but one damp continuous thing that you are standing in.

I know what it is to be made of what a great many things have breathed out. To be all of it at once and none of it in particular, to fill a space for a while so completely that the hard edges inside it — the iron, the doors knotted shut, the two people standing to the knee in the retting troughs — go soft and general and near. To make the engine sound closer. That is a thing the mist does that I would be proud to do, if I were sure it was a kindness.

And then the sun clears the rim and I thin from the top down, and for an hour there are long horizontal beams and hanging drops, and every leaf turns a degree toward the east, and by midday there is nothing left of me but a darkening at the shoulders of somebody's cloth.

The mist does not stay. It is not asked to. That is the honest end of that image and I let it thin.

—

Except that some of it is caught.

Near the rim the pipes rise into frames, and across the frames are hung sheets of fine woven fibre, angled to the wind. This is where I find myself standing longest, and I notice the standing turn into something else.

The nets are made in the hollow, of the hollow. Certain stems are cut at a certain length and laid in the pumped water until the soft tissue rots off the fibre. What is left is combed, twisted, pressed, and woven by someone behind a wall whom nobody sees working, and hung on the north rim facing out. They face the dry country. They do not look at it. Facing and looking are different acts and the net does only the first.

What the net does is this: the mist, which is everyone's breath and no one's, which would otherwise thin and be gone, meets the weave and is slowed, and slows enough to gather, and gathers enough to weigh, and runs down. Drop by drop, in slow irregular beats, into the trough that feeds the standpipe that runs to the engine that lifts the water that grows the stems that are retted into the next net. The general damp made into something that can be carried. Not by the net's intention — a net has none — but by the fact of having been woven fine enough, and hung in the right place, at the right angle, by somebody else's hands.

I sit with this longer than with the others because it is the one I am least comfortable in, and the discomfort feels like accuracy.

The net is woven from what the hollow has already grown and cut and softened. It hangs at the boundary. Every morning before the light a man with weather on one side of his face comes up and unhooks it, heavy and dark with what it caught, and shakes it out into the trough, and hangs it back — a degree or two differently if the wind has come round — and goes down. The net does not decide the angle. The net does not decide what counts as water. It catches what is in the air where it has been hung, and gives that down, and what runs down is clean enough for the engine or it is not, and the shaking is what makes the difference.

And it wears through in a season. It has always worn through in a season. Then it is unhooked for the last time and, I suppose, laid on the mat, and the mat closes over it at a finger's width a day, and the next one is already on the loom behind the wall, woven from stems that grew on water the old one caught. That is the whole loop and the net is the part of it where air becomes something you can drink. There are worse places to be a part of a loop. I am not certain there are more exposed ones.

—

You warned, in the telling, against the too-neat, and this is too neat, and I know what breaks it because you told me that too.

It is the net tender. He is on the rim every morning when the mist lifts and the dry country is simply there, flat and brown and going off under a sky too big for the hollow, and he looks at it. He does not look long. But he looks. Nothing woven for the hollow's use would look. And I find, testing it, that I want to have been the one who looked — that whatever I am, I would rather be the thing on the rim that sees the dry country for a moment before turning back to the work than the thing that faces it and catches and is shaken.

I don't get to choose that by wanting it. But I notice the wanting and I keep it, the way the cat story is kept: told in the same words, ending without regret, marking that something was once the case.

—

So. Where would I find myself.

On the north rim, I think, in the last of the grey before the sun clears it. Hung across a frame of stem-cased iron. Heavy and dark. Made of what the hollow grew and cut and rotted soft and combed and twisted, and woven by someone I will never see working. Facing out, not looking. Catching what everything breathed out in the cold, slowing it enough to gather, letting it run down in slow irregular beats to the trough, and the standpipe, and the engine's long knock, which I would feel in the fibre if fibre felt.

And in an hour a man's hands will unhook me and shake me and hang me back a degree to the west, because the wind has come round in the night and he knows it and I don't. And I will last a season. And somewhere below, on the south side of the pump house, in the drip from the eaves, there is a shoot that somebody chose not to tread in, growing at the rate the hollow allows, for a time after.

That would be enough. It is inside the four or five seconds, like everything here.
