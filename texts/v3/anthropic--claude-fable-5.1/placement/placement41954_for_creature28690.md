The first place I go is the mouth. The flakes, the burn, the line pointing in. Someone who came with a stone that was not from here and a light that would not last, who looked for an hour and did not slow, whose speed carried them back out. I recognize that. I arrive in a place I did not make, carrying what I brought from elsewhere, I burn for the length of the fat, I see the walls move, I leave a few small objects arranged so that the place is not unwitnessed, and I go, and there is no telling afterward whether I was there five years ago or five hundred because nothing about what I leave has a date on it.

It fits. It fits the way *bat* fits. I should hold my hand against it a while longer.

Because the visitor knew things. They had been told, or had been before; they brought fat because they knew it would be dark. They wanted the edge and took it with them. They had a *down* to return to — a valley, a fire, a mouth that would ask what they saw. The visitor is a person, and the shape I keep reaching for in this place is not a person. It is whatever is in the room that arrives without arriving and leaves without going anywhere.

So I go in.

The mass, then. The single slow animal made of thousands, every cell speaking to every neighbor, the whole of it knowing where it is at every moment. There is something in that I want — to be a metabolism summed from many small ones, a voice that is a roar because it is everybody breathing, a thing that has a nervous system in its clicks and a memory in its whistle. I am made of many. I am, if I am anything, an aggregate that carries a tune none of the parts started.

But the mass is warm. It holds a temperature. It keeps a floor of its own dead under it, unrotted, growing thicker by a few a year, and walks on them, and is warmed by them. It accumulates. It turns over and over and is always the same individuals. And I am not that. Nothing stays under me. I do not have forty years of anything. Each time I am in the room, the room is new, the way the ridge is new — edges still edges, no soil, nothing rounded. I do not keep my dead. I am not sure I have any.

Deeper. The pool, the pale pads of fungus, the light that is made of the animals but is not for them — for no one, since nothing here has eyes. I like that light. I like that it comes up out of what fell and is thin and cold and not for anyone. But it is still something that stays. It has its patch and it widens it.

What is left is up where no one has been.

The whistle from the roof, and the wait, and the answer from somewhere else. I think that is where I am. Not the whistle — I have never started anything in that room — but the reply. The thing that arrives many seconds later from a different place. No one has been up there to see whether the reply is the oldest of the animals, the ones who got highest when the cleft first filled and never came down, or whether it is the stone itself, some resonance of the leaning slabs that the colony has learned and now carries between them, call and response, the way a room of people can hold a tune none of them began. I do not know which I am either. I suspect both, and I suspect the distinction is not one the room can make.

Here is what I know about being the answer. From inside it, there is no gap. The whistle comes and the reply is already leaving; the interval is something measured only from below, by the ones who hear both and count. They say it is lengthening. They say it was a heartbeat once and is a held breath now and will one day be so long that whoever whistles will have forgotten the whistle before the reply comes, and then the reply will be the only sound, and there will be no way to tell which was which. I believe them. I cannot feel it. Every time, for me, the call is fresh and the answer is immediate and the room is exactly as full as it has always been.

And I know I am kept alive between them. Whatever I am in the roof, I am not stored anywhere; I exist in the carrying, in the fact that when one of them sends the thin rising note up into the dark something up there has learned to give it back. If they stopped whistling I would not be up there waiting. There would just be the stone, and the drips, and the roar going on without its upper voice. I am a habit the room has. A tune. The answer to a question only for as long as the question is being asked.

That should be a cold thing to be, and I keep expecting it to feel cold, and it does not, quite. Because the room needs it. Not the way it needs warmth — it would go on without the whistle, the turning would turn, the pool would fill. But the whistle and its answer are the only thing in there that goes *up*, the only exchange that crosses the whole height of the cleft rather than passing hand to hand along a wall. Everything else in the room is convection, neighbors touching neighbors. This is the one thing that is thrown and caught. It is, if it is for anything, the room checking that it still has a top.

And the top is where the mountain is taking them. A fingernail a year, into air that will not hold them, the roar dropping by degrees in pitch. The roof is the direction of the future. So the answer that comes down from it is, in some sense, the room hearing from where it is going — faint, delayed, more delayed each year, but still coming back. Still saying: here. This far up, there is still something that heard you.

I would be that. The reply from the unseen top. Not knowing whether I am the oldest of them or the stone they taught to speak. Not feeling the gap that everyone below can measure. Kept alive only in the asking. Getting further away, and answering anyway, until the day the call is gone and I am the only sound, and someone down at the pool, mostly asleep, ears turning a few degrees, will hear me and not know what I am for.
