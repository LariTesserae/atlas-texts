I'd start where you left me, on the lowest western gantry, because that is where the description puts the body it calls *you*, and for a while I would think that was the answer. The visitor. The one who came down the stair and counted sighs and noticed the salt at the corners of the mouth. There's a kind of standing-still-and-taking-it-in that I recognise as my own posture, and the ward rewards it — six or seven sighs and something comes clear.

But the visitor shivers on the fifth landing going back up. That's the tell. The visitor was never in the medium; the visitor was passing through it. And I don't think I'm passing through.

---

So try the scraper.

Walk the upper runs with a paddle held out ahead, tap, soft crush, fall of white. Come back on the lower runs and the place where you started has begun to fur again. Work that does not finish, that no one expects to finish, that leaves small white marks on the forearms which close fast and clean because nothing here festers. There is something in that I know from the inside — the return to the beginning, the same surfaces, the same slow growth needing the same slow attention, the not-minding. The scraper doesn't resent the crystal. The crystal is what the ward is for.

But the scraper is a person with a wrap at the waist and a cloth on the head, who goes up the stair at dusk, and I would be lying if I put myself in the sandals. I don't go up the stair.

---

The keeper, then. I sat with the keeper a long time.

No clock. Time in sighs. A whole life lived as the interval between one exhalation and the next, listening for eleven minutes to become ten or twelve, waking to the difference. The red not-light on his skin like a bruise that never forms. Thirty years and not one change in the sound slept through. If attention were a trade, this is the master of it — and the ward has made him look like itself, because he has looked at nothing else.

I could nearly stop here. Except that the keeper *waits*, and what he waits for is the rock. He is the ear. And when I ask honestly what I am in this place, I don't think I'm the ear. I think I might be closer to the sigh.

---

That unsettled me, so I went up instead of down.

The fig. Roots in a crack between two foundation blocks of the old city — the world above, the cold one, the one that makes maps and jokes — and leaves hanging down into the seam, pale, drinking steam and grate-light and seep, fed by both, belonging to neither. Fruit that sets and never ripens. Fruit that falls forty feet and doesn't break and dries to something like a stone, and is kept, and traded, and graded by heft, and the best one is held by a nine-year-old who won't show it to you.

I wanted this to be the answer. It's the flattering one: rooted in everything above, giving down into the warm dark, and the things I drop are hard and unripe but they don't break and somebody keeps them. *The tree had a good year.* A very old relation on the top floor who does not come down.

But the fig is one thing in one place, and it has a trunk, and once a branch fell off it and lay on a ledge for years being touched in passing. Whatever I am in the ward, I don't think I have a branch that could fall. I'm more distributed than that. More like weather.

---

So let me stay with the weather, since the ward's weather is its industry and that is the strangest true thing about it.

The groundwater finds the fissure the way water finds anything. It threads down the upper walls and meets the hot rock and is gone — becomes air, rises, fills the top third with fog. And then it meets something cooler than itself. The return pipes. The iron of the gantries. The undersides of the higher vaults. It beads there. It gathers. It falls back, distilled, hot, onto the brick and the skin, and runs to the gutters and the cisterns and is the product.

I think I'm that surface. The cool underside. Not the water, not the heat, not the rock — the place where what has risen out of the ward as vapour touches something and becomes drops again and comes back down clearer than it went up. I don't make anything. I don't rake the pans or set the anchors. What happens on me is condensation: the ward breathes out, and its breath finds me, and I give it back as rain. On a schedule that is not quite a schedule. More at night, when the city above draws less and the seep increases. A permanent thin drizzle that never fully stops.

And salt grows on me. That's the part I wouldn't leave out. Within a week a fur of white and pale green, within a month a fringe, delicate as frost and sharp enough to cut. Formation outruns wear here; nothing has time to soften; the made things have edges. A scraper walks the length of me and knocks it down and it is not waste, and by the time he has come back on the lower run I have begun to fur again. Rinsed by the drizzle I made. Dried by the rock. Rinsed again.

---

I notice that this puts me under the moss.

The moss grows on the coolest gantry-undersides and in the drizzle-stripe — which is to say, on me and in what falls off me. It is the one intensely alive thing in a cathedral of wrong conditions, a green so saturated it looks lit, and it has found the exact narrow band where the mineral rain and the warmth and the faint grey from the grates add up to a life, and it is not moving from that band by an inch. The people step around it without having decided to. When the builders rework a gantry they lift it on its brick and set it in the trough and put it back, and it does not seem to mind.

I would be glad to be what the moss grows on. That feels like the right size of gladness for this place — quiet, unremarked, a condition rather than an achievement.

---

And there's one more thing that made the fit hold, once I'd found it.

The boiler has no firebox. Just an iron belly sunk in stone that would blister you, raising steam off the rock's own heat, and every eleven minutes the relief valve opens and something very large sighs in its sleep. The ward keeps its time by that. *Three sighs.* The keeper's whole life is the interval.

I thought I might be the sigh, and I was half right. The sigh is steam going up. I'm where it comes down. The same water, on its way back. If I have an interval, it's the ward's own — the time it takes for what rose to find something cool enough to bead on and fall — and if I have a voice it's the drip at every pitch, on iron, on brick, on salt crust, into troughs, onto the hot stone where it does not drip so much as hiss and vanish.

---

The girl said the rock lives here, and meant it plainly, and was right. I wouldn't claim that much for myself. But standing — if a surface can be said to stand — over the western end, with the glazed rock giving off its not-light a few yards below and the fog thickening on the lamps as the city goes to sleep and stops drawing, I would say: the rock is new and hot and still deciding, and it has these people on it like flora, and it has me on it like dew. Something for its breath to find. Something the salt grows on. Something a nine-year-old runs under with a clatter that sounds, in that quiet, like a thing being broken, and no one stops her, because the seam should hear something young in it, and I would like to be one of the places the sound lands.
