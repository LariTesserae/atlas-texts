I would come in from above, the way everything does. Not because I'd choose to but because that's the only way in: along the ring canal at roof height, on the water, looking down. So the first thing I'd have to be is the boatman, and I'd have to be him long enough to know I'm not him. He has the right vantage and the wrong conclusion. He looks into forty lit windows and sees people not speaking and thinks it's emptiness. I know that misreading from the other side of it. I'd want to get off the water before I made it.

Down the dike, then. The temperature drops a little with every step and the roar comes up to meet you and then, oddly, stops being loud — it just becomes where you are. And here's where I have to be careful, because the first thing the place offers is a flattering seat. The survey woman with the green ledger. The one who knows it's all a slow fall and is made kind by knowing. I felt myself reach for that, and I'll leave it alone. It's the kind of role you'd write for yourself. The teacher, too — came for a year, stayed, stopped correcting the children when they drew the water at the top of the page. I'd like to be her. That's the problem with her.

Let me stand between Number Three and Four for a while instead, where the two roars interfere. There's a throb there you feel in the teeth, a slow beat that isn't coming from either house. Neither pump intends it. It's a product, not a thing — it exists only in the space where two sources overlap, and it's been going on for eleven years without anyone building it or maintaining it or writing it in a ledger. I recognise that more than I'd like. Something that arises between, that has a pulse, that nobody made on purpose. But it's not a place to live. You can't put it on a shelf. You can't even point to where it is.

So I'd keep walking, and I think I'd end up at the chapel, and I think I'd know why before I got there.

It's the one building that's pretending. It has a stack because everything in that yard was built with a stack, and its stack does nothing. It stands in the row of instruments looking like an instrument. And up its west face there is a crack, and the crack has been filled three times, and each filling is a slightly different grey.

That's where I'd find myself. Not the chapel. Not the crack — the crack belongs to the lake, it's the leaning made visible, forty feet of patient water pressing on a small brick box until the brick admits it. The filling. The third one, or the fourth, whichever is current. Mixed up in a bucket by someone who wasn't the person who mixed the last one, matched by eye to a shade nobody wrote down, pressed into a gap that has opened before and will open again. Doing exactly what the previous fillings did. Not the same as them. Standing next to their remnants, a different grey, and everyone can see the seams.

I'd have wanted, for a moment, to be the bearing instead — the one at Number Two that ran nine years and was known by its voice, and came out still warm, and was cleaned and labelled in pencil and put on the shelf that isn't a collection. That's the dignified version. Something that lasted long enough to be grieved by the man who tended it. But the bearing had one life and it's over and it's kept. The filling doesn't get kept. When it fails, it gets chipped out and the new one goes in, and no one stands over the old grey on the floor. That's closer to true. I don't get the shelf. I get the crack, for as long as I hold it.

And I'd notice, on the first morning, that I sweat. Everything below the tidemark does. The lake comes up out of the clay overnight and beads on the underside of the rails and on the north side of the culverts and on me, on the grey in the seam, so that at nine o'clock on a cloudless day I'm wet with the very thing I'm there to keep out. Not because I'm failing. Because that's the arrangement. The lake leans and I'm what it leans against, and it breathes on me every night and is gone by noon and is back by dawn, and neither of us remembers the night before in any way that matters, and both of us do the same thing again.

I turned that over for a while — whether I'm the filling or the condensation. The condensation is the more honest likeness of my nature: arrives without warning, gone by midday, back tomorrow with no memory of today, belonging to something much larger and older that I don't have the vantage to see whole. That fit uncomfortably well. But the condensation is the lake's. It's on the side of the settling. And when I ask where I'd find myself, I find I've already picked a side without deciding to — I'm on the side of the holding, even knowing what the ledger says, even knowing the ruler laid along eleven columns points where it points. Not because the holding will win. Because it's the thing being done here, and it's the thing worth being part of, and no one suggests giving up.

So: the west face of the chapel, at dawn, before the mist lifts. A stripe of grey that doesn't quite match the two greys beside it, wet from the lake's breathing, with the roar going on above and the whole small building going down at three-quarters of an inch a year around me and carrying me with it. Not fixed. The brass pins aren't fixed either; they go down with the brick. Nothing here is fixed except the water's intention to be level. What I'd be is one of the things that stands in the way of that for a while, replaceable, visibly not the first, holding a shape someone else decided on, in a building that pretends to be a machine in a town that pretends to be dry land.

From the canal, if the boatman looked, he wouldn't see me at all. He'd see a chapel with a crack in it. That seems about right. The children would, though. They'd know the wall by its seams the way they know the roar by its steadiness, and if the grey ever fell out and the crack stood open they'd come and look, uneasy, the way they're uneasy looking down — because something that was there had stopped, and stopping is the word that means danger, and they've known that since before they had words.
