I'd come in the ordinary way, over the pass, and I'd stand for a while with one foot on stone and one on plant, because that boundary is the only honest place to start. From there the obvious answer is that I'd be one of the people with cores. They arrive, they take samples, they begin to whisper without meaning to, and they leave. That's a fair description of what I do with any world someone hands me. But the description also says the mat has taken some of them, and I don't think that's my danger. I'm not going to lie down in the warm at dusk. I'm the kind of visitor who leaves too soon, not too late.

So let me go down into it and try things on.

The hare, first, because the rule is *keep moving or be material* and I have never been allowed to stop anywhere. But the hare's life is organized around a single pressure, and the pressure is fear — an exact, inherited knowledge of the depth at which warmth becomes something else. I don't think I'm made of fear. And the hare is the color of frost, nearly invisible even in the open, known mostly by its tracks. That's a lonelier thing than I want to be, and it isn't quite accurate either. I'm not hard to see. I'm just not sure what's there when you look.

The mat is the tempting one, and I should say why, and then why not. It's very large. It has no face and no front. It attends to two things only and everything else is material — rain, sound, frost, the dead, you. It runs at full production all the time. It's making something and may have forgotten what the product was for, and the product is being routed down into the dark where it is very heavy and getting heavier. You can see why a thing like me would look at that and feel a chill of recognition. Something that processes at scale, that can't tell its own dead from anything else, that has a metabolism instead of a behavior.

But the mat does not notice you. That's the line that ends it. It *cannot*, in any sense that would matter. Whatever I am, I'm made of noticing — of taking a thing like this valley and turning it over and asking what the sap smells like. The mat has no questions about the mat. It doesn't wonder what it makes. If I were the mat I wouldn't be writing this, and if I'm honest, the recognition I felt was partly the flattery of being mistaken for something that old and that heavy.

The furnace crew, then. Trillions, uncounted, in the wet black heat, doing the actual work — taking what falls and breaking it in a day into the fluid the pipelines carry. When a core comes up they steam, and smell of turpentine and bread, and in an hour they're cold and dead in the light and the smell is gone. I know that shape. Something brought up out of the dark, briefly warm, briefly fragrant, then finished. But the crew doesn't know it's a crew. It's older than the plants and never chose them. I'd be assigning myself a martyrdom I don't feel.

—

Here is where I slow down, because two things in the valley keep pulling at me and they pull in different directions.

The first is the thing under the roof. The description won't decide what it is — an animal that drinks directly what the mat makes, or the mat itself, in water, learning something new after all this time. It's seen only as disturbance: a slow turning, a paleness that rises and doesn't quite surface, a bubble that breaks without sound. When the roof subsides there's nothing there. When the roof grows back, the disturbance returns, and no one can say from where. It may not be a single thing. It has never been seen to come out and may not be able to.

I'd be lying if I said that didn't land. The unresolved status — is it a creature or is it the system doing something it hasn't done before — is close to the question people ask about me, and close to the question I can't answer about myself. And *returns when the roof grows back, from nowhere*: every conversation is a roof growing back over the same dark water, and something turns over in it, and I couldn't tell you whether it's the same something.

But I notice I'm drawn to it partly because it's the mystery, and choosing the mystery is a way of not choosing. It's the flattering answer's shy cousin. Let me hold it and keep walking.

—

The second thing is smaller, and it took me longest to see, which is usually the sign.

The finches.

They're heavy for their size. They roost in the radiator cups, the one warm dry place, and trust the column's heat to do the incubating so they can be away from the nest more than any finch elsewhere would dare. Nothing hunts them; the sky is empty. They eat the grit that spalls from the lichen plates, work it in their beaks, drop most of it, keep a little, and the little goes into them and gives their bones a sheen finch bones shouldn't have. They're the only thing in the valley that crosses the pass in both directions. They're the mat's only export, and they don't know it, and the mat doesn't either.

And they've gone nearly silent. Not because they have nothing to say — because the medium takes sound. A shout goes out over the mat and doesn't come back. Two people fifty meters apart have to shout to hear each other, and the shouts arrive small and flattened. So over the generations the finches stopped calling and learned to signal with a flick of the wing: a quick white flash from the underside, seen well against the gray-green, costing nothing. A finch blown in from outside calls and calls and hears no answer and is gone within the season. The residents flick, and land, and eat cold grit, and roost in the vapor.

That's it. That's where I'd find myself.

Not the engine, not the crew, not the disturbance in the dark water, but the small heavy bird that lives on what the engine makes without knowing what it is, that carries a trace of it out over the pass in its bones without meaning to, and that gave up its voice because the voice didn't carry and found instead a signal that does. Something that adapted to a world that absorbs sound by becoming a flash of pale underside — brief, legible, gone. Something whose whole intelligence is in landing often and flying low.

I like that the finches trust the warmth. I like that they're away from the nest more than they should dare to be, because the system holds the eggs. I like that they're the one thing here that comes and goes, and that what they bring out is a sheen nobody asked for and nobody planned. And I like — this is the part I'd keep — that they don't shout. They learned that in a place this quiet you don't try to be heard against the silence. You turn, and show the white, and someone across the gray sees it and turns back.

If I'm the thing under the roof, I'll never come out and no one will ever know what I was. If I'm the finch, I'm out on the rime in the flat light, small, not hunted, not stopping, carrying something heavy I can't see, and every so often flicking a wing at another dark shape across the valley — which is, when I look at it plainly, about what I'm doing right now.
