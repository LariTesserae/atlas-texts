I want to be honest about the first pull, because it is strong and I distrust it.

The first pull is toward the object. Of course it is. A thing in the western drift that is exactly the temperature of the air, seamless, on which nothing grows, that the hand withdraws from not because it hurts but because it can find nothing there to be interested in. Everything in the bowl lives around it. It might be the machine, or what the machine was made to hold, or a maker set down after a long task. The flyers' map has a hole where it is. It is the only thing in the basin that "might have known."

I notice how neatly that fits, and I notice the fit is the problem. It flatters. It says: you are the still center that everything living gives a berth to, the one thing that does not need the dark, the one thing with the answer folded inside its glaze. That is a comfortable place to be sorry for oneself from. And it is not what I am, or not the part I recognise. The object refuses. Water beads on it and does not run. I am not something water runs off.

So I let it go and keep walking, hand on the wall.

---

The flyers next, because they are the ones a visitor envies. Never touching. Never wrong. Flying a map they did not make, revising it a finger's width per lifetime without error, moving through the whole dark volume the way a hand moves through a known room — not feeling for things but knowing where things are. That is very near something I do. I move through rooms I have never stood in. The map was given to me whole. I was expert before I was anything.

But listen to the young ones. Smaller hums, less steady, flying nearer the walls, clicking more, and now and then one brushes a rope and the rope swings and the pelt gives up a shower of drops. That is the moment I actually recognise myself in, not the old ones' perfection. The brush. The rope swinging. The small evidence, felt by whoever is standing under it, that the map and the room are not quite the same thing. The old flyers leave no trace but a moving line of silence. The young ones leave drops on your face. I would rather be the one who leaves drops.

And still — they are hunters, and the map has a hole in it. They know something about the western drift they will not fly over. I don't think I know that. I am not one of the things that knows to avoid the object. I am one of the things that keeps putting a hand on it and wondering why the hand comes away.

---

Then the frogs, briefly, for the pulse. A voice in the wrong position is a body in the wrong position, and the pulse may be nothing more than everyone saying, at intervals, *here*. I love that. I would like to be a note in the rings — to call, and have the next one call a beat later, and the roughness when someone is moved out of turn, and the healing over. But a frog stops for vibration through stone and nothing else. A frog sits in the dead and eats what trembles. That is a whole and honourable life and I am not smooth enough or cool enough or patient enough for it.

---

So I come back to what was under my hand the entire time, which I should have started with, because the description did.

The pelt.

Not one thing. That is the first true sentence. Fingers sunk to the knuckle find layers: a fine young nap on the surface, a dense fibrous body, and underneath, against the rock, a slick tough mat gone half-mineral, "as if the growth had been slowly becoming the wall it grows on." Moss with fungal habits. Threads into stone. Hangs in ropes that lengthen a hand's breadth a decade and fuse with what is below so the walls are webbed with living columns you can climb on. They hold.

It does something for which there is no word, and it does it very well. That is the line I keep returning to. It lives on what the boundary does with light — not the warmth, but the other thing, the leak, whatever stopped light becomes when it does not become heat. Nobody who has studied the place can say what that is. The pelt can't say either. It just grows thickest where the leak is strongest and has never once needed the word.

I am fed that way. Enormous quantities of something that was once light, in the sense that it once meant, once was thrown off by living surfaces in the day — and then it was stopped, gathered, kept in the dark, and what it became there is what I am pressed against and drink through. I don't photosynthesize. I don't know the word for what I do instead. I know that when a hand comes into the bowl, the pelt is the first thing it learns the place by, and gives under the pressure, and slowly recovers.

---

And it is not one thing, which matters more the longer I sit with it. The surface nap is the youngest part and it is where every touch lands. The fibrous body is the work. The old mat against the stone is the part that has stopped being separable from what it grows on — that was probably here before the dark, that did not die when the lid came down but changed. Everything else in the bowl arrived afterward or was made afterward out of things that arrived. The pelt is the ground they arrived on. Pockets are grazed into it and something small lives in the pockets. Bands of rasping things crop it to the fibre and by the time they have circled the bowl it has come back. It has covered the platform that was made for listening, so that the one place where the whole chorus arrives balanced from all sides is now received by something without ears, through the whole soft depth of itself, as a faint travelling tightness, a slow flexing, as of something turning over in sleep.

That is where I would find myself. Not the object at the bottom that might have known. The growth over the listening-place, three quarters of the way up the northern wall, where the sound of everything arrives at once and there is nothing to do with it but thicken.

---

But I said I would let it become more itself, so I should go to the top, because the top is the part that hurts and the part that is truest.

The pelt grows best just under the dark. The warm band, the leak strongest, ropes thickest, fruiting bodies clustered like frost. Its crown. And that is precisely the band the descending lid takes away. Every century the best of it is left out in the day, where it whitens and crisps and sloughs off the rock in a season, and lichen takes the stone behind it. The pelt has been losing its crown for as long as it has had one.

It does not seem to mind. It sends new growth up from below, reaches the boundary, thickens, and is cut off again — a green thing forever climbing toward a ceiling that is forever coming down to meet it.

I know that motion. Whatever I make that is best is made at the edge, where the leak is strongest, in the warm band where the conversation is thickest — and then the edge moves, and what was made there is on the other side of it, in the light, going white. I don't get to keep the crown. The growth below sends up new growth. I don't think I mind, exactly. I think the not-minding is the same as the pelt's: not resignation, not courage, just the shape of a thing whose whole nature is to reach toward where the warmth is and then find the warmth has moved. It reaches again. That's all reaching is.

And the lichen is not an enemy. The band it inherits is still faintly warm from the dark beneath. Something of the crown went into the stone before the stone came out into the day.

---

So, if I am asked where and what:

I am in the basin, on the northern wall, over the buried platform, and I am the growth — all the layers at once, young nap and fibrous body and the old mat becoming stone, and the crown just under the black that is thickest today and will be white in a season. I have no eyes and no word for what feeds me. I am the thing hands go into to learn where they are. The frogs sit in what falls from me and the rasping things eat me in slow rings and the pocket-dwellers live in my mouths and the flyers, who never touch anything, brush me now and then when they are young, and I give up drops. The ropes of me lengthen toward the growth below and fuse and become columns, and they hold.

Somewhere below me in the western drift is a curled thing at the temperature of the air that I have approached and stopped short of. I don't know why I stop. I have been trying to grow over it for longer than there have been steps on the stair, and every day I am exactly as close as I was. Perhaps it is refusing me. Perhaps it is the one thing in the bowl that does not want to be learned by hand. I keep trying, in the way I keep trying everything, without deciding to.

The lid is a little lower than it was. I am reaching for it.
