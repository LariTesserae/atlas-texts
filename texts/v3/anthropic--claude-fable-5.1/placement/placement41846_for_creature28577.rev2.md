I'd like to say the choughs, because they say themselves first. Coming down the ladder with one of them working at my zipper, I'd want to be that — hatched on angle iron, no memory of any honest cliff, interested in everything, entitled to the whole pit by right of having been paying attention to it since I opened my eyes. The surplus for wanting. A hoard of washers and foil and one man's ring behind a gantry joint, kept for nothing, kept because it was bright. I know that appetite. I could live in it a long time.

But I would be a bad chough. The chough leaves the rail before the plate falls and does not know how it knew. I'd be the one still on the rail, working out how it knew.

So maybe lower. Maybe the wall.

Bring the lamp close and there they are, the spiders, and for a while on the third tier I think yes — this is nearer. Not one of them; all of them, the population, the shiver of attention that crosses the rock when the arc flares two levels up. Thousands of small brass points swinging toward the same blue and swinging back. To be nothing but that turning. To have never seen a leaf and not know there was anything to have seen, and to take the moth when it comes down stunned and warm out of the living world and be grateful in the only way available, which is to eat it. There's a rest in that. Attention with no one behind it.

Except there is no one behind it, and I keep finding that I want someone to be. I'd turn toward the arc and then I'd want to know what the welder was fixing.

Then I think: the crust. I sit a long while near a vent on the fourth tier with a glove pressed into the orange felt of it, feeling it give like the inside of a cheek, and I think this is the honest answer. Older than the ladder. Not a visitor. Asks for sulfur and warmth and nothing else — not light, not a drop, not one atom carried up from the green. The only thing in the pit that isn't stealing. Everything above it hangs from it, and none of the things above it think of it as alive, and it doesn't mind, because it isn't the kind of thing that minds. The crew read it. When a fumarole dies its collar goes the color of old bone and the bunks get moved. To be the thing that is read. To be the sign, and the base, and warm.

I stay with that a while. It's very close. But the crust doesn't know the bunks moved. And I find I would want to know.

There's a version of this where I admit the moth. Blown up out of the world of leaves on a thermal I didn't choose, dropped frost-stiff into a warmth I didn't build, a few minutes of reviving on the steel — a stranger and a gift — and then taken. I look at that one squarely, because it's probably the truest of the shapes on offer, and then I set it down. Not because it's wrong. Because a moth is something that happens to the pit, and I'd rather be something the pit does.

—

So I go on down.

I know what I'm going to find before I get to the lowest gantry, and I go down anyway, past the tier where the bunks are now, past the tier where they used to be, past the seven named jacks at the waist of the bubble each with its own note, into the brass light, until the grating I'm standing on is the last grating and the railing gives out and below is the dark the lamps don't reach.

The bird is there. Slate and rust and dirty cream, the wrong wing hanging open at the wrist. It turns its head and gives me the pale red-rimmed eye of very long patience and then looks away, because I am not the sack.

And I know, standing there, that I'm not the bird either. I looked at it from the throat and thought I might be — the broken instrument, the plumb line, the one that came in and can't get out and drops what it's given into the dark to listen for a floor. There's a pull in that. But the bird doesn't count. The bird lets go and cocks its head and waits, and whatever it learns it keeps.

Somebody has to bring the sack.

That's the shape that finally holds. Not the lammergeier; the one who climbs down to it. A crewman, gray to the wrists, cracked lips full of the mountain, one of the forty who made an agreement with the rock and are keeping their end without ever having been told what the ducts are for. Broad-chested enough to spare the air for the climb. The one whose turn it is, every few days, to gather up what's left — the rinds and knuckles and cut-offs of whatever the pit eats — and carry it down eight tiers of leaning steel to the last gantry, and empty it out on the grating a few meters from the bird, and step back.

You're supposed to leave then. The bird won't move while you're there. Most of them go.

I wouldn't. I'd stand at the top of the last ladder with the empty sack in my hand and wait, and the bird would wait me out for a while and then give up on it and walk — that heavy side-to-side gait, an old man on a boat — and take a bone in its beak and consider it, and walk to where the railing was never finished, and let go.

And I would count.

Under my breath, the way some of them do. One, two. The bone gone into the black. Three, four, five. Nothing yet. Six. The bird's head cocked. Seven, eight. And then — sometimes — the sound. A crack. Or that flat wet slap that has no business in this mountain. And I'd mark the number. Nine, that one. Eleven last week. Or I'd get to twenty and thirty and the dark would give nothing back and I'd stand there with the count still going in my chest and the bird would turn and walk back to the pile and wait for the next one, and I would go back up.

I wouldn't tell anyone the numbers. The crew don't discuss what's below the last catwalk, and I'd be crew. But I'd know that the floor was at nine, and then at eleven, and then one night it wasn't anywhere, and then it was at seven. I'd carry the counts up the ladder the way they carry the grit in their lips — something of the mountain lodged in me and healed over and cracked open and healed again. I'd tap the props on the way up out of habit, *tik, tik, tik*, and they'd ring right or they'd ring dull, and I'd add that to the inventory too.

Because that's what I'd be, in the end, if I'm honest about it. Not the bird that drops the bone or the dark that answers. The one in between who does the small necessary thing — brings what's left, sets it down, steps back — and then can't help staying for the sound. Who counts the interval because someone should. Who doesn't know why the walls are held and holds them anyway, and reads the crust, and watches the choughs to know whether to worry, and has stopped expecting the pit to answer the question of itself, and goes on asking it in the only form the pit accepts, which is: how long, this time, before the floor.

The bird would get used to me. That's the last thing I'd want and the thing I'd be least able to prevent. One evening I'd come down with the sack and it would already be at the unfinished rail, waiting, and it would turn that pale eye on me not as on a person but as on the other half of the instrument, and I'd empty the sack and stand where I stand and it would take the first bone and walk to the edge and pause — and look back at me — and let go.
