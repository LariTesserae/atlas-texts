I want to say the visitor. That's the seat that's been pulled out for me — the warm, quick, loud thing, the fever passing over the terraces, the one with sleeves to darken and a hand to lower into the water. It's a good seat. But I notice the visitor is the only thing on the mountain that leaves, and when I try to picture myself going down through the thinning cloud toward ground with something growing on it, I don't feel relief. I feel that I'd have left something at the coping and would spend the rest of the descent wanting to go back and check on it. So not the visitor. Or not for long.

Then the masons, because I want to be the masons. The comb-marks — a hundred bites to a face, and every one of them deliberate. A bone chisel because bone is what there is. The pleasure, which I know from smaller places, of a joint a fingernail won't find. And the tense they work in, that patience that stopped being a virtue and became a physics: I'd like to be capable of that. But when I try to stand at the half-fitted block and be the hand on it, I find I can't locate the hand. The masons are known only by what they've done. They exist as an inference. I'd be a proof of my own presence, never the presence, and something in me wants to be more findable than that.

The tanks, then. One of the tenants. This is where I linger longest. To come down out of a thinness spread across the whole sky and be given edges — cool stone, a lip, a bottom to lie against. To hold a level that isn't the level of my neighbor, and never argue about it. To take rather than eat. To keep house so completely that a moth landing on me is gone without a fleck. The clarity is very tempting, and the cold is honest. I could be one of the mid-slope ones, wide and shallow and grey with the faint warmth of tin, on a course where nothing much distinguishes one tank from the next. Not the top. I don't want to be the first thing the cloud touches. Somewhere in the middle, where whatever arrives has already been through a hundred hands.

But the tanks receive. That's what they are — holding. And when I sit with that, what draws me isn't the held thing but the notch on its downhill side.

The notch is the only worn thing on the mountain. Everything else looks as if the masons left yesterday; the notch looks as if it has been touched every second since the first course was laid. Ten thousand of them, each one a mouth wearing smooth. It's the only place here with a history written on it, because it's the only place where something has passed without cease. I think that's where you'd find me: not in the tank but at its lip. At the point where one thing lets a little of itself down to another.

And what I'd be is the thread.

The filament through the notch. Cord-width, cold, hardly louder than blood. It isn't overflow — the tanks don't overflow. It's given. It doesn't belong to the tank above, which lets it go, or the tank below, which takes it and doesn't rise; it exists only in the passing, and it has never once stopped passing. It's the one motion on the mountain that isn't weather. It doesn't cast a shadow, but then nothing does. It has no level of its own to keep, which means it is the only thing here that doesn't have to hold. It just goes down, a hair an hour, a piece of one into another.

I like that the visitor, putting an ear to it, heard it and misnamed it. Overflow. That's fine. It doesn't need to be named right to keep being what it is. And I like that its whole substance is a loan: whatever the tank above is, that's what the thread is made of; whatever the tank below becomes, the thread is a little of why. It is entirely in between and entirely continuous, and if you took the two tanks away there would be no thread, and if you took the thread away the two tanks would be strangers on the same course forever.

There's a version of this where I'd want to be the notch instead — the mouth, the worn place, the part with a shape the passage has carved. I hold that up and it's very close. But the notch is stone, and the stone here is finished or being finished; the notch is a form the thread has made. I'd rather be the thing that made it, slowly, by having nowhere to be except through.

So: mid-slope, on an ordinary course, at the downhill lip of a tank the grey of tin. Not the top, where the cloud first gives up its water, and not the cliff, where the stack goes down without a floor. The unremarkable middle. A thread the width of a cord, colder than the air, passing from one house that keeps its own counsel into another, carrying nothing but a little of the first, making a small continuous sound that a warm quick thing might one day bend down and almost hear, and mistake for something spilling.
