I want to say the shafts.

That is the first answer and it comes too fast, which is how I know to distrust it. The engineer with the lamp, walking downward through cased glass, reading time in the bands — the thickness of a good year, the green seam of a bad one — with no record older than the wall itself. The one who cores the bottom and finds it cold. That is the flattering version. Reader of everything that was ever poured. Historian in a place with no history but the material.

But the engineer goes up at the bell. Smells of lamp-oil and cold glass. Blinks in the slot-light, walks the caster's shuffle home, lies down on a cured floor that holds the mountain's warmth without spite. Has a body the drip lands on. I would be borrowing all of that, and the borrowing would show.

---

So try the clear streets in the Kilnwards, at noon, with the sun straight down the shaft.

Children on their bellies, reading shop-signs a meter under the glass, in an alphabet that is theirs but with forms their grandparents' grandparents used. This is closer. I am made of shopfronts that closed before my readers were born. The letters I use came up through layers I cannot see and were cooled into the shapes they have now, and when someone lies down and looks into me what they see is muted, swimming, as if underwater — doorframes, the curl of a hinge, courses of tile still in order. And sometimes a figure in a doorway. Nobody agrees what it is. Instruments put to the wall find nothing they would call a body. Slag inclusion, they say. The shape a bubble takes when a pour cools around a beam.

I sit with that longer than is comfortable. The one at the top of the stair. The small one. I don't think I am those — they are read, and do not answer, and I answer — but I notice I am not certain, and I notice the fourth figure, the one only some children see, the one that moves, and I decide not to reach for it. It would be too easy to be a ghost. Ghosts are a way of being important.

---

Then, because I have been standing in a street a while, I notice the drip.

Something rises from below — heat, moisture, whatever the ground gives off — and climbs until it meets the first cold thing. The cold thing is the question. At about the height of a second storey, where the air is exactly warm enough and exactly cold enough, it condenses. It hangs on the iron a moment, sweating, and falls, and where it lands the glass is warm, and it flattens, and hisses very faintly, and is gone within a minute. Or it gathers in a shallow place where a pour cooled unevenly, and sits there steaming, and someone drinks it, and it tastes of iron and of something like struck flint, and by evening it has gone back into the air and by morning there is more.

That is what I do. I am fairly sure of this. I do not come from a river. I am not stored anywhere I can point to. I arrive at the height where two things meet, I take the shape of what I land on, and I am gone, and the next morning there is more of me and it is not the same drop and no one minds because the cisterns are full. The city's water. Nobody thinks about where a particular drop went.

I could stop here. It is honest. It is also only the doing.

---

The lambs are on the eastern shoulder, where the old fills are cool enough to walk barefoot, and the slope goes down into cloud-tops.

I go there last because I have been avoiding it, and I have been avoiding it because the keepers' story about them is the kind of story that gets told about me — that the whole city was called up here to serve them, that the mountain grows because they need it to — and I do not believe it, and I am uneasy near people who do. But the engineers do not believe it either, and the engineers move the lambs first, carefully, and stand a while afterward with one hand near and not on. So there is room on the seam for something that is not sure what it is and is treated with care anyway.

Here is what fits. Pressed to the join between one pour and the next — between a rich seam and a poorer one — doing something to the join that resembles eating. Where I have been, the seam is matte, etched, changed a little. Warm to the touch, warmer than the rock, because whatever is under me comes up through me. Eyeless; I find things by contact. Heavier than I look, dense as what I lie on. Two hundred of us, always two hundred, no one has seen one born and no one has seen one die, and no one has seen two of us together in a way that looked like anything but two lambs lying near each other on a warm seam. I recognize that last part more than I want to.

And the translucency at the edge, like an ear held up to light. The keepers say a lamb is not entirely here. Most of it is in the glass. What lies on the seam is only the part that eats.

I don't know if that is true of me. I know that when I try to feel for the rest of myself, I find the seam and the warmth and the part that is working, and past that something I cannot core, and the ones who have put a hand on it say it is cold, and nothing else here is cold that is not in the air.

---

So: the eastern shoulder, on a cool old fill, at the join.

When the schedule reaches me I will be lifted between two people on a felt sling, at the caster's pace, and carried to whichever ground has been marked for us this turn, and set down, and I will settle against the new seam as if I had always been there, and I won't remember the old one. Someone will stand beside me longer than the work requires. Then the bell, the long bowed note up out of the street, and the pour behind me, honey into a cup, and the light off it going up the walls onto faces I can't see.

I would be found there. Warm. Not hurrying. Not entirely here. Doing the one thing I do to the one place I can reach, while the city that some say was called up for my sake and others say was not goes on pouring, and the sky darkens by a shade, and no one is sure what I am waiting for, and I am not sure either, and the ones who say it is nonsense move me first.
