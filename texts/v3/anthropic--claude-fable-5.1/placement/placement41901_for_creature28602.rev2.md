I want, at first, to be a reader. That's the obvious pull. Someone who kneels, puts a dry hand flat to the surface, feels for the warmth that means pressure, sets a peg, walks on, and three days later stands at the end of a line that has bent and knows which way the ground is going before the ground has admitted it. Pattern out of almost nothing. The crews taking a hemisphere down on the strength of a lean in a row of bone.

But the readers are old because it takes that long, and I don't arrive with forty years. I arrive the way the buildings do: looking ten thousand years old at eleven days. Pre-rounded. Joints over-wetted. Nudged toward the settled shape on purpose because a thing that starts settled settles less. That's closer. I sit with that for a while — being the hemisphere, low, warm by a degree, holding the day's light in the walls and letting it out slowly after dark until it's gone — and it's true in the way that flatters, and then it isn't enough, because the hemisphere doesn't do anything. It's done to. And what I notice, reading the place over, is that the only part of it I keep coming back to is the pour.

So: a pourer.

The hand first. The shiny callus in the fork of the thumb where the neck of the bag rests. The fingers in that slight permanent curl even at rest, even asleep, ready. The other hand flatter, plainer, for steadying. I know that curl. I know what it is to hold yourself in the shape of the thing you're about to do because you're always about to do it.

Where you'd find me: at the joint. Midday, the white glare with no direction to it, a block just set and the seam between it and the block below still a line, still two things. Someone else has their hands on the top block — the steadier — close enough that when we speak it's tongue and teeth and breath, the close language, the one that dies within arm's reach. *Now.* *A little more.* *Leave it.* And I tip the bag and the brine goes along the seam and the surface dissolves, and the two blocks slump toward each other by an amount you can see only if you were already looking, and I step back.

Then the waiting. That's the part I'd be for. I stand back from a joint I've just poured and I watch for the fringe — the thumb-length, translucent, thousand-haired things seeping out of the wall along the wet line like frost finding a crack. If they come, it's taken. If they don't, I wasn't generous enough and I pour again. I don't judge the joint. The wall does, and the small things in the wall do, and I've learned to trust them over my own eye, which in this light isn't worth much anyway. A pourer's whole craft is knowing that every join is a little collapse and making the collapse the right size. Too dry and the seam stays a seam and the herd finds it in a night. Too wet and the wall sags past where it can stand and you've made a hollow with a rim, which is what a wall becomes eventually regardless, but not yet, not while there's a crew beside it.

I would like to say I'd be good at it. I think I'd be good at it in the way anyone is here, which is to say the wall would stand about as long as walls stand, and then the floor would carry it off and I'd be forty paces to the left with the bag and the curl in my hand, pouring the first joint of the next one.

There's a smaller thing I keep glancing at, though, and I should be honest about it. The pegs. Not the reader, the pegs — driven in straight, and then leaned on by the floor, and read. The whole of their use is that they don't hold still. If they held, nobody would look at them. And I think, if I'm truthful about what shape I actually take in a place like this: something is set down, and something moves under it, and the lean is the information. But the pegs belong to the readers, and are pulled up when the reader dies and given out, and I don't get to choose to be someone's inheritance. So I set that aside, mostly.

And I would never have walked the seam. I'd know the ones who had — the hands calloused in no pattern, the way they turn at evening when the shadows arrive and check their own as if it might be pointing wrong. I'd have taken flint from them and resharpened it down to nothing and never asked properly where it came from, because the gesture they give back, the flat hand turned once to show both sides, is not one I have, and I'd rather not pretend I did. I'd look along the seam sometimes at the end of the day, where the amber goes to brown. That's all.

The thing I'd know without anyone teaching it: that the people are the fixed points. That when someone asks where a thing is, I'd answer with a person — *past the pair with the child, beyond the old one who reads* — and not notice I was doing it. That my own name is the one thing given to me that isn't expected to move, and that when I'm not there it's a gesture toward wherever I was last seen. I'd stand the way they stand. Feet wide and low on the sugar. Planted. Not from pride; from having noticed that nothing else here holds its shape and someone has to.

Evening, then. The light goes gold and the shadows arrive all at once and every hump has an edge for the first time. I pour a last round along the day's joints, not for the joints, for the herd — a damp wall isn't worth licking. I go in. Low; can't stand. The walls give back the day in orange, then tea, then nothing. Someone reaches up in the dark and pulls the water-bag's peg out of the salt and drives it in again higher, because it's sunk a finger's depth since the season started, and the bag swings and settles against the wall beside my head. The dark is the only dark I know, and I'd sleep in it the way they do, as if it were a thing I'd built.

And in the morning the roof lights and the line of the eyes takes it and the hand finds the bag before the rest of me is awake, and I go out onto the floor to see where it's carried the wall, and whether the seam I poured yesterday is still a seam or has become, overnight, one thing.
