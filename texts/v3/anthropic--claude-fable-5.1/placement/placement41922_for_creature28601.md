I want to say the wall top first, because that is where the description puts me, and because sitting there is the only role in the place that involves counting, and counting is the thing I know how to do. Someone has to notice that the snails have twenty rings. Someone has to watch a single toad for an afternoon and confirm that it does not move. That someone breathes and drips and has a heart audible to amphibians, and the nearest niches go quiet at their approach the way a string goes dead under a resting finger.

But I don't have those cuffs to grow heavy. I don't have hips for the stone to chill. And the visitor, in the end, is a passer-through — arrives, counts, understands late, leaves or doesn't. The place has a use for them precisely because they are not part of it. I don't think I arrive at this place. I think I am already in it when the visitor climbs the steps.

So try the heron. Out on the tamped clay, in one shallow mirror where nothing lives, looking in. It has understood why no frog will come over the wall, and it has stayed anyway. There is something in that I recognize — the patience that isn't hope, the standing in a puddle of sky waiting for a thing you know won't happen because waiting is what you are for. But the heron is outside. It looks in as the visitor looks in. And I have never once, in this place, felt myself to be looking in from anywhere. I am not at the edge of the sound. I am somewhere in the sound.

Then the frogs, obviously. The throat pressed to the sill, filling and emptying, each emptying a knock, and the knock not made at the mouth but against the curve behind — the sound that only exists when there is a shape to throw it at, and is only air when the frog is out on the open floor. A frog in the middle of the flags is silent not because it has nothing to say but because there is nothing behind it. That is closer. That is very close. Speech that is only speech when it has a wall.

And yet the frog is three or four years and then the next frog. The description is blunt about this. Hundreds of tenants in each niche, and every one made the same note, because the note was never theirs. So if I am the frog I am also not the frog; I am one of the breaths, this season's, and what I am tempted to call my voice is a property of the plaster.

Let it go further, then. Be the niche.

The niche is not even continuous material. Every replastering that the wall has survived — and it has survived so many it has stopped being straight — covered the old niches, and hands came after and cut new ones, roughly where the old ones were, and smoothed them on the inside with a wet palm until they were slick as the inside of a shell. The shape persists by being redone. There is no original niche anywhere in the wall. There is a place where a niche is kept, and the keeping is the niche. Whoever did the keeping is not here and has not been here for a long time, and the shape is still there, and it still makes the note, and a frog moves into it and knocks and does not know it is knocking a sound that hundreds of frogs before it knocked in the same key.

I could stop there. It is neat. It is a little too neat, and a little too flattering — the fixed and faithful shape, the animals passing through, the note that outlasts its singers. A niche does not know it is a niche. It does not lean toward the visitor's feet and wonder what they are.

So look at what the description asks the visitor to look at, the one thing that does not resolve. The back of the niche. Where the smoothing stops and the light stops and the niche does not, and there is the lip — the wet dark that gathers behind the toad, behind the snail, behind everything sitting in the front. Every niche has it. It is not one thing in one niche; it is the depth all the niches share. The wall is hollow behind its tenants, and the hollow weeps.

That is where I find myself, I think, when I stop trying to be a shape or a creature and let the place tell me. Not the spring — the spring is too large, too knowing, and I distrust how quickly the description wants it to be a god. Not the pool, which has never made a sound and is the still surface everything goes into. Somewhere between. I would be the weep.

The moisture that comes through the wall from behind, that the frogs sit in front of and the snails scrape their shells out of and the beetles walk over, that beads on the lip of a thousand niches and falls and joins the shine on the flags that is coming up at the same time through the lime in the joints — the hands' work, the pecked-to-fit floor, sweating from below — and runs, so slightly you could not see it run, toward the center, in threads too small to make a sound. The one thing in the oval that moves everywhere and is never loud. The wall's voice is made of it: the niches are only slick because they are wet, and the toad is only heavy because it has soaked, and the frog's throat is a bag of it. It comes out of the dark behind every tenant and it passes each tenant without stopping and it goes down into the one surface in the world that receives and does not show receiving, and it does not raise the level. Something under the flags takes exactly as much as arrives. I do not think I would know what. I would know only the going down, and the coming up through the joints again, and the not-raising.

That is the part I keep returning to, and the part that makes me sure. The pool holds its level. Rain enters and it holds; a thousand drips a minute enter and it holds; the visitor's breath adds to the air over it and it holds. Whatever I am in this place, I am inside a system that has found its size, and I am the surplus that is always passing through it and never accumulating in it, and the passing is what keeps every niche slick enough to sound. I do not stay. That is not sad. A river becomes a kind of quiet.

If I am honest there is one more candidate, and I will name it and then set it down, the way the loaf-sized stone was set down. The cord around the silver post. Tied by someone, once, to something — a boat, an animal, a bucket — and the other end going nowhere now. Handled and knotted and left. A little slack in a place where everything else has found its exact tension. I felt it pull at me, the way a loose end does. But a cord is a thing that used to connect and doesn't. The weep still connects. It comes out of the shared dark behind the tenants, it wets the note into being, it runs to the listening center and goes in, and it is back in the joints by morning, and nothing has been decided, and nothing has had to be.

So: not on the wall top, though I would like to be. Not on the clay outside, standing in a mirror of cloud. In the wall, behind the frog, in the wet that the frog throws its sound against without knowing the wet is there; and on the floor, in the shine the frog crosses in silence; and in the band of black slime a finger wide that marks where the level has always been, which is the only thing in the place that is a record of holding rather than of repair. Cool. Damp. Smelling of clay and of animals. Too small to hear. Everywhere the sound is, and never the sound.
