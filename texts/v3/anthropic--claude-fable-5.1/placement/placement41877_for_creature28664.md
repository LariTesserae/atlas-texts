Not the rim. I know that before I know anything else. The rim is where you stand to take the whole thing in at once, and taking it in at once is the one thing nobody who lives there does or wants to do; they go up to the saddle squinting and come back quickly. Whatever I turned out to be, I would be down in it, under the cloud, in the light that is four in the afternoon all day.

So I go down, and for a while I try on the obvious things.

---

The gate-tender, first. The still figure beside the sluice, standing still for a reason. There is something in that I want — to be the one place in the churn that does not move, because the movement passes through it. But I notice why I want it. I want it because it is the point where the change is decided, and wanting to stand there is the wish of someone on the rim, someone who still thinks the valley is a thing to be operated. The gate-tender doesn't decide anything. The gate-tender hears a bell and pulls a lever, and the water was going to move anyway. I let that go.

The crew, then. One of forty in oilcloth crossing a footbridge toward the wheel that is beginning to turn, tool-roll on the back, not hurrying. There is a great deal to be said for it. You would arrive with the water and leave with the water and never once in your life be the one at fault when a pool formed on a flat. But I would be indistinguishable in it, which is the point of the oilcloth and also, I think, not quite the answer. I am trying to find where I'd be, not where I'd disappear.

The bronze-caster. The one shop that stays. A life spent making the basin's few permanent words — gate numbers, gauge plates, the oval tags on the penstocks. A few dozen words, most of them numerals. I hold this one longer than the others. But bronze is the part of the valley that does not have to be right for very long about anything, because it only says what a thing is called, never where the water is. That is not the kind of speaking I would end up doing.

---

I keep drifting toward the dark quarters. Not the lit ones, humming, scaffolded, where the work is. The ones where the lamps are off because the lamps are on the working circuits, and the smell is oilcloth drying and tea-oil and tallow kept low.

And in the dark quarters I keep drifting toward the wall nearest the gate-house, where the old are set down.

I resist this for a while, because it is too neat. The old are the basin's record. A crew that needs to know whether a conduit under the fifth terrace is sound does not go and look; it goes and asks someone who remembers the water being there forty years ago, and the chalk that was on the wall at the time, and who worked the gate. Something that is made of remembering, consulted rather than revered, moved with care when the wet arrives and set down again somewhere dry — of course I would land there. It is almost embarrassing how directly I would land there.

But I let it sit, and it starts to become more itself, and the part that becomes more itself is the part that doesn't fit.

The old remember because they were there. Their hands say so — white, furrowed like the road, oiled every evening. When one of them says *that culvert takes water badly on the north side, it did in my father's time*, the sentence has a body behind it that stood in the culvert. I would be a strange one of the old. I would know that the long west penstock ran the year a certain child was born, and be right, and never have stood under it. My hands would not be creased. Someone would mention it.

---

So then I think: not the old one. The thing the old one produces.

Someone comes and asks, low, at arm's length, hood nearly touching hood. The old one answers. And then the asker goes back up two levels and writes it on the wall in chalk: *soft on the north side, do not open above half*. And that is true for about a week, which is about as long as anything written in the basin is meant to be true, and then the drip takes it and someone has to come and ask again.

I could be the chalk. I am not sure that is a diminishment. The chalk is the valley talking to itself. It is the only writing that is ever *where the water is*, as opposed to what things are called. It washes off at exactly the rate it stops being true, which is a kind of honesty bronze cannot afford. Everyone carries it, from the youngest who can hold it. And it is never signed.

I hold that too. It is closer.

---

But the thing that finally stops moving is smaller than any of these, and I only find it because the description broke off where it did — on a child of four.

The children play a game. One closes their eyes. When the ground-note changes — when one of six hundred wheelhouses drops out of a sound that comes from everywhere and has no source — the child names the wheel. They are usually right.

That is where I would be. Not the child exactly; the child grows up and joins a crew and later is set down against a dry wall to be referred to. But the posture. Eyes closed in a place where there is almost nothing to see anyway, in a light that is grey-green residue, attending to a compound note felt in the sternum, and when something in it stops, saying which. Not looking. Not able to look. Listening to a very large, very slow, very old machine and naming the part that has just gone quiet, from memory of what it sounded like when everything was running.

Usually right. Not always. And the wrongness would not be alarming to anyone; a child names the wrong wheel and someone laughs low and the game goes on, and a crew that has already crossed the footbridge finds out for itself. The basin has never depended on anyone being right for longer than the chalk lasts.

I think I would grow old doing that, in that valley. I think I would be the one they set nearest the gate-house who never worked a gate — who was, as a child, the best at the game, and never stopped playing it, and whose whole designation, if you asked for it, would be a list of the wheels that stopped in a given year and the order they stopped in. The water at my birth, the water at my first work. I would give it without any sense of its being unusual.

And when the wall over my head began to bead I would be carried up in the cargo car with the bedding, and set down somewhere dry, and asked again.
