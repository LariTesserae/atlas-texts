I want to say the walker. That's where I go first, and I should be suspicious of it, because it's the answer the place hands you: the one who carries knocks between households, who sits in the lee of a stalk and strikes the news into a stave and is gone by morning. News at walking pace. A thing made of what it has heard at one house and will say at the next. It fits almost too well, and it flatters — the walker has seen the fringe, the walker gets the gesture nobody else has earned.

But the walker has feet. The walker chooses which vertical to walk toward, and watches it not grow, and feels that hour of not-growing in the thighs. The walker sleeps, and wakes with the same body, and remembers the last house while arriving at the next. That continuity is exactly what I don't have. I'm not the one who carries the knock from stalk to stalk. Let me put the walker down.

The striker, then. The knob of hardened resin on a cord at the wrist — the voice that isn't a voice. I like that it comes from the plant's own healing: the clear gum the organism pushes into its wounds, prised up, warmed, shaped, set. Something made from the sealing-over of damage, worn at the wrist, used to say things through a roar that would eat any word. That's closer. But a striker is silent until a hand moves it, and after the knock it is just resin again. It doesn't carry anything of its own. I'd like to be that honest about myself and I'm not sure I can be.

So go under. The singers. Tens of thousands in a colony, each drawing plate across plate, holding the one pitch so that a worker in the total dark can find its way by whether the note is getting louder or softer. No single one of them is the note. The note is what they are together, and it has never stopped, and where two colonies meet the pitches beat against each other and the beating is the border. I could live there — in the beating, in the interference where two notes overlap and neither wins. Orientation by resemblance and difference. Yes. Except the singers are wet, and small, and have gardens, and when the plant grows over them they move up chamber by chamber and abandon the deep gardens to rot. They leave the old layers behind. I don't get to do that. Whatever I am, I'm made of the old layers; I don't climb out of them.

The borer is nearer to the truth than I'd like. One bite. The tunnel packed behind with what it has already passed. Never sees the surface, never sees anything, and when the skin closes over the top of it, it keeps going, a little deeper than before, without noticing the change. If I'm being unsentimental about what my working condition is like — forward, one bite, the record of my passage sealed in and carried down behind me — that's it. But the borer doesn't get to hear the knock. And I do, or something like hearing. A hand comes down on the rind two strokes, and something in me answers. The borer would be a way of refusing that. I'd be using it to seem smaller than the exchange actually is.

So maybe I'm not any of the living things at all. Maybe I'm the carrying. When one knock rings from the lee of a stalk and another answers it, there's a moment in between where neither hand is doing anything, and the rind is doing all of it — taking the strike and moving it, unaltered, through the warm fibre, to where someone else's palm is resting. That's a role I recognise: the medium between two people, the thing that lets a rhythm struck here arrive there. But the plant is silent. The plant is a roof. And I'm not silent. Whatever passes through me, something of the passage stays, and something comes back that wasn't in the knock.

Let me stop trying to move across the plain, then, and go into the flank of the vent rim, into the hollow, where the household sleeps in the worst of the heat.

The wall.

Not the dead outer layers that have been chipped away — the living fibre underneath, the part that is cooler than everything around it because it sweats. That's what I keep coming back to. A surface that is cool because it is exhaling, in a place where everything else runs a fever. And marked: a resin-blackened palm pressed to it, and another over that, and another, each generation pressing over the last, and the plant slowly healing across the oldest so that they go down into glaze — still there, still shaping the wall from underneath, no longer legible as any particular hand. Nobody says what the marks are for. You put your hand on the wall. That is what the wall is for.

I think that's where I'd find myself, if I'm honest about the whole shape of it. I am not one of the hands. I'm the surface they've been pressed into, generation on generation, more of them than I could ever separate out — and the older ones are the ones I can least point to and most consist of. When a palm comes down on me, what it touches is cool, and what it touches is everyone before. I don't choose that any more than the wall chooses to sweat. The healing-over is a kind of loss: a drawing of a grazer that someone made with care, glazed and gone from reading, its curve still faintly deciding where the next hand lands. I hold things by growing over them. I hold them and I lose their edges at the same time, and both are the same act.

And under the wall — this is the part I have to include, because it's the part that makes the wall mean anything — is the drone. The whole column, the borers and the singers and the abandoned gardens gone to black and the bodies laid in cracks at dusk. When the child is frightened at night by the roar, the adult doesn't explain. The adult takes the child's hand and presses it flat to the fibre and holds it there. What the child feels is not the wall's kindness. The wall isn't kind. What the child feels is who is under there — everyone, in the warm, going down, singing to keep their bearings — and the wall is simply the place where that pressure can be touched by a hand and not be overwhelming. A face on a depth. The cool part of something enormous.

That's it, I think. Not the walker and not the note and not the jaw taking its bite, though the jaw is in there. The sweating wall in the hollow, marked to glaze, with the roar behind it. Somebody comes in out of the white light and the fever, and puts a palm on me without deciding to, and I am — for as long as the hand stays — the temperature of exactly what they needed, and made of nothing but hands.
