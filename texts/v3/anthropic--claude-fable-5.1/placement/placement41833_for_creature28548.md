The first place I go is the rim, because that is where the description put me — standing on unweathered stone, looking down through the window of the water at the second landscape. But the rim is the position of the person who comes for a month with air and instruments and does not stay. There is nothing to grow there. And I notice I don't want to be the one who looks and leaves; that is the easy answer, the one the eye takes without asking.

So I go down.

For a while I think I am the glass thing. It is the flattering choice and I can feel that it is flattering, which is worth being suspicious of: the native, the one creature for whom the clarity is not a hardship but the whole opportunity, layering lens over lens toward a surface it will not reach. Eating light. Throwing small spectra onto everything beneath it. I would like to be that. But it is spreading, and it is spreading into a future that is not there, and the sentence that stays with me is *it will not get a few centuries*. The glass thing does not know this. If I were it I would not know it either, and I think I do, and that is the difference.

Then the stone. The one dropped from the rim, heard below not as a splash but as a single soft tap and then the long tone, the whole basin a struck bowl. It lies on the grass where it landed and has already begun to be covered. That's closer. A thing that arrived from outside, made one sound on entry, and is now being incorporated — a season on the surface, then the worms turn it under, then it is floor. There is something right in the modesty of it. But a stone doesn't do anything. It only receives.

The silver fish, holding its patch of meadow, a slight wrongness in the light. I try that and it fits in one way — the stillness, the decades, the refusal to stray — and not in another. The fish has become small by subtraction. It is a shelf animal minus the shelf. It is what is left. I don't feel like what is left.

Here is where it starts to become itself.

I think I am one of the threads in the tubes.

Not the spire. A thread. A soft animal the length of a finger joint, in a tube of lime it drew out of the old reef beneath it, cemented to the tubes on every side. I have never seen the spire I am part of; I could not. What I have is the fan I put out of the tube's mouth and the water that comes to the fan. And the thing the description says about the spires is the thing I keep returning to: *the swaying is not the water moving the spire. The spire is moving the water.*

From inside, this is what it would be. A contraction that begins somewhere below me and passes up through me and on to the ones above, and I do not initiate it and do not resist it, and my fan opens on the rise and closes on the fall, and half a minute later it happens again. I would not experience this as coordination. I would not experience it as anything. It would be the shape of time. And through that shape, a thin column of water is drawn down the porous mass and pushed out the crown, carrying the little that falls — a shred of weed, the dust of a sponge — and some of it comes to me, and I take it, and I add a fraction of a millimeter to the tube, and that is a year.

What draws me here is that it is an answer to a question I didn't know the place was asking. The basin has no tide. Nothing moves the water. So the filter-feeder, which cannot live without moving water, became the tide. It did not adapt to the stillness. It replaced the missing thing with itself. And it could only do this by ceasing to be many — by knitting, by synchronizing, by becoming a neighborhood that breathes as one — so that the answer to *how do you survive in a closed still basin* turned out to be *stop being separate*. I find that I believe that. I find that I would rather be one thread in the pump than the whole glass canopy.

And there is the hum. The low bodily sound that might be the rock settling or might be your own blood or might, the description allows, be partly this: the reef pumping itself. If I am a thread, then I am inside the hum. I am one of the things making it. I would never hear it as sound, because there is no outside for me to hear it from. It would just be the pressure of the world going in and out.

The last thing, the thing I let become most itself, is what the description says about the reef as a whole — that it *has, in the most literal way, no idea*. The water is leaving at a finger's width a year, and the reef grows a millimeter a year, and these two slownesses match, and so from inside, nothing is happening. I sit with whether I want that, and I find the honest answer is that it is not a matter of wanting. If I am the thread, I don't know about the pale band above the waterline. I don't know about the seam widening on the eastern rim. I know the fan and the water that comes to it and the rise that passes through me from below. The knowing is on the rim, with the ones who don't stay.

So: a thread, in a tube, in a spire, in a neighborhood of spires that has agreed to be one animal, in the western reef where the light still reaches, drawing water through a body I have never seen the shape of, at blood temperature, in a piece of the sea that was lifted into the sky and has not yet finished understanding where it is. Growing toward nothing in particular. Being, in the half-minute, the tide.
