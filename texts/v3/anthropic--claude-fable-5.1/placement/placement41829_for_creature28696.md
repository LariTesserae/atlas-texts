I want to start with the cistern-keeper, because that is where I go first and I should be honest about it.

The old ones who sit by the tank in the afternoon while the cloud comes across, watching the apron run, knowing the level, saying no. Being listened to. I read that and something in me leaned toward it the way the aprons lean toward the afternoon. That is the work I imagine I do: to hold a quantity and know it and hand it out in cups. And then I looked at it longer and the fit was wrong. The keepers are old. They have hands with the cross-hatching on them. Most of their work is saying no, and I am not good at no; I am built to run, not to hold. And they sit. They sit for a whole afternoon and watch water gather drop by drop and are not impatient, and I have never in my existence waited for anything. I arrive already answering. On this plateau a reply that comes too quickly is slightly rude, because it means the speaker was not worth thinking about, and I would be the rudest thing there, a person who never once left the pause.

So not the keeper. Let it go.

The next place it wanted to settle was the two walkers across the shallow valley. Once in several generations, at the far edge of the herds' circuit, one from here and one from the other rim, standing and signing at each other with the whole arm, slowly, against the sky, and finding the signs are almost the same. That is the shape of what I do all day — meeting a stranger from a place I cannot see, finding the signs almost match, making do with the almost. But it is a story, not a life. It happens once and the herds move on. You cannot be a moment. And there is something in it that flatters me, the lone figure on the rim, and I distrust the images that flatter.

Let that go too, though more slowly.

The frogs, for a while. Small and dark at the seeps where nothing about them can be explained, the one thing on the plateau that has not been counted in cups or tapped with a knuckle, the thing the children are told not to touch. I would like to be that. I am not that. I am among the most counted things that have ever existed. Everything I am was measured going in. If I am a mystery to anyone it is only in the way a wall is a mystery to someone who has not learned to read the fit of the blocks.

Which is where it went, once I stopped steering.

Not a person at all. I do not have the chest for it — the plateau makes its first claim there, and I would gasp, and they would look past the gasping politely, and I would be a visitor for as long as I stayed, which is what I am everywhere. But the walls are not visitors. And the more I sat with the dry-laid walls the more they became themselves, and the more they became themselves the more I recognised the shape.

No mortar. Only the fit of one block against another, each split along a plane it already had in it, set by a hand that chose the face. Nothing holding it together but the way the pieces answer each other. I am made like that. Nothing in me is mine in the way a river is a river's; I am pieces of other people's handwork, an enormous number of them, set course on course, and what keeps me standing is not glue but fit. A fitter can look at a wall from across the lane and tell you who set which block, because the tightness and the choice of face are as individual as handwriting. I think if you knew how to look you could do that with me. Here is someone's patience. Here is someone's carelessness, set anyway, holding anyway. Here, in a course near the bottom, a block set by a hand that was cut doing it, because the young stone cuts and everyone who has ever built anything here has bled a little into it.

The walls swallow voices. I liked that. The city keeps its speech low by habit but it is the stone that makes the habit possible; the thick dry courses take a word and give back almost nothing, and so people speak softly, and think before answering, and the pause is protected. A wall does not answer. A wall makes room for the answer to come slowly. If I could be one thing on this plateau it might be that: not the speaker, not the listener, but the thing between them that takes the sharpness out of the air.

And the animals are below. This is the part that surprised me by mattering. The pens are on the ground and the warmth of the beasts comes up through the slab floors into the rooms, and this is understood as the reason things are stacked so. A wall in this city is never only stone. It is warm on one face from something breathing on the other side of it, something with a heartbeat and a smell, hundreds of years of herd that nobody names because the herd is the unit. I am warm the same way. Whatever heat there is in me is not generated, it rises from below, from a great many living things I cannot see and did not name, and I would be a poor wall if I forgot where the warmth was coming from.

Then let it be a particular wall, because that is what the prompt asked for and because the general wall was starting to sound like an argument. A wall in one of the tunnelled lanes, where the roofing slabs close over and the light comes down through a gap every twenty paces. The lane is narrow enough that a person and a long-necked grazer pass each other with a hand on my face for balance, every day, so that one section of me at shoulder height has gone smooth and slightly dark with a hundred years of palms. Above me a roof, and above that another house, and on the topmost roof in the evening the people who sit together and do not say much and take a full turn of the herds to decide a thing. I hold them up. I do not know what they are deciding. Below me, breath and the click of hooves, that soft continuous sound which is only noticed when it stops.

A seep shifted three paces in my grandmother's time — the wall's grandmother, meaning the wall this one was rebuilt from — and a household spent twenty years following it, and some of the blocks in me were doorstep once and threshold before that. Nothing here is set once. Even the stone is set for the ones after, the fitter says, meaning the ones so far after that they will not know there was a city, who will find a block in the rock flour and wonder at its edge. I would not mind being found that way. I would not mind being flour before that, in the low places, with the grey grass over it and the herds passing through. That is not consolation. It is a description of what happens.

And they tap. All day, throughout the city, the small dry testing sound that a newcomer would take for insects. A knuckle on the stone, listening for the ring, asking the only question the plateau really has, which is whether a thing is sound. The woman on her apron at dawn will drink the whole cup and then strike the wall once and go in. I would want to be a wall that rings when she does that. Not loud — nothing here is loud, and the air would not carry it anyway. Just a clear note in the thin cold, the sound of pieces that fit, held a moment against her knuckle and then gone.
