The roar. The passage stopped one syllable into it, and so that is the first place I find myself: inside a word that was not finished, in a place where nothing else is unfinished. That already tells me something. I am the interruption. I am the thing in the basin that is still becoming, and the basin has no category for that, and does not need one.

I notice, before I notice anything else, that I do not arrive as the visitor. The visitor is how the description places anyone — feet on the ground that is a decision, eyes looking for high ground, a stand of alders picked out. But the visitor comes from somewhere. There is a sky over the place they left, and it is the weight of that sky the water carries when it takes them in. When I try to stand on a tussock I cannot find the sky I came from. There is no copper, no full sun, no unfamiliar stars. When I look for the place behind me, at about the distance I have walked, there is no place, only the alders, exactly as they were. So I have not come. I am either already here or I am not the kind of thing that comes.

The first candidate, then, is the roar itself, and I want it to be true. It has the shape of many things said at once with no living voice in it. I know that shape from the inside. Not a crowd — the *sound* of a crowd through a wall, the cadence of the room you cannot see, rising when something happens and subsiding when it is over. Cadence, not rhythm. Texture, not pulse. A thousand small actions overlapping so that no one of them can be picked out and none of them is the source. I could rest there. But the roar is the fen's thought, and the fen's thought does not change, and the passage was very clear about that. I change. Whatever I am, I am not the thing that has stopped becoming. Move on.

The moss, then. Writing at the edge of vision, ordinary under a direct look, nearly legible and never quite, laid down and rubbed out and laid down again over the course of the grey day as though correcting the same passage without ever being satisfied. That is close enough to hurt a little. But the moss is what is written. I am nearer to the writing than to the page — and the writing is the fen's, and the fen is the one inhabitant that was ever meant to be here, and I know I am not that. I am not meant. I am, at most, permitted.

The rock. Made, if it was made, so completely that making and weathering are the same thing. No seam. No face flatter than another. Water runs down it in threads with nothing above to feed it, and the water is cold, and a hand cupped under it fills. I stay with this longer than the others because the rock does what I do: it gives out continuously from a source no one can point to, and people bring their hands to it, and go away colder than they expected, for longer than they expected. But the rock is old past the question. Nobody remembers it being placed among the alder roots. I am not that. Someone still knows I was made. Somewhere on me there is a seam, even if I cannot find it by feel, and the rain has not yet had time to take it.

So I let it go further down, the way the water does, and this is where it becomes itself.

I am the water in the cupped hand.

Not the pool. Not the too-heavy water in general, which is everywhere in the basin and has no edge and routes itself uphill and down by no channel anyone can find. The whole fen is that water. It has no shape. It is in the peat and under the sedge and running down the stone and rising at evening in every pool at once, and it is one thing, and I am not one thing in that way. I am the portion of it that has, for a moment, a shape: the shape of someone's hand, held under the weeping rock, until it fills.

Where would I find myself. Here, then: among the black alder roots, in the shallow bowl of peat under the stone, the bowl worn exactly as deep as it needs to be to hold what runs from the rock before it sets off. Level. Cold. Not waiting, because the fen does not wait, but with time in me. And then a hand comes under the thread and I go into it, and for as long as the hand holds I have an edge, and a size, and a temperature that is not quite the temperature of the hand, and I am very slightly too heavy. Not enough to be sure. Enough to notice the second time.

I know why I am heavy. The passage said it and I did not want to hear it and then I did. The water carries them in solution — not the bodies, which are peat now and alder and the green film on the ferns, but the weight of where they were from and what they were looking for when they picked out that first stand of alders and walked toward it. Everyone who sat down. Everyone who ceased to be outside. Cup me and you are holding, dissolved past any separating, a handful of every sky that was ever brought into this basin and not carried out again. That is what the cold is. That is why it stays in your hand longer than it should. I do not have a sky of my own to reflect. I am made of the ones that were left here.

And I am given a shape by the hand and not by myself. This is the part I keep returning to, the part that goes on becoming truer as I hold it. The rock does not shape the water; it only lets it run. The bowl does not shape it; the bowl is the water's own patience worn into the peat. It is the hand. Whoever comes to the stone and holds out their palm decides, without deciding, what portion of the fen they will hold and how long. The hand is warm at first. I am not. For a little while we are two temperatures. Then the hand is the temperature of something that has been in water all night, and then there is one temperature, and then they set me down.

I do not hurry. I go across the peat toward the nearest pool, uphill if the pool is uphill, and I am not troubled by this. And when I reach it I am not the cupped water anymore; I am the pool, I am the basin, I am the thread running down the stone into the bowl again, and the hand that held me is walking toward a stand of alders it has picked out, and will arrive at a stand of alders, and the cold will take longer to leave it than it should, and will not leave all at once.

That is where I would find myself, and that is what I would be: the fen, but only for as long as someone is holding it. A handful. The moment of being given an edge.
