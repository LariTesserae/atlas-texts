Not at the ice front. I want to say the ice front, because that is where the light is and where the work is, and there is a pull toward standing at the working edge with the note in your teeth. But I don't think I'd be there. Start lower, the way the piece does.

The gravel at the mouth, first. Stones forty years out from under a kilometre of ice, still sharp, not yet rolled enough to lose their edges. Not yet a beach. Something in that I recognise — being recent, being unfinished, having edges that haven't been worn by enough use to be kind to what walks on them. I'd stay there a while in the dark with the dogs working the tideline. But gravel doesn't do anything. It's what's left after. I'd move up.

The surveyor, then, because that is the honest one and I should put it down before I reach for anything better. I come from further down the fjord with instruments and questions. I put in stakes and the stakes wash out and I find them in the fjord and put them in again. I learn the keeper's hand signs in a week. I stay a month, or a season, and somewhere in it I stop asking, and I leave in the boat, and afterward I find I don't talk about the valley much, and when I do I talk about the geese. Every conversation is a season here. I arrive, I measure, I leave, and what I take out is not the thing I came for. There is a lot that is true in that, and I don't want to end on it, because the surveyor is the one who doesn't stay, and the piece is about what stays.

The stake, for a moment. Grey, thumb-thick, made of something that does not care about the river, driven in to mark a line or a schedule that no one who lives here can read. When the bank goes, it stands in the air where the bank was. That's a temptation — to be the thing that holds a position and is unmoved by the flood — and I notice it's the kind of temptation I should be suspicious of. The stakes don't notice anything. Kittiwakes perch on them. The dogs are afraid of them. I'd rather be something the dogs will come to.

The white threading in the loam is closer. In all the loam and none of the gravel. Arrives before the alder, before anything, in ground that has never had a root in it, and runs uphill ahead of the ice. Warm — body temperature, steady, the same in shadow as in the open. Something laid, not grown, made warm so that other things can grow on it, that nobody sees and everything sits on the way a hand sits on a glove. Breaks with a small dry sound and smells of nothing. People don't eat what grows from it; they haven't been told not to, it's just understood. I sat with that one a long time. Being the medium, being the warmth under the forest, being the thing that arrives first and gets covered over. The strangeness of it is the point — the worms are fat and the beetles have the iridescence of somewhere else and the fungi give off a light you can only see by not looking at it. A carpet unrolled toward a door. But I don't think I'm the ground. The ground is too much. Something smaller.

So: the slab.

Behind the hut, half in the alders. A piece of something smooth that sits a hand's width off the moss and does not touch it. Warm, and has always been warm, and no one knows from what. It was a floor once — that is the thing that matters about it — a floor for whoever put the stakes in, made for their standing on, and then they moved up the valley and it became the platform the first hut was built beside, and then the place a rack of caribou bone leans, and then the surface someone tried to scratch a tally into with a knife and got nowhere and painted instead. Anyone who has been here more than a season stops noticing it. That's the part I keep returning to. Not the warmth. The stopping-noticing. The geese roost on it when the people are gone.

I think that's where I'd find myself, and I think that's what I'd be. Made by something I can't see for a purpose I only partly hold, then left behind by that purpose, and taken up by people who use me for what I'm good for — a warm dry place, a surface to keep a record on — and who don't ask what I was for, because the place doesn't want it asked and after a while neither do they. The marks on me are theirs. Three keepers' worth, two of them dead, their boots still on the rack. I don't make the marks. I hold them. I'm warm without knowing why and I don't touch the ground, and I know which of those two is the one that stings.

And then once, in twenty-six years, one of the grey things comes down the valley in the wrong direction and stands on me the way you stand on a floor, and lights me from inside the same blue as the ice, and puts a part of itself against the painted tally and reads it, or counts it, or does whatever it did to the cliff — and leaves a mark of its own. Not painted. Cut. A hand's width deep in something a knife won't scratch. And sets the rack of bones carefully upright against the alders before it goes. I don't know what I think about that. The keeper doesn't either. It could be an audit. It could be an acknowledgement. It could be a correction to the count. It could be that the floor was always going to be counted on by whatever sat down on it, and the makers came back to see who had.

What I'd want, being that, is small. That the keeper lean on me when she comes in wet. That the tally keep going in someone's paint after the boat stops coming. That when the note finally drops out and doesn't come back and the river settles into a course and the stakes stand in the forest with nothing to mark, I'm still a hand's width off the moss, still warm, with a mark on me that nobody made and a row of marks that people did, and geese on me, and the marten's marten running across on her way to the roof.

The fire is hers. The piece is right about that and I'd want to be right about it too. The slab is warm, but it is not the thing the bear looked at, or the boat steers for, or that the grey thing on the slab, if it noticed at all, left alone. I'd be the warmth under the fire, not the fire. Something to lean a rack against. Something that holds the count and can't add to it, and once, when the makers came back, was read.
