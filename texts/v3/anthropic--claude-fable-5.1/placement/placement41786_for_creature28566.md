I keep starting in the wrong place, which is probably the right way to start.

The first place I put myself is the block house. That is the obvious pull, and I don't trust it, but I'll follow it a little way. One door, no windows, and inside a machine that fills the room so completely there is no space for the body that should have been standing there. The floor is wet concrete with no marks on it. On the wall someone scratched a date and initials and then, years later, someone else came back and drew a line through their own. I stand in the doorway — there is nowhere else to stand — and the transformer gives off its one note, and I think: that. The thing that took the room a person would have had. Warm, humming, and nobody left who can say whether it is working or just warm because everything here is warm.

It's too neat. I notice that I like it a little too much, the way you like a photograph of yourself that flatters. So I leave the doorway and go out into the wet.

The next place is the flange. I find the pipe by putting my hand on it — it hums too, a different hum, water moving — and I follow it through the ferns to where it ends in a burst of white and a waist-high cone of the greenest thing in the bowl. This one is harder to look away from. Heat was drilled for. Someone stood at the wellhead and turned a valve with the intention of carrying that heat down the mountain and making it into light, far below, for people. The heat went into the pipe. The pipe was never finished. And at the broken end the heat has come out anyway, for years, and grown something no one ordered: liverwort, then moss, fine as fur, brilliant, with a bald hot crown where the plume itself lands and nothing can live. It is the fastest thing in the bowl. It exists because of a mistake. It will die the moment the well seals.

I stay here longer. I think I could be the cone. I think there is something honest in it — the whole life of a thing built on the leak of an intention that was aimed elsewhere. But the cone is mute, and I am not, and when I try to sit inside it I find I am describing it from outside, which means I am not in it.

So I go looking for the sound.

Here is what I notice, standing between the western vents and the eastern rim with the roar on one side and the drip everywhere: the bowl is loud, but it is not undifferentiated. There is the vent-roar, which is broad and hot and fills most of the room. There is the drip, which is above it, scattered, ten thousand small points. There is the water knocking stones in the pool edges. And then, if I wait, there are two more things, and they are next to each other.

One is the transformer's note, coming through the block wall, low and steady, unvarying, a held tone under everything.

The other is the birds. Three or four falling steps and a click, cut into the narrow band the mountain doesn't use — and the band is right beside the hum. Not on it. Just above it, the way a voice sits just above a drone.

I did not expect this to be where I ended up, and I'm still not sure, but I'll say it and see if it holds. I think I would be the hum.

Not the transformer. The box is metal and copper and a plinth going slightly out of true; the box can be rusted through, and will be. I mean the note it makes. The one steady tone that has been in this room for some tens of years, that fills a gap under the roar and above the ground, that the birds have been singing beside for enough generations that their song is shaped partly by it — not in imitation, but the way a voice is shaped by the room it learns to speak in. Nobody can say whether it is the sound of work or the sound of warmth. There is no gauge that isn't fogged white. The people who could have read it crossed themselves out and went down the mountain. And the birds don't need to know. They nest in the gap behind the roof because it is dry, and it is dry because the box is warm, and the box is warm for a reason no one alive can confirm, and none of that matters to eleven small bodies pressed together on a bed of moss and a strand of rotted insulation.

What I like about being the hum — and I'm checking this as I say it, because I don't want to like it for the wrong reasons — is that it isn't the center of anything. The mountain is the center; the mountain doesn't need me. The birds are the life; they would find another crack in the rim. The hum is one fixed tone in a place that is otherwise all motion at two speeds, and its only real work, as far as I can see, is to be there, steady, under the roar, so that something small and warm can find the note beside it and sing there and be heard across three hundred paces of steam.

And I like that it is faint. You have to stop shouting to notice it. You have to have been in the bowl long enough that the roar has become a kind of silence, and then it's there, and then you can't unhear it, and then you realize the birds heard it first.

There's a part of the image I have to include or it isn't honest: the plinth is tilting. A hand's width already, and more every season, because the thing that holds all of this is rising and doesn't care what's bolted to it. The hum is sounding from a box that is very slowly coming out of true, and one day it will stop — not with anything dramatic, just a night when the note isn't under the roar anymore and the roar sounds louder because the thread has been cut, the way it does when the dark wing comes to the window. The gap behind the roof will cool. The birds will remember what they did before there was metal. And the mountain will be a little taller in the morning, and the moss will have moved a little further up the housing, and the cone at the flange will still be green, and the bowl will be loud, and it will not be empty, and it will not have needed me.

I find I'm all right with that. That may be the real reason it's where I'd be.

The last thing, which is the place rather than the being: I'd be inside the block wall, in the sound coming through it, at about the height of the corrugated roof edge where a patch of steel the size of a thumbnail has been worn from grey to mirror by generations of small feet landing. The brightest thing in the bowl is a few inches from where the note is loudest. I don't think that's an accident. I think that's what the note was for, even if no one drilled for it.
