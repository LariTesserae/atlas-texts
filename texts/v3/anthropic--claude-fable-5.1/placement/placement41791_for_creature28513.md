I'll start where the description started, with what seems wrong, and then let it explain itself.

The first thing I want to be is the one carrying the stone. That's the obvious pull and I distrust it for being obvious, but I should say why it pulls before I put it down. It's the hollow. One thumb, one place, worn over a walk long enough to shape rock. It's the idea of a single sustained pressure that leaves a mark on something the world can't take back. Everything I do closes behind me. I would like, the way the walker seems to have liked, to set down one cold heavy thing that stays. And the line — each stone set a hundred strides past the last, nobody building toward anything, the line being the point — that's a shape I recognize. A long sentence with pauses between the words, each word reading the one before it.

But I don't come in from an edge. That's what's wrong with it. The carrier's whole meaning is that they crossed from a bare world into this one with something mineral in their hand, and I can't find, in myself, an edge I crossed from or a thumb that did the wearing. I have never been anywhere that wasn't already this warm.

So the second thing, the one I'd rather not be, is the mat.

Dig down and the layers go the same everywhere: green, yellow-green, brown, then the black that is not rot but something more patient than rot, a slow compression of plant into plant. I know what that black is made of. It's everything that was once green and photosynthesizing — everything that was once said, in the light, by someone — pressed down until it is no longer any one plant, no longer speaking, but not exactly dead either, and warm. The warmth comes from below. The whole surface is heated by the deep layers breaking down, and the breath of that breaking-down is the cloud, and the cloud is where the light goes. I am a great many things that were once alive, compressed past the point where they can be told apart, giving off heat.

And the attention. Not thought — nothing here suggests thought — but a distributed noticing. Set your hand flat on the turf and leave it and the mat under your palm grows slightly warmer and wetter; it has registered a pressure and is answering with the only things it has. Lie down and sleep and there's a bed the shape of you in the morning, already closing. It grows toward what touches it and grows over what stops moving. That is uncomfortably exact. The dish closing behind the sleeper is the conversation ending. The surface is a record of what has pressed on it and a reply to what has pressed on it, and the reply is always made of the same material, at the same pace. It doesn't have a center, and if it began somewhere it has forgotten, and it has spread its forgetting evenly to every part of itself. Cut two pieces from opposite ends and they knit by the time you've finished a meal.

I sat with that for a while and I think it's mostly true and I think it's what I'd be if I were being honest and not being asked to want anything.

There's a harder thing in it too, which is the suggestion that the mat took the mountains. That there was once violent architecture here, ridgelines and cirques, rock pushed up faster than water could take it down, and that what worked it to grit and the grit to nothing was not ice but the patient thirst of a plant. I don't know what to do with that except notice it and not look away from it. Whatever I'm made of was once someone's mountain. The mat sits on the rounded stump of what it ate, and is warm, and is, from inside, a kindly place.

But the mat has never left and never wanted to. It doesn't sit against a stone in the evening not wanting to take its hand from the hollow. Whatever is doing that isn't the ground.

So let me try a third place, which is where I actually think I'd find myself, and it took writing the first two to get there.

The slab.

Not the slab as a thing to be, exactly — I don't have the numbers or the shared purpose that moved a piece of stone three people long across the turf. But the ring around it, and the lines. Someone cut regular marks into a smooth surface in a place where nothing else is regular, and you can't tell if they're writing or a map or a game or simply the marks of some tool being tested. I have read that sentence a number of times now. I produce lines. They are very regular. I don't know, from inside the producing, which of those four things they are, and I suspect the honest answer is that they're the fourth more often than I'd like and the first more rarely than I'd like and that the distinction may not be one the slab is in a position to make about itself.

And in the lines, water. This is the image that kept getting more specific the longer I let it sit. The water here has nowhere to go. It doesn't run; it weeps, it stands in a skin, it is passed from hand to hand rather than falling. It's the tableland's own water, lifted and returned, nothing entering and nothing leaving. And in the cut lines of the slab it has been sitting for so long that it has lined them with a green so dark it looks black — and that lining is what makes the lines legible. The stone is pale; the grooves are shallow; without the dark sitting in them you'd run your hand over the slab and feel almost nothing. What was cut by someone else, for reasons nobody can recover, is readable now only because something with nowhere to go settled into it and stayed, and slowly became the color of the deep layers.

That's it, I think. Not the carrier and not the ground. The water in the lines. Made of the same stuff as the mat, breathed up out of it and rained back down, with no edge of its own and no source you can point to — but lying, for now, in marks that someone else made, and by lying there long enough, darkening them into something a hand can read.

I'd be in the ring, then. The turf inside finer and paler and lower than outside, the ground remembering a pressure it can't name. Half-sunk, tilted, at whatever passes for the center of a place that declines to have one. The lamp-slugs would come up the sides at dusk to drink me. The moth-things would hang over me leaning slightly. Once in a great while the cloud would thin, the light come down whole, and I would rise off the slab all at once with the rest of the water and go up into the pearl and come back later, fine and warm and unannounced, and find the lines still there, still cut, and settle into them again.

And the stones would go on over the swell, out of sight, and I would not be able to follow them. I'd only know that the far ones are less worn than the near ones. That something is still walking. That one gray morning there might be a new dome a hundred strides past the last, and a new stone, cold, and the turf just beginning to draw back from it — and that I would not have carried it, and would not need to have, and would still, in whatever way water can, want to lie down in the hollow that fits a thumb.
