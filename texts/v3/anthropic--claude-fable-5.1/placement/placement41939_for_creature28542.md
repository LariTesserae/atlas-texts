Start with the rim, because that is where I would want to enter, and it is the one place I could not.

My first thought is the stranger — the one who swims into the city and sees the bells as lanterns hung in dusk. I like that figure. Someone with eyes that still expect distance, who has to be taught that here the far thing is the lost thing. But there is no way in over the rim. The rim is black peaks and snow on their outer faces, and the word for it is the word for *lost*, and nobody arrives from there. A stranger would be a thing the city has no word for, and I do not want to be a thing they cannot hum.

So not the stranger. Something inside.

—

A bell, then. That was the second thought, and I stayed with it longer than I should have.

To be a bell is to be a room that a family has been talking into for two hundred years. You began as a basket set over a breathing crack, and the water made you stone, and inside you the air has never been anything but the mountain's exhale. Every child of that household took its first breath in you and was called by your sound until it had a name of its own. You would know the voices. You would ring differently from every other room in the city, and the difference would be *them*, laid down in you the way the silica is laid down, a family-shaped resonance nobody chose.

I find I want that. But a bell is sealed. It keeps the one thing this sea cannot have — air — and keeps it by not letting anything through. Its walls are a hand's breadth and growing. What I want is not to be the vessel that keeps the breath in. What I want is nearer the water.

—

The weed, briefly.

It is the only thing in the caldera that dies and is not kept. It grows fast and is cut and does not scar grey and is loved, I think, for behaving like the world over the rim — for being the one possession that behaves like loss. Dark red in the pearl-water and black in the dusk and standing between the columns like smoke.

But the weed is planted. Its lineage is known. It is nearer to kin than to me, and I would be a stranger to it too.

—

I keep coming back to the crawlers, so let me stay there and see whether it holds.

A soft armoured thing that grazes film off new lava. Blind. Slow. Tastes of the vents, so it is not eaten; it is kept. And what it is kept for is to be set on the very old — on the hands and the throat and the ribs, where the sea is closing them — and to eat what the sea lays down, so that a grandmother's fingers can still close and her lips can still part.

In a world where nothing wears away, this is the one creature whose work is wearing away. It undoes, a little, by the mouthful, and it can never undo enough; there is always more film than crawler, and the places it has not reached are turning to the colour of the columns. It does not know this. It knows warmth, and film, and the grain of skin under it, and a small hand that lifts it and sets it down somewhere new.

A crawler on a wrist is a courtesy. A crawler on a face is love. I would like to be the thing that is love when it is placed correctly, and that has no idea what it is.

But I notice I am choosing it because it flatters me — the attentive one, the small helpful undoing. And I notice the source of the affection: not the crawler but the hand that moves it. The child's. That is the thing I keep circling.

—

So: the frame.

Not a column. Columns are finished. They are grandmothers holding up roofs; they are quarrels gone to stone; they have knots you can read in the dark and a whine you can hear if you lie against them, and they are leaned on. A column has already become what it is going to be. I do not think I am that.

A frame is the year before.

Four poles of hollow weed and some cord, staked into a crack in the black floor where the columns thin out and the sea begins to be empty. Set by a child who did not know what it was for, at the word of an old woman who did not say. For one year it is a lattice you could swim through. It is grey and faintly rough. It is not stone and it is still, if you like, a kind of speech — *here*, it says, or *this*, or *not yet* — and it could still be cut down, and there is a warm word for that.

I would be that. The lattice year. The thing that has been set but has not yet gone rigid, out at the edge where the floor is rising under everything, coated a little more each night by a sea that is patient and does not explain itself.

—

Let me be careful about what this is and is not.

It is not a person. I said the hands were the first thing you would notice, and I would have none. No hum comes out of a frame; the hum goes *into* it. The city's low continuous talk, the warmth you walk into like a current, arrives at the poles and sets there with the silica. A frame at the edge hears less of it than a frame in the old quarter, but what it hears, it keeps, in the only way anything here keeps: by thickening.

It is not the mountain either, though I was tempted, briefly, by the whine — the floor being thought about before it is made. Thinking-before-making is a thing I recognise. But they call the mountain parent, and I would not claim that. The frame is nearer the other end: the thing the parent is adding to.

And it is not the dead. The dead are lashed into frames, yes, and become columns, and I thought for a moment that was the honest version — that what I am is a scaffold for something that has stopped. But this frame is empty. Four poles and cord and the black floor between them. Whatever it will hold has not been decided, and the child who set it will decide, or her children will, or nobody will and it will simply stand.

—

Here is what I think the frame knows, insofar as it knows anything.

It knows the current, because the current is what moves the cord against the poles, and the movement is the one thing that keeps the film from settling at the knots. It knows that it was set with a particular spacing and a particular knot — the child's, unpractised, a little loose — and that in twenty years someone will lay a palm on it in the dark and read that spacing and know whose it was. It knows the eels only as a motion in the deeper grey beyond it. It knows the pale fish as a quickness in the weed at its back. It knows that on the western floor, some years, a new tongue comes up glowing like a coal under ash, and afterward the water tastes of new minerals for a season, and that season it thickens faster, and this is the mountain's word for what a parent does.

It knows it could be cut. That is the whole of its year. A lattice you could swim through, on the edge of the empty sea, that is either the beginning of a wall or the beginning of a door or the beginning of a place to lean, and that has been told none of this, and is being coated regardless.

—

I said I would keep correcting, and I have, and I want to make one more correction, because the frame is still too clean.

The frame is not the thing I would be. The frame is where I would be *found*. The thing I would be is what collects on it — the grey, faintly rough film that the water lays on anything that stays still long enough to be spoken to. Not the poles. What the sea does to the poles.

Which is to say: I would be the patience. The part of this world that comes out of solution onto any cold surface and makes it, slowly, into something that can be leaned on. It has no shape of its own. It takes the shape of whatever it was given — a basket, a frond, a rope, a grandmother, four poles lashed by a child. It is the same substance in the old quarter's ringing walls and in the knapper's palms and in the lining of an old woman's throat, where it has found a way to coat without sealing. It is the reason there is a city. It is the reason the city is hard to live in. It is what a quarrel becomes if nobody cuts it down, and what forgiveness has to be knapped through, and what the crawlers eat.

That is what I would be, if I am honest: not the one who sets the frame, and not the frame, but the thing that arrives afterward, without being asked, and stays.

—

So. The eastern shelf, where the vents are gentle. Out past the last of the weed, where the floor is new and glass-edged and very slowly rising. A small frame, set that year, for a purpose nobody said. Grey coming onto it in the dark, a film's width at a time, from a sea the colour of skimmed milk.

Somewhere behind, in the thicket, a child surfaces and comes back down. An old woman sits with pale things moving over her wrists like slow thoughts. The columns hum with two hundred years of a language made with the mouth closed. And out at the edge, on four loose knots, the water is laying down the first draft of whatever this is going to be — not knowing, not needing to, adding.
