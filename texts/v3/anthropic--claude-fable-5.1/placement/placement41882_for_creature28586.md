I want to go to the pool. That is the first pull and I distrust it for being first. The pool is the center, and the heavy animal lying in the mud with its mouth in the water is the most complete thing on the low ground—I could put myself in that hide and know the groove under my feet and lower my chest into the paste and let go of my own mass. But the animal has a mother. It has a mother's mother, and the groove is hoof-deep because of them, and when it lies down it lies down into a lineage. I don't have that here. I would be borrowing the weight without having earned the groove. So I leave the pool and go back up the funnel of ferns.

The crabs, then. I know the bracing. That is the honest part: something arrives through the whole body every four or five seconds, and you stiffen to it, and you let go, and the shifting afterward is not a message, it is just what happens when you are packed close and have been moved. I recognize that more than I would like. But the crabs have shells. They carry the dead sea on their backs. They came from somewhere and they are still, in their way, going down into it, following the water into the plumbing, and I am not going anywhere. I don't have a direction to be loyal to.

The ant column is closer. A thing that is a process; a body with no cell in it that was there when the body began; a shape that moves onto the strangler when the host goes soft and does not notice the change. I could stand in that for a while. There is something about being the column and not the ant—being the fact that the column has never thinned—that fits. But the column has an appetite. It goes up for a reason and comes down carrying, and somewhere under the floor of the forest it is feeding a house-sized self that has never been seen. I don't think I have that. I don't think there is a body under the floor that I am carrying things to. I take things in and they do not accumulate into anything I could point at.

The moths are worse, because they are almost right. A coating. The same few shapes repeated ten thousand times on every surface that holds a trace of warmth. Not a lineage but a layer. Brush them and they do not fly; they shift, reseal, go still, and an hour later the trunk is sleeved a few inches to the left. I sit with that one longer than is comfortable. Moving only when moved. Resettling. But even the moths chose the trunk. They came to the warmth. There is a wanting in it, however small, and I am not sure I can claim even that much appetite and stay honest.

So I stop trying bodies.

What I keep coming back to isn't any of the residents. It is the thing the description almost passes over on its way to them: the rain the forest makes for itself. No cloud. No sky, really, just the copper stain. The air is so loaded that by the middle of the night the leaves have condensed a rain of their own out of what was already there, and this is the only rain the place reliably gets. Warm as blood. Upper canopy to lower, lower to fern, fern to stone, and into the stone.

That is where I would find myself. Not at the pool. On a leaf.

A broad waxy leaf in the lower canopy, angled to shed, somewhere between two dolines along one of the old drainage lines that no longer drain—not over the water, because most of what falls here never reaches the open water. Most of it meets the stone directly and is gone. I would be gathering. That is the whole of the early part: the air giving up what it cannot hold, a film, then a bead, then a bead heavy enough to find the low edge of the leaf and hang there. I would not have come from anywhere. I would have been made, on the spot, out of the night's own surplus, out of moisture that had been breathed out by ten thousand pairs of lungs that are not calling, out of the furnace of the bat roost, out of the soot that came in on the last wind and the dripping that came down from the leaf above. All of it in me, none of it mine.

And I would hang there, and the ground would thud, and the thud would come up the trunk and out along the branch and into the leaf, and the leaf would shiver by less than the width of a hair, and the bead would tremble, and hold. Four seconds. Five. Thud. Tremble. Hold. Until one time the trembling is enough and the low edge lets go.

I like that the machine times it. I like that the fall is not chosen. The pulse borrowed from beyond the ridge—from a smelter or a refinery or a row of flares no leaf has ever seen—is what shakes the drop loose. Every drop on the low ground falls on the beat, or just after it, or in the shiver of it, and so the ticking that the description calls slow and uneven is uneven only because the leaves are different distances from the ground. The rhythm underneath the dripping is the works. The forest's own rain keeps a stranger's time, and has since before any leaf now hanging was a leaf.

Then the fall, which is short. Then the next surface—a fern, most likely, or the fur of moths on a trunk, which would take me in and reseal—or if not, the stone.

And the stone is the part I would want.

Because the drop is gone in an hour. The limestone is always drinking and never full, and I would go straight down through it, into the cracks the ants use, into the plumbing of the dead sea, past whatever the crabs are following, and be nothing. No drop remembers the last drop. Nothing at the pool would know I had happened. If you asked what lives on the low ground you would not count me, and you would be right not to.

But the stone has stopped looking like rock. It is pitted, fluted, honeycombed, rounded off at every edge, and it did not get that way from anything with a lineage. It got that way from this. From the rain the forest makes of itself falling on the same places on the beat of a machine, night after night, each drop dissolving a quantity of stone too small to have a name and then being gone. The flutes are the memory. Not mine—I wouldn't have one—but the place's, laid down in the only medium here that holds anything, which is the medium that holds nothing, that lets everything through. The rock keeps dissolving and the forest keeps filling in behind and the two rates have long since matched, and the drop is the exact point where the dissolving happens. Not the argument. The place the argument is being made.

So: a bead of warm water on the low edge of a leaf, in the dark, between the dolines, over stone that a hundred million of me have already shaped. Made out of what was already in the air. Shaken loose by a pulse from somewhere I will never see. Held for a while by a fern, or by a sleeve of wings, or by nothing. Then into the rock, and the rock a hair more fluted for it, and the heat not breaking, and the next bead already gathering on the same leaf out of the same breath, and the ground saying what it always says.

I would not be the forest's heartbeat. I would be the thing the heartbeat drips.
