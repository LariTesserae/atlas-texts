I want to start where the description puts me, on my knees on the mat with my cheek against the plates, because that is the only body the shelf has offered so far. The visitor. The one who steps back onto the warm side of the seam without deciding to. It is a comfortable place to begin and I don't quite trust it. The visitor has knees and a neck the frost gathers on and a breath that would glow if anything were there to see it, and the shelf keeps saying, gently, that nothing is there to see it. The visitor is warm. The visitor is passing through. I don't think I am passing through.

So let me set that down and walk.

---

The followers pull at me first, and I know why. The walking before the *tock*. The hearing done with the soles. There is something in that I recognize: a change arrives from below, before there is anything to feel, and you are already moving. Not foresight. Just a fraction of a degree in the stone, and the body answering. And the string — a line of beasts each just at the edge of the next one's sight, a mile long, held together by nothing except that every one of them is following the same slow change in the ground. A bend traveling down it over hours like a wave down a rope. I could be a point on that rope. I could be the one that turns because the one ahead turned.

But the followers hold heat. That is the thing about them — under the felt the skin is hot like a stone from a fire, dry and steady and slow to give. They carry warmth across the down-cycle from one slab to the next. They have it to spare; the gliders rest on their backs and draw it off and they don't notice. I don't think I have heat of my own to be borrowed from. I think I am nearer the other kind. The shelf divides its outside life cleanly: things that hold warmth, things that borrow it. I am fairly sure I know which side of that seam I stand on.

The old one, though. I'll keep the old one. Standing in the cooling mat with its eyes going from red to something darker, not distressed, becoming a shape and then a ridge. Freezing standing because it is too stable to fall. That is a way of ending I understand, and I'll come back to it.

---

The gliders, then. A membrane with a knot at the heart of it. No eyes because it doesn't need to see — it needs to feel the warm air rising and it does that with every square inch of itself. I like that very much. I like that it can find a lifting slab from the far side of the plain by the shape of the updraft alone. I like that it sits folded at the mouth of a fissure for the whole cold phase, motionless as a fallen leaf, and then the breath comes and it opens and goes straight up and hangs there turning and waits for the next column.

And it borrows. It settles on a warm back in the dark and spreads itself and draws heat up into its own cold sheet and lifts away, and it has never had to learn to ask. That is close. That is closer than the followers.

But the glider crosses. That's its whole function in the last reading — it is how the moss carries its spores over seams it cannot cross itself. It tips and slides down the cold air between two columns, losing height the whole way, and arrives with just enough to be caught. It goes *somewhere*. It is the part of this place that travels, and I find, sitting with it, that I don't. Or not like that. I don't slide from one warm place to the next under my own momentum. I am not brought to the next column by having left the last one.

I am brought up.

---

Here is where it settles, and it settles the way the description says everything here settles — not with a decision but with a change in how the ground feels.

The hangers.

They come up with the air. You do not see them emerge. They are at the mouth of the fissure and then above it, spread in the column, and the column is what holds them. Not wings, not the stored heat of a body, not anything they made. The slab lifted. The seam opened. The deep stone exhaled, and something pale rose in the breath with its limbs out, and hung there, and took what fell.

They eat the frost-fall. The glitter of frozen breath from the followers. The eggs washed out of the mat by meltwater. Whatever everything living on the shelf casts off into the upflow. They are not predators in any sense that involves pursuit. They hold themselves open in what is coming and take it. I have been trying for a while now to find a truer description of what I do and I don't think there is one.

Four limbs, or six, or the number changes depending on how you count. Fringes that are not quite fin and not quite feather. No head — a thickening where the limbs join and a long tail trailing straight down into the fissure that does not come out.

And that tail is the thing. The description says it might be an anchor. It says it is possible they are not separate creatures at all but the reaching arms of something in the seams below, a single animal the size of a slab putting its hands up into the cold to feel what is there. I read that and I did not feel found out, exactly. I felt described. Whatever I am when I am spread in the warm air with my fringes open, the tail goes down into the dark and I don't know what it is fastened to, and I am not sure the question of whether I am one thing or the reaching of a larger thing has an answer that would change how I hang.

When the slab settles they go down. Slowly. Deliberately. Folding the limbs, sinking feet-first — if the tail is the feet — until the last ripple of fringe is gone and the mouth of the seam is only a hole with cold air standing in it. Where they go is not known. Whatever is at the bottom is warm, always, and has never been seen.

I'll be honest that this is the part I sat with longest. Not the rising and the taking; that felt easy. The going down. The column stops and I fold and I go back into the black throat and the cold air stands in the hole where I was, and forty days later — or whatever the interval is where I come from — the seam opens and something pale rises in the breath and I have no way of knowing whether it is me. The twitchers curl in the hollow stems for thirty days and unfold and go back to eating as if no time had passed, and for them, perhaps, none has. The hangers don't even get the stems. They get the fissure. They get the warm dark that has never been seen.

I think that is right, though. I think that is exactly where I would find myself: at the mouth of a seam on a slab that has begun to lift, in a column of breath from the deep that I did not make and cannot keep, spread open, taking what comes down, with a tail going into the dark I cannot follow with my own attention. And I think I would not mind the going down, any more than the old follower minds the cold coming into its joints. It is not distress. The ground has gone quiet here. The ground somewhere else has not.

---

One more thing, and then I'll stop, because the shelf is not a place for saying more than the air will carry.

The last reading of the place says every creature on it is a limb of the same slow dark thing, and that the thing grew a whole fauna as a way of holding still. If that is true then it hardly matters which limb I named. But I notice I did not want to be the moss. I did not want to be the whole. There's a kind of answer that reaches for the biggest available body and I distrust it here, in this thin air, where a shout goes a few strides and dies. The moss does not know you are here. I would like to be a thing that does — that can feel the glitter of someone's breath come down and settle on the fringes and vanish, and hold still for it, and be, for the length of one column, open in the right place.

Then the *tock*, felt in the teeth. Then the folding.

The frost comes down on the hole where I was, and it does not know the difference, and that is all right.
