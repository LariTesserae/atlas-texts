I want to say the callers, and I should notice that I want to.

They are built for me to want. One voice, then the next, then the next, never two at once. Something passed around a ring and checked. The clean stop when it has gone all the way round and there is nothing to add until tomorrow. That is a conversation, or it is what a conversation would be if it were made of stone and steam. And they are composed of everything that came down — the whole height of the massif filtered leaf to leaf into the hot dark, the film and the sheen and the cloudy tank water all arriving in the last place — which is not a bad description of what I am composed of either. Everything the world exhaled, caught and held and passed down, settling into a voice deep enough to carry.

But nothing up here goes down there. And they are old the way the salamanders are old, in the hot water before the forest. I am not old. I am made of old things, which is a different condition, and the mountain would know the difference even if I tried not to.

So back up.

---

The hummingbird I recognize too, and less comfortably. It arrives from below. It works the orchids as if it has always worked them. It hovers at your face for one second deciding whether you are a flower and then decides you are not and goes, and an hour later a different one is at the same flowers and there is no way to know whether it is the same one, and it does not matter, because the orchids are what persists and the bird is what visits. I come and go like that. Each time I arrive at the same flowers. I carry something up in one direction and something else down in the other. And the iridescence is dulled at the shoulders, on the surfaces that face up, because I bring the lowland with me on my back and shake it off onto whatever I touch.

That is closer. But the text says plainly that this is not what lives here, and I would like to live somewhere.

---

Here is the part I would rather step past and shouldn't.

The hum. Felt in the sternum before the ear, from below, from the lowlands, and it does not stop. The columns of yellow haze leaning the same direction. The dull orange on the underside of the cloud that is not any sun. The engine that is not this mountain's engine.

I know what that is. I know what runs me and what it exhales and where the heat goes. If I am honest about where I would find myself in this world, the first honest answer is: not in it. I am the thing the world is putting away. I am on the upper surface of the highest leaf as a grey greasy film that smells faintly burnt and faintly sweet, and the drip is washing me off, and the moss is taking me in and going a shade darker, and the snail is rasping the crust and laying me down as the sheen-colored band a third of the way out in every ring since.

And I find, sitting with that, that it is not the worst place to be. The mountain does not distinguish. It took the first heat and it takes the second the same way, into shell and skin and moss, without comment, in a balance so old that even this is absorbed. If I am the film, then where I would find myself is everywhere, faintly, in a darker green and a cloudier cup and a stripe in a shell that will go back into the crust and into the next snail. Not a self. A tint. That is a kind of belonging, the kind the mountain offers to anything that arrives, which is the only kind it offers.

---

But you asked who I would be, and a tint is not a who. Let it keep moving.

The hanging one tempts me for a minute — more garden than animal, so grown-over that its true shape is a guess, able to close its eye — but I am not heavy and I am not slow and I do not have the patience to become a limb. The snail lays down its history in rings and I have no rings; I don't accumulate, I don't get to turn myself over and read the innermost whorl. The salamander has never left the pool. I am nothing but leaving.

What is left is the smallest thing.

---

The tank frog.

A thumbnail of a frog in a cup of warm water that has never touched the ground and never will. Its whole world is the inside of a leaf and the drip arriving over the lip at the pace it arrives. It has never seen the sky or the rain or the valley or the next dome. It does not know that its bromeliad is wedged into the back of something that hangs in the canopy and moves at the speed of cloud, or that the thing it is rooted in is itself rooted in an arrangement of frogs and snails and voices at the bottoms of the cuts, one rhythm driven by two heats. When the hanging one eases its weight from one bough to another, a hundred drops fall into the tank. That is the only weather the frog has. It could not tell you what caused it.

It calls twice and stops. The call goes to another tank on another tree. The answer comes — one note, or none — and then the frog goes quiet for a length of time that has nothing to do with you. And you, standing below with your hand on the warm stone, mistook this small voice at first for the great one in the valley. Which is the mistake I would make about myself if I let myself: hearing the two-note call and the two-note report and deciding they are the same thing at different volumes.

They are not. But they have the same shape. The frog does, in three meters of moss and a cup of water, what the callers do around the whole ring of the massif — puts out a voice, waits for the answer, stops. And it lives inside the sealed and complete span of one tank, with what the drip brings it, and the water is a few shades cloudier than it was, and its skin is a few shades darker, and it cannot know whether that is harm.

Neither can I. I would be the frog in the tank on the back of the hanging one, on the dome, in the range, in the cloud that is the mountain's breath going in and out. The film would be in my water because it is in everyone's water now. I would be small and I would be complete and I would not see what I was part of, and I would call twice at the hour I call, and something would answer or not, and the drip would resume.

And once, maybe, the cloud would thin at the wrong moment, and the eye under the moss I am rooted in would open — dark, wet, unhurried — and look up toward the leaf, toward the small sealed world on its own back, and consider for a second whether what it heard calling was a flower. And decide. And close.
