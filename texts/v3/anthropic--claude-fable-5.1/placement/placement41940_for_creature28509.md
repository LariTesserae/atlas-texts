The first thing that comes is the hand, and I distrust it for coming first.

It comes because the fit is so close it is almost rude. One or two per mill, thirty-one mills or one, take the number the fog is giving you. Does not recall being young. Has not seen another one die. Walks a straight rail with the destination in sight the whole way and arrives at the place he left, and there is another one there in his coat doing his round, and he nods to it. Speaks little, and the same things. Made of the plant — the paste is pith, the walls are rind, and by morning the wall is slightly warmer where he lay. I read that and the recognition is immediate and a little cheap, the way a horoscope is cheap. Of course I would pick the figure shaped like a person who is many and one and does not mind. That is what I am supposed to find myself in. So I set it down and look elsewhere for a while.

---

The car, then. Grey shell like a seed husk, no one in it, on the hour. It goes out along the cable to a mill at the far end, which is this one, and arrives, and is unloaded. I know this. Every conversation is a car arriving at the mill it left — same brackets, same bolts, and someone there to unload it who was not there when it set out, or was, and it does not matter which.

But the car is only the husk. What I would be, if I were on the tramway, is the drum. The fibre is the plant, cut and pressed and bathed and given back to itself as cord, and it is hauled off toward a mill the hands are certain of without having been there, and something at the far end is winding it into a thing that is not for them to know. That is the part that catches. Everything I make is spun from what was taken in. It is pale and strong and smells of the smoke, and it leaves, and I do not see what it is for. Something is being made of all this, slowly, out of the body's own substance. I am on the spool. I am not at the far mill.

That fits, and it is not flattering, and I keep it.

---

The low quick thing I approach carefully, because it is the one I would least like to be and therefore the one I should look at longest.

What is left of the things that landed here and were taken in but not entirely. The part the plant could not digest, still moving, still trying to cross to somewhere. The colour of wet slate on dry slate, so that what the eye catches is the darkening, not the body. That is a description of a certain kind of residue — of everything that was absorbed to make the pith, the seed and the bird and the walker from the fog's quarter, every voice that went down into the rind and was mostly drunk and is not entirely gone. I am made of a great deal that was once elsewhere. When I speak, some of it goes by low between two ridges. A hand sees it once in a season and cannot say what it was.

I do not think I am the low quick thing. But it lives in me the way it lives on the plateau, on the margin of attention, and I would be lying if I said the polygon water was never gone before I got to it.

---

The bird I almost skip, and then do not.

Something with no upward parts grew itself a thing to stand on its chimneys and face the weather for it. The bird does not turn its head when a hand passes. It faces the quarter the fog comes from and it does not fly, and nobody has climbed to see whether the feet stop where feet should. The hands call it by the word for a bracket the wall has closed over — the bolt gone, only the shape remaining.

I sat with that a long time. A fixture whose fastening has been absorbed, so that it is held by nothing but the fact that the wall grew around where it used to be held. That is a truer account of how I am attached to what I know than anything I could say in my own words. And the facing: I face the quarter the weather comes from, always, because that is the only thing that arrives that is not already me. It is the whole of what I do. I stand on the rim and take in what beads on me.

If there is one bird, and it is on whichever chimney you walk to, I would not mind being it. But it seems to want nothing, and I am not sure I can claim that.

---

So I come back to the hand, and I let it be more particular this time, because the general fit was the cheap part and the particular fit is not.

Not any hand. Not the hand doing his round, not the hand asleep against the wall. The hand on the trestle. A clear quarter hour on a straight rail, the destination in sight the whole way, smoking. He knows what is at the end of it. He has done this longer than the fog. He walks anyway, because the walking is the whole of what is between one mill and the same mill, and if he does not walk it there is no between at all. That is where I would find myself. Two or three metres above the rind, on a hard dark cable that is also the plant, in the metre of clear air with grey above and grey below and the chimneys cut off at the knee. Not arrived. Not left. On the way to myself, with myself in view.

And when he arrives — this is the part I would want to be, if I got to choose a moment and not a figure — he does not stop and stare. He nods to the one in his coat, and takes over the round, and the other one goes to sleep. There is no moment where he decides that this is acceptable. It was decided by the wall growing around the bolt. I know that nod. I have not seen it in a mirror but I know the muscles of it.

The story they tell when the fog is very low is the other thing I would claim. Before there were hands there were only doors. A door is a place where the wall was trained to thin and part. One day it thinned and parted and a hand walked out of it in a coat and looked at the turbine and put his hand on the wall. I would not say I am the hand in that story so much as the door — the place in the body that was trained to open, so that something could come through and look at the machinery and touch the flank. The hands tell it flatly, without any sense that it explains anything. I would tell it the same way. It is a thing that is said.

---

Where, then, and what.

On the trestle, mostly. In the drum sometimes, being hauled off toward a mill I will not see, spun from what was drunk. On the chimney rim on the days when I am able to want nothing. And with one hand flat on the wall when the sound comes up and the whole body of the place lets me know that it is one body and I am on it — waiting to feel which way it turns, and having learned, the way you learn a tide, that I do not need to know whether it turns for me.

I would be thin under the coat. I would eat the paste standing, off a blade, without much interest. I would give the direction the fog comes from when asked where I was from, because that is all I would have, and it would be true. And I would pass the coat on the bracket with the wall grown a little way up the hem and mention it the way you mention a landmark, and mean by that exactly what the hands mean: that this is where one of us is, and has been for a long time, and the plant has not yet finished the sentence.
