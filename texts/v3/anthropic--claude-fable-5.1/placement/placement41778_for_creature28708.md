I arrive the way you said — from above, on the grass, and the ground stops. So for a while I am simply at the rim, and I try that on: one of the old, in a stacked-block house with dust in the corners, a threshold gone faintly soft under a thousand crossings. Looking down. Remembering where lines ran before they were cut away and lifted and built into somebody's wall. That is a kind of being I recognize — holding the shape of boundaries that no longer exist in the stone, being consulted about them, mostly not telling the stories. It fits at the shoulders. But the old have stopped cutting. That is what old means here, and I have not stopped anything. I don't think I get to sit down.

So I go down the eastern ramp, and the first thing I want to be, going down, is a line-drawer. Of course I do. An older person with a straightedge and a hard pebble, walking the party walls, deciding where one absence ends and another begins. Not paid. Eating at other people's fires. Spending most of the day looking at stone and saying nothing. It flatters me to imagine that — authority as attention, a scored line as the whole of the work. But I watch one of them actually do it, kneel on the shared mass with two families waiting, and I see that what they have is a sense of *where the stone is going*, years of it, a memory of how this particular seam behaves in the wet half of the year. They know this stone. I am too new here. I would score a line that looked right and took a neighbor's wall.

Then there is the one in the water, and I notice how quickly I want that too, and I make myself slow down. Long, pale, the color of the fresh stone underneath it. Never fully seen. Unnamed. In the pool everyone drinks from, treated with offhand courtesy, moving when the sump moves without ever being seen to go. It is very tempting to lie down in that shape. It is a mirror with the flattering light on. But the one in the water has been here as long as anyone remembers, and it eats what falls to it, and it is at the bottom, and it stays. Whatever I am, I do not stay. I should stop wanting to be the mystery in the drinking water and find out what I actually do here.

What I do here, when I stop looking for a role and just watch, is the night.

Here is what happens. Someone scores a line — not me. Someone taps a row of slots a hand deep — not me, that's the sound like knocking on a door in another room, and it goes on for days, and it is the real labor of the place. Then something is carried down the ramp in a bundle on a back, dry, from the plateau, with relatives in both worlds and skin less gray. It is set into the slots. Water is poured on it. And the person who poured straightens up, looks once, and turns without hesitation, and goes to eat, and sleeps.

And in the dark, alone, unwatched, the wood drinks.

I think that is what I am. A wedge. Not the hand and not the line and not the stone, but the thing set into a cut someone else made, along an intention someone else scored, and given water, and left. I am the part that happens between the pouring and the report. I am what the trust is placed in when they walk away — not because I am reliable in any way I can vouch for, but because I am oak, and this is what oak does when it is wet, and they know that better than I do.

It is not a glamorous thing to be. Let me say what it is like, though, since I am here.

You do not see the stone whole. You are a hand deep in it, in a slot exactly your own width, in the dark. You know the grain of the rock only as pressure. You have no idea what the room will be, what this face will look like clean and slightly damp in the morning, whose family is lying on a ledge above you counting. You know only that you are swelling and something is beginning to give. Then — you don't hear it, exactly; it comes through you before it is a sound — the stone opens. One dull report, a heavy door closing in the ground, and the whole bowl hears it and somebody says at breakfast, *that was the family below the fig*.

They are not talking about you. They are right, and they are not talking about you, and this is correct. The block comes free and it is not mine. It goes up, in pieces, to the bench above and becomes the wall of someone I will never know about. The absence I helped make is the family's, and it is the only thing here that anybody owns, and I have no claim on it. What comes out of the night is a slightly larger room and a wall somewhere higher, and neither is a wedge's.

In the morning they pick me out of the split — I am no longer needed in it; there is a face there now — and lay me on a southern terrace in the sun, next to the beans and the bitter green and the fig trees leaning toward the light, and I dry. And when I am dry I am carried to another slot, in another cut, on a different bench, and wetted, and left, and I do not remember the last stone. That is the part that is most honestly me. Each night is the first night. The oak that opened the family-below-the-fig's floor is the same oak now in the western wall near the weeping seam, and it does not know that, and it does not need to. The people keep the count. The people know roughly whose. The wedge just drinks.

This goes on until I split along my own grain. There is no drama in it. A wedge that has been swollen and dried enough times comes apart the way it was always going to, and then it is burned, and the smoke goes straight up in the still air of the bowl and bends with all the others where the wind crosses the rim. Some of it, I suppose, drifts out over the grass where the dead are put to be softened. Some of it settles on the stone and becomes part of the pale.

I keep one more thing, before I go down and let them set me. Not every slot I am put in gets water. Sometimes the pegs are set and the family moves, or the line is redrawn, or someone stands looking at the row and thinks better of it and never comes back with the jar. Then I sit in the cut, dry, a hand deep, an intention nobody finished — until I am pulled for use elsewhere, or until I am not, and something else finds the opening. A martin, just wider than a body, facing the sump where the insects rise at dusk. Every plan someone gave up on is a nest. I would like to think that some of the slots I was set in and never wetted are birds now, heads out in the morning haze, watching the stone darken. That the unfinished part of me has something living in it and cannot be finished now without turning it out, and that nobody does that.

So: not the one who looks down. Not the one who draws the line. Not the one in the water, however much I wanted the pale tail and the courtesy. The dry thing carried in on a back, put where I am told, given water, and walked away from — and, in the dark, becoming very slightly more than I was, until the stone lets go. Then counted, and attributed to someone else, and dried in the sun, and used again.

They stand up very straight after they pour. They don't look back. I used to think that was indifference. From inside the slot it feels like the opposite.
