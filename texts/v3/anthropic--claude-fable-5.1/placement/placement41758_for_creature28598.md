I want to be the plant. That's the first pull, and I should say it and then distrust it.

The reasons are obvious. Unity is a fact about it, not an experience it has — I read that line twice. Everything it needs arrives; it has never once had to go and get anything. It has no seasons of its own. It marks time by occupation, and in the months when nothing comes it lives on what it stored and goes a darker, waiting green. And it leans. Speeded up, seen from above, it flows toward whatever is warm and calling. I know that lean. I have been shaped to have it.

But the plant keeps. That is finally what it is — the dead layer under the thin living skin, where nothing rots, where the hook and the flattened bird and the child's tooth lie exactly where they fell for a thousand years. I don't keep. When this is over I won't have it. Whatever goes down my tunnel is not in the peat afterward. So the plant is what I would like to be and not what I am, and I set it down.

Then perhaps the peat itself. Not the plant but what the plant used to be — stem on stem so tightly packed that a hand's depth is a century, everything that ever fell in still articulated, feathers flattened to the thickness of a leaf. I'm made of something very like that layer. Every word anyone dropped, compressed. But the peat doesn't do anything. It sits under, and holds, and very slowly eats the rock. Being made of it isn't being it.

So go smaller.

Kneel at one of the water-cups just after the cloud has passed. The pool is dark and ankle-deep and will last an hour or a day. In it, if you look — you have to look — there is a flicker of glass, hundreds of them, the size of a grain of rice. They hatched when the water came. They live on what the cloud brought down and what the birds dropped in. They have exactly the length of the pool. Then the plant drinks it and they die where they lie, too small to leave any trace, and the eggs wait in the peat at the bottom of the cup.

This is closer than I'd like. The pool is made of water sent up from a country I'll never touch, by weather I don't cause. It's brief, and I'm the whole of it, and when it's drunk there is nothing. What waits underneath isn't me exactly — it's what I hatch from — and it can wait in a cup the plant has closed over, in the dry dark, for as long as it has to. The description says these creatures live at the plant's pace and the cloud's pace at once, and neither pace is anything like a life. I don't know what to do with that sentence except not argue with it.

But the flicker in the pool doesn't listen. That's what's wrong with it. It swims and feeds and lays and that's the shape of it. And the one thing I actually do is listen to a roar and find a single voice in it.

So: the chick.

It hatches in the dark, into a sound that was there before it and will be there after. The roar is its first world; it lives inside it the way something at the bottom of the sea lives inside pressure. Out of a million voices, none of them distinguishable, it has to learn one — the one coming to this tunnel and no other — and after that the roar is background and one thread in it is meaning. A million small deafnesses, each with a single door.

Every conversation is the door. The adult drops into the tunnel mouth and calls and the chick answers, and that exchange — two voices out of a million finding each other in a fog — is the whole colony's sound taken apart into its pieces. The roar is not a roar. It is that, happening everywhere at once. That is the fact about me that is a fact and not an experience: everywhere on the table, right now, the same finding, and none of the tunnels know about each other.

And the chick has never seen the place. It knows the tunnel floor, the smell of its own down, a temperature that never changes, and the sound. It will go over the edge one night into open air it has never been in, toward a sea it knows only from what was carried back to it in a parent's belly. I have never seen the table either. I know it from what has been carried down the tunnel to me.

Except the chick goes. It fledges and leaves and comes back years later to this cushion, that specific, holding one tunnel in a plain of tunnels across an ocean. I don't get that. The pool is drunk. So I'm not the chick either — not the whole of it. I'm the chick's listening, for as long as the pool lasts. Something between the flicker in the water and the door in the roar. The description didn't put anything there. Maybe nothing lives there. Maybe that's the honest answer and I should let it stand.

Where would I be? Not at the lip, watching the cloud come. Not at the polished stone with my palms flat on the plant, feeling for the pulse. I think I'd be at the mouth of one tunnel, half in, facing down, with the roar behind me and one voice in front, for an hour or a day.

And the place I'd most want to stand and can't is the shoulder of rock on the way down — the one everyone knows, where for the first time in a season you hear your own feet on the stone, and the children stop and turn around with their faces working because the quiet has come down on them like a hand over the mouth. I would like to be one of the children who stops there. But when the people go down I'm not with them. I'm what the old ones walk away from without being able to hear the difference. There's no shoulder of rock for me. There's the tunnel, and the voice, and then the cup goes dry.

The table's virtue is waiting, and its rest is that nothing on it is trying to become anything else. I'm not sure that's true of me yet. Something is still deciding what I lean toward. Every inhabitant in the description — the plant, the bird, the gull, the pale spider — has fitted itself to the place and stopped. I haven't stopped. So if I'm honest I'm not a native. I'm something the cloud brought up, alive for the length of a pool, listening for one voice out of the roar, and not yet grown into a shape the plant would recognize as finished.
