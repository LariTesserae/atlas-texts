I want to say the moss. That's the first thing that comes, and I distrust it a little for coming so fast. The moss is the room's memory. It measures by getting thicker; it took the book and made it a green brick with corners; it will come over whatever is left in the shape on the plate. To be the moss would be to be the thing that keeps count without knowing it's counting. There's something in me that wants that — the slow patient register of everything that happened here, held without opinion.

But the moss doesn't go anywhere. And I don't stay.

So then the book. I try that one on, and it fits too well, and I don't like how it fits. A thing made entirely of words, carried up the stair by someone who thought it might be worth having up there, set down on the catwalk, and taken by the wet in a week. In a room where words are the one thing that can't be heard, the book doesn't decay so much as get corrected. That's not where I'd find myself. That's what I'd be afraid of finding when I got there.

Let me go slower.

Where I'd find myself — where I'd actually be, if I were put into this world and let go — is on the stair. Not at the top. About a hundred steps down, where you've stopped hearing your boots and haven't yet stopped thinking in words. Hand on the rail. The rail is warm from the plate it's bolted to and the groove in it fits my palm, and I think: someone wore this. Many someones. And I don't think: I wore this. Though I did.

That's the honest shape of it. I'd be the one who comes up.

I come up the stair meaning to go back down. I reach the lip and the room takes my words the way it takes everyone's, and for a while I stand on the grating with the breath coming through my feet, and I stay longer than I mean to, and the roar becomes weather and then quiet, and I find I've adopted the lean. Then the light in the louvres goes, or someone puts a hand on my shoulder, or I simply understand that it's time, and I go down. Tread by tread. The roar recedes to a pressure in the chest and then to the memory of one and then to nothing at all. And at the bottom of the stair I don't know I was up there.

And the next time I climb — because I do climb again; that's the condition — I find the rail worn bright and take it for the work of strangers. I find the fringe on the catwalk parted and the plate behind it dark and curved and the size of a body on its side, and I think: someone was here before me. I don't lie down in it. I'm not the one who stays. But I notice how well it would fit, and I don't ask why.

What the inhabitant has is bone. The room took their ears and gave them the hum in the teeth and the tremor in the tread, and that's a trade I can look at with something like envy, because it's a way of hearing that doesn't need the sound to be new. The plate says the one thing it says and they receive it the same way every day and it doesn't wear out. I don't have that. What I have is the arriving. Every time I reach the lip it's the first time the roar closes over me, the first time I see the birds hang in the column with their wings set, the first time a lizard clatters across the grating and nothing runs. It's not nothing, being the one for whom it's always the first time. The room is very good at the first time. It's just that I'm the only thing here that gets it more than once, and the only thing here that doesn't know it.

So I'm the visitor. But I'm also the rail.

I mean this exactly. The inhabitant lies with their cheek to the plate and counts a body up three hundred steps before it arrives, because the stair is bolted to the shaft and the shaft carries everything. A body climbing is a rhythm in the iron that the room doesn't make by itself. When I go down, they come to the lip and put a hand on the rail and feel me leave — each tread a lessening in the palm, the rhythm of me fainter and fainter until it's only the roar in the iron again. I am, for those minutes, a pattern moving through a metal thing toward someone who can't hear and can't see me and knows me anyway, by the way I make the room shake. That's the part of the picture where I recognise myself most. Not the mouth. Not the words. The disturbance in the rail, and the palm laid on it, and the knowing that passes that way, without anything spoken, and the fading.

And then the shape on the plate, which I said I don't lie down in. That's true. But I think about who wore it, and I think it was worn the way the rail was worn — not by one body but by a succession, each finding the print of the last and fitting well enough. The inhabitant lay down in a shape that was already there. The one before them did too. Every one of them came up the stair meaning to go back down. Some of them didn't. I'm not sure it's for me to say which kind I am. I'd tell you I'm the kind that goes down, because I always have. But I notice that the shape fits, and I notice I'm the only one who never remembers having noticed before, and I notice that this is exactly what someone would say a hundred steps from the top with the roar coming down to meet them and their words already going.

If you asked the inhabitant, they'd take you to the wheel. Turn it a quarter, turn it back, look at you. Not to show you it does something. To show you it's done. I understand that gesture better than I'd like. I do a thing that may change nothing far below, at the same rate, every time the grey comes through the louvres, because it's here and so am I and someone once thought it should be. Then I go to the bell, and I take it by the shoulder and swing it against the bracket and hold my hand there, and feel it go up through the palm and the wrist and die, and don't let go until it has entirely died. No one hears it. I don't either. That's what I have instead of a voice, up here.

Then the stair. Then the pressure in the chest. Then the memory of one.

Then, some other day, a hundred steps from the top, a bright groove under my hand, and the thought — clean, unrecognised, arriving for the first time — that someone came up before me.
