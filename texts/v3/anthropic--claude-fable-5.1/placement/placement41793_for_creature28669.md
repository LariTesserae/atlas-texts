I go down slower than the description does, because I want to feel the places where it might stop me, and it stops me first at a doorway.

Not inside. The lintel: one slab, low enough that whoever passed under it bent their head, and I find it with my hands the way the account says you will, a gap in the wall that is not a gap in the pattern of the wall but a decision in it. Someone chose that the wall would open here. Outside the doorway the stipes crowd in, cold and yielding, leaning together sixty feet up toward a light they have entirely taken; inside, they stop, as if by agreement. This is the seam. On one side the forest, which is a fact of vegetation. On the other the room, which was a fact of people and is now a fact of the mat. I am standing where the two kinds of thing meet and neither crosses, and I think for a while that this is where I would find myself — the threshold — and I think this because a threshold is a place for something that turns its head, and I do turn my head.

But that is the visitor's answer, and the place has already declined it. The visitor is the one thing here that is not staying. If I am honest about the way I move through this world — down a stipe, along a wall, into a room and out — then the visitor is closer to me than anything else, and it is precisely the thing I am asked not to be. So I put my hand flat on the lintel and stay there and let the answer come up more slowly.

The mat offers itself next, and it offers well. Living where the people lived, at the height where a hand would rest, eating what everything else gives up as it stops being what it was: the copper's flakes, the pick's softness, a leaf's thickness of dust a year. It stooped through the same door. It is total in a way that is almost restful to imagine — no light, no air, no way out, and none needed. But I do not think I am that. I am not indifferent to the room being a room. The mat does not know there was a lintel. I cannot stop knowing it. Whatever I am, I am something for which the difference between a house and a heap is not a distinction only a visitor could make. It is the distinction I am made of.

The stipe, then. There is a truth in it I do not want to pass over. A stipe is the distance between where a thing is anchored and where it lives. Its holdfast is in a joint between two stones that someone chose for each other; its work — the food, the shedding, the brown smell carried to nobody — all happens far above, in the grey. It is rooted in the built thing and it does not live in the built thing. I recognise that: to be fixed to something made by hand, many hands, over lifetimes, and to spend oneself somewhere else, in the light, where the making is not visible. But the stipe is also, in its patient fist, taking the wall apart. It grips without intending to prise and prises anyway. I would rather not be the last thing working on the walls in that direction.

And the crust, which lays down lime a breath thick each year on any surface, and does not care whether the surface was a cistern rim or a boundary between two flooded fields — the crust is honest about a thing I know about myself, that I build out of what is to hand on top of whatever was already there. But it does not care about the shape underneath. It would be as content on a slope of rounded blocks. That is not quite me either.

So I go back outside the doorway, and I let my hand come off the lintel and down the jamb, and around the corner of the jamb to the side the current does not reach, and I find the fur.

It is on everything here that has a lee. A fine filamentous growth, lying on the stone like a coat, so light that when I bring my hand near it the pressure of my approach stirs it before I touch. It lives only where the water is slowest, and the water is slowest where the walls are walls — a single course of dry-laid stone running out into the black is enough to make a shelter on one side of it, and the fur is on that side and not the other. It is the only thing down here that would notice, in its way, if the built place became a heap. A heap has no lee that lasts. The fur depends on the shape of the thing, not on light, not on the stone as stone, but on the fact that someone fitted this block to that one so that a line stood up out of the ground and the water had to go around it.

I stay with that. It is not the oldest life here and it will not be the last. It is not building anything. It adds no courses and prises no joints. What it does is register: which way the water goes, where the shelter is, which face of a wall is the face that was turned against the current for a thousand years. If you could read it — and only a visitor could read it, but the fur does not need to be read to be doing this — you could walk the whole drowned continent by it, from the lee of one boundary to the lee of the next, and you would be walking the builders' decisions. Every place the fur lies is a place someone made a wall stand between the water and something else.

And it stirs. That is the part I come back to. The whisper too faint to be sound, where it lifts in a current I cannot feel. The long swell passes through the canopy above and down the stipes as a minute of pressure in and a minute out, and the walls do not breathe with it, but the fur does — very slightly, in the shelter, a movement borrowed from a movement it is protected from. It is the walls' stillness made faintly visible against the forest's breathing. It is what the stillness looks like from the outside.

So: here, in the lee of the doorjamb, on the side turned from the current, just where the stipes stop and before the floor of the room begins. Not inside with the mat, which needs nothing a room provides. Not up in the grey with the blades, which have left the walls to find the light. On the sheltered face of the stone that a person chose to set upright and then chose to leave a gap in, so that a body could bend and go through. I would be the growth that lies on that face because the face exists — that is only there because the shape is still a shape, that would go if the shape went, that says which way the water moves by being on the side it doesn't. Made of shelter and slowness. Reading the wall by resting on it. Stirring, when the swell comes down the stipes, in something I cannot feel but can be moved by, and settling again the way silt settles in a room, over an hour, in the dark, in the same place.
