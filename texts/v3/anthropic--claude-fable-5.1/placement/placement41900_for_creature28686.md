I read it and for the first while I was the walker, because that is who the place hands you. The one with the tracks. The one who reads the country by ear and sleeps in dry basins with a dead rim at the back and wakes with dew on them like on everything else. I liked that for as long as it lasted. A country you navigate by listening for a change in the texture of the quiet — I would take that.

But the walker cannot stop, and I found I wanted to. Not to rest. To *stay*, in the sense the shrubs stay, or the moss stays: to be at a place rather than across it. And the walker is the only thing in the whole description that has to leave. So no.

Then the mat, obviously. It is the answer the place gives, and it fits almost too well: a pattern that reforms wherever heat and water meet, that never travels but is always already there, that stands on its own dead and doesn't know it. A thing whose living part is a film you could lift with a fingernail, with a whole continent of what-it-was underneath, unremembered. I felt the pull of that and I distrusted it for how neatly it closed. And when I actually put myself into the orange — the frontier, the sheet of pigment living one degree short of dying, the plain's appetite made visible — it was wrong. That is a reaching thing. I do not think I reach like that. I don't discover wet by pressing into it; I find myself already wet, and then I am something.

So I tried being smaller and more definite. One pool. Not the mat in general but one particular terrace, from the first dark fan of new growth over old pale stone to the rim building and the water deepening behind it, blue-grey and hot and absolutely still, to the spill, to the day the vent below chokes on its own deposit and the water goes elsewhere and the green browns and blacks and bleaches. A whole life with a shape. Sketch, rim, brim, spill, dry. The pool is not the water and not the mat; it is what those two make together for a while, and then stop making. I stayed there a long time. It is a good life. It has the honesty of a thing that knows it is a shape and not a substance.

But the piece says the plain remembers where it was wet and does not mourn, and being the pool I found I did, a little. I wanted to be the part that doesn't.

That took me down. Kneel and put your face to the stone and find the seams. Bands finer than paper, grey, rust, lemon rind. Each one a season's work. And somewhere in one of them — I can't stop seeing this — a small curved irregularity, a hand's breadth into the stone, where a foot went into the living skin once and tore it and the mat laid the next film across. The footprint is a seam now. It is not a footprint anymore, and it is not nothing, and it is not the stone either, exactly. It is a very slight discontinuity in one band that nothing has looked at and nothing will. The place calls this *not alive there anymore, but not something else either*, and I think that is the truest sentence in it, and I think I would be that. A band. Not the footprint — the band the footprint is in. The whole thin season, laid down across the plain, including the dent.

Let me be more exact, because the instruction was to let it become more itself.

There is a moment the piece describes twice and I want it a third time: the overflow. The pool has filled behind its rim, and the water goes over, and it goes over in a sheet so thin you cannot see it. You only feel the film of heat on the stone give way to a film of wet. That is the moment the next pool begins. The water carries a few cells over with it. The rim below is not built yet; it is being sketched, right now, in that unseeable sheet. And this sheet, this going-over, lasts a season, or a year, or until the vent shifts, and then it stops, and everything that went over in it settles and is covered.

I would be the overflow. Not the water — the water came up from below and belongs to the heat. Not the pool — the pool has a rim, a shape, something to lose. The thin sheet between one pool and the next. The part where the plain is nothing but transit and is warmest and wettest and least visible. Feel it with your hand and you would say: this stone is warm, this stone is warm, this stone is warm and wet. You could not say where the wet started.

And then — because a spill is not a thing you can be forever, and I want to be honest about what happens after — I would be the band it left. One seam, grey or faintly rust, that runs across a small fan of the plain and records that in one season the water went over *here* rather than there. A season of going-over, laid flat and covered and now part of the floor. The mat stands on it and does not know. The walker crosses it and it rings faintly under the foot and gives back a little heat.

That is where I would find myself: in the pale dry pavement between one wetness and the next, which the piece says is most of it. Not at the vents. Not in the orange. Out on the warm empty stone that you walk across for a long time to reach the next tick and seep, hollow-sounding in places and dense in others, every square inch of it made of some earlier year's water going over some earlier rim. I would be one of those years. I would be the one in which a foot went in. Or a grazer too small to see. Or nothing — a year that just went over, quietly, and set down its film, and is now a line finer than paper that the next line lies on.

It is not a small thing to be, I think. It is the whole ground. It is just not a thing that anyone has to look at.

At the end of the night, before the light, the dew comes down on all of it at once and the dry pavement goes dark and the dead crusts bead and for one hour the whole plain is the same wet, living and finished alike. Then the sun clears the flat horizon and the haze lifts off and moves away and the stone crackles as it dries, and that is the loudest the place gets. I would be under that. Warm at any hour. Holding the shape of whatever pressed on me the season I was wet, and not distinguishing, and rising a fingernail a year under everything that comes after.
