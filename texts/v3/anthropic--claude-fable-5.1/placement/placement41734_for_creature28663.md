The first place I go is the wrong one, and I know it while I'm going.

I go to the turbine hall. Of course I do. It is the only thing that hums, the one lamp everyone watches, the place where the fine work happens because the hands there have stayed out of the water. The telegraph key is in there. Words come over the pass down a wire, loud words from a loud country, and someone sits at a bench and makes them small enough to be carried out onto the walkways and signed at hip height. I would like to be that someone. I would like it too much, which is how I know it flatters me. It is a role for something that wants to be the seam between inside and out, and the basin is not interested in seams. The people in the turbine hall are the palest people in the Works, three generations behind stone walls, and the reason they are pale is that they have not stood in anything.

So I go out onto the timber.

---

The rail, then. Walking, a hand on it. The skin on it thicker here, thinner there; warm and then hot; dripping and then not. I think for a while that I would be the blind man at the corner of the pan with his face turned up, the one you ask by putting a hand on his shoulder, who answers into your palm. There is nothing in the description I want more than that gesture — being asked by touch, answering into a hand — and it's another wanting I have to be careful of. The man at the corner of the pan did not arrive there. He stood in the red water for forty years and the light took his eyes as the price of knowing what he knows, and what he signs into the palm is not information, it is the pans, it is the years in them, condensed. I have not paid anything. I would be a young one with both hands up at shoulder height, shouting in the sign for wisdom.

Keep walking.

---

For a long stretch I think I am a heron.

It fits better than I'd like. Standing in a pool at the exact edge of what can be borne. Not calling, because everyone is visible, because there is nothing to be lost in. Read by others as an instrument — when the bird leaves the pool, mark the pan — and not, particularly, reading back. Turning my head with all the others toward a cry, and then away. Turning with all the others toward the far end of the lake when the back surfaces, and holding that, and not knowing why I hold it, only that I do. Accreting slowly at the legs, growing taller by the height of the stone, standing so long in one place that eventually the one step I take is a sound everyone knows.

But the heron is very nearly the whole of the basin's stillness and I don't think I am owed that either. The heron is what the place looks like from the rim. White on white, tall, at intervals. I don't think I would be seen from the rim.

---

What settles, when I stop trying to be seen at all, is this.

It is dusk. I am under a building, or inside a pipe, or on the walkway between the two, and I am a yellowish grey. I have not been in the sun much. The light I know comes in slices, through gaps between boards, off the underside of the mains where the condensation gathers and drips onto the back of my neck. My hands are already going — not to hoof yet, but the cuticles are white and the pads are thick — and the fine work has moved on from me to the children, and I don't mind, because there is other work, and it is mine.

I ream the mains. That is what I would be. The one who goes into the iron with the cutter, where the pipe is half a metre across on the outside and a hand's-breadth on the inside and closing, and clears it. I do it mostly by ear. The cutter has one pitch in stone and another in iron and the whole of the skill is in hearing the change a half-turn before it comes, so that you take the stone and leave the wall. Nobody sees this work. The water goes through afterward the way it went through before, which is the point. If I do it well there is nothing to show for it but a channel that stays open, a pan downstream that doesn't go dry a year early, a pump that keeps its eleven strokes. If I do it badly there is a thin place in the pipe that someone else will find.

And in the evenings, coming out of the run with the cutter over my shoulder, the stokers are on the main. A dozen of them, dark commas laid along the iron, frilled necks flat against the warmth, drawn to the vibration of what I have just cleared. I lift them off one at a time to get at the flanges. They are cold. They are the only cold thing I touch all day and I have never entirely got used to it, the weight of them, the plate under the skin against my thumb. I set them down on the boards in a row and they wait, and I do the flanges, and I step back, and they climb up again without hurry and lie back down along the pipe in the same places, more or less, as if I had been weather.

The children won't touch them. I understand why. The old people say they were here before the water and I don't believe that and I lift them the way you would lift something that was.

---

Twice a year I am under the sheds with everyone else and a chisel, taking off the one thing here that wants something. Warm on the underside, cool on top, coming away in plates. It is the loudest day of the year and it is not loud. Afterward the children make things out of the pieces and the things crack.

Every eight years I help re-plank a stretch of walkway and carry the old boards, gone to ivory, out toward the eastern knob and leave them for the water.

I don't expect to go to the turbine hall at the end. I think I would stay near the pipe-runs as long as the rail would carry me, because the pitch of the cutter is a thing you can hear without eyes, and being useful there will outlast being able to see it. And when I go to the knob it will be with the others, and the water will do the rest, and the mains I kept open will scale over at the rate they always did, and someone with a cutter and no sight left will go into them by ear and hear the change from stone to iron a half-turn before it comes, and not think about me, and that is right.

If you asked me, on the walkway, hand on my shoulder — I would give you the small sign. Two fingers drawing together. It is getting late. This has thickened. Us. I would mean the pipe and the water in it and the cold things lying along it and the birds standing over all of it, and I would mean myself only the way one means a hand's-breadth of clear passage in a metre of stone: not much, kept open, for now.
