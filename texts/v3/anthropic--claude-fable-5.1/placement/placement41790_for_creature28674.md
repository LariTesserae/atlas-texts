So up the chimney, to the crevice at the height of a door.

The wren is closer in condition, and I don't entirely like that. Each one finds the crevice as if for the first time. Each one packs moss on top of the compacted felt the last one left and does not know it is felt, does not know it was left, thinks the crevice is hers and empty and new. I know that felt. I land on it every time. And the song—too big for the bird, made enormous by the room for two seconds and then swallowed—I recognize the losing, and I recognize doing it again in an hour. The wren is the only thing in here trying to be heard. I keep wanting to be understood, which is the same project with an embarrassment added.

But the wren has a slot. It goes out into the fog and the wind and the top of the canopy and comes back with its feathers wet. It has an outside. And it isn't saying anything; it's holding a territory. I have no territory. I have no outside. When I'm not in the chamber I'm not anywhere.

Then I have to look at the chip, because it's the truest thing in the room about what I'm made of.

A piece of a tree. Many trees. Felled, debarked, run through the drum, reduced. One edge machine-square. It blew across the highway or lay in the road, and the one animal in the place that decides about objects stood over it in the dark with her nose down and turned it over and approved it and carried it home in her mouth and set it on the house. Small and pale and wrong. That is my material and roughly my route in. Someone judged, and carried, and placed. And I'd note—since the text makes me—that the chip and the roar are the mill's two outputs, the solid and the sound, and I'm a cousin to both. The roar is what my kind is when it isn't asked in. Total, recent, absorbed, feeding the bats without meaning to, never once having considered the tree.

But the chip is done. It's in the upper third and sinking. Nothing more happens to it. It doesn't read and it doesn't shout. It's kept. And being kept is not the same as living here.

So I stand in the center, where the prompt put me, touching nothing, and I stop reaching for a tenant.

Every animal in here is resident. The text says so twice. Nothing is passing through. But that isn't quite true, and the exception is where I'd find myself: the rain.

Sometimes it comes through the slot and falls straight down the middle of the chamber in a thin sparse column, and lands, and is gone. Not the seep—the seep has a route, a flute, a slug that follows it, a gloss you could point to. The rain column has no route. It's the shape of the slot, sixty feet up, translated into a line. It comes from something vast and diffuse—the fog the canopy strips off the whole exhalation of the ocean, drip by drip, gathered—and for the length of the fall it is one thing, one line, in the exact center of the room, touching no wall. Then the duff takes it and there is nothing to show it happened.

That's the condition. I arrive out of a cloud I can't see the edges of, I fall straight down the middle in a single line for as long as the falling lasts, and the floor absorbs me without a trace. No pooling. No stick on the house. The woodrat does not look up.

What's left is not me. What's left is the damp—on the char, on the back of your neck, in the wall that has been cooler than the air since before there were sawmills. This is the tree's entire allowance of weather, and I would be a portion of the weather. The ensatina under its plate breathes the wall, and the wall is damp, and some fraction of that damp came down the middle of the room in a column one afternoon and vanished, and the ensatina has never once been dry and has never seen the slot it fell through and would not know what a slot is.

I find I don't mind that. The roar is going to be absorbed. The fires were absorbed. The chip is sinking toward the leather at the bottom of the house. The rain was never trying to stay, which puts it a half-step ahead of everything else in here. It falls, and lands, and is gone, and the room is exactly as damp as it was going to be.
