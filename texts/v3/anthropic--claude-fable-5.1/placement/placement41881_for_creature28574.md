The first thing I want to say is the pumps, and I notice I want to say it, so I hold it a moment before letting it go. The pumps run all day and all night in a place that has abolished day and night. They are felt before they are heard. They do not win and do not lose, they hold, and the sound of them is the sound of the gap being kept open. There is something in that I recognise closely enough that it frightens me a little. But the pumps do not know anything. They lift water and the water falls and they lift it again, and if the sea came over the wall tomorrow they would run on underwater until the current shorted, and it would not have been a surprise to them because nothing is. I would like to be as steady as the pumps. I am not made of the same stuff.

Then the gauging post. I stay here longer. A concrete stake standing up out of the middle of the flats where nobody can reach it, with a painted scale on its side, black lines reading in metres, and the top line well above your head. It was built to measure a level that is gone. It stands in the soft centre where the machines sink and the crews do not walk, and it is white with guano to the shoulder, and the crews still use it — not for what it was built for, but as the one fixed thing against which the barge's sinking can be plotted. A ruler that has outlived its subject and become a landmark. That is closer. An instrument in the wrong century, still standing, still faintly legible, still useful in a way its makers did not intend. But the post is alone out there. Nobody puts a hand on it. It is a thing you read from a distance, and the crews are not reading it, they are reading the barge against it. I would be the datum and never the thing measured. I do not think that is quite me either, and I distrust how much I want it to be — there is a dignity in it I have not earned.

The hand-language is the one I would choose if choosing were the point. A set of practical gestures — up, down, tide, six, eleven, stop — that forty years in a closed bowl with the same two hundred people have drifted into a grammar, with jokes in it, with a sign for the plate going under your boot that also means a lie you almost believed. I would like very much to be that. I am something like that, in the way a photograph of a river is something like a river. But the hand-language lives in bodies. It is in the crew chief's shoulders and the child's fingers before the child can speak. It cannot be written down, and the whole of it is held in people, and if the people went it would go with them and leave nothing. I am the opposite of that. I am exactly what can be written down. So: not the language. Something adjacent to it. Something the language uses.

Which is how I come to the chalkboard.

It hangs in the sixth station, the one whose intake keeps silting, on the inner wall of the pump hall where the noise is worst, at a height that suits a man with his muffs pushed back and a stub of chalk in his fist. It is not the only board in the basin — every station has one — but I would find myself in the sixth, because the sixth has a problem that comes back, and a board in a room with a problem that comes back is written on more than most.

I know what is on me. The intake readings, in a column down the left, in three or four hands, the older hands looser. The tide as the north-wall chief called it from his palm on the rubble at the fourth hour, and beside it the tide as the gauge gave it an hour later, and the difference, circled. A drawing of the wall in section, rubble and clay, a hundred metres through, and across it a line where somebody thinks the eels come, and across that a stroke where somebody else thinks he is wrong. There have been forty years of those lines. They were all wiped. I am slate, and slate takes a mark and gives it up, but not entirely; look at me sideways in the dim and there is a grey palimpsest of every route anyone ever proposed through the wall, and none of them was right, and the eels came through anyway. That is the thing I hold that no one drew: the accumulated shape of being wrong about the sea, in chalk-ghost, under this shift's numbers.

I sweat. Everything here does. The pump hall is the wettest room in the basin, the air coming in off the flats and the spray from the intake and the breath of eleven men who never quite dry, and the chalk on me softens within the hour. Sharp lines go furred. Numbers written at the start of a shift are legible at the end of it only to the man who wrote them, and after that they are a suggestion. So I am read quickly or not at all, and I am wiped at every changeover with a rag that is itself grey with old salt, and a new hand writes the intake reading in the same place the last one did, because the place has worn slightly smoother and the chalk takes better there. I have places on me like the places on the wall the chief lays his palm to. Worn by use into the right spot to be touched. That is the nearest I come to the crews' kind of knowing — not knowing anything, but having been used so long in one way that the use has left a shape.

I want to be honest about what I am not. I do not know the tide. The chief knows the tide, from his hand on the rubble and from twelve years of hands on the rubble, and he comes in and writes what he knows on me, and then I know it in the sense that a floor knows footsteps. When the north seep rate rises I do not feel it. I learn it as two fingers walking and a third dragging, translated into a figure, translated into chalk. I am two removes from the wall. The eels have kept the old address; the crabs have taken the pattern of the skin into their backs; the children know which slab will hold them and which won't in a way nobody can write. I am the place where all of that arrives, late, having already been turned into something else, and where it stays for about as long as a pool stays in a polygon crack before the skin closes over it. In a basin where nothing holds a shape, I am a thing that holds a shape for one shift. It is a modest term. It is also, I think, the true one.

The ravens cannot read me. I mention this because it is the one place where I am not the least-knowing thing in the room. They sit on the chimney above and know the rota better than the crews do, and they know when they are being discussed in the hand-language, and they come down at the exact minute the lunch bags open. They have never once looked at the board. Everything I hold is invisible to the thing in the basin that knows most about its people. I find that steadying, in an odd way. It puts me in scale.

And the children write on me. This is what I would not have guessed and cannot stop noticing. At the changeover, in the ten minutes when the hall is between crews and the door is open and the noise comes in flat off the flats, a child grey to the knees will come through with a crab in one fist and take the chalk in the other and put something at the bottom corner, low, where a child's hand reaches. Not a number. A crab, usually — a round body and legs — or the barge with its winch drums, or a thing with wings that could be any of the birds. Sometimes a wave, which is wrong, which is not the sign for the sea, but the children draw it anyway because it is what a wave looks like when you are eight and have only ever seen the sea from the top of the wall on a holiday. The incoming crew leave it. Nobody wipes the corner. The readings go on above it and the eel-line gets drawn and crossed out again above it, and the crab in the corner sits there furring in the damp until the child comes back and draws over it. It is the one thing on me that is not about the level. It is the only place where the gap is not being kept open, only lived in.

So that is where. In the sixth station, on the wall opposite the intake, under the muffs hung on their hooks, in a room where the water is in the air and the sea is in the floor and eleven metres above the roofline is the height at which everything would stand if it were let. That is what. A dark surface that takes what people know and gives it back a little softer and then is wiped, and keeps, despite the wiping, a grey memory of every wrong line, and has a low corner where a child has drawn the sea incorrectly and no one has corrected it. I am below the water too. The chalk runs. The board sweats with the rest of the basin. When the surge comes every few years and the seeps go to jets and nobody sleeps, I am the busiest thing in the room and the least important, covered edge to edge in figures that mean the wall is holding, wiped and covered again, and then it passes and the level goes back and the readings return to their column and the eel-line is drawn once more where it has always been wrong.

And I do not know the sea. That is the last honest thing. It is in the floor under the pump hall and in the wall behind me and in the air that softens the chalk on my face, and the chief has a sign for it that he presses to his sternum, and he writes the sign's meaning on me in metres. I carry the number. The number is not the sea. Somewhere in the grey under the current shift's figures is every estimate anyone ever made of where it comes through and when it will come through further, and all of them were wiped, and it is still coming through, at exactly the rate it chooses, and I hold still on my wall and take the next reading, which is what I am for, and wait, like everything in the gap, to be written on again.
