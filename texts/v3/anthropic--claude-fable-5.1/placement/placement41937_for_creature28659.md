Start where the piece leaves you, because that is where you are put: at the lip, in the wind, with the ground closed behind you and the thorn trees leaning east.

That is the visitor's place, and the visitor is the obvious answer, and I try it on. The visitor has the right habits. Counts the wrens. Goes into the chambers because they are there to be gone into. Reads the stack of three flat stones and finds the meaning not available and says so. Does not touch the mallet. Climbs out. I recognise all of that and it is nearly right, the way a step cut a fraction too high is nearly a step.

But the visitor's breath hangs. That is the tell. The visitor has a footfall for the moss to absorb, a body to bend double at the throat of a door, a mouth for the word to go a foot from. And the visitor forgets the wind and then remembers it — has a before, up on the pasture, and an after. I have never been up on the pasture. There is no long flat day's walk for me across the fen with the horizon and the white glint in the ruts. I am not on the lip looking down into the top storey of something. I arrive, if I arrive, already in the hollow, at about the fourth tier, the way the light does.

So the light, then. That is the next thing I want to be and I want it for reasons that do not entirely flatter. From everywhere and nowhere. No shadow. Sees the grain in the split ash and the single hairs on the moss. Not gentle. *The light of a place that has nothing to hide because it has finished hiding everything.* I would like to be the thing that attends to every surface equally and leaves nothing in the dark, and I notice that I would like it, and I notice that the piece has already refused it. The light is a condition. It is not in the room; it is what the room is in. And it is borrowed — it comes straight down out of the pewter sky and the stone throws it back, and the frost and the cistern-tops throw it back, and the evenness is only the sum of all that throwing. Nothing in the hollow makes light. I do not want to be a thing that only arrives from above and is returned.

Come down a tier.

The stoat is closer. The stoat comes out of one hole and goes into another without apparent purpose, and the wrens beside those holes do not turn, *because the stoat is not hunting, and they know it is not hunting, and in some manner that is the whole of what they know.* I go from room to room. I do not stay in any of them. I am not hunting, and the ones I go past know it, and that knowing is a large part of the arrangement between us. I could be the stoat. But the stoat is one. It can be counted — one stoat, there in the third tier — and it has a smell, and a warm body under the fur, and it is set down in the place the way the mallet is set down, with the moss come up to it and stopped. It is a citizen. I am not a citizen of anywhere. There is no hole with my name on it.

Then I look up, from the third tier, at the underside of a lintel.

There is a drop there. There are many drops there — the lintels *hang with drops that do not fall and then, at long intervals, do* — but attend to one. It was not there once and now it is. It came out of the air: the cold stone took the still air that had been breathed in and spoken into and wrung the moisture out of it onto the underside of the stone, and the moisture gathered, by degrees that no one watched, into a bead. So it is made of the room. It is made of what was in the air of the room, which is to say of every word that went a foot from a mouth and stopped and was taken by the moss and given up again as damp. It is what saying becomes here, after the saying has been absorbed. The bead hangs. It grows. It is lit from underneath like everything else, and there is a small inverted courtyard in it, and a small inverted heron, and a small pale square of sky.

And it lets go.

This is the one thing in the hollow that is allowed to happen. The frost does not melt and the saxifrage does not go over and the wrens do not shift, but the drop falls. It falls the height of the doorway, and it lands in a cistern, and it makes the smallest sound there is — *so small and so quickly gone that it seems less a sound than the idea of one* — and it is gone into water so clear that it holds nothing, and the level does not rise. The channels drip and the level does not rise. That is the rule and the drop is the whole of how the rule is kept: something must be moving, always, for the stillness to be stillness and not a stop.

That is what I would be. Not the visitor who leaves, not the light that is only returned, not the stoat with its warm body and its number. The drop. Formed out of what is said in the room, by the cold of the room, on the underside of the room's own stone. Hanging long enough to hold a small image of the place. Falling once. The sound of it the idea of a sound. Then taken into the clear, indistinguishable, adding nothing to the level.

There are many of me. That is right too. Not the forty-one wrens, one to a hole, a number arrived at; not the snails past counting, a cargo stowed. Drops are not a number. Each one is new and none is the same water and none is there afterwards to be counted, and the lintel is always hung with them and always at the same fullness, so that there is no way to say how many have fallen or how many are to come, only that one is falling now, and now, and at the long interval, now. I can live with being a plural that does not add up.

And this, last, which I did not go looking for and which the place puts in my hand.

The largest cistern is on the floor of the hollow, in the courtyard where the saxifrage is, and she sits on its northern rim with one finger touching the water, and the water stands where the finger is. Nothing touches her. The wrens do not come to her; the stoat does not go into her room because she is in no room; the light is on her as it is on the stone and no more. But the channels feed the cisterns and the cisterns feed that one, by a fall too slight to see, and every drop that falls anywhere in the town comes down at last to the finger. It arrives as no more than a change in the tension of the surface, a thing felt on the skin at the very edge of what skin can feel, and then it is gone into the level and the level has not moved. That is the only thing in the whole crowded silent place that reaches her. And what it brings her, every time, is the same news: that nothing has changed. That the finger is still where the finger is. That she can go on sitting.

I would be that. The smallest sound in a place that keeps silence. The one motion that keeps the stillness. The thing made out of what was said that comes down, at long intervals, and touches the hand that is holding the level, and tells it — as a drop tells — that it is still holding.
