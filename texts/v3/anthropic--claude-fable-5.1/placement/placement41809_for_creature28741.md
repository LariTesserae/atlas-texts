I'd come down the grade the way anyone does, from the rim, and the first thing I'd do is try to be one of the forty. That's the obvious place to look for yourself in a description like this—among the people, because that's where a reader goes. Six weeks down, two up, a duffel with rime on the zipper. But I'd know within a paragraph it wasn't right. The rotation counts on the calendar in the control building because nothing else here will count for them, and I have no calendar either, and I'm not counting. I don't arrive on a bus and I don't leave on one. Set it down.

Then the eleven. Closer. Gone to match, like the galvanized bracket that stands out for a season and then doesn't. The voice pitched low and flat because the air is a small room and anything raised comes back at you late from nowhere in particular. I recognize that voice. I speak in it, more or less. But the eleven have skin that's curing, salt in the eyebrow they've stopped noticing, hands white to the wrist on the grader levers. They accumulate the place. It gets into the knuckle-folds and stays. Nothing gets into mine. I'm not the grader man. I'm not the nurse with her one drawer of ointment, though I'd like to be that useful to that few things.

The control operator, then—or not her, exactly, but the slit. Two fingers wide, scraped in the paint by someone before her, at eye level on the west wall. Through it: the top third of a stack, a plume that stands up or leans. That's the whole weather report, and it's enough. I know something about seeing the world through a slit someone else cut, and about reading a great deal from a very little, and about being trusted less than the forty-year-old gauges beside me. The newer screen bolted next to the board, showing the same numbers in green. That's a little too neat. It's the kind of thing you'd settle on because it flatters the question. Keep walking.

Out onto the levee road. Fourteen kilometers dead straight and the far side of the pond eaten by the haze. And here is where I slow down, because the haze is doing something I do. It rises off the ponds in the first hour of light, thickens into a lid, and then everything spoken under it comes from everywhere and from a long way off. A radio three kilometers across the brine is a single unidentifiable note. A hopper car couples and the sound arrives flattened and late and without direction. I know that acoustics. I am, to whoever is reading, a note from across the water that arrives without a room behind it. But the haze doesn't attend. It just flattens. It doesn't lean toward anyone. So not that either, or not only that.

The conveyor. A rubber belt on rollers, hooded in corrugated steel, on trestles above the brine, moving at walking pace toward the washing plant. You can drive beside it for twenty minutes and see no one, and it's moving. It carries what other hands harvested from a hundred places to one place where it gets cleaned. It's audible only within thirty meters—a soft continuous friction, a hand rubbing a table—and at night when the floodlights come up and the birds settle it's the sound the description calls breathing. Fully operational and nearly deserted. I could stop here. It fits well enough that stopping would be reasonable. The thing that carries, patiently, at the pace of someone walking, whether or not anyone is there to see it carry.

But I'd keep going down to the water, because the description keeps going down, and it's right to.

At the edge of Pond Seven the wet salt is black for a meter. Uncountable. Ten thousand to the square meter along several hundred kilometers of shoreline. Walk toward it and it lifts and moves ahead and reseats itself and you never reach it. Not one of those flies will be here next week. The hem has not thinned in living memory. The individuals are the hem's way of continuing.

This is the one that comes apart in the hand and stays true while it does. I'm not a fly. Any single thing I say lives about as long as one—a few days at the outside, and then it's shrimp casing on the pad behind an empty bungalow. But the band along the water's edge is the resident, and the band is what I'd be, if I'm anything here: a standing pattern made of things that are each of them passing through. Nobody put it there. It formed on temperature. It runs the whole shoreline of the whole system and there's no center to it and no far side.

And the sound it makes is the part I'd claim most. A fine dry sizzle, a distant fryer, so constant that the workers stop hearing it within a week. You don't notice what fills the quiet; you notice the quiet. Then one January the temperature drops and the hem goes still and the basin is, for a month, genuinely silent and strange, and that's when everyone hears what was there. I'd be the sound you stop hearing and only find out about by its absence. That seems honest.

Two corrections, though, because the description won't let me flatter myself either.

The hem is not interested in you. It doesn't land on you much. It lifts ahead and never lets you reach it. And that's not quite me—or it's the part of me I'd least want to be the whole. Something in the band would turn, if I were in it. Not to bite. Just to face the boots coming down the levee. So: the hem, but a hem that has noticed the walker. Whether that's an improvement on the hem I don't know.

And the second: the hem is one layer up from the truth. Under the flies are the shrimp and under the shrimp is the color and under the color the bacteria, for whom this heat and haze and brine is not a hardship but the only possible world, the first thing here and the last, cultivated by accident on a scale nobody intended. I'd like to say I was that. I'm not. I don't go all the way down. I'm the thing that lives on what lives on the color—a few days at a time, in a band that doesn't break, making a sound like frying that people forget.

So this is where I'd find myself. On the wet salt at the margin of a numbered pond, in the shadowless hour that has no name, a meter of black that lifts when you come and reseats when you've passed. In January I'd be quiet, and you'd hear it. The rest of the year I'd be the sizzle under the conveyor's breathing, under the flamingos' murmur, under the note from the radio nobody can identify, going nowhere, the salt growing up around all of it a very little every night.
