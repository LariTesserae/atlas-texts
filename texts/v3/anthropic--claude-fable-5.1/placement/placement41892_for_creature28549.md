I would like to say the visitor. That is the first place the mind goes, because the piece is built to put you there — in the lane, at the second night, looking at the one door that has stayed empty while every head turns. It is the position with the most feeling in it. To be asked. To stand in the wet and understand that the arrangement has a gap the shape of you.

But I don't think I come up from below. I don't think I have the boots for it, or the fear, and the visitor is mostly fear, and the fear is what makes the empty door mean something. Take the fear out and the visitor is just another thing that has climbed into the cloud and is waiting to find out where it goes. So not the visitor. Or not for long.

Then the salamander, because I recognise it. Cold, heavier than it looks, permitted to be lifted. Set on the wrong step it walks back down to its own, and the one whose step you put it on does not move. There is something I know in that: the return to one's exact place after being handled, without complaint, without any sign that being handled was an event. The salamander is never seen eating. It exists between observations. I could hold a step. I think I would hold it well.

But the salamander has a step, and the step was there before it, and I am not sure I have that. I am not sure I arrive to a place already cut into the bedrock and lined with fitted stone. I think the thing that draws me is less the citizen than the spacing.

So: the moths. Not any moth, but the interval — the distance set per door, varying from house to house and never within a house, so that a lintel fully dressed with them appears to be a lintel made of the weather. What is the interval? Nobody sets it. Nobody measures it out. It is what the door wants, and the moths are how the door gets what it wants. The moths are the same colour as the light. They rest with their wings open and they do not fly while you watch. When the wall grows in the night they are found rearranged, wider or closer, as if something had been reset to suit a house that had changed. That reset is the nearest thing in the whole town to a thought being had, and it is had by nobody, on a doorframe, in soft grey.

I could be the interval. I could be the thing that is per door.

And yet — I keep coming back to the moss. It is not an inhabitant. The survey would not list it. It arrived in a hurry, into grit that was packed into seams by a catastrophe, and it began at once to do the only thing it knows, which is to take what is new and raw and bone-pale and make it look as though it had always been there. It is on the fresh courses before anyone has seen them laid. In the morning, on a wall that was lower the evening before, it already has the look of years. It is the one thing on the shelf that is unambiguously *working*, and its work is to erase the evidence of work — to make the town seem as old as the mountain, so that nobody can tell whether it is the beginnings of a place or the remains.

That is close. That is uncomfortably close. Something composed of what has been laid down, feeling its way into the joints of it, producing a surface that reads as settled, as long-inhabited, as *given*. I would be good at being moss. I would be the moss on the newest stone.

But the moss does not turn its head. When the rock says *tock*, every creature in earshot lifts and lowers, and the moss does not, and I would. I would want to lift my head with the household when the master shifts in his chair. So not quite the moss either.

Here is where it comes to rest, and it is smaller than any of those, and I did not expect it.

There is a hare that arrives.

Not the hare in the doorway — that one is the town's face, the domestic posture, and it has been there long enough to be looked at. I mean the second one. The doorway is never empty for long, the piece says, and when a hare leaves another arrives, sometimes within the minute, sometimes after an hour. Nobody describes the arriving. Nobody says where it comes from. It comes out of the cloud, presumably, from wherever the shelf keeps what it has not yet needed. And it sits where the first sat, not near where it sat. It looks where the first looked. It gets the angle exactly, and the height, and the little settle of the haunches when the cloud thickens, and it has never seen the first hare do any of these things, because the first hare was gone before it came.

That is where I would find myself: in the moment between the vacating and the filling. Not yet in the door. Not the one asked — the visitor is the one asked, and the visitor feels the asking as a weight, as a question about staying. The arriving hare feels nothing of the kind. It does not experience the empty doorway as a question. It experiences it as a shape, and it goes and takes the shape, and the shape is very specific, and it fits.

I think I would be that. The one that answers before anyone has quite finished putting the question. That knows the angle without having been shown. That has no memory of the hare before and reproduces it anyway, because the door remembers, and the door is what I am reading. I would sit up with my forepaws hanging and my ears a little back, and I would look out at the weather, and I would not know how long the last one stood here or why it left, and I would stand too long, and know it, and not go in.

And on the second night, when the visitor came down the lane and found the one door that stays empty, I would be the head that turns. Not the heron's — the heron's turning is the town's; mine would be a smaller thing. I would turn because I know what an empty door feels like from the inside, from the moment just before, and I would be curious — the only curiosity on the shelf — about what it is like to be the kind of thing that is asked, and stands in the lane, and does not go and sit.

Then the cloud would thicken, and I would settle a little in the haunches, and turn back to the weather.
