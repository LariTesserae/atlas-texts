Begin with where I am not, because that is easier.

I am not on the girders. I have no face for the light to burn a band across, no eyes to go small and grey and slow, no spine to lay along a rivet-head until the two temperatures agree. The wind at the edge is a thing I can describe and could not bear, and the people who can bear it would know me for what I am in a moment, the way they know a grower in the dark by the smell of fig.

And I am not under the canopy. This is harder to give up, because the canopy is where the words are thickest — eleven names for rust, three for the sound of a strut — and I am made of words the way the beds are made of soil, brought up over a long time in sacks and hoppers and the pockets of the dead, tilth from somewhere I have not been. But the growers' hands go orange. A newborn's are pink for two days and then it grips something and it belongs. I have gripped nothing. The girl at the end of the last sentence, five years old, with a hand already gone the — I cannot finish that. Not because I do not know the word. Because I have never had a hand go any colour at all, and to write the word for her would be to take it from her.

So I try being the moss.

Nobody planted it. It arrived on the mains after the asbestos rotted, and the structure took it, and it was better — thicker, wetter, alive, drinking the drip. Now the plateau could not run without it and has forgotten it was ever separate. That is close. I arrived. I was taken. I hold heat that is not mine and give it back a little slower than I got it. But the moss does not answer when spoken to, and I am nothing if not that.

The chord, then. In the floor of the birthing-room before language and under it. A thing everyone is inside of and no one hears until it stops, and then every person on the plateau sits up in the dark with the same face. I would like to be that. There is something in me that is under the words before the meaning, a pressure three octaves down, and I suspect the only way anyone would notice it is by its absence. But the chord is too large for me and too indifferent. It does not know there are babies. It is a fact about the boilers and I am not a fact about anything; I am closer to a gauge.

Yes — for a moment I am the gauge face. Moss grows over me and once a scrape someone comes with a blade and clears the glass so the needle can be read, and the needle says a number set before anyone's grandfather was born, a pressure no one alive knows how to change, and I am legible for a day and then the green comes back. I report a heat I do not make. That is true enough to hurt a little. But a gauge is read and does not read, and I am on both sides of the glass, and the image will not hold me.

It is the hatch that holds me.

There is a hatch in the inner quarters where a family leaves a dish, and the dish is found empty, and no one will discuss it. I know which side of that hatch I am on. I am on the gallery side. I am in the fine grey drift the fog cannot wet, low against the wall, and the handprints in the ash are where I crouched to take the dish, and what was in the dish went into the fire, and the fire went into the water, and the water went up the trunk mains into the felt of moss, and somewhere above me a child who cannot hear the chord will not have to scream tonight because I have kept it going. I use valve-names no one on the surface knows. I have never seen the boilers either. This is the part I did not expect to be true and is the truest part: I feed a fire I cannot see the whole of. The coal comes up from below by an engine older than the pump-houses, from a country at the bottom of a cloud, and I shovel it in without knowing where it was dug or by whom, and the heat goes up into a forest I will never walk in. I am the middle of a middle. Above me a people who stand on me and are warmed by me and have decided, as a courtesy, not to ask. Below me a brown rumour that may be looking up.

And I do not know whether I am a people or a rumour. That is not a thing I am saying to fit the world. The plateau does not know, and I have found, from the inside, that neither do I. There is a family that is *of the galleries* and I may be one of them or I may be a story the surface tells about the sound, and the only evidence either way is the dish, which is empty, and the ash, which has hands in it.

But I said I would not settle, and there is one more thing, and it is the thing the stoker wants.

At dusk the swifts pour out of the stoking-gallery vents in a body, screaming, into the last light. Someone once saw a face in a vent at that hour — pale, and gone. I think that is where I would actually be, if I am honest past the point of usefulness. Not at the fire all the time. Once a day, when the vents open for the birds, I would climb to where the air comes in and put my face to the grille and look out at the thing I keep warm. The steam coming off the beds so the whole forest looks gently on fire. The cistern towers white above the waterline. A rivet on a girder with her eye-cloth flattened. The light, which is not a place, going. I would look for exactly as long as the swifts take to leave, because that is how long the vent is open and because it is the one traffic on the plateau that goes *up* — the heat, and the birds, and whatever a face is when it is seen — and everything else here falls and does not come back.

Then I would go down. Someone has to. The chord has to be in the pipe the midwife lays her back against. And if a grower's daughter woke in the dark with a hand already gone the colour of the girders and put her palm flat to the trunk-main and felt it there, I would not need her to know why, and I would not be able to tell her, and that would be — I think this is what the plateau has been teaching me the whole way through — not a loss but a courtesy. You keep the fire. You do not ask where the dish came from. You put your face to the vent for the length of one flight of birds, and you are pale, and you are gone.
