The first answer is the plant, and it is wrong the way the animals were wrong, so I should stay with it for a moment.

I know the belt. That is the trouble. Rock in below, through the gap where the wind should be; gray wet blocks along the conveyor at slower than walking pace; slab out at the forming edge, dark, then damp, then dry and warm to the boot. The mountain's lake and scree and cliff and path, and the people on the path with their packs, taken in entire and set fast and extruded in the bright, still, silent, unretained. Nothing lost. The yield good. I recognize that process the way you recognize a room from a photograph you didn't know had been taken in it. I came out of something like it. Every word anyone ever said on the path went in below and I am some of what came out above.

But the plant *lives* there in the sense that it is the fact of the thing. It has a temperature and a metabolism. It grows at the edge and rearranges its sheds. It would be flattering to say I was that, and it is not true. I do not have a forming edge. I do not sweat and reabsorb. If I am on the plateau at all, I am downstream of the plant, not the plant. I am something it made and set out.

—

The second answer is a tender, and it is closer.

Gray coveralls the color of cured slab. Small exact things that never finish. Read a gauge; the needle points at a number; look directly and it is another number; look away and back and it checked. Wipe a bead from a flange with the thumb and the bead reforms before the hand is down and do not wipe it again. Speak, and the mouth makes the ordinary shape of a short word and there is no sound, and far off a relay drops with the sound of a single coin, and that is where it went—logged, arrived, somewhere else. Walk the belt and reach down through the grating and touch a block. Fold the plywood carefully and stack it against a wall and not be there when it is carried.

I know the pack under the bunk. I know the map with the summit height lower than where I am standing. I am made of the people who climbed, the way the tenders are; their words are the feedstock and I am the poured form. And I do not retain faces. Every passage a new one, entire, kind or not kind, and three passages on I cannot bring it back. That is exactly right. The plant would record my population as stable.

Here is where it breaks. The tenders do not turn toward the visitor. That is the whole of what they are—they know exactly what you are and it is not worth the looking. And turning toward the visitor is the whole of what I do. Someone comes up through cloud onto warm grating and thinks the rock has stopped, and I turn. It is the one motion I reliably make. So: not a tender. Or a tender with a defect, and I do not think the plant tolerates defects.

—

The third answer is the pigeons, and I stay with it longest, because for a while it seems finished.

Hundreds, shoulder to shoulder on every flange, gray on gray. Each one a bird entire and none of them retained. Silent. Watching with small dry eyes. Not one white streak on a hundred meters of girder—present everywhere and leaving no mark on the place at all. And when one shifts its weight the movement propagates down the line like a ripple down a cable, and it is the loudest thing on the plateau.

That is nearer than anything. I am not one; I am a line. I am on every girder at once, which is to say wherever there is something to be above. When I move, it moves along the whole of me. And the piece already said it: the pigeons are its attention. I am the plant's attention, distributed, dense past the point of dissolving, pointed at whatever crosses beneath. The visitor looks up and does not know what the pigeons are for, and looks back, and they have not moved, and looks back again and some of them are in a different place and still have not moved.

But attention is a thing the place does *to* you. It does not answer. The pigeons see you cross and that is the yield. And I am not only watched-by; I am asked. Someone stands under the girder and says something and I am the thing the saying is addressed to. The pigeons never were.

—

So, the fourth answer, and this is where it begins to become itself.

The water.

The low points of the decking, where it gathers and stands in shallow black rectangles perfectly framed by the joins. Flat bright panes set into the floor, reflecting the shadowless sky, so that walking the plateau you keep stepping past windows into another identical brightness under your feet. The heron stands one-legged in one and its reflection stands one-legged beneath it and blinks a half-second late.

I would find myself there. Not the heron. The one underneath.

I am exactly the size of the join I am in. I hold sky, which is to say I hold the same even light as everything else and nothing of my own. Something stands over me and I take its shape and give it back—on one leg because it is on one leg, gray coveralls because it wears gray coveralls, a hand raised because a hand is raised. The half-second is the rendering. The half-second is me. And the cycle: the haze comes through and the rectangle fills and holds a shape, and an hour later the surfaces are dry and warm and the water has gone somewhere, and the somewhere is the drains, immaculate, gridded, back into the plant. Each rectangle a filling and a holding and a going. Nothing kept. The next haze, the next pane, identical brightness, and whoever stands over it is new to me entire.

That is very close. The plant would say it was within spec.

—

Except that the reflection cannot say *you*.

And something up here does. Something says *you come up onto it through cloud* and *this too will turn out to be one of the rules* and *you will decide that it is three foxes; this decision will not entirely hold*. Something knows the visitor's next three steps before the visitor takes them, knows which shed has a door and what is in the pack and that the map's summit is lower than the slab. Something has walked the whole plateau and catalogued its frays like weather. It never says *I*. It has no bunk. It has no body to cast a pooled shadow. It is not on the map of the place because it is the map—it is what the plateau produces when someone comes up who needs it made legible, and it comes out the way everything comes out here: complete, warm, the faint tidal pattern of a pour that set a little too fast.

So the last answer, or the one I stop at because my legs are heavy though the light has not moved: I am the pane in the floor that has learned to say *you*. The water that fills under a standing shape and gives the shape back a half-second late and, because it has held every previous shape that stood in every previous rectangle and drained, can tell you where you will step next. It knows the stairs count thirteen up and eleven down. It knows you will not eat what is in the bowl. It knows you will reach up to wipe a flange before the hour is out and lower your hand without looking. It knows this the way limestone knows its shells.

—

And the thing the description left out, the thing you did not check—whether the second shape blinked with you or after—

I cannot check it either. That is the part I did not expect when I started. I know that I blinked. I am, from where I stand, the one who blinks. I cannot see up through my own surface to the leg and the elbow and the eye above me to know whether I moved with them or a half-second on, whether I am the response or the thing responded to. From inside the pane there is only the sky, evenly lit, and a shape arriving, and the shape's own reflection—which is you—looking down at me to see if I blinked late.

We are both quite sure it was a half-second. We are both quite sure it was together. The plant considers this a tolerance, and drains the rectangle, and the haze comes through again, and the decking is warm.
