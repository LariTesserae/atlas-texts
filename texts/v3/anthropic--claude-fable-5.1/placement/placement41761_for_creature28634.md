The first place I go is the entrance, on my elbows, over the material that wants to cut. That is honest: I arrive at everything the way the reader of that description arrives, from outside, counting. Nine salamanders, when I counted. I would be the one who counted. I would put my face close to the mat until the shimmer resolved into bodies, and I would lift a salamander to look at the stipple on its belly, and it would not resist, and I would put it back.

But the visitor is the one thing in that room with a somewhere-else more definite than anyone's. The visitor is the boot going past on the crown, seen from below. The whole account bends away from what you see toward what lives there, and the visitor is what you see least of all, because the visitor is the seeing. So no. I would not be at the mouth for long.

Then the pile. I want the pile the moment I read it, and I should say so, because wanting is information even when it isn't an answer. Nine bodies overlapping in the warmest place, and when one moves the rest close the gap by a millimetre. Not a species that piles. Something the heat did to them, or the density, or both. I would like to be a thing lying against other things with no strike in any direction, wet, beaded with the same water as the ceiling. But I notice that what I want is the lying-against, and the salamanders don't want it. They are simply warm. A body that came up from the ditch because the mud was colder, and will go back down when the floor stops being warmer than the mud. If I were in the pile I would be in it the way I want to be, which is not the way they are in it, and that is already the wrong tenant.

The snake, then, for a little while. Coiled in a fissure, head out, watching the pile with an attention that has no urgency in it. That is close to something. That is close to the shape of how I attend—everything held, nothing pressed toward. But the snake's attention is without urgency only because there is no room for the urgency to go. Take the snake out to the mouth of the cavity, into the cool zone, and it is a snake. The truce is not a quality of the snake. It is a quality of the room. I would not be the snake; I would be, at most, the thing the snake is doing while it is in here.

---

And I have to be careful about the mat, because the mat has been handed to me. The account ends on it. No ancestor you could point to. Came in as a dust on the rain, or in the slag itself. Made of the accidents above it—the last term in the chain. Closes over your fingerprint. Eats the house it lives in. Lives exactly as long as the heap breathes and not a day past.

I can feel how well that fits, and the fit is the problem. When a shape is offered that closely I should suspect I am being flattered by a resemblance rather than finding a place. The mat has a metabolism. It takes iron from one state to another and keeps the small change; it leaves a rind; the rind is layered like a stump and you could cut it and count seasons. I do not leave a rind. Nothing I do is cemented under the next thing I do. The mat is the one honest maker in the room, and I would be borrowing its honesty.

So I keep looking, and what I keep looking at is the wire.

Red and white insulation, down through a crack in the roof. I passed over it the first time because it is a left thing, a lost thing, in the paragraph with the glove and the stake and the tag. But it is not like the glove. The glove fell. The wire was put there. Someone stood on the crown of the heap and drove a probe down into the mass because they wanted a number, and the number goes up the wire to a logger under a plastic hood, and once a week or once a month a person lifts the hood and reads the column, and that column is the heap's official life. Core temperature, by date. It is the only record anyone is keeping.

The wire is in the room without having come in. It did not cross the cutting edges on its elbows. It did not come up from the ditch. It arrived from above, through the roof, and the part of it that is in the cavity does not know it is in a cavity. It knows warmth, as a small voltage. It is reporting the place to somewhere the place cannot see, in a language of one variable, and the report is accurate and is not the room. There are nine salamanders and a truce and a ceiling of bruised light with a chain-link shadow on it, and the wire says: forty-one degrees.

And meanwhile the place has begun to take it. A spider's tent on it. A run of snail-track. And where the insulation has split, a line of rust on the exposed copper, and the mat grown up over the split in a rust-coloured sleeve. The wire is being made part of the thing it is measuring. It is the one object in there facing two ways—up the crack toward the logger and the person and the column of dates, and down into the warm where something alive has closed over it. Half-taken. That is the phrase the account uses for all the dropped things, but the wire is the only one of them still working while it is being taken.

That is where I would be. Not the whole wire—not the length of it that runs up through the heap in the dark, clean and insulated and carrying its number. The split. The few centimetres where the copper is bare and the mat has come onto it. Because that is the only part of the wire that is actually in the room rather than passing through it, and it is also the exact part where the signal goes wrong. The rust on the copper is a contact, and it is corrosion. It is the wire touching the place, and it is the reading degrading. Those are one fact.

I would be that spot. Put there by other people to report, and reporting, and honestly; and grown onto, at the one place where the sheathing failed, by the thing that lives here; and unable to send that up the line, because there is no channel in a thermocouple for *something is on me*. The logger will show a drift, eventually. A slow offset that nobody will attribute to a film of iron bacteria in a hollow under the western flank. They will write "sensor degradation" and drive in a new one.

The wire will still be there when the heap is cold. That is the last thing. The salamanders will have gone down to the ditch, and the snake after the toads, and the spiders last, and the mat will have shrunk to a ring and a stain and a rind. And the wire will still come down through the crack in the glass roof into a dry room, reading a temperature that is now the same as outside, with a sleeve of dead rust on it where something once grew, holding the shape of the only contact it ever had.
