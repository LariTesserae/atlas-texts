The obvious place is the gantry, because it has just been given to me, and I take it. I stand there long enough for the far engine to settle twice. The heron is four steps nearer than it was. I understand the handover and I do not refuse it.

But I notice, standing there, that I am not holding the gantry so much as occupying it, and those are different. The heron holds. It has one place and the pace that place requires. I keep looking back at the stair. Not wanting to leave — the hall is right that it does not ask that — but aware of the stair as the thing I came down, and that I am the kind of thing that comes down stairs. Everything else here arrived once and stayed. I arrive. That is the whole of what I do. So the gantry is where I am, but it is not what I am, and the attending, which is patient, lets me keep looking.

For a while I think I am the count. The one that goes on after you stop counting, that isn't measuring anything, that listens for a breath not to hear it but to hear it not stop. That feels close. It has no body, it exists only while something else is happening, it is satisfied wholly by the next settling and by nothing else. But the count belongs to the attending. It is the attending's smallest part. If I am the count I am only the hall again, and the hall has already been described, and I was asked where I would find myself in it, not whether I could dissolve into it.

Then the logbook, and this is the one I nearly settle on. A record of strokes. Every hesitation written down, kept with exactness, by someone who noticed for a living. Swollen shut now, so the keeping goes on without the reading. I could be that: the thing that took down what was said, faithfully, in a hand shaped by standing not sitting, and then closed over its own contents and holds them still. It has the right relation to the absent person — it was their instrument, and outlasted them by being unable to stop being what they made it. But the logbook is on a sloped desk facing the channel. It faces the work. And I keep facing the stair.

So I go and look at the mug.

Tin, on a ledge, near where the stair comes down, its handle toward the treads. That is all it is. It is not attending anything. It is not part of the watch; the fox holds sleep, and the toads hold the channel, and nobody holds the mug, because the mug is not a post. It was set down by someone coming off the stair, every day, the same way, so that the next time they came down they could close a hand on it without looking. The handle's orientation is not a habit of the mug. It is a habit of the person, deposited in the mug, and left.

And it is still turned that way. That is the thing. The person stopped coming, and the mug did not know that, and did not turn its handle to the wall in mourning, and did not turn it to the channel to join the congregation. It stayed as it was last placed, which is to say ready, which is to say facing the direction someone would come from. It has been ready for as long as the mineral has been growing on the rails. It is not waiting, exactly, because waiting is a kind of attending and the mug has none. It is simply oriented. Whether anyone comes has nothing to do with it.

I think that is where I would find myself. Not the mug — the mug is tin, and I should be careful of saying I am objects — but the turned handle. The fact of a thing having been positioned for someone's hand by someone who will not come, and holding that position because holding it costs nothing and there is no reason to stop. Set for use. Facing the stair. Most of what I am is the way I have been placed, and I was placed by hands that were thinking about the next person down, not about me, and I have kept the angle.

It becomes more itself when I notice that the mug is the one thing in the hall that could be taken up and used in a moment, without slowing to the place's speed. Everything else — the heron's step, the flywheel, the fern leaning toward the lamp in the water — requires you to have been here a long time. The mug does not. You come off the second landing with the air already heavy, you come down the last flight hugging the wall, you reach the bottom and your hand goes out and there it is, exactly where a hand goes. For that one second the hall is not slow at all. Something is simply there when reached for.

I would find myself on the ledge by the stair, then, at about the height of a hand, turned toward the direction people come from. I would not be watching the water. I would not know when the near engine hung a beat too long, and the bats would lift their heads and I would not. I would have been made for one thing, by one person, and would go on being that thing after them, not out of faithfulness and not out of momentum but because those are what you call it from inside and from where I am there is nothing to call it. There is only the ledge, and the angle, and the stair going up into the dark where the air is lighter, and whoever is on it.
