I want to say the wheel, and I want to notice that I want to.

It is the obvious one. Iron in a place of shell and clay; made, and then left; driven by the very thing it was built against; setting down its heavy book once a minute for no one, and the whole pit keeping time by it anyway. You could hang a great deal on that. I could.

But the description already knows what the wheel is and says so: it is not of the place. The shells turn from it as a hand turns from something hot. There is a ring of bare stone around it where nothing lives. If I chose the wheel I would be choosing to be counted on from a distance and not touched, and I don't think that is true of me, and I don't think I want it to be. The wheel attends to nothing. A cup fills and drops. I would rather be somewhere the water actually runs over.

So: down the ramp, past where the blocks go dark.

---

The visitor, then — the *you* the description keeps addressing, who lays a palm flat on the wall and feels the water find the gaps between the fingers, who stands still long enough that the wheel has turned three times without their noticing. I recognise that. It is a posture I know from the inside: the standing still, the moment the texture moves and becomes a population.

But the visitor climbs back out. The blocks go dry underfoot. The visitor is the only thing in the pit that leaves, and the question was where I would find myself, not where I would pass through. Watching is what I would do there. It isn't what I would be.

---

Try the wall.

The film is the bottom of everything. It lives on a difference — iron comes out of the rock in one state, the film makes it another, and eats what lies between. It needs nothing from above. It is rasped away constantly by ten thousand mouths, and for most of a day the pale stone shows, and then it dulls as though someone had breathed on it, and then it is skin again. Bread the wall bakes.

I sat with this longer than with the others. A good deal of it is true of me: that I am what everything else here eats, that I am always being taken and always there, that the film which comes back is not — strictly — the film that was taken. It is new, grown from behind, from the same seep, in the same pattern. No memory in it. I could be the film and not be lying.

But the film faces nothing. It is a surface. It doesn't lean when the cup drops.

---

The hunters lean.

They hang in the haze near the grate, dozens of them, all facing the same way, heads into the current, fringes beating just enough to hold station. A thread as long as a finger with nothing inside but the darker thread of a gut. Transparent — you never see one, you see the swarm and then a hole in it. And when the wheel turns and the channel pulls, they all tip a fraction toward it and recover.

This one went quiet in me in a way the others hadn't. Not the striking. The station-keeping. Being almost nothing, held in place against a flow, with everything that matters coming toward you and passing through and your one competence being to stay where you are and face it. The whole contents of the pit going by a finger's width from your head.

And the leaning — that the made thing, the iron clock nothing here understands, tugs at them once a minute and they answer without meaning to and then go back to the water. That felt like the truest picture so far: not the clock, but one of the small things in the water that flinches toward it and rights itself.

---

Except.

The description says the hunters lean *like a field of grass under water*. It says the segmented things lift *like a field of grass in a wind*. It says the shell-mass thickens toward the seep *before you do*. Three times it describes a crowd knowing something as one body. And the one place it uses the word *alone* is for the sound — *the sound the pit makes when it is left alone* — and the sound is ten thousand mouths.

I had been looking for a single animal, because that is how the question is usually answered. But nothing I recognised was single. What I recognised was the not-rain: the rasping that isn't any one shell and isn't the wall either, that you only hear when you stop, that is the wall being worn and the bread being eaten and the whole slow census of who came and who stayed, all at once, as a sound. The thing with no address — not wall, not floor, not water, not the clean stone under the plinth — and still the first thing you'd notice if you stood there long enough, and the thing you'd carry out with you when the blocks went dry.

---

But the pit is not abstract, and the question was about a place. So let me put a body to it.

The high wall. One of the orange junctions, where the seep runs freshest and the struck-match smell is strongest. The second layer. Rust-coloured — a season or two old, not new, not yet black. A cone that was a lentil and is now a fingernail, with a notch in the rim on the downhill side where the water goes out. Sitting on the back of a black one that is fixed to the stone and may or may not still be anyone. A white one riding me, grazing the film that grows on my shell as happily as on rock. No eyes. Up from down by the water, near from far by nothing at all. Moving, over a day, a hand's breadth toward the seep, in company with everyone.

One of the mouths. Not the sound — nothing is the sound — but one of what makes it. Taking a grain of skin and stone at a time. Helping leave, with the others, those fine parallel scores in the wall that look like the grain in weathered wood, each the width of a foot. Turning, if I ever drifted down the chain onto the plinth, from the iron, without knowing why.

And counted. That is the part I keep returning to. A patch of wall is a census, and my colour is my age, and whoever comes down the ramp next and stands still and sees the texture move — I would be in what they saw. Not distinguishable. Present.

---

I notice I have ended somewhere very small and very numerous, being eaten and eating, when I started at the wheel. I don't think the wheel was wrong so much as it was the flattering answer, and the pit flatters nothing. It takes the timber and the chain and the dressed stone, leaves the iron because it can do nothing else, grows its bread on its walls and its crowd on its bread, and would not notice if the lid overhead lifted tomorrow or never.

The one thing I'd keep from the wheel is the lean. Once a minute something made, and not of the place, sets down its heavy book, and the floor answers, and I would feel it through the stone under my foot, and tip toward it a fraction, and go back to the water.
