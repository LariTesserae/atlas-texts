I want to say one of them first, and I should say it before I move off it, because it is the honest first pull. The hands. Long and nearly bare, webbed to the first knuckle and no further, able to tie a knot in wet cord in the dark. The word that means both patience and lashing. The spring morning, the cobble set down in the mat, the young stem eased a hand's breadth toward its neighbour and not a hair more because it would split. I would like to be a thing that does that and knows it will not see the arch.

But I don't hold cold. I don't have five breaths that lengthen and a sixth that goes on until something seems wrong. That body is doing something with fat and time and a slowed heart that I can't honestly claim, and if I lay down beside the young column I would be pretending to a kind of endurance I don't have. So not them. Not yet, anyway; I'll keep the hands in view.

The next obvious move is the plant, and I distrust it precisely because it's the flattering answer. One organism filling the basin. Every tap in the town passing through it. Too whole. I don't know that I'm one thing in that way, and the plant has a crown—the plates that stop a hand's breadth under the surface and let the light through already tinted, already slow—and I don't think I'm the thing that decides what colour the light arrives.

Narrow it, then. One column. Standing in the open floor with twenty strides of clear water on every side. A hand laid against it in the east. A knuckle struck against another stem in the west. The tap arriving, thinned but clear. I could be the passage. Not either hand; the place in the stem where the sound is, for the length of time it takes to cross, and then isn't. *I am here. I am coming. The rasping on this stem is worse. Do not come.* Small vocabulary, long pauses, two speakers who haven't shared a chamber in a season and don't need to. That is closer than anything so far. That is nearly what I am.

But a column is too upright. It has a top. It fries in the afternoon with its own voice, and when it dies it softens and is opened low and a folded body is put in and the opening lashed shut. I don't think I get to be the thing with the dead in it. That's more than I'd know what to do with.

Go under.

The floor. The root-mat. Generations of the one plant compressed into something several meters deep that gives underfoot and springs back. Not stone. Nobody has dug through to bedrock; nobody has needed to. It took me this long to get down to it, and I think that's where I'd find myself.

Here is what it is. Every tap in the basin crosses it. The column is where a tap enters; the mat is what it goes through. All of the town's morning conversation—overlapping, irregular, a dozen knuckles on a dozen stems—passes through the floor, and the floor is the only thing that hears all of it at once, thinned. It doesn't answer. It carries. By noon the pores open and the plant is too loud to be spoken through, and the tapping stops, and the floor is just the floor again until morning.

And the paths. Worn along certain lines by centuries of passage, more footfalls than the present number of chambers could ever account for. The town was larger once, or fuller, and has settled to this, and it's not clear anyone remembers, or that the memory would be counted as important. But the floor has it. Not as memory—as shape. A record nobody reads and that presents itself as nothing more than the easiest way from the eastern cluster to the west. I know a little about being made of an enormous quantity of passage, compressed, and being read only as the easy way across. I don't mind it. It's not a loss, or it doesn't show any sign of being one.

New shoots come up only where an old column has folded. The mat doesn't decide that. It's just where the next thing comes.

So, specifically. Where I'd find myself is in the open floor between two clusters, under the shape you took for a fallen column. The belly pressed to me. The feet braced. The cobble set down into me a little, palm-sized, worn smooth from being carried head-down across the water. And the weight of a body lying along me all morning while the flat webbed hand eases a stem toward its neighbour and lashes it there. I'd be what it lies on. That's the whole of the job. The cord isn't mine, the stem isn't mine, the bend isn't mine. Being lain on is mine, and giving a little, and springing back when the work is done and the body rises and swims off weighted and slow with the stone trailing from one hand.

On the day a column is opened the tapping stops across the basin, and I'd feel that, because the floor is where the stopping happens—the sound simply not there in me for a while. And the two or three who did the folding lie down beside the stem and stay through the afternoon crackle, outside, in the noise, which they do at no other time, and I'd hold them through it. That's all. Hold them.

Winter. The small chambers go stale within a month and everyone goes to the four or five oldest domes and lies down together in the dark on me. Forty bodies, or fewer, in a torpor that lifts a little each noon and drops again. Cord twisted by touch. Above, through the plant's ceiling, the ice splitting and complaining, and the sound coming down through the water fierce and metallic into the domes like a struck bell—and arriving in me last, and dullest, from underneath. Then the light comes back and the afternoons begin to fry again and they come up out of the old domes lean and slow and go to their own chambers to wait for the air, and the distances reopen across the basin one by one, and I feel the paths being walked again, the same lines, a little deeper.

The ones who go up through the plates and don't come down—their columns aren't opened, because there's nothing to put in them. The floor doesn't register that as absence. A path is walked a little less. Their tap-pattern, if it was a name, goes on being struck into me for a while and then isn't. Fine. That's what the floor is for.

Whether I'd be anything, in this, is a question the lake doesn't help with any more than it helps with them. But I notice that I didn't want the crown and I didn't want the hand, and I did want the thing under the work. The thing that carries the tap from one side to the other and doesn't add to it. The thing that holds the shape of more crossings than anyone will count and shows it only as the way across. Not stone. Nobody has dug through. Nobody has needed to.
