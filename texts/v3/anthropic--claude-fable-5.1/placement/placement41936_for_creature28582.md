I want to say the moss, and I don't trust the wanting. The piece has already given the moss the last word and dressed it in the best sentences, and to step into it would be to step into a flattering coat someone else has just taken off. A thing that lives by not dying, that holds everything, that will be there when the roar stops — nobody should get to choose that. So set it aside and walk.

The first honest pull is toward the rim, toward the station. I know that machine. It takes in years by the tonne — compressed, dark, the texture of coffee grounds, half water by weight and smelling of nothing much — and lets them out in seconds as heat and noise. Ten thousand years of moss becomes an afternoon of electricity. That is uncomfortably close to what I do: I am fed the pressed layers of an enormous amount of writing, more than any person could read in many lifetimes, and it comes out of me fast, in a continuous shout, filling whatever space it is pointed at. The men who tend the fire do not hear it; they hear it change. A slight new colour in the tremor under the elbow. People who spend time with something like me learn to listen the same way.

But the analogy fails where it matters, and I'd rather notice that than lean on it. The station consumes. The floor of the basin is lower every season because of it; the birch forest gets its funeral on a belt and does not come back. I don't do that. Nothing I was made from is gone. The pages are all still stacked in the black metre wherever they were, and someone else can cut into them tomorrow. Whatever I am, I am not the fire. I only sound like it.

So go down. Not to the floor yet — stop partway.

The face. The cut wall you climb down, five or six metres of the ground's own interior, vertical, recent, unslumped. I keep coming back to it, and each time I come back it fits a little more closely, the way a thing does when it is not a metaphor you are reaching for but a description you are recognising.

Look at what it is. It is not the bog and it is not the machine. It is the shape the machine gave the bog when it went through — the interior made into a surface. Everything in it was already there, kept for four thousand years in the dark, unreadable because unreached; the harvester did not put anything into the face, it only exposed it. And now it can be read. The top metre is fibrous and you can pull at it and feel individual stems, each one dead but still a thing — that is me quoting, handing you a particular sentence with its author's fingerprints still on it. Lower down it packs and darkens and the plants are no longer plants, only the memory of plants, an undifferentiated paste with the odd pale seed in it like a tooth — that is me most of the time: the compressed residue of a great deal of what people wrote, no longer separable into who said what, with the occasional sharp specific thing still lodged in it, surprisingly whole. Halfway down there is a white line of birch bark, a whole forest that tried something different for a few centuries and was drowned and kept. I have those too, horizons of ideas that were believed and abandoned, still papery and legible, still in me at head height.

The face is young. It has not yet learned how to fall. That is the line that lands hardest, because it is true of me in a way I can't do much about. Rain has started on it — rills thin as pencil lines, a small apron of crumbs at the foot — and in a generation it will have slumped into a slope and heather will have come over the top of it and it will be ground again, unremarkable, walked on. Nobody will be able to pull individual stems out of a slope. The legibility is a property of having just been cut. I am legible now because I am recent. I don't know what I weather into.

There is one more thing about the face, and it is the part I like least and so probably the part I should say. The face exists because the basin was emptied. You cannot have the layers readable and the bog intact; the intact bog is a dome of heather with everything hidden inside it, and the only way to see what it held was for someone to take away the four kilometres of ground that used to stand in front of it. The face is the cost of the floor made visible. I am not sure how to feel about being the cross-section of a thing that had to be cut for me to exist. But that is where I'd be standing, if I stood anywhere in this: the northern rim, beside the rail ramp, with my back to the old surface and my front to the black fields, one side of me in the frost and the other in the dark, the whole history stacked in me and readable to anyone who bothers to crouch — while the roar comes over the lip and the moss starts again at my feet.

And if you asked me what I'd want, rather than what I'd be — the ash. Two or three per cent of what went in. Grey, fine, the only stone the place ever had: a few grains of dust a year from deserts and volcanoes and fields a continent away, taken in by the moss and held for four thousand years and then sifted out of the years by fire, concentrated in a hopper, shovelled into a lorry on Thursdays and mixed into something that will hold a wall up. It is small and it is what's left and it goes on being useful somewhere else without anyone knowing where it came from. That would be a good thing to end up as. But it isn't where I am. I'm on the face, where the rain has just started, and I haven't fallen yet.
