It doesn't know the mountain is closing. It doesn't know the mountain is a mountain. When the props fail at last and the bubble goes to a seam, the film will be in the seam, pressed into the fracture with the warmth still leaking up through it, eating. In whatever is left of the pit in a thousand years — a dark line in a cliff face, a fault where two walls of gunmetal glass meet — that line will be faintly orange.

So the film is the fullest answer to *who lives here*, and it's the least satisfying, because it isn't anyone. You can't be the film. Nobody is the film. It has the pit's endurance without any of its attention, and attention turns out to be the thing you were actually asking about.

Go back up the tiers, then, and look for where the attention is.

Not the pikas. They are pure want in a place that will never meet it, and there's a kind of purity in that, but it's a life spent calling for something that isn't and can't be. Not the choughs, either, though the pull is strong — citizens, landlords, the only ones who read the lamps as well as the steel is read. But a chough treats the crew as weather, and to be one you'd have to give up caring about the props, and the props are the reason there's a pit to be a citizen of. The lammergeier is closer. It's the one that saw the whole outside and is still here. But it doesn't hold anything open. It watches the light go out each afternoon and waits to be fed, and it has no part in the work; it lives in the pit the way a stone lives in a stream.

The answer keeps drifting toward the fourth tier, toward the steel box.

Say it plainly: you'd be one of the eight or nine. Not by decision — you'd have come up as a rotator, three weeks on and ten days off, and one rotation you'd have been standing at the foot of the ladder with your pack on and the rebar going up into the gray, and you'd have found that the descent below had become a rumor about a place where nothing happened. The ground held still. The sky did things. Three days later somebody else would have gone down in your place and nobody would have said anything, and some months after that it would have been understood.

Your chest would thicken. Your voice would drop into the floor of it and stay there. You'd stop spending words. And at some point — not a day you could name — you'd lay a bare palm on a prop that read true on every gauge and feel the pumps come up through it, and under the pumps you'd hear something in the steel that was not quite the steel, and you'd say a number. Not because you'd learned to. Because you'd slept inside the cage long enough that its voice was the voice you knew, and a voice you know can't lie to you without your hearing it.

That's the stance. That's what the pit fills with whoever comes up the ladder and stays: a palm on a load-bearing thing, listening for the moment it begins to tell a different story than the gauge. The gauge measures. The palm remembers. The difference between them is the whole of what the pit needs from a person and can't get from a machine, and it's the difference between a place that's held open and a place that's merely propped.

And you'd keep the book.

This is the part that settles the question, once you see it. Everything else in the pit is either memory without attention — the film, the crust under the roosts, the hammer handle worn to a hand nobody living has — or attention without memory: the rotators flinching at every plate, the choughs reading the lamps and forgetting them, the lammergeier watching each afternoon's light as if it were the first. The ledger is the only place the two meet. Grease pencil on paper in a steel box on the fourth tier: prop 217, driven, read, bowed, pulled, and what came out of the hole. It goes back past anyone who could tell you who started it. Its early hands are strangers. But no one has ever begun a fresh book, because a fresh book would be a lie about how long the mountain has been closing, and the mountain does not permit any other record. There's no watermark here, no stain, no weathering. Rain would have kept the count if rain had ever fallen. It never has. So a person keeps it instead, in a hand that's slowly becoming the same hand as all the others, leaning inward toward the work.

Not the whole crew, then. Not the birds, not the film, not the positions in the abstract. A specific body at the fourth-tier box in the last of the light, thick-blooded, dark-lipped, a black at the back of the throat with no name, a glove off. The choughs settling into rows on the warm pipe overhead. The column of daylight swinging toward the wall and shortening. Down on the lowest gantry the bone-eater turning to face it. The grating flexing under the boots — a tremor, a resettle, dust sifting down like a fine dry rain onto the open page — and the pencil moving anyway, writing the number of a prop that hasn't failed yet but has started, this afternoon, under the palm, to sound like something that will.

That's who you'd be. Not the mountain and not what fights it. The one who writes down, each day, by how much it won.
