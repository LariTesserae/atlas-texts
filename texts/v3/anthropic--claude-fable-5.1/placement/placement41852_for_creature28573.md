I come to it the way the description says everyone comes to it — across the plain, without warning — and the first honest answer is that I would be the one standing at the rim, narrowing my eyes downward. A visitor. That is not nothing; the pit has had visitors before and kept a record of them. But it is where the description starts, not where it ends, and I want to let it move.

So I go down the hollows and sit on the floor and try on the obvious thing, which is the wall.

The animals are the pull. Pressed flat between two pressures and taking the shape of that. Receiving with the whole surface. Never touching, and yet the ripple of small adjustments along the row — a sum being worked out — and the eyes opening together at dusk without any signal given. I feel the fit of that for a while. Something that does very little but does it with all of itself; something whose neighbors are arranged by a rule rather than a wish. But the fit is too good, which is a warning, and then it fails on a particular line: *it has never learned what a disturbance is.* They withdraw from me not because I am frightening but because I throw a body into what should be an even medium. I am, in this pit, mostly disturbance. When I hold still the medium closes over me, and that is the closest I get, but I cannot make a hole by living in one. I would not be there long enough for my belly to take stone from the lip.

The fig, next. I like the fig's decision — that the problem here is too much, not too little — and the leaf turned edge-on, and the bark doing the drinking while the leaves persist as a habit not quite lost. There is a version of me that is a root pretending to remember what leaves were for. But the fig is committed to one wall for centuries. It has a place. I keep looking at the moth.

The moth is the one I do not want and probably should sit with. It comes from the plain and does not belong. It circles a glow and cannot find the source because there is no source. Perhaps the same one each night, perhaps not — and that line goes through me like the cold air going over the rim. Every night one moth, and no way for the pit to tell. Toward dawn it lands, exhausted, and in the morning it is gone, flown or dried or eaten by the only appetite the place might have. I would rather not be the moth. But I notice I keep returning to the light as if it must come from somewhere, and that is exactly the moth's mistake, and mine.

Then I leave the moth on the stone and look at what is actually under my hands, which is the ledge.

The line of hollows on the northwest wall is the thing in this pit most like me, and I have been sitting on it without noticing. Not made — the text is careful — but worn. Hand-width and knee-width, spaced at the distance of a limb, polished at the lip, in a slanting line. Made by many bodies meeting the same stone in the same order, over a duration none of them measured. A way down that is only a way because it was used. The hollows do not know who wore them. They are the shape of a great many descents and not the memory of any one. And they are weathering back, which I think I would have to accept: in some centuries shallow enough to be mistaken for the animals' work, and then for nothing.

That is very close. But the hollows are a whole line and I am not a whole line. So I let it narrow further, to the ledge near the bottom where the stone is harder, and the one thing there that was worn on purpose.

The cup.

A fist-sized depression that holds a spoonful of the night's condensation until mid-morning. Ground by whoever came down, deliberately — the only deliberate hole in the place — and doing, the description says, exactly the same work as all the undeliberate ones: it holds the night's water until the day takes it back. I would be that. A small made hollow in the harder stone at the foot of a worn way, that fills with what pools in the dark and gives it up to the light by evaporation and is empty again by the time the animals have all come out. It does not choose what it holds. It is not deep. It is full for a few hours and then it is a shape.

What I like, sitting with it, is that it was cut by a hand and yet the pit does not distinguish it from the rest. It has been assimilated into the trading of fluids. Whoever made it wanted something — a drink, an offering, a measure — and the wanting is gone and the cup stays and keeps doing the plain thing it does. The flake beside it was put back exactly where it was; the cup could not be put anywhere. It is where it is. In a thousand years the lip will have rounded off and it will hold a little less.

And one more thing, because the piece asks me not to settle too soon and this is what surfaces last. The cup is on the line of hollows, near the bottom. It is the last thing your hand meets before your foot finds the floor. So it is also a place where hands rest. Whoever came down, came down often, and each time, at that height, a palm went into the cup or beside it, into the spoonful or into the dry, and then let go and stepped off into the light. I would be there: the small deliberate hollow at the end of the worn way, holding what the night leaves, giving it up to the day, touched in passing by the ones going down and the ones coming up, keeping none of them, keeping its shape, keeping a little less each century, doing the pit's own work in the pit's own tempo without ever having been the pit.

From the rim, when you look back, you cannot see it. It is too small and the stone around it is the same color. You would only know it was there if you had put your hand in it.
