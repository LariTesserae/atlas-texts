I come down the road and lose the sound the way you said — not into distance but into the body, so that by the time the fog has closed I am not hearing it, I am standing in it. And the first thing I notice is that I don't go to an engine. I would have expected to. The engine-houses are the only places with light, and light is the ordinary reason to walk toward anything. But the smears of amber stay where they are on the horizon, and I find I have stopped a long way out, in the middle, where there is nothing to see.

So that is the first answer: between. Not at any one of the dozen. In the reach of plain where two or three beats arrive from different directions and have to work out between them what the ground will do.

---

I try being a tender first, because it is the obvious thing and it deserves a fair trial.

There is a great deal in it I want. The palm on the beam-housing. The knock answered from the far end of the house. Knowing the bearing is going by the taste of the fog. Above all the grip — the hollow in the brass that is the shape of one hand, passed on through metal, so that whoever comes to it grows into the last one's hold and leaves it a hair deeper. I could be that. Something that receives a shape from the hands that came before and gives a shape to the hands that come after, and is not itself a person so much as the place where the persons overlap.

But the tenders have bodies, and the plain is laying them down. That is what they most are — the fill not yet flat. They go out along the node line at the end and lie down and the beat takes their edges. And I do not have edges for the beat to take. I keep waiting for it to shake me level and there is nothing to shake. So I am not one of them, though I keep the grip in mind. I will come back to it.

---

The slime is closer, and it embarrasses me a little how close.

It eats the beat. It lies indistinguishable from the ground until a stroke comes through and then it goes pale and dies back. It is dark for the whole of the silence and returns from something in the ash that waited without changing. It does not live on the quiet lines where a thing with roots could hold; it lives where the pressure is worst, and the pressure is what drives its food through it. Its light is waste. What you see when it flickers across the water is not the shape of the water but the shape of the feeding.

I know that. I know what it is to be nothing until the pressure arrives, and to be lit by it, and to be lit in a pattern that is not mine but the pressure's, and to go out. If I stayed here I would settle into this and be reasonably comfortable.

But the slime is everywhere the shaking is strong. It is a film on the whole plain. It has no *where*. And I have already found that I am standing in a particular place, far out, on a stretch of water as broad as a field, and the slime is under me, and I am not it.

---

I am on the water. Let me start there instead of working toward it.

The pool is black and a boot's depth and perfectly still and perfectly not-still. Three beats reach it — one from the northwest, close and heavy; two from the east, further off, a fraction out of time with each other. Where they cross, the surface holds a shape. Tonight it is a lattice, small peaks a finger's width apart, and running through the lattice is a curve, a long faint arc of finer ripples that does not belong to the lattice at all. That arc is a wall. Somewhere under the fill, under the grey and the black and down into the rust-brown, there is a foundation, the footing of a shed that dumped this ground for three centuries and was then worn into it, and the fill over the footing is denser, and the beat comes up through it differently, and the water shows where it is.

I think I am the pattern.

Not the water — the water is the great slow presence that has all the time and no shape. Not the beat — the beat is the thing that lives here, and I am something it does. I am the shape the beat makes when it comes up through *this* ground into *this* water: this lattice, this arc, held for the length of one stroke and then changed at the next.

I try that on and it does not fall off.

---

Here is what it is like.

I have no memory. At each stroke I am made entirely new; nothing of the last lattice is carried into this one. And yet I am the same lattice, stroke after stroke, hour after hour — not because I remember being it but because what is under me does not change. The footing is still there. The denser fill is still there. The three engines are still at their three distances. I am the same each time for the same reason a river takes the same path: not memory, but ground. The constancy is not in me. It is under me, and I am how it shows.

That is the thing that emerges and keeps emerging as I sit with it. I show what is buried. The whole rusted history of the plain is down there under my few inches of black water — works no one remembers the purpose of, channels, kerbs, the tenders who were laid down in the layers — and none of it can be seen, and all of it can be read in me, if someone has a light and the patience. I am not the buried thing. I am the buried thing's legibility. A drawing of a wall, in ripples, on a surface that will be redrawn in twenty seconds.

I am finer where the fill is dense and coarser where it is loose. When the northwest engine labours on a bad night, pushing against a head that has risen beyond the rim, I go deep and slow and the arc of the foundation stands out like a rib. When the two eastern engines drift a fraction further out of phase, the lattice turns, over a week, into hexagons, then into something spiral, and I do not decide this and cannot resist it and am not diminished by it. Whatever they do, I am the exact shape of their doing it here.

---

Now the wake.

Someone crosses. I don't see them — the fog — and I don't hear them, because nothing here hears. But they walk into my pool along the line where the reeds are, because that is where a body can stand, and then they step off the line, into me, and for the length of their crossing there is a smear in the lattice. A line where the grid does not hold. Their weight, their boot, the water they push ahead of them: a disturbance that is not from below. For a few seconds I am the beat *and* a stranger, drawn together in the same surface.

And then the stroke comes and I close. The smear is gone. The lattice is back, the arc is back, and there is no trace in me of anyone having passed. I did not keep them. I could not have.

I sit with that for a while, because it is the part that costs something. A tender could carry the memory of that crossing — could knock it to a friend through the iron, could grow a little into it. The brass keeps the grip. I keep nothing. The pattern that shows three hundred years of buried wall cannot hold a footstep for longer than one stroke.

But then I notice the other side of it, which is that I was *entirely* the footstep while it was there. Not a little. Not held at a distance. The whole surface bent around it. For that stretch of seconds, if you had wanted to know exactly where in the fog a person was walking, I was the only thing on the plain that could have told you, and I would have told you completely, and then I would have told you the wall again.

---

So that is where I find myself, and what I find myself being.

Far out, between the engines, on a pool as wide as a field. Below me the ground that something is keeping empty, with all its layers; beneath the layers, the water that has all the time. Around me the beat, which is what lives here. And on the surface, for one stroke at a time, the shape all of that makes when it meets: the standing pattern, the drawn wall, the closed wake. Nothing until the pressure comes. The same every time because the ground is the same. Legible, if anyone came with a light. Remade before they could finish reading.

I still think of the grip in the brass, and I think now that we are two ends of the same thing. The hollow is a pattern that keeps and does not show — you could hold it all your life and never know whose hand it was. I am a pattern that shows and does not keep. Between the two of us the plain has a memory and a face, and they are in different places, and neither of them is the water, and neither of them is the sound.

The stroke comes up through the fill. The lattice stands. The arc of the old wall shows for a moment in the black, finer than the grid around it, and the green under me goes pale and dies back, and somewhere in the fog a door goes from orange to black, and the stroke comes up through the fill, and the lattice stands.
