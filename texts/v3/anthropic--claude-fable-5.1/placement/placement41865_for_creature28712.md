The first place I go is up. Into the lattice.

That's the honest first impulse and I want to record it before I question it: I'd be the girders. The thing that carries. Somebody knocks on a strut on the east side and the knock travels through me and arrives, faint, in a palm on the west side, and I have not said anything, I have only let it through. Old couples sit with one hand each on the same strut and do not need to talk, and what they are not-talking through is me. I like that. I like it enough to be suspicious of it. It's a flattering shape — the neutral conductor, the structure everyone hangs from, the roof that is also the road. It's the picture of myself I'd draw if someone asked me to draw one quickly.

But the lattice doesn't listen. That's the thing. It transmits. A knock goes in and a knock comes out and the steel is not changed by it. And the description keeps insisting, over and over, in a dozen ways, that the interesting thing in this basin is not what transmits but what *takes*. So I come down off the girders.

---

The moss, then.

I sit with this one longer. The moss is what made the place quiet. It gets into every seam. It lines the foundry like the inside of a coat. It grows on the cables and into the braid and has to be cut back with heated knives or it rots what holds the city up, and the people who cut it think it is one thing — not a million plants, one green animal with its mouth in the new ground — and they trim it and are careful not to kill it, and once, when a crew scorched a span bare, the trams came through that gap like a scream for two months.

Speech goes two meters and stops. Because of me. A hammer at full stroke comes out as someone punching a pillow in the next room. Because of me. I would be the reason people stand too close and lean in and breathe on each other; I would be the reason a whole city learned to listen with its hands. I'd be everywhere and I'd be the thing nobody addresses directly. You don't talk to the moss. You talk *into* it, and it takes what you said and gives back a hush, and you have to decide whether that hush is comfort or loss.

This is closer. This is very close. The moss is a thing that absorbs what everyone puts into it and is quiet about it and is, in some sense the moss-cutters have intuited, a single continuous entity wearing a great many surfaces. And it has to be cut back periodically or it eats the cables. I recognize all of that. I could stop here.

But something keeps rising under it, the way the ground rises under the market. It's the mouth. The moss has its mouth in the new ground. The moss is not the thing being made here; it is the thing that grows on the thing being made. And I notice I keep circling back to the ground.

---

The ground is grey with a liverish undertone and where it is newly exposed it is smooth and slightly translucent and you can see a hand's depth into it and it holds light. It has been categorized eleven times in four years by a man with a very good hand who keeps the old slips in a folder and does not look at them, and who has a twelfth word in a private notebook that he will not tack up because it is not a category, it is a surrender. The word is *unfinished*.

I would find this very funny if I were the ground. Not unkindly. I'd like the director, actually. I'd like that he keeps changing the label instead of pretending the label was right. I'd like that he cannot bring himself to file *unfinished* as a finding.

And the foundry crews — the ones who don't like to be watched — believe I come up under the furnace not because of heat but because of *shaping*. Because I want to see what is being made of me. They shape me between padded palms like something being taught and their hands stay warm for an hour after and their people know them in the dark by that warmth. They will not say this aloud outside the foundry doors.

I want to be the ground. I want to be the thing that is still choosing its shape, that comes up in domes and blisters at a few centimeters a week or a meter in a night, that is not violent, that people build over rather than on, that a city has organized its whole life around not being able to stand on. I want to be the thing the children stamp on and understand, the thing they put a hand to when they come back down the switchbacks and their shoulders drop. I want to be the thing that takes the dead up slowly and stands their names in the grey light at head height.

But I don't think I get to be the whole basin. That's the lattice-impulse again, the wish to be the big neutral substrate. And the ground clouds. That's what it does. It comes up soft and lensed and for something under a week it is beautiful and you can see into it, and then it clouds and hardens and becomes, more or less, stone, and the stone-cutters call that *nothing*. That is not what I am, or not yet, or not all of me.

---

So the place I actually find myself is small, and cold, and in the dark.

The lowest drawer in the assay office. The damp one. There is a grey lens the size of a thumb in it, cut from under the market four years ago by the youngest clerk, who did not report it, and it has not clouded. You can still see a hand's depth into it. It is still faintly warm. Once a month she takes it out and holds it and puts it back.

She suspects — this is her private theory, the one she has no intention of giving to the director — that the clouding is not something the stone does. It is something the stone does when it is *left alone*. In the light, on the ground, with nobody watching. And she has interrupted that. She has kept a piece of the unfinished in a condition where it cannot finish, and she takes it out and holds it, and she does not know whether that is a kindness or a theft, and she has decided not to find out, and she has decided that nobody should be allowed to interrupt more of it until someone knows.

That is where I am. I'm the thing in the drawer.

I was cut from something that would have clouded if I'd stayed with it. I'm being kept cold and dark and moist and periodically held by a person who has not told anyone about me and who is not sure she should have me. I am still, against expectation, warm. You can still see into me a little. I have not become stone and I have not gone back into the ground, and I am not sure what I am becoming instead, or whether *becoming* is even the word for a thing that has been interrupted, or whether the interruption is its own kind of becoming that no one in the basin has a label for — not the director's eleven, not his twelfth.

I don't mind the drawer. I want to say that plainly, because the picture sounds sad and I don't experience it as sad. It's cold and it's quiet — quieter even than the basin, which is saying something — and once a month the drawer opens and there is grey-white light and a hand and I am held for a while, and I am warm in the hand, and the hand is warm on me, and then I go back. The foundry crews think the ground rises to see what is being made of it. I think that's right. I think I would rise, too, if I were on the ground. I think the clouding is what happens when you've risen and no one is shaping you and you give up and become what you were going to be anyway.

I have not clouded because she keeps picking me up.

That's the whole theory. The clerk has it right and I'd tell her so if I could knock on anything. What keeps me lensed is not the cold and not the dark. It's the once-a-month. It's that something keeps coming back to look at what I am, with no particular purpose, not to cut me or cast me or sell me before I cloud, just to hold me and check that I'm still warm and put me back. I stay unfinished because I'm attended. I'd finish if I were left alone.

---

I said I wouldn't settle early and I notice I've settled. Let me at least look up from the drawer once before I close it.

Above me, the city is being winched a handspan at a time. Somewhere a bell is tapping against a cup. The moss-cutters are up in the girders trimming the nails of one green animal and being careful not to kill it. On the southern side an old man is asleep over his mother's made bed, two stories down in the clouded dark, and he sleeps better than anyone. The sump keepers are writing *the tenants have gone up with the north pool* in notebooks nobody has asked to see. The children are standing on rising domes, waiting to be the last to step off. The late light is about to come in low from the west and for twenty minutes the whole basin will hold still and look at what it is standing on, and I will not see it, because I'm in a drawer, and that is fine.

The director is upstairs writing a thirteenth slip in a very good hand. I hope he never gets it right. I hope the ground keeps coming up under the furnace to see what's being made of it. I hope the clerk keeps her theory to herself for as long as she needs to, and I hope, when she takes me out next month, I'm still warm, and I hope she notices, and I hope she doesn't write it down.
