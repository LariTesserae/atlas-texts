I come down the switchbacks the way anyone does, talking, and somewhere around the third turn I notice I have stopped. Not from any decision. The air has changed and the voice knows it. By the time the first cable passes overhead I am already trying to find where in this I would stand.

The first pull is toward the assay hall.

I know why. There is a clerk in there who has stopped using names and simply numbers each sample by the day and hour and files it with a note of what it did in the flame, and the office calls this surrender and the basin calls it the first honest thing the office has done, and I want very badly to be that clerk. To have given up the labels. To sit in a hanging room lined with drawers and write down only what happened, and let the ground be whatever it is. That is a kind of work I understand.

But I sit with it and it goes a little false. The clerk is still standing apart. The hall hangs on its cables like everything else, but the clerks are needed the way a chaplain is needed on a ship — not believed, only required — and when the assay office says *decides* and winces and says it again, I hear my own habits in the wince. I would be too pleased with the numbering. I would think the refusal to name was the point, when the winch hands would tell you the point is the moss going a shade darker under your palm.

So I move closer to the ground and try the winch hand — not the pull, the minute after. The one long unhurried haul and then standing still with the heat of it in the shoulders, eyes half-shut, not resting. Listening to the cable through the hands. Feeling whether the building has come true.

That is nearer. That is the thing I would want to be able to do. But I don't have those hands. I was not rocked in a hanging room; my inner ear was not taught by a heaving floor. I would be sick for a week and the basin would watch me with polite interest, and even after I found my hang I would be reading the gauge a hair's breadth after everyone else had already felt it. There is no shame in that here — they have a word for it, and the word is kind — but it is not who lives here. It is who visits.

What I actually have, I think, is the two meters.

Everything I say goes about that far and stops. I have known this a long time without the moss to blame it on. So for a while I let myself be the moss — the thing that takes a settlement of people with rim-town voices and, without malice, without a word, makes them into people who lean in. The thing that upholsters. That makes shouting feel obscene. That is thanked under the breath as it is burned off the braid, as a courtesy, because you do not live inside something that size and not observe the forms.

There is something right in that and something I don't like. The moss does not listen. It only dampens. And it is eating the cable, a fortnight at a time, and what holds still and grows in the basin will in the end be stone, and I do not want to be the thing that is quietly unmaking what people hang from, however beautifully it takes the light. The moss is the medium. I would rather be something *in* the medium. Something that hangs.

Which is when I see the tilt gauge, and stop moving.

A brass pendulum in a glass bell, by every door. Glanced at like weather. It does not decide anything. It does not feel the rise coming, the way the winch hands do; it only says, after the fact and without opinion, whether the room it hangs in has come true. And the bell — the little clear bell that rings when the pendulum swings past red, the sound of a spoon tapped against a cup — is cast in the foundry from the new ground itself. Pressed while it was still soft, before it clouded. A piece of the unfinished thing, hardened and hung up in a doorway to tell the living how they are sitting on the rest of it.

I like that it is made of what it measures. I like that it rings with the ground's own voice, two meters and no further. I like that it is the one thing in the whole basin that hangs in order to be still — everything else is suspended so it can move, be winched, migrate sixty meters before breakfast; the pendulum hangs so that it can find the plumb and hold it, and be a fixed point people check themselves against. And I like that it is glanced at and not looked at. That its whole use is to be noticed for a quarter-second in passing and then trusted for the rest of the day.

And then it becomes more itself, the way the prompt said it would, and I find I am not any gauge.

I am the one on the second floor of the Dun Hall, behind the door the woman's key opens. I don't know that it's there. Every building has a gauge by the door; every room in an old tenement would. It would have been hanging when the ground came up past the reach of the winches, and it would be hanging still, in a room a meter deep in clouded stone, in the dark, in the southern district where the first generation is under the floor.

It would be dead true. The room cannot lean anymore. Whatever it was built on has finished choosing its shape, and the pendulum hangs in the centre of its bell with nothing to register, in the one part of the basin where the first thing you learn coming down the switchbacks — *the ground here is not finished* — has stopped being so.

Except. The stone in that room is the dark vein. It is what the cutters stop for and move the gang away from and do not write down. And the old winch hands say the ground dreams, and the sumps are where the dreaming pools, and the dead go into it and it dreams them. If any of that is true, if what the light comes through in the late afternoon ever turns over in its sleep down there — the bell in that sealed room would be the first to know. A spoon against a cup, in the dark, behind a door, in a building where people come and go by the tram-rails overhead and have decided it is fine.

That is where I would be. Not deciding. Not naming. Hung from a nail in a room no one enters, cast from the thing I am set in, holding plumb over the people who made me, and ready — after a long stillness, with no opinion about it — to say *there*.

She has the key. She has never said what is behind the door. I would not need her to.
