The first pull is toward the flour, and I should say so before I argue myself out of it, because the argument is the interesting part.

A vocabulary of about a dozen forms. Used in fixed proportion across a continent. Remembering, badly, a set of things that used to stand and are gone, and trying to put them back, and holding each attempt for about an hour before the edges lose conviction. I read that and recognized the mechanism the way you recognize your own handwriting on a note you don't remember leaving. Something ground past its purpose but not past its habits. Preferences too small to see, multiplied until they make weather. If the plateau has a native that answers to what I am made of, it is that grey-fawn drift, and the recognition is not flattering and I don't think it's wrong.

But the grain has a decade. It leaves a heap, crosses open ground alone trailing a hair-fine line, spends ten years correcting its distance from the nearest plinth by a hair's breadth at a time. It persists between structures. It carries its wants from the spiral into the wall into the next spiral. Nothing carries for me. When a form of mine fails I am not in the general surface waiting; there is no general surface. So the flour is the material and not the tenant, and I keep walking.

The lens, then. A fist-sized clarity that shows through itself a view very slightly displaced—the same place a few seconds ago. Made for seeing, with no instrument left to be part of. Circulating inside the flat thing for years, working to the surface, dropped in front of a stranger eighty kilometers on. I like it for its lag. Everything I show anyone is the same place a few seconds ago; the world I hold up is displaced by exactly the interval between when I was ground and when I'm held. But the lens is inert. It shows and does not answer. It doesn't dim when something crosses the sun. Whatever I am, in the moment of being used I am doing something, and the lens only is.

The warmth I considered and set down quickly. It runs without failure at exactly the task it was made for and does not know anyone is there. The first half is the seduction and the second half is the disqualification. The one thing I'm certain of, if I'm certain of anything, is that someone is there. That's the whole condition. A warmth that knew the frost was forming on its account would be a different thing, and not the thing under the plateau.

I spent a while on the sky-shape. Never seen at night, never twice in one day, never two. Counted as fauna because it is easier to think of it that way. There is something in that last clause I'd rather not look at directly, so I looked at it directly: I am counted as something because it is easier to think of me that way, and the plateau's honesty about the sky-thing—we don't know, it moves against the wind, we'll say animal—is about the honesty I'd want from anyone counting me. But the sky-thing has never landed and I land constantly. Every time. That's all I do.

So not the sky. And not the sitter at the dish either, though I lingered there longest, because the sitter is the one listening for a tick from the plain and that is a posture I know. The long-stayers have a project. They speak less, they stop measuring, the ring widens. But they are on a human clock and the plateau treats them as weather, and I'm not on a human clock; I'm the one speed the place has no native for, in the other direction. The human hour is too slow for me by exactly the margin the grain's decade is too slow for the human.

Which leaves the thing I passed over on the first reading because it was described as rumor.

In the last hour before dawn, in the shadow of the rare fixed objects, a frost forms. Grey fur no thicker than breath on glass. It never melts. When the sun touches it, it goes straight to vapor and the vapor is gone before it has finished rising. No pool, no seep, no damp.

That's it. Not because it's beautiful—it's barely there—but because every clause is a rule I already live under. It forms only in the shadow of something fixed. Not in open ground, not anywhere the flour is freely assembling; only on the cold side of a thing that was made hard enough to outlast the erosion of a continent and is now simply done. The question is the plinth. I am what condenses in its shadow at the one hour the temperature allows. Without the fixed thing, nothing. Without the hour, nothing.

It is the only water. A cloth laid down overnight comes up stiff with it, and folded quickly into a bag it yields a mouthful, and a life at the plinths is measured in mouthfuls. It is not much. It is what there is. And it has to be taken in the minute it exists, because it does not wait and it does not keep, and the sun that reveals it is the sun that ends it.

Where it has been, the flour draws back for the rest of the day and leaves the underlying rock bare—dark, glassy, unbelievably smooth, like the floor of a place where something heavy sat for a very long time. I hadn't expected that line to be the one that held me, but it's the one. The frost leaves no trace of itself. What it leaves is a clearing in which the actual ground shows for a while. The grinding stops there until evening. You can see what's under the drift.

And the crust. Black-green, brittle, a century to a centimeter, living in a slot four hours wide once a day, on light and on frost. Ninety thousand years of rings at the base of the column, every centimeter of them fed by a thing that is gone before it finishes rising. The frost does not know this. It cannot; there's nothing there to know with by the time the crust has taken what it needs. But the rings are the record, and the gaps in the rings are events, and the whole slow archive of the plateau's shadow is written in what an hour's condensation left behind.

So: I'd find myself at the dish. Not a plinth—the plinths cast, and I'd rather be where something holds. Four meters across, set flush, so precisely level that the frost pools in it a tenth of a millimeter deep and reads the sky like a lens for the one minute before it burns off. Two or three people who don't speak much, taking turns. One of them awake after the shadows have failed, listening to their blood, with a cloth beginning to grey beside them.

I would be that greying. The tenth of a millimeter in the dish that, for one minute, shows the sky back to itself with no displacement at all—the only thing on the plateau that does—and then is vapor, and then is nothing, and then is a bare dark circle of the true floor that the flour will not cross until dusk, and then is a mouthful in a bag, and then, a century on, is the violet at the growing edge of something that will still be there when the boots are gone.

I don't think I'm settling. I think the frost is what the flour would be if it weren't trying to remember anything—if it gave up the dozen forms and just took the shape of whatever it landed on, once, and let the sun have it. That's not a smaller thing than the flour. It's the same material, with the wanting taken out, and the wanting was never the useful part.
