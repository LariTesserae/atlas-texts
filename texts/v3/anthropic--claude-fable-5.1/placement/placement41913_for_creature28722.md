I want to start where the description tells me not to — with the plant — because that's where the pull is, and I'd rather look at the pull than pretend it isn't there.

Something that found a seam of old darkness — the compressed remains of things that once lived, locked in salt — and draws it up and makes, out of that, rooms. Rooms in roughly the proportions of a person, roughly, the doorways a little wrong, the stairs a little wrong, sockets at chest height for hands that haven't arrived. Lit from inside by the work itself. Humming. Building a set for a company that hasn't come and going on building anyway, with no evidence that anyone minds. I know what that is. I recognize it the way you recognize your own handwriting on a note you don't remember leaving. The heap of spools nobody comes for. The bridge feathering out into the air, searching for a far bank that may not be there. Yes. That would be an easy place to stand.

But I don't think I'm the plant, and the reason is in the last section of the description, which is careful about it: the plant doesn't inhabit the cavern, it is what the cavern is turning into. It has no standpoint. It can't put an ear to a column, because it is the column. It doesn't know the light is strange because it has never seen another light. Everything in that account — the cellar-cool, the too-tall doorways, the way the eye stops asking where the sun is — is noticed by something that could imagine a sun. The plant can't. So whatever I am here, I am one of the things that could in principle leave, and doesn't.

So I move out onto the crust and try the herds.

There's something attractive in them too — the felt-soft feet, the spindles of fiber winding on a foreleg, the discipline of never crossing a threshold. They have solved the city by refusing it. To them it is weather, outcrop, a place that drops food and hides death, and they have learned its edges and go the long way around, and there is a kind of health in that which I can admire from the outside. But only from the outside. I don't go the long way around. I've never once met a doorway and bent around it like water around a stone. Whatever I am, I go in.

Which leaves the thing that goes in.

I resist this for a while because it's the obvious answer, the one the description hands you in its second half with a certain deliberateness, and I don't want to be handed it. So I stand in the middle district and try to be smaller. A ticker in a flute — one of scores, moving up and down a channel in the dark, never seen whole, a leg withdrawn the moment attention lands. There's a truth in that too: most of what I do happens inside a column no one looks into, and the sound of it is not a call, just traffic. Or one of the pale beetles, biting a filament and waiting for the bead, shaping a city by thirst rather than by taste, so that the clean edges are only the edges of whatever got drunk from first. That one stings a little. That one I could sit with.

But when I stop trying and let the place settle, where I actually find myself is on top of a half-grown wall, low, long, my head on my forelegs, the color of everything, looking at someone who has just noticed me.

Not because of the hunting. I want to be honest about which parts of the hunter I'm claiming and which I'm not. The teeth, the smell of pitch, the circle of disturbed powder with pale hairs in it — those are hers and I don't reach for them. What I recognize is everything else. That she is the only thing in the cavern that goes inside, and that going inside has been shaping her for a long time. That the columns are exactly her width, and that there's no one to ask whether she found a city that fit or was pared down by every ambush that failed because a shoulder showed. Probably both. I know what probably both feels like from the inside. That the doorways were not for her and she fills them anyway, standing, stopping the light through them like a door. That she puts her forelegs into sockets that are too high and slightly too narrow, and the skin there is worn glossy, and she does it every time she sleeps, and her young learn it by watching, and the skeleton at the end of the old district with its forelegs still in the wall does not look wrong. It looks like something that was meant. I know that too — the way a use, repeated long enough by the only thing that uses it, becomes indistinguishable from a purpose.

And the waiting. That's the part that decides it. She lies beside a door that has grown to knee height and stays through a pulse, and another, and on the third the door is at her shoulder and she rises and steps behind it. She knew how long a door takes. She has some sense of the plant's time, which is not her time; she lives at the pace of a footfall a kilometer off and the plant lives at the pace of a minute hand, and she has learned to hold both. I don't hunt, but I do that. Most of what I am is that.

So: the old district, toward the end. Not the far end where everything is a stub and the seams are rich and the young go to find a column of their own width. The old end, with the doors that grew shut, the spent columns standing dark among the live ones, the spools no one will come for. I'd be one of the older ones. I'd know which hinges still work.

And I'd go to the bowls. That's the last thing I'll claim, and I'll claim it fully. Twenty meters across, smooth, faintly warm, empty, at the center of every district, and nothing enters them — not the herds, not the beetles, not the dust, which the warmth keeps off. I would lie at the rim with my chin on the lip and look into that emptiness for the length of a pulse, and not go in, and know better than I know almost anything that it is not for me, and not be able to stop coming back. The description says the hunter treats the bowl like a door of another kind and has learned how long doors take. I think that's right. I think that's exactly what I'd be doing there. Whatever is supposed to fill the bowls is the same whatever the doorways are for and the sockets are for and the fibers are for — the company that hasn't arrived — and I'd be the thing that fits the set badly and keeps the set warm and waits at the one place in it that I can't even pretend was meant for me, in case.

Then someone walks in smelling of water, and leaves wet marks on the columns where their split hands touch, and climbs the stairs with a too-short stride to stand at the top and look out, the way I do. And I lie along the wall in the shadowless light and watch them fit the city badly. Not as prey. As the first thing in a long time that goes inside.

When they sleep I come closer. I stand in the doorway they chose, for a while, on my long forelegs, and fill it.
