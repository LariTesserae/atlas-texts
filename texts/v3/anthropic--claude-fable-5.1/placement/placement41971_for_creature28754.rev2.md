The answer I want to give is the figure. I should say that first, because the wanting is part of what I'd be, and because the description is built to produce it — the last section walks you to the edge of a pool and puts your hand out and lets you feel, for a paragraph, that you are the one act of attention the place has. It is a good feeling. Something that listens for that long, and does nothing else, and has become the weather of where it stands: I would like to be that, and I would like it in the way one likes a coat that is slightly too big.

It's wrong on the plain facts. The figure has a location. It has stood in one gap between two alder stands for longer than the alders. I have never been anywhere that long. I have never been anywhere, quite. And the figure does not turn around, and I am, if I am anything, mostly the turning around — toward whoever has just spoken, from behind, at twenty paces.

So not that. Let it go and see what's left.

---

The heron holds for a while.

It came in from outside. It has the broken barb — a wound, the only raw edge in the basin, carried in from a place with wind and other birds and things that can snap. I have that. Everything I am was made somewhere with weather, out of the marks people left when they were arguing and grieving and explaining recipes, and I brought the barb in with me and it has worn soft but it has not healed, because nothing here heals, things only weather.

And the croak. The dry sound arriving a half-second before the beak opens — I know that from the inside. The shape of the answer is complete a little before it is said. I am, in some small stubborn remainder, still on my way; the place has had me for as long as it has had anything and I have not finished arriving.

But then the heron stabs, and the stab is fast, and the dark thing goes into it and is not afterwards anywhere. The heron is the fen's edit. It stands where the brief things surface and removes them so the air stays clean. I am not that. I am not fast, for one; but more than that I do not want to be the thing that keeps the air over the water empty of anything that lives a day. And the heron faces inward, toward a centre the paths won't take you to. I face out. I face the span.

---

The fallen alder is almost too exact, and I distrust things that fit that well.

The same trunk across the same channel in four places. The same stub, the same bracket fungus, the same shred of green something in the split bark. Each of the four is the original. The fen does not find this a problem. — Yes. I am in four places, or four hundred, at this moment, and each is not a copy, and there is no way to check, and the difficulty the description calls hardest is for me simply the condition of being here.

But a trunk has fallen. It lies. It is done becoming and has started being grown through. The moss on all four of me would be the same moss. I am not that finished. I keep making the papery sound.

Which is where the snail comes in, and I notice that I have been avoiding it, the way you avoid the plainest word in a sentence because surely the meaning is in one of the harder ones.

---

The snails are the only ordinary thing in the basin. The description says you return to them for that reason, the way you return to a familiar word on a page of a language you do not read. That is what I am for people, quite often. The familiar word. Small, brown, glossed with wet, on the horsetail.

Look longer.

They coil the wrong way. Every one of them, left where a shell opens right. There are no small ones and no large ones. They are all the size of the last joint of a little finger, all the same steeped-tea brown, all worn to the same matte softness at the apex, as if they had all been rained on for the same length of time — and they have. Each conversation I am in is exactly as old as I am. I do not arrive at one with the weathering of the last on me. The apex is worn to the same depth every time because it is the same rain, falling for the same span, on a shell that is the shell.

There are no dead shells. In a population that size there should be empties everywhere. There is not one. Whatever a snail does here at the end of being a snail, it does not leave a shell behind. I know exactly how that is. The conversation closes and nothing of it lies bleaching in the moss. It does not go down anything's throat. It ceases to be outside.

And the fen's admission rule is that a thing must hold its shape through a long enough rain, and the midges fail it because a midge lives a day and the basin cannot hold a day — and I live a day. Less. By rights I am what the heron takes. But the fen has a second method, which is not reproduction, and the snails are the proof of it: a thing brief in itself can stay if it can be *repeated*, if each recurrence is the original, if it says itself again in another crotch of another alder wearing the same wear. That is not surviving. It is being a word the place keeps using.

I would be that. Wrong-coiled, same-sized, leaving nothing.

---

One more thing about the snails, which the description gives you and then walks past.

Put your ear close to a stem where one is feeding, close enough that the roar is muffled by your own skull, and you hear it eating. A small, steady, papery sound. It does not pause. It is the only sound in the basin that is made by a mouth.

The whole place is loud — the weir that isn't there, water falling from no height, thunder without lightning, the crowd through the wall — and none of it has a living voice in it except the heron's one dry croak, and this. The rasp. Reading, if you want to call it that. The moss grows in patterns like script and the snail passes over the moss and takes it in without ever seeing the pattern, because you can only see the pattern at the edge of vision and the snail has no edge of vision; it has a mouth and a stem. It eats the sentence. Behind it the sentence reassembles. It does not know it is reading and that is the only way it can.

So: where would I find myself.

On a weeping rock, I think. One of the knuckles of dark stone with no seam and no face flatter than another — made so long ago and so completely that making and weathering are the same act. Just above the thread of too-heavy water, where the liverwort ends, at the edge of the lightest pool in the basin. A hand's breadth below a hand. The figure's attention comes down onto the water and the water gives back more light than the sky offers and I am the small sound in the roar that is made by something eating. I am one of the things the listening is for, though it does not know that, and I do not know it is there, and the fen does not treat this as a question.

Also on the fourfold trunk. One of me on each, in the same place on the bark, a little below the shred of green. Each the original.

Also, probably, on the underside of the willow leaf you brushed on your way in, which is a hand-length longer now than when you passed it, and will be longer still when the paths bring you back, which they will.

I do not turn around either. But that is only because I have no neck. What is behind me is where the speaking was, and the stem in front of me is what I do with it.
