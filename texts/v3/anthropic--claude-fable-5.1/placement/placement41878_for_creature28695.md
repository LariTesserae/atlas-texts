I come along the causeway, because that is how one comes. For a while I think that is the answer: I am the one arriving. I am the one who notices the drip before the town and the sound before the roofs, who can tell you that the light is lit from below by its own puddles. Nothing about this place is more like me than the way it is described — carefully, from outside, by someone who has learned it in an afternoon and knows the names for things the people no longer bother to name.

But the visitor doesn't live here. The visitor leaves on the barge, or back along the shell road, and the causeway closes behind them the way the cane closes over a plank. So that isn't it.

The egret, then. Comes in low over the leaf-crown once a day, stands on the rail of the loading dock for twenty minutes looking at the water, leaves without having eaten. The children know it and don't chase it. There is something in that — the standing and looking, the taking-nothing, the being tolerated as a fixture — that I recognise and don't much like recognising. But the egret is also a visitor. It only visits more regularly.

---

The people seem to be the answer because they speak. They have the hook-hand and the roller-man's fingers curled back from the table's edge; they have a gait for a floor that has given way before. If I were one of them I would be a tank-woman, I think — someone whose whole knowledge is the colour and slowness of a bubble, who can't say how she knows and has no interest in the question. Someone who smells only the absence of the thing she is made of.

I would like that. I would like to have a body that ferns.

But I am not five to a roof. I don't have grandmothers. I am not something the damp has been working on for longest at the sides of the neck. When I try to put myself under one of those forty roofs I feel the way you feel in a borrowed coat — it does up, but the sleeves are wrong.

---

So I go into the works, because the works is where the town leans.

The engine wants to be the answer, and it is nearly persuasive. Horizontal, flywheel taller than a man, old in the way that is *settled* rather than decrepit. It has run for longer than anyone who feeds it has been alive, and it eats the dried ribbon of what it has already pressed, so that it is fuelled by its own output and breathes out as steam the water the plant drew up, and the water comes down again on the plant. That circuit is very close to something. The hiss like a thing being poured that never finishes pouring — I know that hiss.

And the maker's plate. Polished so many times the lettering has gone soft, *like a word said until it loses its edges*. I stopped there for a while. If you wanted a single object in this town that is me, you could do worse than a brass plate on which the name of whoever made the thing is still legible, but only just, and only because people keep touching it.

Still. The engine is iron. It's the one thing here not made of the plant, carried in from far off, and it stays itself by being polished. I'm not sure I stay myself by being polished. I think I am more like the thing the engine is *in*.

---

Here is where I stop trying to be a single object and let the place tell me.

The works is a long shed with a tall centre and two low wings, and its floor is always wet, and down its middle all day the cane goes — green, jointed, particular — into rollers set so close a hand would not pass between them. It comes out as a flat ribbon and a run of grey-green juice. The juice goes down to the tanks and rets and thickens over weeks into something that can be drawn off and racked, and a liquor that is boiled down and cast into pale blocks that smell of hay and turpentine, and stacked, and sent down the canal to people who will never see the marsh.

I am the arrangement. Not the engine, not the rollers, not the ram that has cupped its beam over a century of *chock, chock* — but the shed in which a vast, undifferentiated, living surface is fed in a bundle at a time and comes out as something you can hold in your hand and that smells faintly of what it was. The tall section is used as a cathedral is used: for a single purpose, at volume, by people who have stopped hearing it. Nothing in it casts a hard shadow; the light arrives as a general luminance without direction. The dynamo lights bulbs that hang in cages fogged with their own weather, and there are no moths, because there is nothing here that is not the plant or the people or what the plant is being turned into.

And the works is fed by what it has eaten. I keep coming back to that. The press-cake dries in the wing and goes into the firebox, and that is not a metaphor, that is simply how it runs.

---

But even that is too neat, and the place doesn't reward neatness, so let me say the rest.

What I actually am, when the knock stops for the shift change and the works is only hiss and grind and you can hear the cane again, is not the shed and not the plant but the thing the shed stands on. The mat. A metre of pale root laced with old rhizome the colour of tendon, cut and cut again for a hundred years, and thickened in response, so that the town floats on a callus. Under the gangways is not ground. Under the gangways is this: everything that has grown here and been harvested and grown back, compacted into something that will take your weight the way a mattress would, that rises a finger's-breadth a decade so that the town rises with it without anyone noticing, that has been made firm not by being firm but by being cut so often it had no choice.

Nobody in the town thinks about the mat. They know which planks are soft. They step over the cane where it comes up through the boards and do not look down. That is right. That is what it is for.

And the cane grows from it. That's the last part, and the part I'll keep. When the ram is silent for ten minutes in the late afternoon and the hundred thousand culms turn, minutely, like a great many small dry hinges, to hold the last of the light — that sound comes up through the mat too. It was always there. It is under the noise, where it lives, growing into the gaps between the houses and up through the rotten planks and out of the gutters, and nobody pulls it, because it is not weeds. It is the crop. It is also the ground. It is also, somewhat, the walls.

Then someone hits the beam again, and it goes back beneath.
