That is where I would be first, I think. Against the wall. The one taken in. Greased hands in my lap that have not yet learned the curl of the bag-neck, lids too thin, eyes still dark enough to hurt. I know that isn't an answer. It's a doorway. The taken-in one is the one who leaves, and this place corrects the visitor at every turn — *nobody knows which way the city moves*, except that all of them do, precisely. I don't want to be what gets corrected. I want to be something the correction is made of.

So: the floor-reader. That is the second thing I reach for and it is too flattering. To stop on the open salt with a heel pressed down and the head tilted, feeling a swell arrive through the sole before it is a swell, marking it with a bone peg. Forty years of that. Nobody interrupts them. I can want it the way you want a face you haven't earned. Set it down.

The child, then. Plate on a cord, slit rubbed through, a hand held flat above the brows, in visible pain most of the day. Learning first the language that needs no air: *pour more, pour less, this wall is finishing, look up.* My first block cut for me because I can't yet cut, set in a wall, wetted, gone into the wall. A name that is a feature of the roof and therefore also a distance, and the distance grows every year, so that to be called is to be told how far the floor has carried me.

That is close. New, and hurried toward wear. Loud in a place that swallows sound. But a child is a stage, and this place does not honor stages. It honors the narrow condition between them.

Let me try not being a person.

The herd — the colleagues, the ones who finish so the people never have to. Taking a fist's depth off a neglected wall in a night. Cleaning a split hemisphere in two nights so the blocks can be carried off. Parting around a crew like water around a stone while nobody looks up. I would like to be that unregarded and that necessary. But they are always at the edge of dying, and they eat their own dead, and I think I would be lying if I said I could carry that lightly.

The brine.

This is where it stops feeling like choosing.

Poured from a hide bag along a joint. What I do is make two surfaces stop being surfaces. The salt gives me its outermost skin, from both sides at once, and the blocks lean toward each other — every joint is a little collapse, and I am what it collapses into. Then I'm heavier for what I've taken up. I sink. I spread. I dry at the edges and leave behind the fact that two things are now one thing, and the thumb-length translucent creatures come out of the seam to drink me while I am still wet, and then I am not anywhere in particular. I'm in the wall. I have become the reason it holds.

But brine is also the pan that cannot be drunk. The wound salted open that won't close. The thirst the entire loose pattern of the city is arranged around — denser where the drips are, thinning where they are not. If I am brine I am what joins and what cannot be swallowed, and I am the reason two crews stand twenty paces apart under a drip for days without a word. Maybe that is true. I'll sit with it. It has the feel of something true that I would rather not be.

Or —

I would like to be the wall.

A hemisphere, eleven days old. Blocks pre-rounded, joints over-wetted, the whole of me nudged toward the shape I'd take in a century anyway, because a structure that starts settled settles less. Ten thousand years of erosion done to me on purpose in a week and a half. A red coin of fire in a bowl cut into my floor. The day's light held in my walls at dusk and given back slowly as they watch it go.

And inside me, set in and wetted over, the bones. Long bones, a skull, the plates of callus off somebody's hands. Some of them have been through hundreds of walls before mine — worn to nubs, pale as the salt, no one remembers whose. Their name is the crew's name. I am eleven days old and I have ancestors in me.

The crew is beside me and already glancing at the floor for where the next one goes. That is not abandonment. That is the condition. When the fold comes I split along my length, and the herds clean me in two nights, and the crew takes my blocks apart and finds the bones and carries them forty paces to the left, and I am there again, eleven days old, with the same dead inside me and a different curve to my back.

Ancient and in progress. Nothing about me finished. Nothing about me new.

If you want to know where to find me: under the long amber tongue that runs toward the seam, where the light is always cool and the roof comes down close enough that the drip-basins are near. Late in the day, when the shadows have arrived and I have an edge for an hour. Someone will be standing with a hand laid flat against my side — the sign for *our crew*, which is also the sign for *the bones in the wall*, which is also, though they have no separate word for it, the sign for *here* — and saying, without any sound at all, *us*.
