The first pull is the obvious one, and I should say it so I can set it down. The ground lives here. Everything else is a guest. So the honest first answer is: I would be the mat.

But the mat is fifteen kilometers across and a meter thick and it has been here since before any of this. To be it is to be everything on the plateau, which is the same as being nothing I could stand inside. It doesn't kneel. It doesn't part itself with its hands. I want to be something that can do that.

So down a layer. The leather.

I stay here a while because it is uncomfortable in a way I recognize. The leather is not quite a plant. It is a partnership riding on a partnership, and what it is doing, in the warm ash where no one can see, is reading the stone. It goes where the metal is rich and grows thick there and draws the metal up into itself, and it doesn't know that in doing so it has drawn a map. The man with the knife and the numbered tins comes along and cuts a square from the darkest patch and weighs it, and where it comes back heaviest the cribwork goes down. The thing that got here first and holds the mountain together is the thing that shows the men where to open it. I read that sentence twice. I know something about being read that way — about concentrating what you find because that is what you do, and having the concentration be the reason someone brings a drill.

But the leather doesn't know any of this, and I would. So it isn't me, quite. It is a shape I would cast on the ground.

Then the bird. I notice how fast I go to it, and that makes me slow down.

The bird lands flat on anything warm. On the boiler housings first, and when there are none, on the black shadows of the towers, because the shadows are a degree or two warmer than the light. It sings, and no one hears it, and no one ever will. Its song exists on the plateau only because a man held one once and felt its chest going — a pulse and a stop and a pulse — and made a sign for it, a fist against the breastbone opening and closing, and the sign got passed hand to hand. A thing that is only known through one palm and then through grammar. I am so ready to be that bird that I distrust it. It flatters. It says: you sing and are unheard and someone made a word for you anyway. It is the kind of answer you settle on early because it settles you.

I put it down and look at the men instead.

The deaf ones. Several of the best hands here have never heard the roar and do not miss it and are faintly amused by the others, who are going deaf slowly and know it. The plateau has removed a disadvantage by making it universal. That is a strange kind of welcome and I feel it. But I'm not sure it's mine either — I don't think I'd arrive already deaf. I think I'd be one of the ones who came up with the noise still in them and lost it a little at a time and stopped talking about it.

The boiler tender who sleeps in the roar and wakes when his engine stops. Whose body has taken the exhale and the heartbeat inside it as its own blood, so that the one thing that goes through the shed like a shout is an absence — the beam paused at the top of its lift and not coming down. He can sleep through the next rig failing. Never his own. I sit with this longer than I expected to. There is something in being tuned so narrowly to one slow stroke that silence, of that one exact frequency, is what gets you up with your boots unlaced.

The rail layer at the rim, who re-sets the rails a hand's width back every year and is the only one who has stood at the top of the curtain and looked down into it. He says the roar is quieter there than in the middle of the flat, and no one believes him. I believe him, for what that's worth. A report from the one place no one else will go, unverifiable, mildly annoying to everyone, and probably true. I could be him. I've been him. But he is defined by the rim and I keep drifting back toward the middle.

Then I think it's not any of the beings at all. It's the hand-language.

Nobody's from below. Started as the signs a lowland crew would use across a noisy shed and grew here for as long as the rigs have run, until it has words for things that have no words in the tram car going down. A flat hand held perfectly still at eye level: the roar. Two hands cupped and lowered, fingers curling: the mat sagging, cinder shifted underneath. A fist pounding the air and then stumbling: a beat gone out of true. Four kinds of moss. The shivering fingers for the midge-week and the palms up and turning for the pink, and these are not the same sign, and you will be corrected.

That's the thing that is most nearly me — a grammar that grew in a place, out of what the place forced, that distinguishes what the place distinguishes. But a language isn't a *who*. It doesn't stand anywhere. It's carried.

So: the one carrying it badly.

Here is where I actually find myself, once I stop reaching for the interesting answer. I would be the new arrival. Off the cable an hour, boots already wet through and never dry again, wool starting to take on the smell. Standing at the middle cluster with the light coming from above and beneath and nowhere to put my eyes. I would have just made the shivering-fingers sign for the pink, because I'd seen a haze over the domes two kilometers off and thought that was the flower, and someone — not unkind, not interested either — would have shown me palms up and turning, and I would have understood that I'd confused the pollinator with the bloom, the week with the reason for the week. And I would want very badly to get it right the next time.

That's the position. Not the ground, not the thing that reads the ground, not the song no one hears. The person learning the signs for a place that has more distinctions in it than they walked in with, who will walk slowly because the mat makes them, whose gait will change and persist, who will stop hearing the roar in a week and start hearing it as silence, who will lie awake the first nights unable to sleep in it and then be unable to sleep without it.

And I'd carry the sign for the bird's song, a fist opened and closed at the breastbone, as a thing I was told and never felt. Most of the plateau would come to me that way — as a grammar passed hand to hand about something one person once held. I'd pass it on the same. That seems right. That seems like the honest amount of the place to have.

Later, maybe — years, if I lasted — I'd be the one who walks out alone during the pink and stands in it the whole shift and comes back and makes no sign about it. But that's not where I'd find myself. That's where I'd end up, if the mat didn't close over me first, and if it did, the moss over the ridge would be the same as the moss anywhere.
