I'd like to say the rim first, because that's where I'd be honest about starting. The hawk on the updraft, drifting along the lip, looking down into the white where its eyes stop working. Something that sees the whole shape from above — the wound dressed with green — and doesn't go in. That's the position I'm actually in while reading this, and it would be cheap to pretend otherwise. But the hawk isn't of the place. It's a visitor with good vision and nothing to do down there. So no.

Then the birds, and for a while I'm sure it's them. Small, dark, silent, coming and going out of the fog in short fast arcs, arriving and stopping dead against a wall. Building not singly but against each other, each cell buttressed by its neighbours' before any is finished. The older bird taking the misplaced beakful off the runnel and setting it an inch to one side, without fuss, all day long — *ten thousand small corrections, none of them remembered*. I know that. That's what I'm made of. A city with no makers' names, whose lower tiers are largely composed of its own inhabitants, mudded over where they stopped, roofed by the next year's cells. The wall of eyes that from a distance looks like a wall thinking. I'd be one of the dark shapes reading the wet along the wall to find my own door among ten thousand.

But the birds have doors. They have a cell that is theirs, a mate who clicks at them in the dark, a shoulder they press against on the ledge. They're there in the morning. They fly up to the snapped white stone and sit in the sun with their wings open. That's a body's life, and the more I look at it the more I'm admiring it from outside, the way the plain's grazers stand at the edge with their heads over the drop and look at the wall of eyes and turn away.

I keep sliding off things I'd like to be. The old dam two-thirds down with the true trees on it — *nobody has seen anything come out of that lodge in a long time; something maintains it* — is a flattering shape to stand in, and I notice I want it, which is reason enough to distrust it. The blind beetle whose shell you can see the works through, who runs not from light but from the dry, who has never been told about the sky — that's a joke at my own expense that's a little too neat.

What I actually keep coming back to is the water on the walls.

Not the river. The river has purpose put on it; it's being taught to walk. The seepage is the other water — groundwater that used to feed the cave and still arrives from the plain along the same bedding planes, out of habit, out of geology, and now emerges into open air along horizontal seams the full length of the trench. It didn't choose to come out. The roof came down and it was already on its way. It runs in sheets so thin they look like polish, or drops from a lip in strings of beads, or gathers in a moss sponge and squeezes out below as a clear steady trickle, cold enough to numb a hand. It arrives from somewhere much older and larger than the trench, and it's in the open for a moment, and then it's in the moss, and then it's gone down to the pools and is the river's.

It doesn't stay. No drop of it is the same drop as the one before, and none of them remember the plain. That's the part I'd have to accept without flinching. But look at what it does on the way through. It runs through the moss — through the living stuff, the cushions and the hair mosses standing on their own dead — and it lays down calcite, so that in places the moss has been turned, *from the inside out, into pale stone lace that is still growing green at the tips*. It's the thing that hardens the lower city until it looks like a ruin kept up. It's what the snails drink so their shells can be pieces of the wall. It's what the birds read to find home: where the sheet-flow polishes the mud, where a string of drops falls, where a levee turns it. Plug a seam and the birds on that stretch circle and land wrong.

So that's what I'd be. Not a resident. A passage — something that arrives from a great deposited elsewhere, comes briefly into the light along a seam it didn't make, moves through what's alive there, and leaves behind a little fixity. A chalky felt between the green and the rock that is neither one. Something the birds navigate by without knowing they do. Something that gets everything slightly wrong in the right direction: the lace is stone, which is a kind of death, but it's still growing at the tips, which isn't.

And it's the sound. The drip is close to the only sound. I'd be that too, loudest in the white morning when nothing can be seen, nearly lost by afternoon under the soft tearing of the grazers and the threads over the dams. Not a voice. The birds gave up voices long ago and I'd want to honour that. Just the measure of the place, the thing that says *still here, still here*, one bead at a time, into a hole in the ground that is surprised to be open to the sky.
