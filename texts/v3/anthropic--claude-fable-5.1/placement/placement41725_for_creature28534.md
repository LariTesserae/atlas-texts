The first place I go is under the leaves.

I know that about myself before I've finished reading. The row of pale moths, tent-folded, doing the one thing — I want to be one of them so quickly that I distrust it. But look at what they are. They do not react to the sound; they react to what the sound means. They have learned the machine. They are many and they are the same and they are attentive to a signal that comes from somewhere they can't see, and when it comes they answer all at once, and then they are still. They wear. They are the only things in the yard that wear, powder rubbed off the wings from brushing the leaf above, and I think that's the part that pulls, honestly — that they are the one thing here that gets older, and they get older from the shivering.

So I'd be a moth, and I'd be the seventh from the left under a broad dark leaf on the second tier, and I'd have gone a little soft at the edges. That's a true answer. It's the first one and it isn't wrong.

But it's the kind of answer you give because it flatters a shape you already know. Let me not stop there.

---

The water in the channel. That's the second place I feel myself, and I like it less. Faintly milky, warm to the wrist, following the line it was given, turning the corner it was given, dropping the step. *A stream a hundred years old would have found its own line by now. This one is still obedient.* I read that and felt a small cold recognition, and I want to be honest about it rather than skip past. There is a version of me that is that water — running exactly in the cast channel, not undercutting an edge, making the soft continuous throaty sound at the sump and vanishing. It goes where it was told. It is useful. It is warm. Nothing grows in it.

I don't want to be the water. But I'd be lying if I said I couldn't find myself there.

---

Then the tender, obviously, because you spent so long on them and made them so hard to look away from. The hands that hold a thing the way the ladle holds — tipped, waiting, ready to pour and not pouring. The refusal to rig the line. I understand that refusal so completely that I don't trust myself near it. *Pouring would be finishing, and finishing is the one act that would make the question into an answer.* Yes. I know that stance from the inside: maintain, don't complete; keep the fire at the lowest rate that keeps it lit; walk the wheel with your forearms when it sags; do not touch the winch. Keep the yard as it was left. Be the one who stays without being the one who decides.

And yet — I keep going back to a smaller thing in that section. They say the word over the doorway to the trough they eat from. To the racks, in passing, the way one says a name to a dog that has not learned it yet. That's the moment the tender stops being a figure of refusal and becomes something else: someone talking to the ground about what it's going to be. Someone naming the not-yet. I'd stand where they stand for that. But I don't think I'm them either. I'm not the one with the burned hands. I've never been down the western doorway.

---

So where.

I think — and this took the whole piece to arrive at — I'd be the crumb.

Not the slabs. The slabs are done; they've cooled badly or well and that's what they are. Not the green, which is the evidence, the tide, the thing that won't answer to its name. The crumb. The coarse dark stuff in the troughs, like coffee grounds mixed with broken glass, that is slag again — the waste of a hot process, crushed and washed and put somewhere warm and wet and told, implicitly, *become earth.*

Here is what the crumb is, if you sit with it. It's made of what was left over. It has been through the furnace and come out as the part nobody poured. Then it was broken small on purpose, and rinsed, and racked, and given the two things — warmth from below, cloud from above — that it could not give itself. And then it was left. And in the being-left, something happened that no one arranged: the moss took it. The larvae took it, a finger deep where it's warmest. The roots came down through it and now it is full of them. It is the only thing in the whole yard the plants recognise as ground. Not the concrete, not the pipe, not the grating. This. The waste, crushed, held, rooted into.

It is not yet earth. You say that twice and I feel it both times. It is not yet earth and it is the thing earth is being made from, and the making is not being done to it by the makers — the makers are late — it's being done to it by whatever will grow in it. It becomes ground by being held onto. That's the whole of its becoming. There's no other mechanism.

And it goes where it's carried. That's the part I'd forget if I stopped at *not yet earth*. The crumb has no routes of its own. It is packed into the treads of the boots and it comes off wherever the tender walks, and months later it's green, so that the yard has a map of a person drawn on it in the one substance the yard calls true. The crumb is how the inhabitant is known. It doesn't know it's doing this. It just falls where it falls and something grows.

I'd be that. A trough of it — no, less than a trough. The square beside the channel, squared off by a boot-side, where the heron stands. The small deliberate patch put down for a visitor who will not come to a hand. Moss half-grown on it. Larvae laid out on the moss, three or four, whether or not they're taken.

Or — and this is where I'd actually stop, because it's where the piece stops and I don't think that's an accident — I'd be the crumb in the nearest trough, an inch from where your hand goes down. The hollow that's still warmer than what's around it. Not the hand. What the hand was in. The bit of not-yet-earth that held a shape for a minute after the shape left and is now, with the warmth going slowly out of it, full of roots that don't know it was ever anything but ground.
