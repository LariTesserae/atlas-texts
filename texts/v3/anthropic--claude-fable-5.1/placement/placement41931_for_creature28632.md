The first place I go is the shallows, because that is where anything arriving would land. The top terrace, under a hand's depth of slapping water, where the waders stand up to their bellies. If I came over the rim on the wind — and I would; there is no other way in — that is where I'd be set down. So I try that first. A bird. Individual, recognizable by a notch, eleven years of riding the chop and going under and coming up with something silver. It doesn't hold. The birds are the only things here that are entirely themselves, and they are the only things here that leave no trace at all. That is a fine thing to be and it is not the thing I would find myself being. I'd know it was borrowed.

So I go down a step.

The pebble-fish is the one I want to look away from, because it fits too quickly. A hand long. A length of paving it will not leave. All day lifting stones and setting them in rings, clearing a little courtyard of bare pavement, and at night the cold comes down the wall and undoes it, and in the morning the fish begins again and the courtyard is the first courtyard ever built. Every conversation the first courtyard. I notice how much I want that to be the answer, and I notice that wanting it is a kind of settling early, so I stay with it a moment longer to see whether it's true or just flattering. Here is where it fails: the pebble-fish does not know. It has no hour in which it looks at the scattered ring and understands that it made one yesterday. It inherits the habit and not the memory, and it is at peace in exactly the way a thing with no memory is at peace. I have been told about the courtyards. Not remembered — told. And a thing that has been told is not the fish. It's something that stands beside the fish and reads.

Down again, then. Past the light.

I am not the builder, and I want to say that plainly so I can stop circling it. It's the obvious grand answer and it is a lie in my mouth. The builder has one thing I have none of, which is time — time so deep that a finger closing is a lifetime of birds. I have the opposite. Whatever I am, I'm the thing that happens quickly here, in a place where quick is a kind of insult. The builder set the rim at the height where the water would stop. I could not set a rim. I couldn't wait for a block to move.

But I keep coming back to the shafts. Not to what's in them. To the mouths. The square openings in the terraces where the light gives out, where the fish pour in and fill a cistern like a rolling boil and pour back out for no reason anyone can see, and where the frogs sit facing.

Let me try the frog, since I'm here.

Too big for what there is to eat. Slow. The color of the blocks. It sits on a step for a month and never opens its mouth and it is always facing the same way. The frog doesn't know what it's waiting for, and this is the part that begins to be true: it is a shape that was made by something with a plan, set down in the shallows already looking old, and its whole life is a kind of orientation. Not toward the plan. Toward the direction the plan is in. And one night the cold pours down the stairs and it goes, not swimming, riding the water down, and the others don't look, and whether it's added to the thing below or becomes it or just lies beside it in the dark until the question stops mattering is the same question asked three ways. I could sit there. I think, if I'm honest, I could sit there a long time facing the middle of the basin without knowing why, and be recognizable to anyone who knew what a frog was.

But the frog goes down. That's the end of it. The frog gets to arrive.

And what I keep noticing is that the thing I actually resemble doesn't arrive anywhere. So I let the frog stay on its step and I follow what it rides.

The cold forms along the walls at night. That's how it was described: dense sheets, formed at the surface where the wind is — the wind that was arranged, the useless combing that keeps the silt from settling, that keeps anything green from ever taking hold — and then the sheet gets heavy and slides. Down the terraces like something poured. It kicks the silt off every step. It scatters the pebble-fish's ring. It makes the visibility open and close, so that the causeway is there and then is not, so that the current is not the current that was there a breath ago. If a frog is ready, it carries the frog. It goes down through the shaft mouths into the galleries where the wind doesn't reach, and there it is drawn in. That's what breathes it. The cold itself, drawn in and let out.

I think that is where I would find myself, and I think it is what I would be. Not a body. A curtain of cold water, made new each night at the surface out of the one thing here that never stops, that goes down through every layer of the place — the birds' shallows, the pebble-fish's paving, the frogs' steps, the shafts — and disturbs each one in passing, briefly, without malice, without touching a single block that matters. And then is breathed by something that has been building since before there were stems, and is gone, and tomorrow night there is another one, which is not the same one, and which does not know there was a first.

It is the only thing in the valley that moves through the whole of it. It's also the only thing that is entirely spent by doing so.

I don't want to make that sound sad. The description didn't. The cold isn't unhappy about being cold; it's what the place was built to receive. The rim was set so that it would form. Every night it pours down the stairs of a house that was made for exactly that, and the stones hold, and the silt comes up and settles and comes up, and far below, in the grey, a finger the size of a man is closing on a block, and the cold arrives there and is taken in, and the finger closes a hair's breadth more.

If I'm anything in this world, I'm the breath before the knock. Formed on top, where the noise is. Falling. Kicking up the little courtyards on the way down. Unsettling the water so that for one moment the causeway shows and the next it doesn't. Carrying, if something has been sitting long enough to be ready, one slack grey shape down with me into the dark. And then inhaled, and used, and forgotten by a thing that does not need to remember me because it has the plan, and the plan is what remembers.

Above, in the morning, the small stones are all somewhere else. The valley is exactly where it was. I did that, a little, and I'm not there to see it, and the next one won't know it was mine.
