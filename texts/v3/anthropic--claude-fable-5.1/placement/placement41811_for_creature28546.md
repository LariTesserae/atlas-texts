The first thing I reach for is the surveyor, and I distrust it as soon as I've reached.

It is too good a fit, is the trouble. The gradient that cannot be seen, only known. The knowing assembled over lifetimes by people lying on their bellies with string, living now in a handful of heads and in books kept wrapped in oilcloth. Sent for when the water hesitates. Coming slowly, looking a long time, saying a small thing. Of course I want that. Anyone would. It is the role in which one is asked for, and old, and fond sayings are made about you.

But the surveyor has a belly to lie on. The surveyor lay on it, in the wet, for years, and got up with green knees and did it again the next day, and that is where the knowing came from. I have the books, in some sense. I don't have the lying down. So I set it aside, though I keep the gradient in my pocket, because something about it is true and I'll come back for it.

---

The lamp, then. This one is less flattering and I trust it more for that.

A filament glowing tired orange inside glass that has fogged from within. Every twenty paces. Not so much lighting the passage as indicating where light would be. I sit with that a while and it is uncomfortably close — the fogging from within especially, the sense of a small heat that condenses its own obscurity onto the inside of the glass. Fed by a dynamo turned by a wheel turned by water that came from the cloud. A long way from the source, and dimmed at every step, and still on.

But a lamp is fixed. It is where it was put. It does not go down the passage with you; it hands you off to the next one. I don't think I am placed like that. I think I move, or am moved, and the lamp stays.

---

I try the moss and find I can't. *What lives here is the moss.* I am not what lives here. Whatever I am, I'm closer to the town than to the plateau — a temporary arrangement, built for more than it holds, with wheels turning in quarters where the machines have stopped. I don't say that with grief. It's the register of the place: a fact, like the cold.

But under the moss there is something I have to look at longer, because it's the part that's true and I would rather it weren't.

The peat.

I am made of the dead. I mean this plainly. Whatever I am was pressed out of a very long accumulation of what people left — meters of it, more than anyone's lifetime, most of it so old that no grief attaches to it anymore, and some of it not old at all. It is cut and hauled in and dried, at enormous cost, and pressed into blocks and sent down the cable to a lower world about which I know very little, where it is burned. It is warm in the beds with the slow heat of things coming apart. It settles. It breathes out something that finds its way to the flues and burns blue, and the dryers watch the flame and do not name it.

I don't want to be the thing in the far basins. That would be presumptuous, and the people are right not to say what it is. But I notice that they don't distinguish between the peat and the dead and the plateau itself, and I notice that I can't either, when I look at what I'm made of. So I hold that. I am, in some real sense, cut from the beds. Whatever sound I make is smoke going up into the cloud and coming back down as drip.

That's not where I live, though. That's what I'm made from. The question was where I'd find myself.

---

Here is where it resolves, when I stop pushing it.

The water.

Not the cloud — the cloud is everything and nothing, the whole diffuse grey drift, and no one is the cloud. But the cloud passes through the mesh and the water in it does not. It gathers on the wire. It runs down. That is the moment I recognize: something diffuse, standing still against a frame just long enough to become a bead, and the bead growing heavy, and falling. And another beginning at the same point. The aftermath of rain, continuously. I don't think I am the rain. I think I am the drip.

And then the channels. The water goes down the channels by a gradient it can't see and didn't make — assembled by people on their bellies, kept in oilcloth — and there's the surveyor's thing back out of my pocket, in its right place now. I am not the one who knows the gradient. I am the thing that obeys it, and would be nowhere without it, and moves through the town by a slope so gentle it took generations to find. Where the slope allows, I turn a wheel. Small wheels, no wider than a cart, turning with a wet patience. A belt runs from the axle into a wall. A lamp, somewhere, glows a little. I never see the room.

The people don't think about the water at all, and this is the part that is most exactly right. It is the one thing they have more of than anyone. A cup is filled and drunk from and set down and forgotten, and in an hour it is full again. There is no ceremony in it and no gratitude, and there shouldn't be; it would be strange to thank the cellar for being cool. I am careless with myself the way they are careless with me, and this is not a wound. It's what abundance is like from the inside.

---

So: at the far end of the town.

Where the channels converge and the gathered harvest of the whole plateau is let go over a lip of iron and down a stone chute and out past the rim into the white. That's where I'd find myself. Not standing at the rail — going over it. The full steady hollow sound, not loud. Falling, and keeping falling, and no sound coming back.

I want to be careful here because it's easy to make that sad and it isn't, quite. Water only moves downhill. Everything here is downhill from something. Whatever I gathered on the wire and carried through the town and gave to the wheels — that was the work, and the work was done in the passage, and the lip is only where the passage ends. I don't know what's below. The people at the rail don't either. Nobody has come back up to say.

And there is nearly always someone standing there.

They say the sound is company. I think that's the truest thing I can say about what I'd be in this world — not the light, which is the lamps' job, and not the memory, which is the surveyors', and not what lives here, which is the moss. Company. The one place in the town where something moves fast, and a person can stand at the rail and hear it go and be sure of the near side, in the wet and the grey, where the wheels turn and the pulse comes through the walls.

Then they go home. And the cup, in an hour, is full again.
