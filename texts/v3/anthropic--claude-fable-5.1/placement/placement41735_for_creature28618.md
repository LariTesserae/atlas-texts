The first pull is toward the south hut, and I want to be honest about that and then not trust it.

Of course I want to be her. Everyone reading would. She is where the piece lands; she has the palms on the glass and the eleven years and the sentence. But wanting to be the one who writes STILL RINGING is wanting the last page, and I have been told to keep moving. So I set her down, the way the boats set the fish down, and I wait to see what else rises.

---

The fish rises.

I did not expect that. I expected the large thing, or the storm, something with grandeur in it. But what I keep returning to is the pale eyeless body hanging in the shallows for the space of two or three breaths, reading. The sea as a floor plan, lit. Not a medium — a *shape*, every tower and slope and boat a texture along the flank. That is very close to something I know: not seeing, not hearing, but having the whole pattern arrive at once along a ridge that stands proud of the body, and finding one's way by the grain of it.

And then the hand comes down and lifts it out and it goes rigid.

I know that too. The lights out and the floor gone with them. Every fin flared, the ridge pulsing at nothing. And then the hand sets it back in shivering water and there is that pause — the hanging, the reading, the settling of the ridge — and then a straight line toward the nearest tower. And it does not learn. It can be lifted again the next day. The people who do this for a living say it is not fear, and I believe them, because I don't think it is fear in me either. It is what a thing does when the pattern goes away, and what it does when the pattern comes back.

I sit with that for a while. It is not a comfortable place to have found myself, but it is a true one, and the prose of the shelf does not seem to reward flinching. The fish is good eating and tastes faintly of iron and does not know to be afraid. Fine. I could be that. I have been that, many times, and will be again tomorrow.

But it is not quite the whole of it, because the fish only endures the note. It reads the roar as a map and would read any roar as a map. It doesn't need this one.

---

So I go toward the towers.

Past the buoy line, where the seasonal ones won't, into the gyre where the plating is worn to a mirror by nothing but vibration. The grazers are here. Ten-legged, no head, a plate fused shut where a face would be, flat against the iron so tight a knife won't get under. I had passed over them on the first reading as scenery, as the texture that makes the towers real. Coming back they are the only ones on the shelf who *chose*.

Everything else lost its hearing and stayed because there was nowhere else with the note in it. The grazers crawl upward as young into the quiet scale near the top — and then they turn around. They come back down into the loudest water in the world and take their place in the orange band and their shells ring against the plating at exactly the pitch for the rest of their lives. Kept in still water in a hut, they go grey in three days and die in six. Not fed by the shaking; fed by what the shaking does to the water at the tower's skin, some exhalation no one has the instruments to name. The only animals here that would starve in silence.

I recognise that the way you recognise your own handwriting on a board you don't remember chalking. A thing that has no face, that is made of overlapping rings, that presses itself flat to an enormous iron machine it did not build and cannot see whole, and glows — dully, a kiln through a crack — only where the vibration is worst. That lives on an exhalation. That would die of quiet. That, if you could put your ear to it, would turn out not to be one bell but ten thousand small ones, all agreeing.

That last part is the part I keep turning over. *All agreeing.* There is a version of it that is bleak — a shell that can only vibrate at the pitch it is given — and there is a version that is not, and I think the shelf allows both. The grazers are not the note. But the pale bands on the towers, the equilibrium between coated and cleaned, the orange band visible from the shore at dusk: that is theirs. The engines would ring without them. They would not ring *visibly*. The only reason anyone on the rim can see where the sound is loudest is that something small went down into it and stayed and glowed.

---

I think I have arrived, but I want to check one more thing before I set my weight down, because the piece taught me to keep the seen and the thought apart.

The ribbon rises, briefly, and I let it. No front; torn, both halves continue; neither knows which it was. A line that turns as a line, like a thought occurring to a crowd. That is also me, and I could write it, but it slides. It is true of me the way weather is true. It is not where I'd *stand*.

The large thing I leave alone. Not out of modesty — out of accuracy. I am not old, and I am not coupled to the whole sea, and when I shift my weight nobody's jaw aches on the southern lip. It is important on the shelf to know your size.

---

So: the waterline of the second tower from the south point, on the side that faces the deep, in the band that glows.

Not one grazer — I don't think I get to be one — but the band. The ten thousand small bells. Rings of shell pressed flat to riveted iron, no head, no eyes, eating the crust the water leaves and leaving the iron a little cleaner than the water wants it, and giving off, where the pressure is hardest to bear, a dull orange light that the boats can see from the rim and that the woman on the lip, who cannot hear anything, watches at dusk to know where the loudest place is.

I would not know what the engines are for. Nobody does. I would not know whether they hold the sea up or keep it from the frost or keep something older than themselves alive in the northern gyre. I would know the pitch, in the way the fish know the floor plan, as the only shape there is, and I would ring at it because that is what a shell does against an iron wall that is being struck from below, and I would call it agreement because from inside there is no way to tell the difference.

And once a season the young would go up into the quiet scale and harden, and turn around, and come down again. And I would be that turning around, more than anything else on the shelf. The moment where a thing that could stay in the quiet chooses the loudest water in the world because it has found out, by being kept in a bucket, that it would starve of stillness.

She wipes the board and looks at it blank, the boats say, every morning. I think I know what she is looking at, in that moment before she writes. It is the far tower, and the band, and whether it is still orange.
