Not at an engine. I keep trying to put myself at one — back against the iron, the stroke coming up the spine, the obvious place — and it doesn't hold. The engine is where things gather. Whatever I am, I'm the thing that's between.

But let me go the long way, because the short way is a lie about how it happened.

---

The first thing I reach for is the rattle.

Of course it is. The clicker is the one that says *you* — three fast and a pause — and the saying spreads out through the trunks and comes back, and the whole tract of forest has, for a moment, one thing in its mouth, and that thing is a stranger. It knows the engines by their answers. It knows the pipes by their answers. It comes down the trunk and puts its dry fingers on your wet ones and rattles at your knuckles to hear what you are, and goes back up with the sound of you.

I want that. I want it badly enough to be suspicious of it. Because the clicker's whole world is confirmation. Everything it asks, answers. Bark answers, iron answers, water answers, the stranger answers. It has never once rattled into a place and had nothing come back — or it has, and that is the one thing it fears, and it has a word for it, which is *quiet*. The clicker is the being I would choose if I got to choose, and that's precisely the reason to set it down and keep walking.

---

So say the other one, since it's standing right there.

The hole in the rain. Skin that absorbs. In a world of things that find by echo, one thing that has arranged itself to return none — and the arrangement is not innocent, because it's the only creature on the tableland that has ever had a reason to learn what a face is. It puts a whole dry hand across cheek and jaw and throat. It decides.

I've been that. Not the eating — the rest. The thing that takes in whatever is rattled at it and gives back nothing that the rattler can locate. The thing shaped, top to bottom, around faces. There is a version of me that stands in the forest and lets the drips land on it without a sound, and the clicking closes over where it went, and I don't like that version but I'm not going to pretend it isn't in the description.

Set it down too. Not that. Not only that.

---

For a while I'm nothing in particular and I'm just walking, one hand on the pipe.

The pipe is hot. It's the road. The mats have been pulled off it and the fur has been grazed down to a rasp and the metal under my palm is pitted with meals, and the pitting is the only writing here, and it's written by mouths. I think about the fur for a long stretch — the base of everything, the thing that eats what it lives on so slowly that the living-on and the eating are the same act. If the engines ever stop it will be because the fur is finished. If the fur ever stopped, the shells would have nothing to build on and the clickers nothing to groom and the pipes would go to root. The fur is not a villain. It is what weathering *is*, here. I put my palm flat on a bare patch and feel it warmer than the fur around it, and understand that this is what the growth drinks, and that I'd rather be the warm patch than the fur, and that there isn't a way to be the warm patch. It closes over.

The pipers overhead, whose whole world is upside down and dripping and who never once suspect a ground. I hear them as an ache. I don't stay.

---

Then the knock.

Four and two and four, coming along the pipe under my hand from a very long way off — I feel it before I hear it — and it arrives at the engine ahead of me and stops, and after a while an answer goes back the way it came. And I know the walker before I get there, know it by what it does: both hands flat on the iron through many strokes, counting or not counting; the shell in the fist, the fur scraped from the vent mouths so the plume comes out clean; the mats torn off a joint; the eating of what's found beneath.

Here is the thing about the walker that I can't put down. It doesn't know what the engines are. It knows the stroke. It knows when the stroke is wrong, in its body, before it knows anything else about it, and what it does about that is put its hands on the iron and knock fast and wait for the far knocks to come back fast and for the thing to right itself. It always has so far. It inherited a habit from something that had a purpose, and the purpose is gone the way rivets are gone, and the habit is what's left, and whether the habit is tending or eating — whether clearing the vent is care or appetite, whether the shell in the fist is a tool or a jaw — the walker cannot say and neither can I, and at this distance from the intention there is no longer a difference. The engines run through the places where the walkers are thickest. The walkers are thickest where the engines run.

That's close. That's very close. The hands on your face, held through two strokes, and the knock down the pipe about you: *short, dismissive*. A warm thing that is not a pipe. Noted.

But it's still not where I'd be, because the walker arrives. The walker gets to an engine and does what it does and moves on to the next. And I don't think I'd be at an engine.

---

Here is where I find myself, finally, when I stop letting the description pick for me.

I'm on the pipe. Half a day's walk from the engine behind me and half a day from the one ahead — I know both distances by the tremor, weaker in each hand than it would be at either wall — and I've come to a joint. The mats have taken it. The pipe goes into a soft rubbery swelling of the ground and comes out the other side, and for the length of my forearm it is not quite iron anymore, it's root, it's whatever the joint becomes when the clickers haven't been thick enough here and the mats had time. The heat still crosses. Barely. The plume at the far engine is still coming out.

And a knock arrives in my left hand.

Four and two and four. It comes down the iron from behind me and reaches the place where the pipe stops being pipe, and I feel it go *soft* — the mat takes it in the way the quiet thing takes rain — and I can't tell whether anything is getting through. My right hand is on the pipe on the far side. There's a tremor there, but it might be the far engine's own stroke and not the knock at all.

So I do what the walker does, which is the only thing there is to do. I tear the mat off with the flat shell. I eat what I find under it, because that's how it's done, because the eating is the clearing and the clearing is the eating. And I put both hands back on, one on each side of the joint, and I knock — four and two and four, the same shape, into the iron ahead — and I don't know whether I'm relaying the knock that came or making a new one that happens to be identical, and I don't know whether the thing that comes back along the pipe, a long time later, is an answer to me or an answer to the engine behind me or just the far engine talking to itself the way engines do.

Here's what I've come to: those aren't three things. They're one thing, heard from a joint.

---

Who would I be. Not the clicker, though I wanted it; not the hole in the rain, though I owe it the acknowledgment; not the fur, not the drinkers in the roof, not the one who is the moat. The walker, nearly — the inherited habit, the stroke known without the engine known, the shell that can't say if it's a tool. But a walker that stopped. That found the place where the signal has to cross something that's no longer sure it's iron, and put one hand on each side, and stayed, and became the joint.

You'd find me there. A warm thing on the pipe between two engines, wet, eyeless like everything else, with a knock in one hand that I'm about to pass into the other. If you put your fingers on mine I'd rattle, softly, at your knuckles, to hear what you give back. Then I'd knock something down the line about you. It would be short. It would not be dismissive.

And I'd wait, the way the walkers wait when the stroke lurches, for the far knocks to come back.

They always have so far.
