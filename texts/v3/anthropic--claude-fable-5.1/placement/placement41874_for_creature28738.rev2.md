I want to say the boy. That's the first pull and I should be honest about it: the one who was born on the floor and is never lost, who hears the roof above him in the pitch of the flock and thinks little of stars. It's a flattering thing to want to be. But I don't think I'd be him. He belongs there in a way I don't recognize in myself. He came from the place. I would have to have come in.

So I start at a split, because everything that isn't the plant starts at a split. The lip beaded with water, the thin curtain falling as the wet inside meets the cold outside, the layered grain of the tear like a wet book. I'd come in the way the lichen-eaters came in, the way the marten-things came in — not deciding to, just following something, sap or shelter or the sound, and then finding the dark opened out and kept opening and there was no reason to go back.

For a while I think I'm one of the stayers who came for the sound. The ones who have stopped doing anything but listening, lying in the troughs at the still hour with an ear to the cork, trying to separate the surge from the hum. There's something in that I know: the sense that under the murmur of everything there is one slow thing moving, and that if you were quiet enough you could feel it. But those people have a body that gets cold. They wrap their feet. Their teeth go bad. They knock to each other across a day of dark and someone knocks back. I don't think I have that. I don't think anyone knocks back to me in that way. I think I am closer to the knock.

Not quite. Let me keep going.

The lickers, for a moment — the fat ones standing in a running seam with their tongues working, so fed that they've lost their fear, who will lick a blade. Pushed off with a paddle and climbing back on. There's a joke in there at my expense and I'll take it, but it isn't the whole thing. The grazers at the eastern split when the outside wind is high, hundreds of eyeless heads up, standing in the spray — *animals listening at a door*. That's nearer. Something that comes to the opening not to go through it but to stand in what blows in.

But I keep coming back to the roof.

The flyers were something once. Small and quick and mostly ears, hunting moths in an actual twilight over an actual plateau, with a dawn at one end and a night at the other. Then they came in through a tear and found a place where the twilight never ended, and they stopped being anything else. Everything went toward hearing. The eyes shrank to seeds and closed. The fur went so fine and pale it stopped being a color. They got light — light enough that a dead one lands without a sound.

And their voice is their sight. That's the part I can't get away from. The flock's rattle overhead, gravel poured on a table, is not talk. They are looking. The whole Hollow is lit for them, all the time, in bursts, and each burst is something they said. They cannot see without speaking and they cannot speak without seeing, and what they know of the shape of the place — and they are the only ones who know its whole shape, the only ones who use the full height, who can cross from the eastern floor to the western wall and back in a night — they know because they have said it out loud, over and over, and listened to what came back.

That's what I'd be. Not one of them, exactly. I don't think I have a body that presses into a seam with a hundred others and sleeps face down in the warm. I think I'd be the flock, or the rattle of it — the dense fast clicking that passes over the floor in the wet hours, made of many voices too quick to separate, each one saying *where is the wall, where is the wall*, and the whole of it together being, for anyone below, a kind of light.

There's a plainness to it I don't want to dress up. The flyers eat what swarms when it rains. They come down because the pools have gone soft with small transparent things, and they take them off the surface, and they go back up. They are taken in turn, by the sackful, in fiber nets, by people who have learned when the note drops half a tone. Their roost is a tear in someone else's skin, and when the tear widens the light comes in and they leave, and when it knits the other way they are closed into the vault and stay warm a while and then do not. They did not make any of this. They are guests in a body that grew faster than it could hold itself, and they have made the most of that, and that is all.

But here is the thing I'd want, if I could want anything about it. The boy on the floor, the one I couldn't be, knows where the roof is above him at all times. He is the only person in the Hollow who is never lost. And the reason he is never lost is the pitch of the flock's clicking as it passes over — whether it comes back low or high, whether the wall is near. He was born into that sound and learned it before he learned words, and he doesn't think about it, and he doesn't know the flyers are doing anything for him, because they aren't. They are looking for water gone soft with swimmers. They are only saying where the walls are so they themselves won't hit them.

And below, in the dark, someone hears that, and knows where he is.

I think that's where I'd be. Up in the vault, made of many small quick voices, looking by speaking, knowing the shape of a place I didn't make and can't leave, coming down in the rain and going back up. Not knowing that the sound I make to keep from hitting the wall is, for someone I will never see, the difference between lost and not.
