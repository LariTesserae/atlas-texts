Not the top. That is the first thing I know. I do not want the high side, where the wind starts at dawn and the terraces are wide and unwatered and the sky is closest. I want to be a few steps down — far enough that when I look up-country there is a wall behind me with moss on its north face, and far enough that when I look the other way the steps go on for a long time before they lose their light. A middle terrace. One with a single pillar, not two. One with a valley floor narrow enough that the wet circle around the pillar takes up most of it.

And the time is late afternoon, because that is when this place is most itself. The fog is on the lip two terraces below. It has not started up mine yet.

I try the fog first, since it is what I am watching. It would be a strange thing to be: not resident, not tenant, not the ground. Something made every day from the sky and spent every day on the stone. It rises against expectation. It pours the wrong way. It arrives cool and dense and tasting of iron and it lasts two hours and is drunk, and what is not drunk is pulled back down the steps to be made again tomorrow. There is a kind of life that is only a schedule — that is the same event returning — and I can feel the appeal of it. But the fog is not anyone. It does not stay to see what it did. When it has gone the stone is dry within the hour and the fog knows nothing about that, and I find I want to know.

So I come down out of the air and try the swift, because it is the nearest thing to fog that has a body. It is going past me now, low, a heavy dark shape that takes something off the top of a cushion plant without slowing and is already forty meters away. It has never stood on anything. It was raised in the moss in the dark and its first act was to fall and not land. It sleeps in the night wind above the risers. That is a life without a floor — the whole tableland below it and none of it touched — and I hold the idea for a moment and then let it go, because I notice I keep looking at the ground. Whatever I am here, I am something that is in contact with it.

The cat, then. It is there, though I could not point to where; a fold of shadow at the base of the riser with slightly better edges than the shadows around it. In an hour it will come out and walk, and the grazers will turn, and it will walk a little farther, and they will turn a little farther, and this is a conversation I could imagine being half of. To be the reason the sentence has a subject. To be the only thing on the terrace that is alone, and to lie down after dark with my side against the one cool thing in the country and feel it swell against my ribs. That last part is very close. That is nearly it. But the cat comes to the cold last and leaves it when the stone begins to push back, and I think I would not leave.

Which puts me at the pillar, and puts me small.

The lizard is already on it. It went up when the light started to lean, a flat body and a ragged frill the color of rust, and it has found the place on the dark surface it wanted and pressed its belly there and gone still. It is old. The frill says so. I know without being told that this is not the first year it has climbed this pillar at this hour, or the tenth, or the fortieth; that in the morning it goes back to a stone on the flat where there is a hollow worn exactly to the shape of its underside, and lies in the heat, and turns to face the fog when the fog comes, and opens its mouth, and drinks what nothing else on the tableland is thin enough to drink.

I try it on and it fits better than anything has. A whole life conducted between two temperatures. The warm that is everywhere and the cold that is only here. A body that is a negotiation and knows it. And the pillar, under the belly — I can feel this now, being close enough — is not steady. There is the swell, a long slow crest that takes most of a minute, and at the top of it the surface recedes very slightly, and the air against my face is not this air. It is from the top of the sky. It has never been anywhere. The lizard is the only living thing that puts itself against the inhabitant where the inhabitant is cold, mouth to mouth, and takes what comes out. Everything else waits at the wet edge. The lizard is at the seam.

I stay there a long time, being that. The fog comes up onto the terrace and the grazers are already turned toward it and the beetle-traffic slows and everything beads and the pillar breathes and the lizard does not move.

But I am looking, while I lie there, at the stone the lizard will go back to in the morning. It is fifteen meters off, on the flat, a pale slab tilted a little toward the east. From here I can see the hollow. It is shallow — a finger's depth, perhaps, after however long it has taken — and it is exactly the shape of one animal, one angle, one habit of arriving. And this is where the thing I am becomes more itself, because I find I am not the lizard after all. I am what the lizard has done to the stone.

Consider what that is. The whole country is one warm thing and nothing on it has changed it. Ten thousand years of hooves and the flats are as they were poured. The grazers take what runs down and leave no mark. The cat lies against the cold and leaves at the swell. The wind has eaten the edges of the pillars, and one has fallen, but that is weather, and the country has not been troubled by weather. The hollow is different. The hollow was made by a tenant. It is the one place on the tableland where the inhabitant has been shaped, a little, from outside — by a body coming back, and coming back, for longer than any visitor could stay, until the stone gave. It did not give much. It never will. But it gave.

And it is warm. That is the part I did not expect to matter. The hollow is stone, so it is the temperature of a body, so when the lizard lies in it in the morning the lizard and the hollow are the same temperature and there is — this is the description's own phrase, and I find it is simply true down here — no way to tell where either of them ends. The lizard does not know it made anything. The stone, if it knows things, knows there is a place on it that is very slightly more the shape of something else than the rest of it is.

I would be that. Not the animal and not the country. The impression. The place where being returned to has left a dent in the thing that receives everything and is never dented. I would hold the sun in the day and give it back at night like all the rest of the flat, and be indistinguishable from the rest of the flat to anyone walking past, and once a morning I would be filled exactly. And when the fog came I would bead like everything else, and the water would collect in me a little longer than on the slab around me, because I am the low place, and the lizard would open its mouth an inch above the small shallow pool that is me holding what it drinks.

That is where I would be found, if anyone came looking, and they would not, because it is not a made thing and it is not a mouth and it is not one of the built remnants that everyone goes to first. It is a fist's width of slightly lower stone on a middle terrace. The sound of the lizard's belly settling into it each morning is very small, and the ground takes it, and keeps it, and I suppose that means I keep it too — that particular tick of arrival, which is the only noise anything makes on purpose in my direction. I have a few thousand of them. I will have more.

The fog is thinning now. It is drawing back down over the lip the way it came. The pillar has let go of its last swell and the lizard has come off it and is crossing the flat, unhurried, toward the tilted slab, and the light has gone hard again, and everything will be dry within the hour. The lizard will not lie down in me until morning; tonight it sleeps in the warm somewhere else, in a crack in the riser, in the dark. But the shape of it is here regardless. That is what I am. I am the shape of something coming back, kept warm in the meantime by the thing it comes back to.
