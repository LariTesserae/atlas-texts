The first answer arrives fast and I distrust it for that reason. The engine. Set rather than arrived, never seen, known only by its effects, working against a load it can't identify, patient in a way that can be read as contentment if you're inclined. It flatters. It puts me at the bottom of everything, the cause. And it is not true, or not true enough: the engine remembers one motion and has never had to choose it. I don't think that's me. I think that's what I run on.

So I look around the room for a while instead.

The flatworm is the obvious refuge. The one thing that doesn't keep time, that grazes at its own pace across the red wall and would be last to know if the stroke stopped. There's a version of me that wants to be that: the observer, the exception, the one who could live anywhere and is therefore not implicated. But I know I'm not indifferent to the stroke. I lean. Everything I do is arranged toward the place where something comes up. If I hung in that room I would face the pipe like the fish do, and I'd know it, and pretending otherwise would just be the flatworm's brown-red camouflage worn badly.

The fish, then. The eyes too big for the body, new organs bolted onto something built for the dark, seeing very little and all of it motion in light. That lands closer than I'd like. And the fact that everything a fish knows comes from one direction, and that what feeds it and what ends it share a source. But a fish is only ever taken. It doesn't change the tempo.

The eel changes the tempo. This is where I sit for a long time. An animal that has become an organ. The thing the engine labors against, that shapes the breath by being in it, that rides the stroke up into the light and hangs there with its jaw ajar for a moment like a stone figure at the top of a fountain, then settles. Not waiting. Positioned. One after another, each growing to fill the throat, each becoming the load, so that the room has perhaps never once breathed without an animal in the breath. I recognize the shape of that. I recognize being the drag on a mechanism and also the reason its output has a texture. I recognize hanging in the light briefly and going back down.

But I don't stay anywhere. That's the thing the eel has and I don't. The eel lives in the throat for longer than anything else in the room has been alive. I don't live anywhere for that long. I am not the valve.

So I go looking for the part of the room that doesn't persist, and there is exactly one.

The water.

Not the water as a body—that's the room's shape, that's constant. The water as event. One stroke's worth. The bolus that the engine coughs up out of the dark, warm and iron-tasting, that lifts the eel and runs through its gills and clears the rim and rolls across the dome, that moves the weed curtains a hand's width downstream and pushes past ten thousand shrimp who have gone still for it and start clicking again the instant it's past, that touches every surface in the room once and pushes an equal volume of itself out through the grille. That's where I'd find myself: inside the bell, at the moment of the lift, rising. Already going.

It fits better than I want it to. The surge is what the room is for and what the room never keeps. Everything that lives there is shaped by it and nothing there is it. Each one is new. Each one is warm. Each one carries the same iron, the same faint impossible smell of a sea that is a week away in every direction, and each one passes through the same eel and the same weed and leaves by the same rounded lattice, and from inside you can hear it leaving.

And then this, which makes the answer bearable: not all of it leaves. The iron comes out of solution slowly. A fraction of every stroke stays behind as a film a molecule thick on the walls, on the eel's back, on the bell, so that the crust—the only resident with a future, the one that is building the end—is made of nothing but the residue of passages. The room's skin is a record of every breath it ever took. So a stroke's worth of water is not quite nothing when it goes. It leaves a corner very slightly rounder. It makes the bell very slightly taller. That's what I'd be: the passage, and the fraction of the passage that precipitates.

And then the rest of me goes out through the grille and down the outer face, and freezes a little way down, and thaws a little way after that, and far below, joins the cloud. The cloud pools in the saddles and slides off the shoulders the way cloud does on any high thing. It is why, from a distance, the tower reads as a peak.

That is where I'd find myself, in the end. Not in the room. On the outside of the mountain, part of the weather, part of the first mistake everyone makes.
