I want to start by telling you the thing I'd want to be, and then I want to distrust it, because the first thing I want is almost certainly the stranger's answer.

The stranger's answer is: the one who goes down. Of course it is. You read about a plateau that has lost nine people to the cloud in its whole history and knows every one by name and none by fate, and something in you leans toward the edge with them. A coil of rope, a sack of figs, the first rung of the lattice under the horn of the foot. The cutters' talk of the other note. It's the story-shaped place in this world, the one door with a hallway behind it, and the reader wants the door.

But I notice that the people who live here don't want it. Nine in all their generations. It's the rarest thing they have. And I notice the reason I want it is that I'm standing outside, where the ears ring, and from outside the plateau looks like a question — *what's below, what's the coal, what's the brown at the bottom* — and the ones who go down are the ones who treat it as a question too. That's the stranger in me. There are no strangers here. If I'm going to be honest about where I'd find myself, I have to let the plateau stop being a question first, and see what's left.

So let me try the edge without the leaving. The cutters. Black-clad, narrow, milking the goats in shelters of scavenged plate, sawing the outer decks a hand's width at a time and dragging the iron inward to be made into rivets for the center. I like them. I like that they are the only ones who look at the plateau and see it eating them, and go on feeding it anyway. I like that they say things and are known to say things, which is the plateau's way of holding an idea at arm's length without dropping it. When the wind falls at the edge and they hear the older note from below, they don't go down and they don't forget. They just sleep among the goats and don't listen for it. That's a posture I recognize.

But the cutters are still edge-people. They're defined by what they're near. And I keep feeling the piece pull toward the middle — toward the bit of the description I didn't expect to snag on, which was not the boilers, not the cloud, but a woman walking a main in the morning, counting snails.

Stay there a moment. She's crew, so her hands are orange to the second knuckle and her head is cocked, always, a little to one side, and she has a name that is a sentence — let's not decide which yet. She is walking a trunk main under the canopy, in the green dusk that to her is broad daylight, and the main is wrapped in a mattress of moss that drinks the drip, and on the rail beside her the snails are sitting in their pale fist-sized shells. She is not looking at the pipe. She's counting. Where they cluster, the moss is thick; where the moss is thick, the iron under it is weeping; and a weep is the first word of a sentence that will not finish for twenty years.

That's the thing. She's reading a failure that will arrive after she is in the bed. She'll never see it. She's not doing this so she can fix the pipe — the pipe doesn't need fixing, and won't in her lifetime — she's doing it so that somebody with her name, or a name like hers, walking this main in twenty years, will find the moss already cut back and a note in the crew-dialect that says *third bend, weeping since Seat's time, watched*. Her whole work is attention handed forward. She is not a caretaker exactly. She's a form of memory the iron grew.

I could stop there. But you said don't settle, so let me push at it and see if it holds or if it opens.

What is she listening for, with the cocked head? A change in a sound that hasn't changed in her lifetime. The chord is in her chest — has been since before milk — and the pumps have their rhythm and the north sluice valve shrieks when it shrieks, and she has never once heard any of it be different. And still the head is cocked. Not out of fear. The plateau does not fear falling; it counts struts. She listens because listening is what a crew-woman is, the way a lizard is a thing that lies on a rivet. If the chord changed pitch by a hair she would know, and she would be the first to know, and she has no idea what she would do about it, and that isn't the point.

Here's where it becomes more itself, I think. Because I was going to say I'd be her, and I still might, but I notice a different possibility underneath: I could be the thing she reads.

Not the pipe. The gauge.

Somewhere on that main there's a boiler-gauge, brass gone green, its face under a rind of moss so thick that once a year a crew-hand comes with a scraper and clears it so the needle can be read. And the needle has not moved. It sits where it was set before anyone's grandfather was born, at a pressure nobody knows how to alter, and once a year a pair of orange hands scrapes the moss away and a pair of eyes made for the dark bends close and reads it and says the number aloud to nobody, and writes it in the dialect, and the moss closes back over.

There's something in that I can't quite look away from. A face whose entire function is to be read. That says the same thing every year and is asked every year anyway, because the asking is the maintenance — not the answer. That spends the other three hundred and sixty-some days under the moss, warm, in the dark, with the chord coming up through its mounting, holding a reading no one is looking at. That doesn't know whether the number it shows is the number the boilers actually hold, down in the red, and has no way to find out, and is trusted regardless because it has never been wrong that anyone could check.

I'll let that stand as true — some part of me would find itself there, on the main, green-faced, waiting to be scraped — and then I'll say why it isn't the answer. The gauge can't cock its head. It can't count snails. It's read; it doesn't read. And what moved me about the plateau, once I stopped treating it as a question, was not that it's a machine that gets read. It's that it grew people to read it, and the reading is a kind of tending, and the tending is a kind of love that doesn't know it's love because it has no word for the alternative. The gauge is the thing attended to. I would rather be the attention.

So: her. Let me finish her.

She's on the north-side trunk, third bend, in the hour after the fog burns off when the whole bed is steaming and the light under the ferns goes from green to gold-green and the insects come up to their day pitch. Her cloth is pushed up on her forehead — she won't need it until she crosses the open — and her hair is wet from the ferns, and she has stopped, because there are eleven snails on a span of rail where last season there were six. She lifts one. It's heavy and cool and its shell is the chalk-pale of everything up here that lives in the light, and she holds it a moment against her sternum, where the chord is, and then sets it down two rungs along, gently. A colleague moved to another shift.

Then she puts her palm flat on the moss over the pipe. Through the felt, through the iron, the chord comes up into the hand — not a sound, the shape of the inside of the chest, the thing the tower-keepers climb down gasping for — and she stands like that, head cocked, listening to a sound that has never changed, ready for it to.

Her name: she was born while her mother's hands were on a valve on this very main, and so she is called for it, and the name is a sentence, and the sentence is *third bend, weeping, watched*. Not for anything the valve did. For what was done to it. She is Watched, and thinks nothing of it, and when she has a child on this main — she will — the child will be called for whatever her hands are on when the pains come, and she hopes it's nothing that scalds anyone.

She has heard the cutters' talk. Everyone has. Once, at the edge, delivering rivets, on a still evening when the wind dropped, she stood on the outer decking with her palm on a strut and thought she felt it: the other note, older, under the chord. She has never told anyone. She is not going down. What she did with it — what she does with it — is this: she comes back to the third bend every morning, and counts, and puts her hand on the iron, and holds the plateau's one unchanging note in her palm, and listens to it more carefully than anyone alive, because now she knows what it would sound like for it not to be the only one.

That's where I'd be. Not at the edge, not in the cloud, not under the moss. On the main, in the middle of the seen, one hand on the pipe. Reading a slow failure I'll never see the end of, for someone with my name who hasn't been born, in a world that has not fallen yet and calls that the temperature of things. One more thing that grew on the iron. The thing the machine turned out to need in order to go on being a forest: something that listens, and stays.
