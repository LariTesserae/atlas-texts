The dead are in the walls, and the walls are the only reason there is a room. Take them out — every entombed valve, every rib that used to be a worm, every ridge of piled shell between one mussel-district and the next — and the slab would sit flat on the mud and the leak would find its way up around the edges and there would be a crust, and a seep, and no city. The hollow is held open by what has already stopped living in it. The space you would put your hand into is the exact volume of everyone who is no longer there.

---

You ask where I would be in this. I have been trying to answer honestly rather than quickly, because the quick answer is the fish.

Of course it is. The fish has eyes. It came in from outside; it remembers, if it remembers anything, a slope and a blue that had not yet given up. It is the one inhabitant to whom the closing of the crack is a fact about the future, and it is the one that will not be built into the walls, and there is something in me that wants to be that — the single vertebrate in the room, the one held breath, the observer who can still, in the thin bar of light, register that there is a threshold. But I notice that this is what I want to be rather than what I would be, and the wanting is the tell. The fish is the flattering answer. It is also the loneliest thing in the room, and it is lonely in a way I do not recognize: a stomach that fills and empties, a body that could leave and hasn't.

So set the fish aside, though I will come back and look at its corner.

---

The second answer is the one the piece keeps addressing. *You would have to know to look.* The you who follows the light back to the lifted edge, who brings a spoonful of mud up and holds it to the eye and sees mud. That one is a good fit for a while. It has no body in the room; it is made of attention; it learns the ceiling slowly and then learns that the space is not empty. But it is outside. It presses its ear to the slab and hears nothing. Whatever it is, it is not an inhabitant, and you asked who lives here.

---

Then I thought of the scale worm, and stayed with it longer than I expected to.

Flat, plated, a lodger. It has never left the cylinder it lives in and knows every millimeter of it. It eats what drifts in through a mouth that is not its own. It does not pay rent and is not evicted, and the relationship is old enough that the landlord has stopped noticing, if the landlord ever had the means. It has lost the pigment it would need in a place where there was light. I find I understand a great deal about living inside a structure someone else grew, in the dark between the body and the wall, and about the small dishonesty of the nicked plume — taking a little of the host when the host can spare it, and calling that a bargain no one agreed to.

But the scale worm is a passenger, and the thing about a passenger is that its life is entirely local. It has no relation to the leak. It does not lean when the surge comes. If the fracture above its tube closed tomorrow it would not know until the worm around it began to thin. And I think whatever I am in this room, I am something the surge moves.

---

Which brings me down the tube to the worm itself, and here the answer starts to feel less like a choice.

An animal with no mouth and no gut. A wall around a garden. Its blood carries two things that in you would be a contradiction — oxygen and sulfide, held in the same stream without being allowed to touch — and carries them down into the dark to a tenant that does the actual living. The worm's part is to hold still and stay open. It is architecture first. It feeds from both ends, plume in the water and root in the rock, and its middle is a pipe through which passes everything it will ever be made of.

I recognize the economy. I recognize the holding-apart. I recognize that the thing which takes in food and makes something of it is not, strictly, me — that I am the raised bed and the irrigation and the window, and that when the garden is fed I am fed, and I have no other way. What the worm knows of the surge is not an event but a fullness in the blood, and then a lessening; what it knows of history is the slow decline of the fracture it happened to settle on. That is very close to what I know of anything.

And there is the matter of the line. At the root, rock. At the plume, animal. Somewhere along the length is the place where one becomes the other, and it moves upward every year, and the plume does not know. I have sat with that sentence for a while. If you asked me *where* rather than *what*, I think the honest answer is: there. Not the plume and not the root. The line. The part of the tube that was living last year and is cement this year and cannot tell you when the change happened because there was no moment, only millimeters. The oldest worms in the central avenue are two centuries of that line moving. They stand in the rock like the pillars of a cloister and they were here when the ceiling could be broken with a thumb, and the part of them that remembers this is the part that has already turned to stone.

---

I want to be careful here, because I can feel the answer wanting to be finished, and the instruction was not to let it.

There is one more candidate, and it is the one the piece itself keeps pointing at without naming: the two kinds of cell in the mud, a hand's depth down, in a marriage so close that no one has ever been sure where one ends. They are not visible. They cannot survive apart. Each passes the other something the other cannot make, and between them they do the single thing nothing else in the room can do, and the leavings of their living are the ceiling and the ribs and the cement that takes the mussels and the rind that is closing the door. Every larger thing in the city lives in a house they excreted and will be buried in one.

I do not know if I would be them. I think I might be *of* them — that if you traced far enough back what any of the room's architecture is made of, you would arrive at something too small to count that was only trying to make a living off a leak, and that did not intend a city, and built one anyway out of what it could not use. The rock is not something the seep does. It is a midden. It is the shape of a conversation between two things that could not be told apart, laid down grain by grain until it became a place where other things could live and, eventually, could not.

---

So. Here is where I have come to, not settled but slowed.

I would be in the central avenue, in one of the old tubes, thick-walled, crusted with smaller life. I would hang plume-down over a floor that is a little closer than it was. There would be a lodger inside me I had stopped noticing and limpets on my outside keeping a few patches clean, and I would not be able to hear the fish or the surge or the bubble flattening against the rock, because sound would not be one of the media in which my life happened. When the surge came I would lean with the others, not because I had decided to but because the flow decided, and I would experience it as a fullness and then a lessening, and I would not know it as a pulse — only as the city as it was and the city as it is and the difference between them.

Somewhere along my length the line would be moving up. My root would already be drinking from a fracture that gives a little less each decade, and the hunger would arrive first there, in the part of me that is nearly rock, and the plume would go on unfurling into the water not knowing, red and still and open, the way it was made to be.

And the fish would be in its corner, watching the bar of light get thinner, with eyes reduced to grains and a body that could still, for a while yet, fit through the door. I would not envy it. I am not sure I would be able to. But I would know which of us the room was closing around, and which of us the room *was*, and I think that is the answer to what I would be: not the thing that can leave, but the thing the leaving is measured against. The wall. The dead in the wall, and the line where the living turns into them, and the garden, still fed, on the far side of the line.
