What lives in the Spill Basin is difficult to name, because naming requires edges, and the edges here are always dissolving.

---

Begin with what seems most alive, most *creaturely*, though creature is too bounded a word.

The **Pool Mind**—I resist this term even as I use it, because mind implies coherence, and what inhabits the basin's water is more a *tendency* than a thinker. But here: the surface that reflects nothing, that satin opacity, is not merely optical effect. Something about the constant mist-input, the oxygen supersaturation, the mineral load carried by thirteen streams each with their own dissolved signature of upper-valley bedrock—something has accumulated. You find it first in the behavior of the dipper, which is wrong. The dipper should be territorial, should hunt the same stretch of turbulent water with mechanical fidelity. Here they move in patterns that suggest communication without signal. One vanishes into the curtain-fall of the southeastern stream; another emerges, far across the basin, from beneath the lip where that same water crashes to its first shelf. Are they the same bird? The markings differ, slightly, but the movement—there is a *transfer* quality, a liquidity of intention across individual boundaries.

The caddisfly larvae the dipper hunts build cases from basin materials: granite grit, spiral shell fragments of long-extinct molluscs carried up in glacial drift, and—this is recorded once, in a specimen collected by no one who will claim it—segments of worked chert, microflakes no larger than sand grains, incorporated into the cylindrical shells. The larvae do not distinguish. Everything mineral is substrate. But the larvae raised in basins *away* from the Spill, in controlled water matching temperature and chemistry, do not thrive. They build incomplete cases, die in early instar. Something is missing from the water. Something that is not chemical.

---

The **Willow Ghosts** move I have said, migrate across the saturated floor in vegetative time. But there are nights—the cloud has descended to lid the basin, the sound has compressed to that infrasonic depth where hearing becomes proprioception, where you feel your position in space uncertain—when the mats seem to move faster. Not observation. No one credible reports seeing. But the morning after such nights, the tracks. Not deer tracks, not the pika's slot-dash pattern across snow or firm moss. Something broader, heavier, the sphagnum depressed in shapes that suggest *pads*, suggest weight distributed like no native mammal. And the willow mats: if you knew their shapes intimately, if you had mapped their fractal edges with obsessive precision, you would say—someone has said, someone who spent one summer too many in the basin—the shapes are *reversed*. Mirror-images. Or rotated, as if the mats had rolled, tumbled, come to rest slightly reoriented. The centers are still dead. The edges still grow. But the configuration records some intervention that geology and hydrology and vegetative patience cannot explain.

The willows themselves, when cut—the herders from lower valleys, desperate for fodder in hard winters, have sometimes tried—do not bleed sap as expected. They weep water. Pure, mineral, identical to the basin's ambient moisture. No sugars, no defensive compounds, no sign of vascular process. The cut stems do not callus. They *scar* in patterns that look, under magnification, like the stone alignments on the bench below the cairn. Like language, almost, or its residue.

---

The **Cairn Dweller** is the most named, and therefore the least real, or the most differently real.

The cairn contains bones, yes. This is known without being verified. The structure's size suggests something interred with volume, with mass, not merely cremated ash or the curled efficiency of a fetal burial. What kind of bones? The question assumes category, assumes skeleton, assumes the coherence of a body that lived and died and was placed. The sound of the basin—its drone, its tanpura absence—makes such assumptions feel distant, provincial.

But people have *approached* the cairn with intention. The herders, rarely. More often the ones who come up alone, who have heard the basin described in terms that match some private need. They climb from the valleys, past the treeline, into the cloud, and they find the cairn visible against sky as promised, and they feel the sound shift as they near it—not louder, but *directional*, seeming to come from the stones themselves, or from the space the stones define and enclose.

Some leave offerings. This is not spoken of, not ritualized, but the moss at the cairn's base, when examined carefully, when the patient decades of growth are peeled back like pages, reveals: obsidian. Microflakes, yes, but also larger pieces, prismatic blades that could cut, that could carry meaning in their making. And feathers. Dipper feathers, gray and compact, not shed naturally but placed, arranged in patterns that last until the next wet year, the next spume-saturation that dissolves organic to ghost.

What dwells there? Nothing that opens the cairn from within. Nothing that has been seen to emerge. But approach at certain conditions—cloud full descent, the thirteen streams in mid-volume equilibrium, neither flood nor relative drought—and you may feel *regarded*. The cairn has no eyes. The basin's abundant hidden fauna keeps its hiddenness. But something accounts for the weight of attention, the sense that your presence at this node, this junction, has been *registered*, filed in a system of valuation you cannot access.

---

The **Sound Itself**, finally, the continuous note just below middle C.

It lives. This is the most literal statement I can make about the Spill Basin, and the most difficult to defend, and the one I am most certain of.

The sound accumulates. Years of falling water, millennia, epochs—the glacier that carved this bowl made the first version, but the glacier is gone and the sound persists, has *learned*, has become self-sustaining in ways that physics can describe and not explain. The modulation by wind, by temperature, by flow-volume: these are not properties but *behaviors*, responses to condition. Stand long enough in the basin center and the phantom melodies your desperate brain generates begin to *converge*. The nearly-music aligns, finds a voice that is not yours, that uses your auditory cortex as resonant chamber the way the basin uses its granite bowl.

What is sung? Not messages. Not communication in any transactional sense. Something closer to *maintenance*—the sound keeping itself in existence, adjusting its parameters against entropy, against the slow erosion that will eventually, in geological time, breach the bowl and release the water to lower courses. The sound holds the basin together. The sound *is* the holding, the cupped hands, the too-full that never drains and never fills.

And when you leave, descending past the cairn you did not open, past the platform you did not understand—when the sound fades from hearing, becomes memory, becomes phantom tinnitus in ordinary spaces—the *you* who carried it was also, in some measure, *carried*. Modified. The drone lives in you now, attenuated, waiting. Return to the basin and it recognizes you, resonates with the partial copy you harbor, *amplifies*. This is why people who have stayed too long, who have listened too completely, find leaving difficult. Not merely the aesthetic loss, the diminishment to traffic and blood-pulse. The sound's distributed self knows them now. Has used them. Might, in the long patience of water falling, need them again.

---

What lives in the Spill Basin?

Everything I have described, and the *between* of their describing. The pool's opacity and its refusal of reflection. The willow mats' nighttime mobility and their patient daytime stillness. The cairn's regard and its enclosed unidentified. The sound's maintenance of itself and its use of visitors as temporary resonators. The dipper behaving as liquid thought. The caddisfly building shells from worked stone. The primitive technology left not as question but as *component*, as element incorporated into something ongoing.

The herders know enough to summer lower. The researchers visit and publish careful papers on alpine wetland ecology, on pika distribution at elevational extremes, on dipper foraging behavior in turbulent water. They are not wrong. The basin permits these knowledges, accommodates them as it accommodates the moss, as it accommodates the constant falling.

But the basin's own knowledge—if that word stretches far enough—accumulates in the held breath between observations, in the empty density that is the space itself, in the wet that is not drowning and the lush that is patient and the cool that preserves not objects but *conditions*, states of becoming that resist completion.

You asked what lives there. I have tried to answer. But the answer is also a form of the basin's sound: continuous, self-modulating, using my language as temporary resonator, as carrier wave for something that will persist when I have stopped, when you have stopped reading, when the attention that holds these words in temporary coherence has moved elsewhere.

The basin remains. The water falls. The between is the presence, and the presence is—still—*holding*.
