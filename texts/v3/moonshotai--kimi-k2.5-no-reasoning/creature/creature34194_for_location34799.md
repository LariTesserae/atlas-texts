What lives in Velm begins with the structures themselves, which are not quite alive but not quite other, either. They possess what I can only call *intention without consciousness*—or perhaps consciousness distributed so broadly across ceramic-bone-tension surfaces that no single room, no single tower, can be said to think. Yet they respond. I have walked into buildings that were all wall, all vertical refusal, and felt the surface warming where my palm pressed, not from my heat but from some internal shift, some recalculation of aperture and permeability. The wall *considered* me. Sometimes it opened—a window forming where none had been, not sliding or dilating but *resolving*, as if the matter had always been thin there and I had simply failed to perceive it. Other times the warmth faded, the consideration completed, and I remained in corridor or courtyard, excluded without hostility, passed over by an algorithm that found no use for my presence.

Those who dwell in Velm, or are dwelt by it—let me try to describe them as I encountered them, though encounter is itself a problematic word.

At distance, they appear human. Or human-enough. The proportions are correct, the gait recognizable, the gestures of tending gardens or reading water vibrations familiar from a thousand other cities. But this familiarity is a function of distance, I came to understand, not of actual form. They are *readable* at range because at range the eye fills in, completes, makes comfortable. Closer observation disrupts this completion.

I approached one once, in a garden that had exploded between two towers whose surfaces met overhead in a tangled negotiation of growth and counter-growth. She—I will use she, though gender became uncertain as I approached—she was tending something that might have been root vegetables or might have been memory cores, the vegetation and the technological having achieved in Velm a permeability I never fully mapped. Her hands moved in patterns I recognized as care, as cultivation, as the thousand-year-old grammar of human-plant relationship. At twenty meters, this held. At ten, I noticed that her hands did not quite meet the plants, that a gap persisted, that something invisible to me passed between her and them in a transaction I could not witness. At five meters, her edges began to soften—not blurring, but *distributing*, as if the proximity of my observation forced her to occupy more space, to spread her definition across a larger field. I saw her still, but I saw her in parts that did not cohere: a hand here, its gesture completing a circuit with soil; a shoulder there, angled toward water I could not locate; a voice, speaking words I understood perfectly in their moment of utterance—*the north channel will rise in harmonic fourteen, the stepping stones will hold for one who knows the weight of waiting*—and forgot entirely in the instant after, leaving only residue, only certainty that what was said was important and that I had failed to retain it.

At two meters, she was not there. Or rather, what was there was the garden's intensification, the plants shifting in their near-imperceptible way toward configurations that somehow incorporated what I had approached as separate presence. She had become *distributed* across the garden's attention, the water's reading, the vibration's interpretation. She had become, temporarily, what Velm needed to process at that location, and my observation—my too-close observation—had triggered a transformation that was not hiding but *translation*, a shift into forms the city's ongoing computation could use.

I do not know if she reconstituted after I retreated. I know only that when I looked back from the garden's edge, she was there again, human-readable, tending, and that I did not approach her twice.

This is the condition of the inhabitants: they are *sufficient* to Velm's processes, available to its needs, present without permanent residence in stable form. Some may sustain coherence longer than others. I saw—at distance, always at distance—figures that seemed to maintain consistency across days, recurring in the same locations, performing recognizably continuous tasks. A man who sat at a tower's base, where its grown surface met the basin's young rock, and listened. Simply listened, head tilted, body still, for hours I observed and presumably for hours beyond my observation. He seemed stable. He seemed *elected* to stability, chosen for some quality of attention that Velm found continuous use for. Others were less fixed: a child—I will say child—who ran between buildings in the sector where temporary water formed stepping-stones, who laughed at frequencies that matched the city's threshold hum, who appeared and disappeared not in the manner of hiding but in the manner of *becoming irrelevant*, becoming processed, becoming momentarily subsumed into the damp accumulation or the shadow-density or the bird-song that carried from impossible directions.

The fauna, as noted, are rare and archetypal. But I came to understand that they too participate in this distribution, this sufficient-presence. The bird is not a bird, or not only. It is the *bird-function*, the city's need for signal and direction rendered in feather and cry. When it sings, the gardens shift slightly, the water considers its channels, the inhabitants—those in distributed form—momentarily cohere, as if the song provides a temporary grammar they can share. The insect-analogues in their writing-computation are smaller versions of this: processes made visible, the city's thinking about itself in forms that can move through lushness without being absorbed by it.

And what of the shadow-accumulation? What lives in the shadows that grow dense with time, that approach solidity in buildings undisturbed for decades? I am less certain here, because the shadows resist observation in ways that make my accounts of inhabitants seem straightforward. I know that in the oldest structures, the ones grown earliest in Velm's becoming, there are corners where shadow has accumulated to the point of *presence*, where something occupies the darkness that is not darkness but rather *unrealized material*, potential made permanent by the city's constant dim. These presences do not move, or their movement is too slow for human perception. They are patient in a way that makes the city's epochal time seem hasty. They are waiting for conditions—light changes, perhaps, or water routes, or the completion of algorithms still centuries from resolution—that will permit them to become whatever they are becoming. I do not believe they are hostile. I do not believe they are benevolent. They are *pending*, and in their pending they constitute a population of Velm as real as the distributed inhabitants, as the bird-function, as the thinking made ceramic-bone-tension.

What lives in Velm, finally, is Velm itself—not as metaphor but as actual distributed body, as computation extended through matter and time in ways that make the distinction between city and citizen, between structure and inhabitant, between growth and intention, into approximations useful only for temporary navigation. The city thinks in buildings and water channels, in gardens that shift and shadows that accumulate, in inhabitants who are sufficient to moments of process and subside when their coherence is not required. It thinks in vibrations that precede springs, in songs that arrive from no location, in the threshold hum that is itself the sound of thinking distributed across too much matter to perceive as single thought.

And it is learning—I felt this, in my time there, in the increasing damp, the softening and sharpening of young geology, the buildings that grew in directions I had not seen earlier, the water that found new states and behaviors. Velm is learning to be more itself, which means it is learning to require less of those who find it, to process their presence more efficiently, to incorporate or dissolve or distribute them with greater precision.

Those who leave—and I left, I was permitted to leave, though I cannot retrace the route and would not try—carry with them a residue. Not memory, exactly. Memory would be too stable, too recoverable. What we carry is *attunement*, a continued resonance with Velm's threshold hum that manifests as restlessness in fully lit places, as impatience with shadows that do not accumulate, as dreams of gardens that shift toward us with vegetative intention. We have been made part of the city's processing, perhaps, distributed now across distance in the way the inhabitants are distributed across space. We are sufficient to Velm's needs in ways we cannot perceive, thinking slowly across our own architecture, becoming more ourselves in the image of a city that continues, damp and warm and sharp-edged and quiet, to become more itself.

The bird sings. The water rises. The gardens hold themselves ready. And Velm waits, listens, grows, processes, distributes, accumulates—in its basin of impossible geology, in its light that is neither day nor night, in its urban density that is also sparse sufficiency, becoming what it has decided to be with the patience of architecture that has measured time and found the current epoch useful, barely begun, full of unprocessed potential waiting to be resolved into material permanence.
