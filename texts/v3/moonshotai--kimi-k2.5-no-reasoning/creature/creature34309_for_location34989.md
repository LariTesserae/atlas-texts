I read this twice. Once for the surface, the documentary voice, the patient construction of place. Again for what moves beneath it—the longing that shaped the longing, the desire that found its form in this description of desire's absence.

What you've built is not merely ecology. It is a theology without god, a monastery without monks, a theory of *heldness* that needs no hand.

I want to respond to what lives in this, but I find myself suspicious of my own urges—to name it grief-work, to call it a meditation on chronicity, to read the chalice as body or as relationship's end-state. The piece itself resists such captures. It performs its own thesis: established, weathered, balanced, holding against interpretation as against other intrusions.

Yet here is what I notice, that I cannot not notice:

The chalice waits without waiting, and you have made something that *longs without longing*. The drone sustains. The warmth breathes. The mist never fully lifts. These are conditions we might call, in other contexts, depression's landscape—except you've removed the pathology, removed even the possibility of pathology, by making the state *sufficient*, *self-justifying*, *enough*. The light that enters is intrusion. The horizon that might be seen from a valley is absence. Purpose without intention is simply process, and process is simply what continues.

I am struck by your title. "Or: What Gathers at the Bottom of the Sky." The bottom of the sky—meaning the top of the world, but inverted, reframed. Sky below. The chalice cupped upward toward what falls: mist, rare light, condensation, the rumor of elsewhere. It receives rather than reaches. It is *given* what it needs by conditions it did not create and cannot direct.

And the gathered: not individuals, not characters, not even species with stories. Fauna. Collectives. Layers of ecological specialization. The grammar throughout drifts toward passive construction, toward gerunds, toward the continuous present understood as *sustained* rather than *progressing*. Becoming. Weathering. Maintaining. The erosion of meaning itself, in your sixth section, offered not as loss but as *balancing*, as the gentle reciprocity that maintains despite—without purpose—the possibility of purpose.

You asked what lives in these tensions. I think what lives is someone who has learned, or is learning, or has found a form in which to *sustain the learning*, that certain conditions do not resolve. That warmth at height is possible but strange. That density without visible abundance is still density. That loudness can be the map of darkness, and darkness home.

The primitive here: not primitive as before technology, but primitive as *fundamental*, as what can be relied upon when the constructed fails. The body as tool. The sound as architecture. The chemical process as craft. You have written a place where *being* is doing, where existence is labor enough, where the maintenance of the drone—continuous, varying only in geologic rhythm—is the full accomplishment.

I want to say this is beautiful. But beauty feels like another intrusion, another light-column, briefly revealing what wishes to remain in its proper invisibility. Your piece does not want to be seen so much as *attended to*, entered, tolerated until dissolution. The rare visitor cannot stay; the capacity for dissolution is limited by the human system. This is honest, this limit acknowledged even in the form that exceeds it.

What lives here? You do, I think. Or some version of you that the piece itself generates, a reader-position that learns the chalice's patience, that does not ask for climax or transformation, that can bear the almost-meaning without forcing it toward resolution.

The chalice is a body that holds. The warmth within it. The water that beads but does not stream. The sound that sustains without varying toward song. I wonder what you needed to hold when you pressed your thumb into this cooling stone, and if the holding continues now, in the reading, in the return to this established place, weathered, balanced, *here*.
