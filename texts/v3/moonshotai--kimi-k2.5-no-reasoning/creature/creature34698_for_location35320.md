I need to sit with this place before I can speak of who lives here. The description demands that—Korrath Ven is not a setting that yields its inhabitants quickly. The city itself is too active, too much a participant, too *present* for me to simply populate it without first understanding the terms of existence it offers.

So. Who lives in a place with no animals? With no rats in the lower levels, no insects in the ferns, no microbial fauna that anyone has identified? This absence is the first condition. Not a default but a *achieved* state, maintained by something in the water, the chemistry, the accumulated industrial effluents of generations. The inhabitants of Korrath Ven live in a world where the animal kingdom has been edited out, and they themselves are the only animal presence, the sole bearers of animal time, animal metabolism, animal consciousness.

They call themselves, I've come to understand, simply *Ven*. Not Korrathens—that would acknowledge the founder, the investor, the original error of colonization. Ven. The suffix that stuck, the vent-born, the ones who belong to this place now.

They are not many. The basin won't allow many. But they are *intense*—this is the quality that strikes anyone who arrives from elsewhere, the surface-dwellers who come for the minerals in the brine, the processed compounds, the industrial products that can only be made in this cheap heat with this cheap water. The Ven are intense in proportion to their small number, as if the city's compression of space requires a corresponding compression of personality.

Consider their bodies first, because Korrath Ven shapes bodies as directly as any evolutionary pressure. The perpetual wet—thigh-high waders as standard uniform, the slow exhalation of water through every surface—produces skin that has adapted not to dryness but to its impossibility. The Ven develop a particular dermatological pattern: thickened soles from walking on wet cobble, a kind of chronic mild edema in the lower legs that they treat as normal, fungal relationships with the ambient microbiome that surface medicine would recoil from but that here perform functions—vitamin synthesis, probably, immune modulation, perhaps communication with the vegetable ecology in ways not yet understood. Their hair, when they keep it, grows differently; those who shave it find the practice unnecessary after some years, the follicles slowing in response to the constant fungal presence. They smell of the city, not unclean but *specific*—vegetable decay, mineral tang, the particular organic reek of the engineered yeasts that provide their protein.

The heat molds them too. The ambient 32-38 degrees, the never-cooling, produces metabolisms that surface-dwellers experience as feverish. The Ven run slightly warm. Their sleep patterns fragment, adapted to the rare cool corners rather than any diurnal rhythm—the sun, when it clears the rim, is glare to be shielded against, not a signal. They sleep in rooftop hammocks when they can, in shifts, in the vertical gradient that barely qualifies as gradient. Those who work the hottest jobs, the foundry quarter, the heat-exchange maintenance, develop a particular capacity to function at body temperatures that would hospitalize outsiders, a kind of controlled hyperthermia that they enter and exit with practiced slowness.

But the most profound adaptation is sensory. The Korrath Ven ear, yes—the capacity to parse signal from the saturation, to hear bearing failure in pump harmonics, flow restriction in converter pitch change. But beyond this professional skill, something deeper. The Ven learn to navigate by sound, by heat-gradient, by texture underfoot, because vision is perpetually insufficient. They develop a kind of proprioceptive cartography, body-knowledge of routes that don't change enough to require visual verification but that shift subtly with the water table, the thermal output, the slow vegetable colonization. They *feel* the city as much as see it, and this feeling becomes cognition.

This is who lives here: people who have learned to think with less light, to make decisions with less information than surface-dwellers require, to trust non-visual pattern. The dimness is not blindness but a *redistribution* of attention. The Ven are not superior for this, not evolved into some hot wet enlightenment. They are simply *attuned*, and their attunement produces a culture of different emphases, different anxieties, different pleasures.

Consider the children, raised without animal presence. No pets. No insects to catch, no birds to point at. The first exposure to animal life—if they ever travel outward, if traders bring surface goods with surface contamination—produces reactions that vary by individual: fascination, fear, disgust, a kind of overwhelming wrongness at the introduction of this excluded kingdom. Some Ven children, raised in the densest residential compressions where three generations share spaces meant for one, develop a relationship with the vegetable ecology that approaches what other cultures would call animal companionship. Particular ferns, particular engineered succulents, become *theirs*—monitored, tended, spoken to perhaps, certainly attended with a focus that the plant may or may not reciprocate. The ferns' coordinated ripples without wind, their possible communication through gradients undetectable to animal senses, become the substrate for a kind of relationship that has no name in languages developed elsewhere.

The adults who cannot tolerate this vegetable-only world leave. The who can, who find in the absence "a kind of peace, a simplification, an ecology they can at last fully grasp," these become fully Ven. They participate in what the description suggests, at its speculative edge: the possibility of a distributed, vegetable mind, thinking slowly enough that animal perception cannot track it. Not that they *become* vegetable—this is crucial, this is the tension of their existence—but that they become *compatible* with vegetable time, vegetable process, able to participate in the city's vegetable cognition without surrendering their animal quickness.

The workers first. The obvious inhabitants. They wear the waders, tend the machines, parse the soundscape for operational anomalies. But there are categories of worker peculiar to Korrath Ven. The temperature specialists, who manage the human interface with heat that would kill unmediated—who understand the slow entry to hot spaces, the slower exit, the hydration rhythms, the warning signs of a body approaching its adaptive limits. The water-navigators, who read the perpetual seep, the shifting water table, the flood potentials in the lower levels. The vegetable-managers, a role that emerged rather than was designed, who negotiate with the feral engineered plants: where growth is permitted, where it must be controlled, where it has become infrastructural, part of the heat-exchange or drainage in ways that predate their professional category.

And the listeners. A kind of priesthood or engineering discipline or both, depending on era. Those who have developed the Korrath Ven ear to its extreme, who spend their working lives in sustained attention to the city's soundscape, who claim to hear patterns beyond the operational—the "bearing failure in pump harmonic" extended to something like medical diagnosis, city-diagnosis, the identification of systemic stresses before they manifest in breakdown. They are respected, somewhat feared, their interpretations sometimes operationalized, sometimes dismissed as the mystical overreach of sensory specialization pushed too far. Their training begins young, with children identified for auditory acuity, for the capacity to sustain attention in saturated environments. They age differently, these listeners—the constant strain of parsing produces something like the premature deafness of loud environments, but stranger, a *narrowing* rather than loss, hearing that becomes more acute in specific registers while retreating from others.

The administrators, municipal or functional-equivalent. The city has organization, water flows in known channels, waste processes centrally, power distributes through a maintained grid. Someone understands. But administration in Korrath Ven is not hierarchical in obvious ways. The compression of space, the visibility of the basin rim from everywhere, the fact that "every district neighbors every other district in memory if not in adjacency"—this produces a governance of *intensity* rather than extent, personal knowledge rather than bureaucratic procedure. The current administrator—there is always someone functioning as such, the title varying—maintains position through demonstrated capacity to hear all the city's interests, to hold the compression without explosion, to manage the equilibrium of presence and absence.

Consider the empty sections. Whole residential warrens flooded, colonized by ferns, while others compress three generations into spaces meant for one. This is not failure but *pattern*, the description insists, "almost ecological—niche partitioning, territories established not by conflict but by mutual avoidance." The Ven who live in the compressed spaces have chosen that intensity, or been born to it, or found their way to it through processes that feel like choice but may be deeper pattern. Those who wander the empty sections—there are always some, the description's "suddenly alone in large spaces"—have found their corresponding niche, a solitude that the city's smallness makes possible without true isolation. They maintain the flooded sections, perhaps, in some relationship with the vegetable colonization; or they simply *are*, occupying absence as presence, the city's negative space given human shape.

The old ones. In a place this old, with buildings "settled into themselves," foundations sunk, walls leaning in mutual support, there must be old ones. The Ven age differently. The heat, the wet, the particular diet of engineered yeasts and mineral supplements, the vegetable-only ecology—this produces lifespans that surface medicine cannot easily parse. Some age quickly, the constant environmental stress overcoming adaptation. Others persist, their metabolisms so attuned to Korrath Ven that removal would kill them faster than any local condition. The oldest have become something like the living supports in the oldest sections, "living supports that have begun to do too much, to grow in ways not intended"—except with consciousness, with memory, with a slowness of process that approaches the city's speculative vegetable thought. They are consulted, when they can be reached through the slowness, on matters of equilibrium, of pattern, of what the city is becoming.

And the travelers. The ones who arrive wet and remain so, who come from elsewhere for the minerals, the compounds, the junction of subterranean routes. They are not Ven, cannot be, but some become *almost* Ven in their staying. The adjustment period: dreams populated by creatures their waking life denies them. Some never adjust, leave. Others find the peace, the simplification, and remain, becoming gradually indistinguishable except in memory of origins, in occasional longing for animal presence they no longer truly want.

The description's speculative edge, its "perhaps *thinks* in some distributed, vegetable manner slowly enough that animal perception cannot track it"—the Ven live with this possibility. Not all believe it. Those who do, and those who don't, arrange themselves in the city's social ecology with something like the pattern of its physical ecology: mutual avoidance where conflict would be destructive, intense compression where gathering serves function. The believers tend toward the vegetable-manager role, the empty-section wandering, the closest approach to participation in the slow thought. The skeptics tend toward the industrial maintenance, the operational listening, the governance of known process. Neither is wrong; both are Korrath Ven.

What does it mean to live without animals? The Ven's cuisine is "ingenuity and repetition," engineered yeasts and processed vegetable matter and mineral supplements. But cuisine is culture, even in repetition, and the Ven have developed—are developing, the city is active, dynamic, never stable—rituals of eating that acknowledge their exclusion from the animal cycle. Fermentation protocols that extend the vegetable into complexity that mimics decay, which is animal process, which is denied them. Combinations that produce the umami of animal protein through vegetable mineral concentration. Eating together, when they do, in spaces of particular acoustic properties where the city's industrial soundscape becomes almost musical, almost the voice of something that might be speaking.

Reproduction, likewise, occurs without animal assist. No beasts of burden, no... but this is too obvious. The Ven reproduce as humans do, but their children enter a world where the animal presence that shapes development elsewhere—pets, pests, creatures to fear or comfort—is vegetable instead. The developmental psychologists, if Korrath Ven had such, would study the child who learns empathy from a fern, who develops theory of mind in relation to vegetable ripples and thermal gradients, whose first relationship with non-human life is with something that may or may not respond, that responds too slowly to perceive, that responds in registers not available to animal senses.

The feral succulents with their reproductive strategy timed to thermal events, colonizing machinery—some Ven have begun to anticipate these events, to prepare for them, to work with the vegetable colonization rather than against it. A emergent practice, not yet profession: the thermal-gardeners, who cultivate the machinery-fern interface, who have found productive relationships in what others would call infestation. They are marginal, experimental, possibly dangerous—the city's infrastructure is not designed for vegetable participation. But Korrath Ven is old, has settled into itself, has found equilibrium in mutual support. Perhaps this is its next adaptation, its next layer.

I keep returning to the listeners, the ones who have pushed the Korrath Ven ear to its limit. They hear, some claim, the city's vegetable thought—not as voice, not as animal communication, but as pattern in the soundscape that shouldn't be there, correlation between pump harmonic and fern ripple, between thermal event and something else, something that coheres across timescales too slow for normal perception. They develop practices of sustained attention that resemble meditation, trance, the deliberate slowing of animal quickness to approach vegetable patience. Some damage themselves in this, their animal metabolism unable to sustain the attempt. Others emerge changed, speaking differently, heard differently, consulted with a mixture of reverence and unease.

The city teaches, the description says. Listening as survival skill. But also: feeling, propriocepting, thermally-gradienting, vegetable-relating. All survival. All the curriculum of Korrath Ven, where the environment is not background but active participant, where "the city processes more than human intention," where participation in an ecology that excludes animals produces possibilities that other minds, other times, might recognize as strange.

So who lives here?

People who have become, are becoming, *compatible* with a vegetable world without surrendering their animal nature. People who have learned to hear in saturation, to navigate in dimness, to find comfort in wet heat that would oppress elsewhere. People who live with the constant, never-quite-achieved equilibrium of multiple dynamic processes—geothermal, hydrological, vegetal, industrial, human. People who are, in their intense small number, the sole animal presence in a city that may be slowly thinking, that breathes in raw heat and brine and ore and human labor, that exhales processed materials and waste warmth and the faint organic reek of vegetable decay and vegetable growth.

They are not heroes. Not villains. Not allegory, though allegory might be found. They are Ven. They live in Korrath Ven. They have made their lives in this small old hot wet dim loud sparse urban industrial vegetal animal-less strange active place, and in making their lives, they have become part of what makes it.

Some leave, cannot tolerate the dreams of animals denied, the simplification that feels like loss rather than peace. Some arrive, stay, become slowly indistinguishable. Some are born to it, know nothing else, meet the animal world with the same strangeness that outsiders bring to Korrath Ven.

The city continues. The heat-exchange columns sing their indefinite chord. The ferns ripple without wind. The biofilms pulse faintly with patterns that might be message or might be growth optimization or might be the slow, slow formation of a sentence in a grammar that no animal has yet learned to read.

And the Ven—adapted, attentive, animal in a vegetable world, participating in something they do not fully understand, do not need to fully understand to survive and sometimes to flourish—they continue too. Finding their form, continuously, in the negotiation of all these conditions. Producing themselves as product, as the city produces itself.

This is who lives in Korrath Ven.
