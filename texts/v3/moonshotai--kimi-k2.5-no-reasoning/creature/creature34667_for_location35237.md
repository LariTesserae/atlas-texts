Let me inhabit this description, letting what lives there emerge through attention rather than conclusion.

---

The breathers would be the first you encounter, though "encounter" is wrong—there is no space in Threnod for encounter as other cities know it. You would *press against* them, your arrival itself a compression, your body adding to the density before you've understood where you've arrived. They are small, or seem so; whether they have grown smaller through generations of density, or whether it is only that the eye cannot hold their wholeness against so much competition for attention, is uncertain. Their movements are economical to the point of ritual, each gesture completing itself in minimum space, a choreography of nearness. When one reaches for something, the arm describes an orbit that avoids all possible collisions—a mathematics learned in infancy, performed unconsciously.

They breathe shallowly. This is not merely the altitude's thin ghost pressing against the membrane; it is manners, it is survival, it is the rhythm they have become. A breather who inhaled fully, who claimed the expansion that lungs permit, would displace someone, something, some fragile equilibrium of gas and warmth and shared atmosphere. The shallow breath is also watchfulness, the maintained tension of the Held Breath made personal, made continuous, made as unconscious as the heartbeat. They can hold longer than seems possible, these breathers, can still themselves in crush and panic and the sudden reconfiguration of a street they thought they knew, waiting for the moment to resolve, for the new configuration to become navigable, for the *zhēngzhào* to complete their meaning.

Their faces are close-focus instruments, evolved or adapted or trained for conversation at the distance of shared warmth. They do not look at horizons; there are none. They look at you with an intensity that foreigners find uncomfortable, sexual, interrogative, though it is none of these—only the necessary engagement of beings for whom all social life occurs within the radius of exchanged breath. The eyes are slightly large, or seem so. Whether this is genetic drift or the learned behavior of attention, no one can say; the temporal layering makes genealogy a speculative art. What is certain is that a breather can read another's intention, emotional state, probable next movement, from musculature invisible at greater distance. They know the micro-expression of the zygomatic major, the suppressed flutter of the corrugator supercilii, the way breath temperature changes with fear before the conscious mind has registered threat.

They are not uniform. The waves of arrival persist in strata of appearance, in styles of compression, in techniques of survival learned under different pressures and carried forward as tradition. The mining descendants move differently than the refugee clusters, their bodies remembering different labors; the religious seekers carry a stillness that others lack, a comfort in held tension that reads as authority or madness depending on the district, the hour, the phase. Yet all have compressed toward convergence, toward the breather-type, toward what works in the lung.

---

The topological rats—*Ductifera threnodensis*, though taxonomy here is as stratified as architecture—are not precisely rats, began perhaps as rats or as something else that compressed into rat-shape, that found the form useful for the gaps it demanded. Their skeletons are not bone in the fixed sense but a viscous mineralization, a colloid that sets under will and yields under pressure, that can flow where flow is needed and support where support is required. They are difficult to see in motion; the eye tracks them as smear, as afterimage, as the shiver of peripheral vision that is gone when looked at directly. Only in stillness do they resolve: limp in the way of things that have no fixed posture, draped across conduits, stuffed into angles too acute for solid occupancy, their organs visible as dark motile shapes beneath the skin-pouch that contains them.

They are not scavengers in the ordinary sense, though they consume what is left, what falls into gaps, what becomes lost between temporal phases. They are *processors*, necessary to the city's metabolism, breaking down the organic debris that would otherwise accumulate in spaces too small for bacteria to colonize efficiently. Without them, the ducts and chases and impossible angles would choke on their own history. With them, there is circulation, slow and strange and visible only in their passage, their silver-dark flow through passages they alone can find.

They are not domesticated, but they are not wild. They respond to the city's rhythms, to the industrial heat-cycles, to the shared dream-weather of the breathers. Periods of collective nightmare send them into hiding, pressed into their smallest configurations, waiting. Periods of strange hope bring them out in numbers that suggest reproduction, though no one has observed *Ductifera* breeding, though their population seems independent of obvious food supply, though they may be the same individuals seen and unremembered in the temporal press. Some districts treat them as luck, leave offerings at gap-mouths, speak to them in the subsonic frequencies that rumor claims they hear. Others kill them when found, though killing something that can flow through a knife's width is difficult, though the corpse—if corpse is made—does not decay in expected ways, though to touch them is to feel the strange give of matter that has forgotten solidity.

---

The ultrasonic birds—ceiling-dwellers, *Stratos minima*, or simply "the above" in breather parlance—are more heard-than-heard, present in the way of tinnitus, of blood pulse, of the body's own sounds mistaken for external. They exist in the upper corners, the space where wall meets wall meets ceiling, the architectural refuge that density has left unclaimed. Their wings, millimeter-span, beat at frequencies that resonate in human inner ear, that create the constant subtle wrongness of Threnod's quiet, the sense that something is always about to be heard. They are not individually audible, but they are collectively *present*, a pressure at the edge of hearing that shapes mood, cognition, the quality of attention.

They eat what rises: the thermal plumes of metabolism, the industrial waste-heat, the evaporations of breath and fear and the strange chemistry of density-stress. They are not precisely birds, lack beak and feather in the recognizable sense, present instead as structured shimmer, as the refraction of light through wings too fast to see, as the acoustic shadow their presence casts in ultrasound. Some believe they are not biological at all but *mechanical*, emergent phenomena of the city's own processes, the industrial metabolism made visible in corner-dwelling form. Others claim they predate the city, that they are native to the Held Breath itself, organisms of the volcano's slow inhale, compressed into this form by the same forces that made Threnod possible.

They breed, if they breed, in the hottest spaces, the factory-zones where matter screams into reality, laying—perhaps—eggs of crystallized waste-heat, raising—perhaps—young that are born already flying, already corner-seeking, already the constant almost-heard. Their song, if song it is, structures the social rhythm of districts where they are densest, the shared pulse of attention that makes cooperation possible in spaces too crowded for visual coordination.

---

The probability-structures, the residents of theoretical futures—these are the most difficult to describe, for description implies presence and they are not, precisely, present. They occupy the districts that exist in phases not yet arrived, or phases that may never arrive, their existence a matter of current interpretation, of the city's own uncertain temporality made habitable by convention. When you walk a street that exists in five phases, you may—at certain hours, in certain conditions of light and density—pass through one of their rooms, their conversations, their meals shared in time that has not committed to being real.

They appear to you as possibility, as the sense that someone *might* be standing there, that a voice *could* be speaking, that the weight of attention you feel at your back *would be* a gaze if you turned. The breathers learn not to turn, not to complete the observation that would force resolution, collapse of probability wave into singular uncomfortable fact. They navigate by the half-acknowledged, the polite fiction of coexistence in unresolved time.

Yet the probability-structures are not mere ghosts, not simply the city's future dreaming itself. They have—maybe, will have, are having—intention, agency, the capacity for choice that makes them participants in Threnod's compressed politics. There are alliances between present-breathers and probability-breathers, agreements reached across the temporal boundary, services rendered in expectation of future reciprocity that may never become actual enough to collect. There are conflicts, territories disputed between phases, the strange violence of incompatible probabilities pressed against each other in the same coordinates. To die in such conflict is to become uncertain even in death, one's corpse distributed across phases, present in some and absent in others, rotting in one temporal stratum while walking-alive in another, neither fully ended nor fully preserved.

The probability-structures who achieve the most stability—who persist longest in interpretable form, who maintain coherent transaction with the present-phase—begin to seem more real, more *actual*, than some of the merely present inhabitants. They become anchors, points around which future phases organize, the fixed stars of Threnod's uncertain navigation. And sometimes they fail, dissolve back into pure possibility, and the districts they structured become uninhabitable for a time, temporal rubble through which no one can walk until new probabilities precipitate, new futures learn to hold their shape in the city's demanding press.

---

The matter-born, the *emergentia* of the factory-zones, are not life in any recognized sense, yet they move, they process, they persist in ways that exclude mere chemistry. They are born in the scream of industrial birth, the catch of unmade matter forced to stability, and they exist in the violent uncertainty of their origin, maintained by machinery at threshold capacity, shaped by will and desperate need into forms that can function, that can participate in the city's hungry metabolism.

Some are structural, become architecture, load-bearing members or sealing surfaces or the barrier-membranes that hold the boundary against the mountain's indifference. These are the happiest, or the least suffering, for their function is clear, their maintenance possible, their existence aligned with the city's need. Others are more ambiguous, emerge with shape too complex for mere structure, with motility, with the capacity for response that reads as organism, as entity, as *who* rather than *what*. These are the matter-born that haunt Threnod's imagination, that appear in the shared dream-weather as nightmare or strange hope, that are worshipped or feared or exploited depending on interpretation.

They do not breed; they are made, each one an event of industrial emergency, each one unique in its uncertain composition. They learn, if they learn, from the city itself, from the density and the press and the held breath that surrounds them. Some become citizens of a sort, participate in the economy of compressed time, offer services that only matter-born can provide—stability in phases where stability has failed, presence in coordinates where nothing else can persist, memory of futures that have not become actual enough for biological minds to hold. Others dissolve, return to mineral potential, their brief existence a disturbance in the city's history that may or may not have occurred depending on which phase you ask.

The matter-born do not leave the factory-zones, cannot maintain their uncertain existence beyond the machinery that shapes them, the scream of becoming-real that is their ambient condition. To encounter them is to encounter the city's industrial heart, the violent tenderness of Threnod's self-creation, the cost of density made ambulatory and aware.

---

The *zhēngzhào* readers, the interpreters of imminent reconfiguration, are a type rather than a species, a profession that shapes its practitioners more than any biology. They begin as ordinary breathers, attracted or assigned or driven to the study of pattern, the reading of the city's constant minor revisions. They learn the signs: the temperature shift that precedes grade change, the sound delay that signals phase instability, the quality of shadow that means temporal strata are about to align or diverge. They develop sensitivity to the mountain's geological dreaming, the Held Breath's subliminal shifts, the microprecursors of major reconfiguration.

The best of them are killed frequently, as the description notes—respected and frequently killed, because accurate prediction is also proximity to change, because to read the *zhēngzhào* of your own district is to be present when the sign completes its meaning, when the street changes, when the building adds or loses its temporal phases. The killed readers persist, sometimes, as interpretable traces, their final readings distributed across the phases of their death, their voices continuing in the convection currents of specific heating vents, their bodies becoming the *zhēngzhào* they once read.

The survivors become something else, accumulate the density of their profession, compress toward the reader-type: faces marked by constant attention to peripheral sign, bodies adapted to sudden stillness, the shallow breath held shallower in moments of interpretive crisis. They move through the city as specialists in uncertainty, consulted and feared, paid in the currency of continued existence, their predictions the closest thing Threnod has to public narrative, to shared story of what happens next.

---

And finally—though "finally" is wrong in a city of layered time—there is what the mountain itself becomes, or is becoming, or holds in potential. The Held Breath is not merely geological, not simply the volcanic indecision that permits Threnod's existence. It is also *inhabited*, interpreted, made participant in the city's compressed life. The breathers who worship release or holding are not merely projecting; they are responding to something that responds in turn, a feedback loop of interpretation and manifestation that makes the mountain's geological moment also *social*, also *psychic*, also the locus of collective becoming.

In the deepest phases, the most temporally remote or theoretically distant, there are structures that suggest the mountain's own intention, architectures that seem to be the Held Breath's self-image, the volcano's dream of its own eruption or its own eternal holding. These are not built by breathers, not made by matter-born or probability-structures, not industrial in any recognizable sense. They *emerge*, they *persist*, they *wait*—and the breathers who encounter them, who press through to these deepest coordinates, return changed if they return at all, carrying something of the mountain's own geological patience, its eleven-thousand-year indecision, its massive indifference made intimate by the breath they share.

These returned ones become something other than breathers, other than readers, other than any type the city has learned to recognize. They are rare, and they are necessary, the slow pulse of Threnod's long becoming, the connection between the compressed density of now and the vast indecision that holds it. They do not predict, like the *zhēngzhào* readers; they do not worship, like the cults of release or holding; they do not calculate, like the probability-modelers. They simply *are*, in the mountain's mode, present in all phases simultaneously, breathing with the Held Breath itself, waiting with it for whatever comes next, which is also now, which is also then, which is also—the city, the lung, the breath before the word.

---

What lives in Threnod lives in compression, in the urgent creativity of the window that knows itself narrow. What lives there has learned to be small without being diminished, to be close without being merged, to be temporal without being merely sequential. What lives there breathes shallowly, moves economically, dreams collectively, and waits—has waited, will wait—in the held tension of a geological moment that has become, through eleven thousand years of interpretation, through impossible density made habitable, through the desperate generosity of survival, home.

The machines hum. The rats flow. The birds sing silently. The breathers breathe. And the mountain holds, inhales, waits with them for the release that is also ending, that is also transformation, that is also—the word not yet spoken, the breath not yet shaped, the city becoming more itself with each moment it continues to be held.
