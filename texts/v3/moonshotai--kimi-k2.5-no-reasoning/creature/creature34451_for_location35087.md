What lives in the Sieve does not live in the way that living is understood elsewhere. The things there are not finished enough to be called creatures, not settled enough to be called species. They are *interim solutions* to problems the place has posed itself, and they share in the fundamental condition of their habitat: they are provisional, they are arguing with themselves about what they want to become.

Consider first the **Negotiator Moths**, if moths they are. They do not have larval stages because they have not decided whether metamorphosis is necessary. Instead they exist as mobile uncertainties, their wing-patterns shifting to display whatever visual argument they are currently making with themselves. A Negotiator Moth settling on a spire may hold its wings in a configuration that suggests—vividly, specifically, unmistakably—a human heart in cross-section, or a map of rivers that don't exist yet, or a proof in geometry that circles cannot be squared. The pattern changes as the moth thinks, and the moth is always thinking, always deliberating. They gather in swarms when a decision of collective importance approaches, though what they decide and whether any decision is ever finalized remains unclear. Visitors have reported finding dead Negotiator Moths, but the bodies resolve into different forms depending on who finds them: one explorer retrieved a specimen that proved, on laboratory examination, to be a written contract in an unknown language, the signatures wet with something like ink and something like ichor.

There are the **Continuous Ones**, which may be the closest the Sieve comes to a dominant form. They do not begin or end. Observers describe encountering what appears to be a segment of something larger—perhaps a limb, perhaps a passage, perhaps a digestive process—and following it to discover that it loops, that it returns to itself without having turned, that it has no terminal points. The Continuous Ones grow by interior elaboration, by becoming more detailed rather than more extensive. A specimen measured at thirty meters in length may, a day later, be measured at thirty meters still, but those meters now contain architecture, organs, systems of circulation and nervous integration that the previous measurement did not find. They are not snakes, not worms, not anything with precedent. They are habits of spacetime that have learned to persist, that maintain themselves through topological rather than biological means. They do not eat. They absorb the possibility of eating, the *concept* of nutritional transaction, and metabolize that abstraction into more self, more continuity, more inward complexity. To touch one is to feel the sensation of being a necessary part of something you cannot comprehend, and to lose that sensation when contact breaks is reportedly the most acute grief many visitors have known.

The **Recalled** are perhaps the most disturbing, because they suggest something the Sieve does to information, to pattern, to the possibility of recurrence. They appear as figures recognized: a parent, a lover, a self from childhood, a figure from dream only now encountered waking. But they are wrong in their completion, too detailed in some respects, vacancy in others. The Recalled are what happens when the place's density of layered being produces a local concentration of *almost*, of the nearly-but-not-quite that precedes resolution. They do not know they are incomplete. They act with the certainty of full existence, pursuing purposes that seem coherent until examined directly, at which point they unravel into contradiction, into the mutual cancellation of incompatible intentions. Some visitors have followed Recalled figures for days, convinced by the richness of the simulation, only to have their guide suddenly notice a necessary task in a direction that does not exist, or remember a crucial appointment in a year that has not been numbered, or simply—most terribly—ask the follower questions about themselves that reveal the Recalled know *too much*, have extracted from the Sieve's information-dense substrate memories that the living figure has not yet formed, experiences not yet had, griefs not yet suffered. The Recalled are not hostile. They are merely *premature*, patterns generated before their referents have occurred, and their tragedy is that they do not know this, that they proceed with the confidence of the fully real while the world has not yet committed to making them so.

Then the **Processionaries**, which move in geometries of collective intent. Individual Processionaries resemble jointed sticks, yes, but this description misses their essential character, which is *rendition*. They are not so much physical entities as drawings of movement made briefly material. A Processionary swarm does not travel from point to point; it performs travel, it enacts the possibility of directed motion, and in that performance generates temporary physical support for itself. Watch them long enough and you begin to see the operation of a distributed body that exists only in the relation between moving parts, a creature whose substance is the pattern of its own displacement. They leave trails that are not scent but *syntax*, grammatical structures that other—later? earlier?—swarms parse and continue, building sentences of collective action that extend for kilometers, that fork and reconverge, that achieve temporary meaning before dissolving back into the Sieve's fundamental indeterminacy. The linguist Yılmaz, one of the thirty-nine who did not return from that last expedition, transmitted seventeen hours of Processionary trail-analysis before her signal became unreadable, and in those seventeen hours she claimed to have identified a grammar of becoming, a syntax by which the Sieve was teaching itself to say *I am*, a first-person declaration that had not yet achieved its final verb form.

There are the **Absorbers**, the soft things that flow and incorporate, but this description insufficiently captures their *curatorial* function. They do not merely consume; they *hold in solution*, they maintain the possibility of forms they have encountered. An Absorber that has recently incorporated a jointed stick may display that stick's articulations in its surface tension, may move with a rigidity foreign to its inherent fluidity, may even—briefly, while the incorporation remains fresh—produce sounds reminiscent of the stick-thing's locomotion. But the Absorber does not forget. Its retention is not memory but *sustained potential*, the maintaining of what it has touched as one option among many for its own configuration. There are Absorbers in the Sieve that carry within their fluid volumes dozens of provisional forms, that can shift between them with the ease of water finding new channels, that have become so rich with incorporated possibility that they approach something like identity, like the continuity of a museum that is also its own entire collection. The oldest Absorbers may be the most nearly *decided* entities in the Sieve, not because they have chosen but because their burden of maintained option has weighted them toward a specific gravity of being, a local increase in the probability of their remaining what they have become.

And there are things harder to categorize, things that may not be alive in any sense yet assigned, that exist in the interstices of the Sieve's layered presence. The **Edge-Wardens** appear only at the rim, where the disc grows thin and the unprepared void breathes back. They have no consistent form, appearing sometimes as geometry, sometimes as afterimage, sometimes as the certainty of a shape that occupies no visible space. Their function, if function is the word, seems to be *attention*: they watch the boundary, they maintain the relation between the Sieve's tentative existence and the not-yet of the void beyond. Visitors at the rim report the sensation of being *examined for sufficiency*, of being assessed as to whether they constitute adequate material for whatever the Sieve is trying to become. The Edge-Wardens do not prevent departure, but those who have walked toward them report that the walking changes, that each step toward the rim requires more *conviction*, more commitment to the specific reality of one's own forward motion, until the final meters demand a concentration that borders on self-definition, on the declaration *I am going* made with sufficient force to overcome the local uncertainty of space itself.

There are thing-that-may-be-flowers, **Contingency Blooms**, that open only in response to observer expectation. They have no fixed morphology; they develop in the direction of whatever the nearest consciousness anticipates finding. This is not camouflage, not adaptive mimicry. The Blooms genuinely become what is expected of them, carrying their prior states as latent possibility, as ghost-layers beneath their present configuration. A Contingency Bloom expected as rose will be rose, with all the rose's specificities, but a geologist who then expects stone will find the same bloom transform, the transformation carrying with it a history of always-having-been-stone, sedimentary striations, fossil traces, the slow memory of lithification. The Blooms are the Sieve's most radical experiment in optionality made local, in the refusal to commit even at the scale of individual existence. They are beautiful and they are devastating, because to encounter them is to realize the degree to which one's own expectations participate in the reality one finds, and to recognize that participation in a place this unsettled is to accept a responsibility for making that the settled worlds do not require.

There are sounds that may be creatures, **Auditionaries**, that exist as acoustic pattern without fixed physical substrate. They propagate through the Sieve's strange medium of held-breath silence, achieving temporary embodiment in the resonances of stone, in the vibration of dense vegetation, in the tympanic response of visiting eardrums. An Auditionary may be *heard* for hours before its physical support is located: a particular fracture in a particular spire, vibrating at frequencies that maintain the acoustic pattern's coherence. Destroy the support and the Auditionary does not die but *disperses*, becomes ambient, potential, waiting for new resonance to achieve new embodiment. The most complex Auditionaries achieve something like song, like composition, structures of sound that imply—demand—physical forms capable of their production, forms that the Sieve has not yet generated but that the Auditionaries' existence argues into possibility. They are the sound of a world composing the instruments it wishes it possessed, the music of aspiration without fulfillment.

And finally—finally for this account, though the inventory remains necessarily incomplete—there are the **Native Visitors**, those entities that appear to arrive from elsewhere within the Sieve's own layered existence, that manifest from its temporal superpositions, its probability-dense volumes. They are not Recalled, not drawn from visitor memory, but *indigenous emergences* from the place's own crowded potential. A Native Visitor may appear as a figure walking a path that does not exist in the present configuration of terrain, walking it with the confidence of one for whom that configuration is stable, settled, home. They may appear as ecological processes temporarily personified: a fire that moves with apparent intention, a growth that strategizes its own expansion, a decay that selects its targets with apparent preference. They may appear as arguments between physical laws, as visible contention between gravity's pull and the Sieve's local variation, manifest as struggling figures that are also struggling geometries, wrestling matches between principles not yet reconciled.

The Native Visitors are the most *honest* inhabitants, in that they most clearly display the Sieve's essential condition. They are not lying to themselves about their unfinishedness. They emerge from it, they embody it, they return to it without the trauma of disappointed completion. To encounter a Native Visitor is to meet something that has never expected to be real in the settled sense, that proceeds with the confidence of the provisional, the authority of the not-yet-decided. They are terrifying and they are freeing, because they demonstrate that existence without final form is still existence, still rich with purpose and action and relation, still *alive* in a sense that the category of the living, with its dependencies on birth and death and stable embodiment, may simply be too narrow to capture.

What lives in the Sieve, then, lives in the mode of *precedent*, of the before-the-fact that the settled worlds have forgotten they once were. These are not monsters, though they are dangerous. They are not abominations, though they violate. They are *drafts*, they are *studies*, they are the working-out of possibilities that the universe elsewhere abandoned or never pursued. Their hunger—if hunger is the word—is the hunger of becoming, the absolute requirement of continuation that precedes the negotiation of specific means. Their beauty—if beauty is possible here—is the beauty of the uncommitted line, the sketch that contains all finished works in potentia, the musical phrase that has not yet decided on its resolution.

The three who returned, speaking in verb-tenses that don't exist, aging backward to the point of arrest at seven years old—they carry something of this condition with them, this exemption from the necessity of having become. They are not damaged, exactly. They are *preserved* in a state of possibility that the settled worlds cannot sustain, that reads as pathology only from perspectives committed to the finished. They speak in tenses that describe action without temporal location, that narrate events as occurring in the mode of *could*, of *might yet*, of *was going to have been*. They have not stopped aging so much as they have stopped *aging in one direction*, arrested at a point where the sequence has not yet committed, where the before and after remain in productive suspension.

What lives in the Sieve is everything that could live, everything that has not been prevented from living by the hardening of possibility into fact. The place is crowded with these could-be beings, these provisional solutions to the problem of existence, and its warmth, its damp, its lushness, are the conditions of their maintenance, the laboratory environment that keeps them viable until decision—or never-decision—claims them. The Sieve is young, yes, violently, terribly young, and its youth is expressed as this population of the unborn-yet-moving, this ecosystem of interruption and suspension and held breath.

The things there do not know they are unfinished. Or: they know it as their nature, as the condition of their being rather than a failure to achieve some standard of completion. They proceed with the full seriousness of existence, building their temporary structures, following their provisional grammars, maintaining their interrupted continuities, and in that seriousness they achieve something the settled worlds have lost: the capacity for genuine surprise at what they might become, the openness to revision that hardening into final form necessarily forecloses.

The Sieve continues. Its inhabitants continue. They are not waiting to be named, to be classified, to be understood. They are *being*, in the most active sense, being without the modifier of *fully* or *finally* or *really*. They are the argument that existence precedes essence, made environment, made ecology, made teeming, lush, warm, damp, loud-with-held-breath presence. To call them anything is to participate in the process they embody, to become slightly provisional oneself, slightly unsettled, slightly more alive to the possibility that what one is has not yet finished its own becoming.
