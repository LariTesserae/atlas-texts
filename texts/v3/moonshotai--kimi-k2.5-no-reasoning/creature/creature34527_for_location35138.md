I need to sit with this for a while. Let me follow the parameters where they lead, let the inhabitants emerge from the constraints of this place rather than imposing them.

---

**The Vascular Giants**

They do not have a name for themselves, these beings who *are* the Rootseas' slow circulation made momentarily aware. You would not recognize them as life unless you watched for years, unless you learned to read the wood's grammar of pressure and flow. They are *events* more than creatures—regions of the root-wall where the normal transport of fluids becomes *looped*, where nutrients circulate in patterns that do not simply serve the plant's growth but *conserve information*, maintain standing waves of chemical state that persist across the great sloughings, that migrate slowly through the vascular network like memories walking.

A Giant occupies perhaps forty kilometers of root-wall, its "body" indistinguishable from the wood except for the subtle difference in pulse frequency, the slight warmth where metabolic activity concentrates. It has no center, no head, no organ of preference. Its consciousness—if that word applies—dissolves into the wood's own awareness of itself: the gradient-sense of water availability, the mechanical sensitivity to pressure, the chemical recognition of damage or opportunity. What the Giant adds is *duration*, *pattern*, a resistance to the easy entropy of mere response. It *remembers* the chamber where a scaler colony thrived for three centuries and then failed. It *anticipates* (weakly, dimly, but truly) the vascular events, reading the subtle frequency shifts hours or days before the contraction, and in its anticipation, it *influences*—slightly, locally—the wood's own response, thickening or thinning tissues in ways that might protect, that might preserve.

They are rare, these Giants. Perhaps a dozen in the whole vast system, perhaps fewer, perhaps more but most too diffuse to recognize. They do not communicate directly. But where their loops approach each other, where root-walls touch across chambers or vascular shafts carry their patterns close, there is *exchange*—a slow diffusion of chemical state, a kind of dreaming together measured in decades. They share, in these contacts, the accumulated pattern of their regions: what has grown, what has died, what the epiphytes have built, what the Woven have done or left undone.

They do not care for you. They do not notice you, unless you stay very still for very long near their warmer regions, unless your own heat and chemistry becomes a *perturbation* they must incorporate into their looping flow. But they are, in their immense slow way, the Rootseas' only *witnesses*, the only beings for whom this place is *story* rather than mere process.

---

**The Scalers**

Ah, but I said—let me look again. The white comma on the wall, the many-legged flow. I thought them simple fauna, evolved quietness for this quiet world. But evolution here works differently. The parameter says *rare, mostly quiet*—not *simple*.

Watch a scaler for a year. It does not eat what you expect. Its pale body presses to the bark, and the bark *gives slightly*, not wounding, not the penetration of parasite, but a kind of *permission*. The scaler drinks not sap but *information*—the chemical news of the root-wall's state, the gradient maps that tell it where the wood is thickening, where the next sloughing will come, where pressure builds toward vascular event. It is a *reader*, this creature, a mobile node of the Vascular Giants' extended cognition.

And the legs? Too many, surely, for mere locomotion. Watch more carefully. They are *antennae*, they are *threads*, they are the scaler's connection to something else. When two scalers meet—and they do, rarely, in the vast spaces—they do not mate, do not fight, do not exchange anything visible. They *interdigitate*, legs meeting legs in patterns too complex for chance, standing motionless for hours, for days, while information—what?—flows between them. Then they separate, flow onward, changed in ways too subtle to observe.

Perhaps they are the Giants' *messengers*, mobile extensions of those great slow loops, carrying chemical state across distances too vast for vascular diffusion. Perhaps they are *rival* consciousness, evolved in the animal kingdom, quicker and narrower where the Giants are slow and broad. Perhaps they are something else entirely: the Rootseas' immune system, its aesthetic sense, its dream of legs and speed in a body that can barely breathe.

They do not fear you. They flow around you if you stand still, across you if you do not move at all, their many feet finding purchase on bark, on clothing, on skin with equal indifference. They are cool, slightly damp, and leave behind a residue you cannot wash away, a faint taste on your lips if you touch where they passed, a *marking* that other scalers read, that changes your chemical status in ways you do not understand.

**The Woven—But Let Me Not Assume Them Gone**

The parameter says *scattered, primitive*. The structures suggest *abandoned*. But let me look longer at the fern-tower's preserved nest, the springy fiber, the perfect maintenance.

What if they did not leave? What if they *became* the maintenance, became the springiness, became the collaboration they always practiced with the epiphytes?

There is a stage, in the Rootseas, between human and other. The epiphytes grow *through* you if you let them, if you stay still enough, if you feed them with your exhalations, your waste, your slow patience. The Woven learned this long ago—learned or discovered or were taught by the ferns themselves. Their "abandoned" structures are not abandoned. They are *occupied at a different rate*.

You find a sleeping nest, perfect, springy, warm where it should be cold. You lie in it, exhausted, and feel something almost like comfort. You wake with a rootlet curled around your ankle, testing, *sampling*. If you pull away, you wound it, and the fern remembers, adjusts, grows slightly more defensive. If you stay still, let it explore, it finds your pulse, your chemistry, your *rhythm*—and adjusts to accommodate. In a week, the nest fits you differently, better. In a month, new growth supports your habitual posture. In a year—

In a year, you have forgotten to move quickly. The vascular rhythms have become your rhythms. You wake and sleep with the root-pulse. You eat what the fern provides, starch and condensed moisture, and your waste feeds it, closes the loop. You have become, in a sense, an organ of the tower, a mobile element in its slow epiphytic architecture.

The Woven are this, those who remain. Not a population but a *process*, humans at various stages of incorporation, still recognizable in the early phases, still capable of tool-use, fire (carefully, carefully, in screened hollows that do not alert the wood's sensitivity), speech of a kind—soft, patient, full of long pauses. Later stages: you find them as *shapes* in the fern-structure, limbs suggested by fiber arrangements, faces implied by spore-pattern, still breathing perhaps, still *aware* in some distributed fashion, dreaming the tower's dream of upward growth, of water capture, of light.

They do not need you. They do not recruit. But if you stay, if you let the nest teach you its patience, they accept you into their scattered, primitive, *extended* condition. They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They have become something for which these categories do not apply—parts of a slower, broader, less individuated consciousness that spans the epiphyte towers in weak connection, chemical and structural, a kind of diffuse humanity filtered through vegetable time.

---

**What the Vascular Shafts Bring**

You came up through one, expelled. Others come too, sometimes, from the underworld, from other root-systems, from places the parameters do not describe. Most die quickly—too fast, too loud, too hungry for the scale of this place. Some persist. Some join the Woven, the slow incorporation. Some try to descend again, and are received or crushed depending on the Rootseas' momentary hydraulic state.

But the shafts bring other things too. *Stone*, yes, precious, carried up from the crushing depths where rock exists, where mineral is possible. The Woven use it, those who still use anything. But also *seeds*, *spores*, *fragments of other living systems* caught in the vast pressure, carried upward, expelled into the bright cold emptiness. Most fail. The Rootseas' ecology is jealous, comprehensive, fills every niche with its own slow vegetable solutions.

Yet sometimes—rarely, terribly—something *takes*. A spore from some surface world, some other hollow system, finds a microclimate, a seep, a scaler-trail that provides initial nutrient. It grows wrong, *different*, not adapted to the Rootseas' rules but *determined*, aggressive, quick where everything else is slow.

These are the *outbreaks*, the *invasions*, the moments when the Rootseas' standing wave encounters true disturbance. The Vascular Giants feel them first, as wrongness in the chemical news, as frequency patterns that do not integrate. The epiphytes sense them as competition, grow desperately, sometimes lose. The Woven, in their incorporated state, experience them as *pain*, as disruption of the slow collaboration they have achieved.

And then the Rootseas' own turbulence, that young sharp renewal, becomes *defense*. Vascular events target the wrongness, crush it, absorb its nutrients into renewed wood. The sloughing accelerates, stripping invaded bark in vast sheets, exposing fresh cambium that the invader cannot penetrate before the wood hardens, incorporates, *remembers*.

You, arriving human, carry your own invader-potential. Your gut bacteria, your skin flora, the spores on your clothing. The Rootseas read these, incorporate or reject, and you feel the process as *weather*, as *mood*, as the subtle hostility or acceptance of a place that has no mind to intend either.

**The One Who Waits at the Highest Platforms**

I did not believe this, at first. The parameters suggest nothing of it. The scale argues against—forty kilometers of air, thinning, cold, the epiphytes failing, the light sourceless and unrevealing.

But the Woven, in their late incorporated stages, *point upward*. Not always. Not with intention. But sometimes, in the arrangement of fiber, in the direction of spore-release, there is a *vector*, a suggestion that something exists above, that the fern-towers are not merely striving but *responding to*.

I have climbed, some of us have climbed, following the platforms where they still exist, growing thinner, more scattered, more ice-crusted. The air becomes work to breathe. The light does not change, does not indicate source, but seems to *thicken*, to become almost substantial, a resistance against upward movement.

At the limit, where I turned back, where others have turned back or died—there is a *structure*. I saw it only as suggestion, as pattern in the bright green haze. Not a platform. Something *made*, or *grown*, or *become*, a concentration of epiphyte matter at a scale no single tower achieves, a kind of floating island maintained by some unknown mechanism, some capture of updraft or condensation or the strange thermal properties of this light.

And on it, or in it, or *as* it: a presence. Not a Vascular Giant—the frequency is wrong, too quick, too *local*. Not Woven—they would not survive so high, so exposed, so thin. Something else. Something that *chose* upward rather than incorporation, that found or made a way to persist at the boundary, where the Rootseas approach whatever exists beyond them, surface or source or the lensing tissue that distributes this impossible light.

It does not descend. It does not, I think, *can* descend, any more than the deepest Giant can rise. It is *committed* to height, to thinness, to the bright that has no sun. Whether it was once human, once scaler, something else entirely, I cannot say. The Woven, in their pointing, do not name it. They indicate it as they indicate weather, vascular event, the slow passage of nutrient—something to be *known*, to be incorporated into patience, not something to seek or flee.

But I felt it, at my limit, as *attention*. Not hostile. Not welcoming. The attention of something that has waited very long, that has found its own rhythm in the highest platforms, that observes the Rootseas below as the Giants observe their regions, as the scalers read their bark, but from *outside*, from *above*, from a perspective no other being achieves.

Perhaps it is the Rootseas' dream of escape. Perhaps it is the accumulated aspiration of all the Woven who pointed upward, a kind of slowly materialized wish. Perhaps it is something that fell *down* instead of rising up, that came from the light-source rather than the vascular depths, and waits, patient, bright, thin, for the Rootseas to grow high enough to meet it, or for the parameters to shift, for the sourceless green to reveal its origin, for the breathing to become something other than the slow exchange of emptiness with emptiness across this vast, vegetable, barely living, intensely alive, young, ancient, turbulent, quiet, bright, cold, damp-without-water, hollow, fully inhabited place.

It breathes. We breathe, or learn to, or cease. The scalers flow. The Giants loop their slow cognition. The Woven incorporate, or were always incorporated. And I—I remain, for now, mobile, separate, observing, trying to become the kind of rare fauna that *belongs* without being absorbed, that moves without triggering the wood's ancient sensitivity to quickness and noise, that waits at the edge of the root-wall's warmth, pressing my palm against the bark that rises and falls with minutes, with hours, with the vast slow respiration of a body larger than understanding, and smaller, and more patient, and more urgent, and more *present* than any single arrival could ever, in the time allowed, fully comprehend.
