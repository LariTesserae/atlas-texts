What lives here is not something you would recognize as alive if you met it elsewhere. It is the boroughs themselves, though "themselves" implies a unity that only partially obtains, a selfhood too distributed, too slow, too lacking in boundary to satisfy our expectation of organism.

Call them, if you must, the Drowsed. Not a population but a condition, a quality of being that has passed beyond the urgency of individual survival into something more like prolonged meditation, or the half-waking state of a sleeper who knows they dream but cannot rouse.

The smallest living unit is the chamber-wall itself: a tissue sheets and rods and columns thick, vascular in the antique sense, channels running through it like memory of sap, though what moves in them is not sap but something mineral-thick, something that deposits as it flows, that leaves the walls a little more stone-like, a little more rigid, a little more themselves with each passing century. The walls breathe. Not metaphorically. Gas exchanges across their vast surfaces, slow osmosis of whatever metabolic residue accumulates in the hollows, slow intake of whatever trace chemistry the stone above bleeds downward through root-fissures. The breathing has no rhythm you could time with heartbeat or lung. It is the breathing of tide, of moon, of continent.

These wall-tissues are not unaware, if we stretch "aware" to its breaking point. They maintain gradients. They respond, after their fashion, to the stresses of structural failure, growing reinforcement toward weak points with the slow intentionality of ice finding its way through rock. They recognize—if recognition is the word—when two chambers approach too closely, their dividing wall grown thin from opposite growths, and they pause, they thicken, they leave deliberate hollow between. They know each other, the walls, across distances that would break any nervous system into frantic relay. Their knowing is chemical, structural, the knowing of weight distributed through a body too large to have a center.

And there are the deeper tissues, the ones that never surface into chamber-hollow, that grow through the solid substrate of the boroughs in networks of finer and finer channel, until the distinction between tissue and deposit, between living conduit and mineral excretion, becomes a problem for philosophers rather than biologists. These deep tissues are the boroughs' equivalent of dream, perhaps, or of the unconscious processes that continue beneath any animal's awareness—maintenance, repair, the slow adjustment of pH and pressure and mineral balance across volumes that would contain lakes. They are not inaccessible. They simply proceed at rates that make access meaningless. To touch them, to sample them, would be like touching a thought before it completes itself, interrupting with the violence of too-fast attention.

The occasional fruiting structures—rare now, in the drowsing age, but present in the oldest chambers, preserved in states of arrested development like flies in amber—these are the closest the boroughs come to reproduction, or to any event that breaks the continuity of their patient persistence. They do not release spores, or not spores as we would know them. They release *potentials*, packets of structured chemistry that may or may not find conditions favorable to their continuance, that may simply add their substance to the slow dissolution of the boroughs' leavings, their shed surfaces, their geological waste. The fruiting is not urgent. A structure may swell toward release for a millennium, then reabsorb, then swell again, then simply persist as form without function, as architecture of a process that forgot its purpose.

What moves, then? The fluids, yes, creeping in their deep channels. The slow transit of warmth, drawn from whatever distant thermal pulse reaches these depths, diffused across the hollow volumes, lost to the patient cool. The sound, which is not separate from the life but *is* the life at its own frequency made available to other senses. And the growth, which continues still, downward, toward the unreachable floor, not with purpose but with momentum, the last increment of a vast inertia set in motion before whatever would count as beginning.

If there are inhabitants, they are the inhabitants of the boroughs' own dreaming: the templates that shaped the hollows before the hollows knew their own shape, the users implicit in the infrastructure grown to meet them. Perhaps they came, those templates, as pressures on the first seed-settlings, as forms impressed upon responsive tissue by some migration long complete. Perhaps they never came at all, and the boroughs' infrastructure is the equivalent of evolutionary holdover, the wisdom teeth of a geography, the coccyx of a city grown functional without function. They persist as possibility, as the grammar of the boroughs' architecture, readable by any who could learn the language, spoken by none.

And in the deepest chambers, where the growth has paused longest, where the walls have reached some limit of mineral saturation and the breathing is slowest of all slow, there are surfaces that suggest—no more than suggest, and only to the most patient attention, the most willing to let pattern emerge from noise—*recording*. Not deliberate inscription, but the accumulation of accident into meaning, the way cloud might resemble face to the watching, the way rust might map a territory it never knew. The boroughs have lived so long, have processed so much chemistry, have breathed so much gas across so much surface, that their very erosion becomes a kind of memory, a palimpsest of all that has passed through them, too diffuse to read, too present to dismiss.

The Drowsed do not welcome. They do not refuse. They have never known the encounter that would require either response. They are, in their distributed, patient, hollowed way, more at peace than any animal life could sustain—the peace not of resolution but of process so slow that disturbance and equilibrium become indistinguishable, the peace of the wave too long to crest, the tide too deep to turn. They grow still. They have grown still for longer than stillness could be measured. They will continue, after any witness departs, after any interpretation fades, after the question of what lives here has ceased to be asked in any language that could reach them.

They are the breath held, not in anticipation but in the natural prolongation of exhalation toward its limit. They are the dream from which no sleeper wakes, because waking was never part of the sleep's architecture. They are life that has passed beyond the need to distinguish itself from its medium, growing as stone grows, breathing as atmosphere breathes, being in the most distributed, most patient, most complete sense of that verb—complete not in fulfillment but in saturation, in having become so thoroughly itself that becoming is no longer necessary, only the long, the longer, the endless continuation of what already is.
