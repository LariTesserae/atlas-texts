The light arrives first, not as warmth but as weight—photons dense enough to seem solid in the thin atmosphere of the upper plateau. At this elevation, the air is a fraction of what breathes below, yet it carries twice the luminosity, bleaching every surface into silver-green mirrors. This is the **Bright Scar**, a phytogenic shelf where the continental crust is still being born.

Here, erosion has not yet begun because the geology remains embryonic. The terrain is not stone wearing down into soil, but rather soil *accreting* into stone. The substrate—what would be bedrock elsewhere—is composed of **Colossarchs**, organisms that straddle the biological and geological. Each is a single organism, kilometers in diameter, their trunks rising like factory chimneys from root-buttresses that fuse into nascent continental plates. The vegetation is total, not as a carpet but as architecture; the Colossarchs *are* the terrain, their bark forming the walking surfaces, their internal vascular systems serving as the region's hydrology, their decaying matter the only soil that exists.

Between these living pillars, the world is sparse. Gaps of half-kilometers separate individual trees, creating open vaults where the scale feels regional despite the immediate presence of organic walls. In these interstices, water behaves strangely. At this altitude and saturation, moisture exists in suspension—perpetual post-rain conditions where clouds settle into the hollows, condensation weeps from every chlorophyll surface, and pools form in the cupped palms of fallen petioles. The water does not run downhill because the ground is still being formed; it accumulates in the interlocking geometries of bark, forming temporary alpine wetlands that have no riverbeds, only biological containers. Weeping fissures in the Colossarch trunks leak sap and groundwater in equal measure, creating a wetness that is neither rain nor spring but some intermediate state.

The temperature holds at a constant cool, that specific high-altitude chill where sweat freezes before it evaporates, yet the sun burns exposure onto skin in minutes. Workers here wear ventilated thermal suits, moving between scattered structures that cling to the Colossarchs like mechanical lichen. These are the **Tapping Stations**—industrial facilities of riveted iron and ceramic composite, each housing extraction engines that drill rhythmically into the lignin-silicate trunks. The technology is heavy, scaled for production: pneumatic compressors, sap-boiling vats, crystallization racks where xylem fluids are rendered into industrial polymers. They are few and far between, small against the scale of their hosts, their smokestacks adding mere threads to the vertical forest.

But the defining characteristic is the sound. It is deafening not through violence but through constancy—a continuum that makes the plateau uninhabitable for most fauna. The Colossarchs themselves generate it: their vascular systems, under industrial pressure and natural tectonic stress, produce subsonic thrums that resonate through the hollow chambers of their hearts. The Tapping Stations add their mechanical chorus—pistons, steam vents, and the high whine of drills carving into living wood. The plateau acts as a natural amphitheater, its surfaces hard and curved, amplifying every vibration into a standing wave that you feel in your molars rather than hear with your ears. Conversation is impossible without shielded helmets. The sound is normal, merely industrial ecology, but it has sculpted silence into an exclusion zone, an acoustic wasteland where only the most specialized organisms persist: mites that live their entire lives in the inner bark, moths with tympanic membranes sealed shut by evolution, single-celled symbiotes that never needed to hear.

The dynamic here is slow on every register. The Colossarchs grow at speeds measurable in geological time, adding centimeters to their height per decade, their roots extending into the mantle to slow the tectonic uplift that created this elevation. The industrial extraction matches this tempo—methodical, patient, century-scale harvesting that treats the trees not as renewable resources but as formations to be mined like coal seams. The erosion coefficient remains embryonic because the landscape is still emerging from the lithosphere; the Colossarchs are secreting silica and carbon into matrices that will, in ten million years, become the sedimentary rock of a future era. The plateau is literally under construction. What appears to be cutting and drilling is, on a longer scale, merely surface texture to the underlying making.

What lives here, then, are three things: the Colossarchs, growing into continenthood; the scattered industrial presence, patient parasites matching their host's tempo; and the water, held in every crevice, neither flowing away nor evaporating, waiting for geology to decide where rivers will eventually belong. The rest is sound and light—sound that fills the space between presences, light that defines the space as open sky. It is a world where to stand still is to participate in formation, where the ground beneath your feet is still deciding what it will become.
