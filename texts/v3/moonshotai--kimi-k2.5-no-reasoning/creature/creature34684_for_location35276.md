I need to find who or what lives in this place, and let them emerge through the writing rather than settling immediately.

---

The one you notice first is not the one who matters.

She passes you on a walkway between levels nine and ten—if levels mean anything in this vertical city where each hollow claims its own elevation—and you mark her because she looks *up*, a motion that draws the eye in a place of perpetual down-gazing. She is old enough that her eyes have lost the tapetal shine, dark now, adapted fully to the dim rather than reflecting it. She carries nothing. Her fungal-fiber wrap hangs loose, water-heavy, and you realize *she has just come from the pools*, though the hour is wrong for harvesting, and her hands are empty, and there is a particular stillness to her that the other passers lack.

She does not see you. Or she sees you and does not react to being seen. Either way: she is elsewhere, returning from elsewhere, her feet knowing the walkway while her attention remains at some greater depth.

You later learn, if learning is possible here, that this is one of the *soakers*. Not her name—names here are private, shared only in the hollowed spaces, never in passage—but her function, her chosen or compelled or emerged role. The ones who go down to breathe the air above the deepest water, to let the cold slow them, to become *pressure-aware* in ways the dry levels cannot sustain.

She was not born to this. None are. There is a season for the descent, a recognition. A young person—it happens to young people, the old say, because their bones are still persuadable, their metabolisms still *negotiable*—begins to notice things wrong with the light. The lichen-glow hurts, perhaps. Or the sound of dripping becomes *too informative*, carrying resonances beyond the weather, beyond the structural warnings, into something like *intention*. They begin to sleepwalk toward the lower levels. They wake in pools not their own, breathing comfortably through water that should close their lungs.

The community does not prevent this. The community *observes*, which is its way of not preventing while not quite allowing. The young person is fed the vision fungi, formally, ritually, where before they might have encountered it only by accident or illicit curiosity. They are questioned, though *questioned* is the wrong word—*attended to*, *listened at*, *held in the medium of collective attention*.

Some return unchanged, frightened, and are not frightened again, are quietly supported in their return to the dry upper levels where they become especially skilled water-engineers, attentive to the slightest shift in drip-rhythm, valued for their sensitivity that never quite crossed into whatever threshold they approached.

Others descend and do not return in the same mode. They become *soakers*, *pressure-ones*, *the drowned-who-breathe*. They learn techniques: how to hold air in lungs compressed by depth, how to let the cold slow heart and thought until a single breath suffices for minutes, how to read the water's chemistry through skin rather than tongue. They spend hours, days in the deepest pools, and when they rise they carry something—*knowledge*, *condition*, *becoming*—that the community needs and does not fully understand.

She—this one you passed—has been descending for forty years, if the concept of years holds meaning in a place without seasons. She is old for a soaker. Most burn, or fade, or finally *complete* their descent and do not rise, their bodies found when the pools shift, when some engineering project drains a section for maintenance, revealed as pale presences in the fungal forest, not dead in any simple sense but *arrested*, held in the cold and pressure like something preserved in amber, only wet, only still *slightly pliant*, the face in some cases still *expressive*, still *present*, visited by the other soakers who speak to them, who report back *what was said*, though the reports vary and the community does not press for consistency.

She rises today. She will descend tomorrow. Between: she walks the walkways, attended by a particular silence, a space the others make without seeming to make it, not reverence exactly, not fear, something closer to *accommodation*, the way you accommodate a draft, a temperature gradient, a presence that operates on rules slightly offset from your own.

---

But she is not the only inhabitant. She is not even the most numerous. Let me find the others, let them emerge.

*The child in the hollow above the main seep.*

You do not see her directly. You hear her, or rather hear the *effects* of her presence: the small sounds of a body learning its dimensions, the particular frequency of a child's voice before the acoustics of the Warrens train it lower, more carrying, more *fitting*. She is perhaps six, perhaps eight, age measured here by water-comfort and vertical reach rather than orbital time. Her hollow is small, a natural bubble expanded minimally, and she shares it with—this is the arrangement that emerges, that the Warrens permit or produce—a grandmother figure, not necessarily genetic, the *elders* here being less kinship relation than *survival achievement*, those who have aged enough to become *keepable*, *learnable-from*.

The grandmother speaks. The child does not answer, not in words you can parse. But you hear, later, when the grandmother has descended to the cultivation level, the child *practicing*, repeating phrases in the ultrasonic register, trying to match—what? The bats? The soakers' reported communications? Something she hears that the adults around her have lost?

She will not become a soaker, probably. Most children do not. Most become water-engineers, fungal tenders, hollow-carvers, guano collectors, cooks of the moss-bread, the thousand ordinary roles that maintain the extraordinary adaptation. But some children *listen* differently, and are listened-to differently in return, and this one—you gather from fragments, from overhearing, from the grandmother's careful, casual mention—this one heard the *thrumm* of a new stream channel two weeks before any adult detected the change, before the structural monitors, before even the bats' altered flight patterns. She described it as *singing*, as *hungry*, as *wanting to come down*.

The grandmother does not know what to make of this, or knows and does not say, or says to the other elders in registers you cannot access. The child continues to practice her ultrasonics. The water continues to find new paths.

---

*The fungal breeder.*

You meet him, if *meet* is the word, in the lowest cultivation level where the air is thick with spore-load and the light—fungal luminescence here, not lichen, a different spectrum, more blue, more *penetrating*—makes your skin feel *seen*, transparent, mapped in wavelengths you cannot name. He is working in a pool that has gone *right*, the opposite of wrong, the rare stable emergence of a new cultivar that retains desirable properties across successive fruitings.

He does not look up when you approach. His attention is *in* the water, *with* the mycelial mat he tends, his hands moving through the substrate with a gentleness that seems almost *reverent* except that you realize, watching longer, that it is *practical*, the only way to avoid damaging the delicate hyphal networks, to not *bruise* the organism into defensive chemistry, into the toxin-production that wrongness can trigger.

He is younger than the soaker, older than the child, in the broad productive middle that the Warrens rely upon. His body shows it: the slight hunch from years of pool-work, the permanent waterline on his skin where fungal pigments have stained, the fingers *slightly elongated*, you think at first from imagination, then from closer observation—from *growth*, from some developmental response to the constant reaching, the constant submersion in nutrient fluid, the mycelium's faint chemical encouragement of the tissue it touches.

He speaks to you, finally, or rather *around* you, to the fungus, to the water, to himself, a running commentary that is half-prayer half-technical-note: *"The third flush, see, the third is when the lipids concentrate, when the flavor becomes—that's for the dry preparation, the smoking, not the fresh—the fresh you want second flush, or the edge of third, before the sporulation shifts the texture—"*

He is not mad, or the word means differently here. He is *attended*, *attending*, the reciprocal focus that the Warrens demands and rewards. When he finally meets your eyes—brief, a flicker—you see that his tapetum is *modified*, not the standard green-reflective but something more *diffuse*, adapted perhaps to the fungal light spectrum, or mutated by chronic spore exposure, or simply *individual variation* that the Warrens' isolation and small population permits to express.

He returns to his work. You are not dismissed; you were never *engaged*. The fungus is the engagement, the water, the long patient cultivation that will outlast his own breathing, the strain he is developing that will carry his *pattern*, his *attending*, into decades after his body becomes substrate in its turn.

---

*The bat-listener.*

She is the one who explains, finally, what the child practices, what the soakers hear, what the *thrumm* means beyond weather and structure. She is old but not elder-old, in the age of sustained capability before the final decline, and she has made herself *expert* in a way the Warrens recognizes: not authority, not leadership—these concepts translate poorly—but *reliability*, *availability-of-understanding*.

She tends the highest accessible roost, the one nearest the surface gap, where a few dozen bats cluster in the remaining warmth of the volcanic stone. Their guano she harvests, yes, the nitrogen-transaction, the nutrient cycling. But she also *attends* them as the fungal breeder attends his mats—with duration, with reciprocity, with the patience to become *background* to their echolocating world.

"They do not map the Warrens," she says, her voice the trained carrying-tone that works here, low, resonant, shaped to the stone's acoustic preferences. "They map *through* the Warrens. The passages are not territory but *medium*, like the air is medium for sound, like the water is medium for—" she gestures downward, toward the soakers' realm, the pressure-knowledge she does not claim but does not disclaim.

"What do they map, then? Beyond?"

She looks at you—directly, the rare full attention—and you see her own eyes have lost some tapetal function, not entirely, a *dimming* that might correspond to her bat-attention, her listening in their registers, the wear of processing echoes not meant for human neurology.

"Places where the stone is *thin*," she says. "Places where something else—something they do not taste as stone, as water, as the familiar biologies—presses *through*. Not here. Not nearby. The echoes return ... complicated. The bats do not like to fly those routes. They *can*, they are capable, but they—" she pauses, finds words, or finds that words will not suffice and attempts anyway—"they return *changed*. Not damaged. *Altered*. Their subsequent echoes carry—residue? *memory*?—of the thin places. Other bats hear this and avoid, or approach, depending on—" she shrugs, the gesture of a culture that has learned the limits of certainty.

"The child," you say, "practicing ultrasonics—"

"Listens," she confirms. "As I listened. As some listen. The bats do not teach, not deliberately. But they *allow* listening. They *permit* proximity. Over time—" she touches her dimming eyes, "—over time you hear what they hear. Or a shadow of it. Or a *translation* that is not quite the original but is not quite *your own invention* either."

She does not say what the thin places mean. Perhaps she does not know. Perhaps knowing and saying are separate negotiations in the Warrens, and she has chosen, or been chosen for, only one.

---

And there are others. The *water-engineer* who spends his waking hours in the sluice-places, adjusting flow by millimeters, his body so *calibrated* to the Warrens' hydraulic personality that he can feel pressure changes in his joints, predict blockages by the quality of vibration through stone. The *hollow-carver* whose craft is inherited and mutated across generations, who can read a bubble's stability by the ring of her chisel, who has *felt* the Warrens settle around her work enough times to trust, finally, the stone's acceptance. The *cook* of the moss-bread, whose fermentation cultures are older than any living memory, whose slightest touch alters the community's digestion, their mood, their *receptivity* to the vision fungi, the soakers' reports, the bat-listener's translations.

They do not present themselves as individuals, not primarily. They emerge in function, in relation, in the *attending* that the Warrens demands and that they have learned to offer. Even the soaker, even the child with her ultrasonics, even the *drowned-who-breathe-who-do-not-rise*, preserved in the deepest cold—they are not separate *from* the Warrens but *of* it, expressions of its watery, stony, fungal, bat-haunted personality, its young violent geology still settling into the shapes that will, in epochs, become smooth enough to be called *old*.

What lives here? Not a people, exactly, though people are present. Not an ecology, though the lichen and fungi and crustaceans and bats and eyeless fish complete their cycles. Something *in negotiation*, the ongoing mutual accommodation of biology and geology, of the unfinished stone and the finished adaptation, of water finding paths and path-finding becoming *intelligence*, *culture*, *way-of-being*.

The figure on the highest walkway, watching your departure: she is one of these, and she is all of these. She has soaked, perhaps. Listened to bats. Tended fungi, carved hollows, engineered water, cooked moss, aged into the dim-eyed attention that is the Warrens' final gift. She does not wave. The gesture means little here, where sound carries more than sight, where the visible is always *suggestion*, *lichen-glimpse*, *mirror-reflection* of water that may be pool or may be sky.

She breathes. The Warrens breathes with her, through her, the held moisture of a million held breaths, the drip and *thrumm* and ultrasonic navigation and pressure-knowledge and fungal metabolism, all of it *continuing*, fitting itself to itself as the stone slowly, slowly completes its youth.

You leave. You carry the dryness, the terrible unbounded sound-propagation, the vulnerability of a body that must warm itself through evaporation, that must *claim* space rather than *fit* it. But some residue remains: the particular quality of the Warrens' air, water-saturated, held, *shared*; the knowledge that adaptation need not mean deprivation; the drip-count still running in some back rhythm of attention, translating weather you cannot feel into the patience of stone that does not need you to feel it, that continues, drip by drip, its million paths downward, finding, always finding, the way to be *here*, to hold, to become what lives in such places.
