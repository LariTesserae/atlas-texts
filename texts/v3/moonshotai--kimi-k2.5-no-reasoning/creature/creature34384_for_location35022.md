There is a thing that the colonial sheets become when they have crept for sufficient centuries. I don't want to call it a stage of life, because that implies development toward something, and this is not toward but *into*. But sometimes, in the knot at the center where pillars draw closest and bioluminescence achieves its stable maximum, the creeping achieves *recursion*. These folded sheets are what I need to describe, though they resist the grammar of description because they resist the grammar of singularity. Call one a *plecton*—from the Greek for interweave, plait, the same root that gives us complex and perplex, these words that gesture toward what cannot be simply held in mind.

And here the reciprocity becomes visible, or as visible as anything here becomes. The stone that a plecton inhabits changes. The stone becomes *plecton-stone*, distinguishable to any sheet that encounters it, that votes upon its surface, that receives the chemical signature of long habitation. The fauna—the jointed things, the gravity-insisters, the beings of too many angles and borrowed organs—have learned, in their slow evolution, to read plecton-stone.
