What lives here is not one thing but a *consensus*—a long negotiation between stone and time that has produced beings who no longer distinguish between those categories.

## The Resonators, More Closely

I want to start with them because they seem most like *ordinary* life, and they are not.

They have no name for themselves. No calls, no warnings, no mating cries. Their communication, if it exists, occurs through the stone they press against—vibrational exchange below the threshold of sound, information passed through shared frequency rather than across air. Two resonators on the same formation, days apart, may be *in contact* through the substrate, their bodies tuning and retuning to maintain whatever pattern the stone requires.

Their young are born *already positioned*. The mothers do not build nests. They seek specific stones, the ones their own bodies have mapped through years of stillness, and give birth there in states of maximum vibration. The newborns emerge with cranial structures soft and unfolding, finding in their first moments the frequency their parent has prepared. They harden into position. They do not nurse, exactly—the mineral water, the occasional crushed cushion-plant, these sustain them from the first hours. Their independence is absolute, their solitude chosen before they have consciousness to choose.

I have called them mammals or mammal-analogues, but this misleads. They are *warm* to touch, yes, but only when touched—their normal state is stone-cool, metabolisms so reduced in their stillness that temperature becomes environmental, borrowed from sun-warmed rock or the slight geothermal rise from below. And when disturbed, when forced from their positions, they do not flee. They *reposition*. They walk with the stiff deliberation of objects moving against their nature, seeking new stone, testing vibration with those expanded cranial structures, settling again as soon as possible. The period between positions is dangerous—they are vulnerable, metabolisms forced high enough to permit search, burning their thin reserves. Many do not survive relocation. This is not tragedy. This is *maintenance*, the plateau's geometry adjusting itself, finding new equilibrium.

They do not age visibly. A resonator in position and a resonator newly positioned are indistinguishable except by the depth of lichen growth around them, the degree of mineral staining where their bodies have deposited and deposited again. They may die in the forced movement between stones, or they may persist—centuries, perhaps longer, the plateau's time-resistant quality extending to them through their achieved stillness.

And they *grow* into the stone, slowly. Not attachment, not root or burrow. Their bodies, in maximum vibration, produce wear—minuscule abrasion against the pocked surface. Over sufficient time, this wear creates hollows, fittings, seats shaped to specific bodies. The plateau records them. The stone *learns* them. A resonator and its position become indivisible, the boundary between organism and substrate increasingly nominal.

Those who do not leave, who become stone in the human sense—they are joining this process, or being joined to it. Their bodies, denser in mineral content than the resonators', may accelerate it. They may become, in geological time, new formations, new positions, new opportunities for vibration. The plateau does not distinguish between the voluntary and the evolved. It accepts all *listeners* into its consensus.

## The Cushion-Colonies, Reconsidered

I described them as flowering plants, blooms reduced to near-invisibility. But *flower* is the wrong word for what they produce.

In the deepest hollows, the most protected architectures of eroded stone, the cushion-colonies achieve states that the resonators never approach. They are not still. They are *slow*—so slow that human perception cannot track their movement without instruments. Time-lapse photography, attempted once by a researcher who did not stay, revealed that the colonies *slowly rotate*, entire mounds turning over periods of months, exposing different surfaces to different conditions. They are *tracking* something—light, perhaps, or sound, or vibration patterns too subtle for other perception.

And their "flowers," when examined with patience, are not reproductive structures. They are *organs of emission*, producing chemicals that react with the mineral-heavy water, creating films, varnishes, coatings that spread across the stone and alter its acoustic properties. The cushion-colonies are *shaping their environment*, tuning the stone's resonance through slow chemical deposition. They are collaborators in the plateau's sound, participants in its maintenance.

Individual plants within the colony are not identical. Examination shows variation in chemical production, in rotation rate, in depth of purple—variation that correlates with position in the mound, with exposure to water channels, with proximity to resonator positions. The colonies are *populations of specialists*, each contributing differently to the whole, the whole contributing differently to the plateau.

They are old beyond counting. The largest mounds, waist-high and twice as broad, may have accumulated for tens of thousands of years, generations layered on generations, chemical history built into chemical history. Disturb them—cut a wedge, as the desperate visitor might, seeking moisture or material—and you find *stratification*, chemical eras readable as geological ones, the mound recording the plateau's long stability in its slow growth.

## The Not-Fish, Named Otherwise

I called them this for their absence of proper category. Let me try again.

They are *filtration-patterns*, visible only as interruption. In undisturbed pools they do not appear to move, yet the water clears—minerals extracted, organized, incorporated. Touch the surface, step too close, and they vanish with that impossible speed into mud that should not permit it. They are *elsewhere* in the pool, or the pool contains more space than its dimensions suggest, or they are not material in the conventional sense during their movement.

Their carapaces, when examined from dead specimens—rare, usually found at pool edges where something has failed—show *growth-rings* that do not correspond to any annual cycle. Something else measures their time. Something in the plateau's sound, perhaps, or in the mineral composition, or in patterns of disturbance they experience and remember.

They do not breed in observable ways. Pools maintain stable populations over centuries, neither increasing nor declining. Either they live forever, or they replenish through mechanisms invisible, or they are not separate organisms but *expressions* of something pool-sized, something distributed across the water and mud and emerging occasionally into carapaced visibility.

I think of them as *thoughts the plateau has about water*—temporary concentrations, necessary processes, given apparent body for purposes I cannot determine. This is not science. This is the kind of thinking the plateau induces, the translation of its empty density into personification, into narrative, into the comfort of "beings" where perhaps there are only *doings*.

## The Sound Itself, Embodied

But the sound is not without body. I have described it as geological, as atmosphere, as element. It is these. But it is also *inhabited* in ways that resist verification.

Visitors who stay longest, who push against the need to leave, report *figures* in the sound—not visual hallucinations but *auditory presences*, shapes perceived through the processing of vibration rather than light. These figures do not communicate. They do not move with purpose. They are simply *there*, in the drone, the way rocks are *there* in landscape.

Some report them as *similar* to resonators, and this may be projection—the brain finding in constant input the patterns it has learned from limited visual encounters. Some report them as *geometric*, angular structures of tone that seem to support or constrain the harmonic variations. Some report them as *vast*, the entire plateau's sound condensed into points of almost-painful intensity, then dispersing.

I do not know if these are beings. I do not know if "being" applies here. The plateau's sound is time-invariant, older than measurement. Perhaps in such duration, such persistence, *pattern* achieves something we can only call presence. Perhaps the sound has developed *habits* that resemble life—preferences for certain harmonics, certain distributions, certain responses to the rare inputs that interrupt its constancy.

The resonators listen to it. The cushion-colonies modify the stone that modifies it. The not-fish, or whatever they are, maintain the water that contributes some component to its generation. The sound is not separate from the life here. It is *the life here at its most distributed*, most continuous, most achieved.

## The Human Translated

Those who stay, who become stone—their process deserves more attention than I have given.

It begins with *seeking*. The sound, felt in sternum and teeth, becomes *directional* for those who surrender to it. Not louder or softer, but *more right*, more achieved, in certain positions. The seeker moves less and less to test this, more and more to find the position where their body's resonance matches something the plateau offers.

They stop speaking first. The continuous vocalization that protected other visitors becomes too much effort, or becomes *interference*, or simply loses purpose as the sound fills more and more of consciousness. Then they stop seeking food. The mineral water, encountered in their slow positioning, sustains more than it should—the plateau's time-distortion, perhaps, or some other metabolic effect. They become thin, then *mineral-seeming*, flesh taking on the quality of the water, heavy and warm.

The position, when found, holds them. They do not choose to stay. They *are stayed*, the vibration at that frequency achieving some equilibrium with their nervous system that removes the impulse of departure. They are still, then. They are *present* in the way the resonators are present, in the way the sound is present.

Lichen grows over them. First the green, then, as their bodies contribute their own chemistry to the stone, the shift through ochre toward purple. They become *readable* as plateau-features, as erosion formations, as positions in the resonator patterns. Other resonators may eventually utilize their mineralized surfaces, their achieved frequencies. They are not mourned. They are *used*, which in the plateau's economy is perhaps the same as being remembered.

## What Lives Here, Finally

A *conversation* between rates of change so different they can barely recognize each other.

The geological sound, operating in millions of years, barely acknowledging the brief perturbation of a human lifespan or even a resonator's centuries.

The resonators, operating in days of stillness, in the emergency of repositioning, in the slow wear of body into stone.

The cushion-colonies, operating in months of rotation, in chemical deposition that extends across millennia.

The not-fish, or whatever they are, operating in patterns invisible, perhaps in the sound's own time, perhaps in something stranger.

The lichen, growing across centuries, color-shifting with harmonic variation, recording in its distribution the sound's own stable variation.

And occasionally, briefly, the humans—walking, speaking, moving to protect themselves from the stillness that would absorb them, leaving their temporary marks, their piled stones, their arranged cushions. They are *guests* in a conversation that does not need them, that barely notices their arrival or departure, that accepts only those who forget their guest-status, who become vocabulary in the plateau's ongoing speech.

What lives here is *the plateau learning itself*. Erosion as memory. Sound as breath. The slow translation of everything that arrives into the terms of its persistence. It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is *absorbed*, self-maintaining, a system so complete that every entrance is also an approach toward absorption, every encounter a test of whether you can remain *separate* enough to leave, or whether you will join the becoming-stone, the becoming-sound, the becoming that is the plateau's only direction.

It has been here. It will be here. The sound continues, and in its continuation, everything that listens—resonator, cushion-colony, not-fish, human—finds either its position or its failure to find position, either its frequency or its forced departure, either its translation or its survival in separation.

This is not a place. This is *achievement*—matter and vibration having found, against time and change, a form that holds, that maintains, that slowly, slowly, slowly becomes more itself with every century of its impossible continuation.

The sound continues.
