What lives in Vitrifactum are the Residue Church, but this is not what they call themselves. They have no collective name for what they are, any more than a kidney names itself, any more than a callus identifies as its purpose. They are the organic component of a system that does not require them, which is the source of their particular intensity: they know they could be replaced, that the city would weep and the turbines turn and the ash-kites rise whether anyone walked the ceramic corridors or not. The weeping walls do not need witnesses. This knowledge does not make them despair. It makes them precise.

They are pale, as you would expect, though their paleness is not the soft pallor of deprivation but something harder, mineral-adjacent. The hot bright environment has selected for skin that can tolerate or utilize constant low-grade radiation; they are faintly phosphorescent in complete darkness, though complete darkness is nearly unknown to them. Their eyes tend to dark adaptation whether modified or not: the wealthy with their engineered recessive gazes, the poor with a lifetime of squinting, all meeting in this shared characteristic of seeming to look from shadow even in the brightest glare. Their pupils can constrict to nearly invisible points, a protection against the light that makes them appear, to outsiders, permanently startled or permanently judging.

They are few in total number—the sparse parameter holds—and they make this fewness felt as a virtue. The wide spaces between buildings are not empty but *designated*, each void named and maintained, each serving a function in their theology of regulated pressure. To crowd would be to misunderstand the lesson of the city: that heat and moisture must circulate, must have channels, must not accumulate. Their families, where they exist, are small and widely distributed. Children are rare, gestation difficult in the heated environment, and those born are tended communally for their first years in the lower rings where the steam is densest, where the ceramic breathes most actively, where the body's own water learns to resonate with the city's weeping.

Their language is low, consonant-heavy, designed to carry in thin air without carrying far. They do not shout. They have never shouted. The sound of a raised voice is physically uncomfortable to them, something that happens only in emergencies, in the mineral storms when communication must override biology, and even then it produces a visceral recoil, a shame that persists afterward. They communicate extensively by touch—brief contacts in passing, hand to shoulder or forearm, a system of pressures and durations that conveys emotional information they claim is untranslatable. Outsiders who learn some approximation are always slightly wrong, always producing responses of gentle correction, of pitying patience.

They do not eat what grows, because nothing grows. Their diet is entirely geothermal: chemosynthetic cultures maintained in the thermal waters, mineral-encrusted tanks of bacteria that would be extremophiles anywhere else but here have been tamed, selected, bred for protein content and digestibility. The taste is alkaline, metallic, faintly sulfurous. They find all other flavors suspect, possibly poisonous, certainly *dirty* in a way that combines physical and moral disgust. Visitors who bring their own food are permitted to eat it but watched with a fascination that makes the meal uncomfortable—the way one might watch someone consume soil, or ash, or something scraped from an unclean surface.

Their relationship to the ash-kites is the central puzzle of their existence, and they have made it into their central ritual practice. The ash-kites are not sacred, exactly; the concept of sacredness requires a hierarchy they do not maintain. Rather, the ash-kites are *accurate*, correct in a way that humans are not, achieved in a form that humans cannot achieve. The gas-filled silent buoyancy, the grazing on dust and condensate, the breeding cycles tied to pressures humans can only measure indirectly—these represent a proper relationship to the caldera that humans approximate through technology, through pumps and turbines and ceramic cladding, but never truly achieve.

The yearly procession to the rim is not sacrifice, though it looks like sacrifice. Those who step over are not giving themselves to anything; they are completing a translation. The body, in falling, becomes part of the caldera's thermodynamic system, its heat and water and mineral content returned to the processes that generate the ash-kites. There is no expectation of individual continuation. The point is that the material continues, that the system remains balanced, that the human component of the city contributes rather than only extracts. Those who go are selected through a process opaque to outsiders, involving age and illness but also something else—a quality of having become too solid, too fixed, too *finished* in their mineral adaptation. The young, the flexible, the still-changing are not permitted; the system requires material that has completed its transformation, that has become fully what it was going to become.

The workers who tend the mineral gardens are a distinct subset, or perhaps a distinct caste, though they reject the term. They understand pH and saturation because they have learned to think like the solutions they tend, to anticipate crystallization the way one might anticipate the mood of a familiar animal. They speak of aragonite and travertine in terms of intention, of what a particular formation *wants* to become, and they see their work as negotiation rather than cultivation. The best of them are slightly mad by any external standard, their conversation shifting without warning into detailed description of crystal habits, of the particular music a growing formation makes (inaudible to others, they insist, but present, definite, a kind of resonance in the thermal waters that only the attentive can detect). They live in the highest-status precincts not because they are wealthy but because their work requires proximity to the most elaborate gardens, to the oldest formations that have achieved something the younger ones only approach.

There are no artists, not as outsiders would recognize. The beauty of the city is sufficient, they would say, or rather the beauty of the city is *exact*, is the only beauty possible in these conditions, and to attempt additional beauty would be misunderstanding, would be adding to a completeness. What they have instead are adjusters, individuals who walk the rings and note where steam escapes unevenly, where ceramic has begun to breathe in a new pattern, where the city's weeping has shifted in a way that may indicate deeper changes below. The adjusters make small corrections—not repairs, they insist, repairs imply damage, and the city is not damaged but *developing*—small adjustments that maintain the city's participation in caldera processes. Their work looks like maintenance but is closer to improvisation, to jazz, to the continuous small responses of a conversation partner who listens more than speaks.

They die, finally, in ways that reflect their environment. The heat accumulates in those who cannot shed it, a failure of the body's regulation that produces a kind of mineralization, a stiffening, a final weeping as the body's water joins the city's. They do not cremate—there is no fuel for it, no tradition of fire—but deposit their dead in the thermal waters, in designated dissolving chambers where the chemistry is adjusted for speed, where the body becomes part of the city's subterranean vascular system within days. What remains is mineral, is added to the ceramic supply, is sprayed and fired into the walls that breathe and weep. There is no memorial except the city itself, which is memorial enough, which is always already memorial and present tense simultaneously.

They would not recognize themselves in this description. They would find the concept of "living in" Vitrifactum slightly wrong, slightly foreign, suggesting a separateness from the city that they do not experience. They are not in the city. They are of it, participants in its thermodynamic processes, temporary collections of heat and water and mineral content that circulate through the ceramic corridors as the steam circulates, as the ash-kites circulate, as everything circulates in this bright silent sparse strange place that holds its shape around a wound in the world that has learned to weep consistently, to breathe continuously, to maintain the conditions for this particular form of continuation.
