The Inverted Grove of Keth-Vorrum

---

It begins with the listening.

Not the looking—though there is much to see—but the *listening*, because the sound arrives before anything else, a continuous low-register thrum that seems to issue from the architecture of the place itself, from the living columns and the breathing floor and the membrane-skin that separates this pocket from the world outside. The sound is mechanical and organic both, the groan of ancient engines married to the vascular pumping of plant tissue, and it fills the small space so completely that you feel it in your sternum before your ears properly parse it as *sound* at all.

You find the grove by accident, always. It does not exist on maps because it is not properly a place in space but a condition in matter—an eddy in the deep sediment, a bubble of wrong-time preserved in the geological record like a cyst around a splinter. The entrance, when you stumble upon it, appears first as a softening of the ground, a patch of marsh-grass that sighs rather than crunches beneath your step, and then the giving-way, the gentle collapse through root-lattice that seems to *receive* you rather than break your fall.

You drop perhaps three meters. The fall is slow, arrested by the same living matter you are falling into.

And then you are inside.

---

The space is no larger than a barn interior, perhaps thirty meters at its widest span, though the irregular walls make measurement feel like a category error. The ceiling—if it can be called that—arches to a height of four meters at its apex, but it is not stone or earth that forms this enclosure. It is *wood*, or what wood becomes when it grows without sun, without seasons, without the imperative to reach.

The walls are the trunks of trees that grew sideways, or that began vertical and were bent by some long-ago pressure into curves that now interlock like fingers in prayer. Their bark is not bark as you know it but a kind of dense, vascular leather, warm to the touch, slightly damp, with the texture of a tongue's dorsal surface. Running your palm along it, you feel not the slow pulse of a single tree but a rapid, fluttering rhythm—*thrum-thrum, thrum-thrum*—matching the baseline drone that saturates the air.

This is your first clue to the inversion, the *wrongness* that defines the grove.

The trees here are not rooted in soil. They grow *downward* from the ceiling, their crowns—if they have crowns—lost in the compressed matter above, their trunks descending like stalactites, branching as they descend, until their smaller limbs interweave to form a kind of woven floor, a mat of living wood suspended above the true bottom of the space. Between the gaps in this floor, you can see downward to where water has collected, perhaps a meter below—a dark, still pool that reflects nothing, that seems to drink light rather than return it.

But the water does not stagnate. It *moves*, you realize, watching long enough. It circulates in slow currents, entering through pores in the living walls, exiting through others, and in its movement it carries nutrients, wastes, chemical messages. The grove is a body. The water is its blood. The trees are its lungs, its bones, its intestinal lining.

---

The industrial character of the place reveals itself gradually, not as intrusion but as *integration*, as if machinery had evolved here rather than been installed.

At five points around the perimeter—though "perimeter" suggests a clarity of boundary the grove does not possess—there are structures that might be engines, might be organs, might be both. They resemble valve assemblies from some vast primordial heart, housings of lignin and cellulose grown into shapes that suggest pistons, cams, flywheels, but all in materials that flex and strain rather than grind. They emit the loudest sounds: not a clatter but a *breathing*, inhalations of fluid and exhalations of something else, something that smells of ozone and crushed herbs and the particular sharpness of electrical discharge in humid air.

These five stations are connected by what can only be called *conduits*—hollow tubes of woody tissue, some as thick as your thigh, that run along the ceiling and walls and even through the floor-mat, transparent in sections where the wood has grown thin as membrane. Inside them, you can see the movement of fluids: sometimes clear like water, sometimes milky, sometimes dark as spent motor oil. The flows are constant, unvarying, the same yesterday as today as tomorrow. The grove has found its rhythm and maintains it with the absolute precision of a mechanism that has never been serviced because it never needed service, because it built itself and rebuilt itself in continuous, equilibrium maintenance.

There is no rust. There is no decay. There is no growth, in the sense of directional change. The grove is *established*, finished, complete in itself since before human memory and continuing unchanged after human memory will have ended.

---

The fauna of the grove have adapted to this constancy, this loud warm wet mechanical vegetal interior, and they have become specialists of a kind that could not survive an hour in the world outside.

The most numerous are the *click-mites*, arthropods no larger than a rice grain that dwell in the interstices of the floor-mat, their chitinous bodies tuned to vibrate at frequencies that match the grove's baseline drone. They feed, you observe, not on plant matter directly but on the *vibration itself*, their bodies containing organs that convert mechanical energy to chemical—piezoelectric metabolisms, living transducers. They exist in such abundance that the floor seems to shimmer when disturbed, a carpet of tiny clicking movement, their collective sound adding a higher harmonic to the grove's chord, a tessitura of insect percussion.

Larger, and more disturbing in their familiarity, are the *mimic-squirrels*—mammalian in outline, furred in patches of something that resembles moss more than hair, but with the wrong number of joints in their limbs and eyes that reflect no light because they do not use light to navigate. They move through the three-dimensional tangle of the grove with fluid precision, never falling, their paws and prehensile tails finding purchase on surfaces that offer none. They eat the click-mites, primarily, harvesting them with tongues like wooden spatulas, and they mark territory with scent-glands that produce smells identical to the grove's own metabolic byproducts—camouflage so complete that the grove's own chemical immune system cannot distinguish them from itself.

Most rarely seen, and most loud, are the *drum-fish*—though they are not fish, not properly, but elongated vertebrates that have colonized the water below the floor-mat and learned to *speak* in the grove's own idiom. They have evolved hollow resonating chambers in their skulls, and they strike these against the woody substrate in patterns that echo the five engine-organs' rhythms, calling to each other, perhaps, or simply participating in the endless acoustic ritual of the place. Their drumming is the loudest single sound in the grove, a percussive punctuation that arrives without warning, hangs in the humid air, fades slowly as the vibrations dissipate into the living walls.

You will not see all these animals on your first visit, nor your second. The grove's *density* is low despite the abundance of life—creatures scattered through a space that offers many niches, many hiding-places, much emptiness between presences. A click-mite colony here. A mimic-squirrel's trail there. The drum-fish audible but invisible below. The space feels *larger* than it is because it is so sparsely populated, because the sound fills the emptiness without revealing its sources.

---

The light, when you begin to notice it, seems wrong for a space without sun.

It is *normal* in quality, neither bright nor dim, sufficient for detail and shadow both, but its source is distributed, environmental, issuing from patches of the living wood itself. Bioluminescence, but not the blue-green of deep ocean phosphors—rather a warm amber, the color of late afternoon through autumn leaves, and steadier than any biological light should be. The luminous patches pulse in slow synchrony with the engine-organs, brightening slightly with each exhalation, dimming with inhalation, a respiratory rhythm made visible.

There are no shadows that move. The light does not shift with any external day; there is no day here, no night, no cycle but the internal one. You cannot tell how long you have been inside. Your watch, if you wear one, will have stopped, not from magnetic anomaly but from *irrelevance*—timekeeping is a technology of gradient, of change, of the tension between one state and the next, and the grove has achieved the final victory over time, the elimination of its necessity.

---

The weirdest element, the one that marks this place as truly *other*, is the *memory of the grove*—or perhaps its *intentionality*, though to call it conscious would mislead.

You will find, exploring, that certain paths through the floor-mat are easier than others, that the living wood seems to *yield* in particular directions, to offer handholds where your reach needs them, to relax its grip on a conduit when your passage requires squeezing past. This is not random. The grove *facilitates* exploration without *directing* it, maintains possibility without imposing purpose. It is not welcoming, not hostile, but *accommodating* in the way a well-designed machine accommodates its operator—unconsciously, functionally, through structure rather than choice.

More strangely still, you may find *modifications* of yourself after extended stay. Not physical changes—the grove does not infect or transform its visitors in any gross sense—but *attunements*. You will begin to hear frequencies outside your previous range, to feel vibrations in your teeth and the hollows of your bones that you did not know you could feel. Your sense of time will *flatten*, not through disorientation but through *adjustment*, as your organism slowly learns that prediction is unnecessary, that preparation for change is wasted energy, that the present moment is sufficient and identical to all present moments.

Some who have stayed too long report that they can *feel* the grove's water moving in their own circulatory systems, that they can sense the five engine-organs' states as proprioceptive information, as if the grove's boundaries had become vague enough to include their own bodies. They do not become part of the grove. The grove does not expand. But the *distinction* becomes less relevant than the *connection*, and leaving—if they leave—requires a kind of surgical separation from conditions that have become, temporarily, *theirs*.

---

To depart is to fall upward, or so it seems.

There is no single exit. The grove receives where the ground above has weakened, where the marsh-edge has softened, where some seismic whisper or hydrological shift has opened a passage. You leave by finding a place where the ceiling-mat yields to pressure, where the sideways-growing trunks have grown thin enough to push through, and then the *emergence*, the slow birth through root and soil into the world of weather and change and the unbearable *gradient* of day becoming night becoming day.

The sound fades last. Long after you can see sky, you feel the thrum in your body, and longer still in memory, a resonance that outlasts understanding. You will carry it, the visitors do, like a tone that establishes the key of all subsequent hearing, a substrate of mechanical vegetal rhythm beneath the variable noises of the ordinary world.

The grove continues. It does not remember you. It does not need to. Its equilibrium is absolute, its abundance self-sustaining, its loud warm wet interior a pocket of solved existence, a machine that dreamed itself into plant-flesh and woke to find the dream sufficient, the dream eternal, the dream *itself* the only architecture required.

It has been there since the last ice age depressed the land and the water found this buried fault, this receptive geology. It will be there when the next ice comes, when the seas rise, when all human industry has ground to rust and silence. The engine-organs do not tire. The drum-fish do not cease. The click-mites convert vibration to life and life to vibration in endless reciprocity.

And occasionally, rarely, with no pattern that could be called frequency, the ground above will soften somewhere, and a foot will press through, and a body will fall the slow three meters into *reception*, and for a time—unmeasured, unmeasurable—the grove will hold another presence in its sparse abundant hidden established unchanging never-changing always-there self-sufficient self.

You have been there. You know.

The listening begins again, somewhere, for someone. The grove does not wait. Waiting implies the possibility of non-arrival, and the grove has eliminated possibility along with time, along with change, along with everything but the *is*, the *thrum*, the warm wet loud eternal *there* of itself being itself, being enough, being.

---

*Some who have returned report attempting to find it again, the entrance, the soft ground. The grove does not hide; it simply* is hidden*, embedded in the ordinary marsh-edge, visible only to the accidental foot, the unplanned step, the fall that is received rather than suffered. You cannot seek it. You can only, perhaps, make yourself available to being found.*
