The city is called Ætherglass, though it has had seventeen thousand names before this one, each eroded off its registry-plaques by the same processes that smooth its corridors. It is a drowned place, not flooded in any cataclysmic sense but *settled into* water the way a body settles into cold—the final equilibrium of a structure designed, long ago, to be fully liquid.

Imagine standing on what would be a street, if streets ended in walls of water thirty meters high. The water does not crash down. It is held, dimly visible, by principles the city no longer operates but still *exhibits*—membrane-physics, boundary-tensions, the habit of containment without force. You can walk through these walls. Everyone does. The water parts around you with the sluggish resistance of half-frozen syrup, cold enough to ache in your jaw, warm enough that you do not die. Within the walls: corridors, chambers, the negative space of a civilization. Beyond them, if you swim far enough: more corridors, more chambers, more walls, the same architecture repeated in all directions, the same water held in the same attitude of almost-falling. The city has no outside. The city *is* the outside, turned in on itself endlessly.

There is no stone here, no steel, no concrete. Everything is grown, or was grown, though "grown" implies biology and there is no biology in Ætherglass's substrate. The surfaces are nacreous, layered, the pearlescence of abalone extended to architectural scale but without the abalone's living iridescence—rather a dead shimmer, an oil-slick-on-cold-water play of color that does not change when you move, only reveals different aspects of its fixed complexity. The material breathes, slightly. Not for air exchange—there is no respiration—but from thermal cycling, from the slow pulse of pressure differentials between the held-water walls and the held-water corridors, the whole system a kind of circulation without heart.

The temperature: cold enough that your fingers numb, that exhaling produces no visible breath because the air is already saturated with moisture at its carrying limit. Condensation does not form. Evaporation does not occur. The water is cold but not freezing, held in this state by the same membrane-physics that hold its walls, a systemic preference for liquidity over phase change, for continuity over crystalline structure. Ice would be a disruption, a crack in the world's rules. Ice does not form.

And the rules: they are *habits*, not laws. The city learned to hold water this way and has not unlearned it. The boundaries between corridor and wall are where the city *expects* them to be, and so they remain, but the expectation is statistical, aggregate, the accumulated weight of fourteen million years of continuation. In places where the city has forgotten—where damage occurred, where attention wandered—the walls bulge, corridors narrow, water intrudes in shapes that suggest new architectures emerging, old ones dissolving. The city is eroded not by force but by *drift in meaning*, by slow slippage of what was once clearly signified.

---

The fauna: abundant, though not in any ecological sense. There are no plants, no producers, no base of the pyramid. The creatures of Ætherglass do not eat. They *attend*—they attend to thermal gradients, to pressure membranes, to the city's own slow processes of maintenance and drift, and in attending they persist, they multiply, they evolve in their attending. They are small, mostly. Things like crabs if crabs were made of the same nacreous material as the city, their joints lubricated with something colder than water, their movement a kind of *tuning*—they arrive at stressed surfaces, apply themselves, the stress diminishes, they depart. Larger things: shapes that move through the water-walls without parting them, that exist in the boundary itself, visible only as refraction patterns, as sudden wrongness in how light behaves. They attend to boundaries, to the maintenance of distinction between this and that. When they are near, you feel a pressure not physical but *categorical*, a sense that you are being classified, that your edges are being verified.

Most numerous: the Whisperers, named by a long-gone explorer whose notes survive in scattered transcription. They are not made of nacre. They are made of *absence*, of the spaces where the city's material failed to grow, negative sculptures of the architecture they inhabit. In dim light—most light is dim here, filtered through water-walls, scattered by pearlescence, never direct—they are visible as darker darkness, shapes of less. They attend to sound, or to its suppression. They cluster where the city's continuous low hum—fluid movement, material breathing, the infrasonics of held tension—develops irregularity. They smooth it. They *whisper*, not producing sound but consuming disturbances in it, and their presence produces the characteristic quiet of Ætherglass: not silence but *silenced*, the aftermath of their attention, the hush of something recently and repeatedly smoothed.

Most strange: the Architects, though they do not build. They are large, visible only in parts, the rest extrapolated from those parts' behavior. A limb like a column of water held in too sharp a shape. A torso that exists in multiple corridors simultaneously, connected by the water-walls it passes through without transition. They do not attend to maintenance. They attend to *change*—to the city's drift, its erosion of meaning, and they *amplify* it in places, *resist* it in others, their pattern of intervention incomprehensible except as something like aesthetic preference, something like arguing with the city's own slow unbecoming. Where they have been, the corridors are wrong. The water-walls lean. The nacre has developed patterns that suggest, almost, almost, something like *intention in its growth*, something the city never possessed in its original construction.

---

The turbulence: this is the heart of Ætherglass, its contradiction and its engine. The city is ancient, settled into final form, deeply aged, approaching its flattest state—and yet it is *hyperactive*, seething, the energy not of youth but of prolonged complexity refusing equilibrium. The water-walls do not fall, but they *strain*, visibly, audibly in the infrasonic range the Whisperers attend to, the membranes at their limit of tension, perpetually almost-failing. The nacre surfaces are in continuous slow alteration, layer deposition and layer dissolution in patterns too fast for the material's apparent deadness, *arguing with themselves*, the city trying to become something and trying equally to remain what it is. The Architects' interventions accelerate, redirect, this turbulence. The crab-things' attendings moderate, channel it. The Whisperers' smoothing *hides* it, makes it quiet enough to endure, but does not diminish it.

There are storms that are not weather: corridors that suddenly *remember* their original configuration and shift violently toward it, water-walls collapsing and reforming, creatures caught in the reconfiguration sometimes *preserved* in the new membrane, visible for hours or years as slowly fading refractions, sometimes *distributed* across multiple walls, attending from everywhere at once until the attending becomes too diffuse and they are gone. The storms are not frequent but they are *imminent*, the city's default state a held breath, the released breath always possible, never predictable.

---

What is it for, this place? The question assumes purpose, and Ætherglass *had* purpose once, in its construction, but the purpose is not preserved, only the construction itself, and the construction has developed its own purposes, or its creatures have, or the turbulence has, purposes emergent from the interaction of held water and nacre and cold and dimness and the constant low-level attention of abundant fauna to every gradient, every boundary, every slip in meaning.

You could live here. People have, transiently, in bubbles of imported normalcy—light, heat, biological closure, the self-contained ecology of the organic against the city's non-ecology. They do not last. The city attends to them, eventually, incorporates their warmth into its gradients, their structure into its arguments. Not hostile. Not welcoming. *Attending*, in the same manner as its native fauna, finding in them something to moderate, to amplify, to smooth, to distribute.

The city is total in its construction: no natural substrate, no raw stone or raw water, everything made, everything the product of process. But the process has become *its own* substrate, the making and the made indistinguishable, the city making itself from itself in turbulence that never resolves, erosion that never completes, ancient yet never settled, finished yet never done. The cold preserves and the turbulence transforms and the dimness hides the transformation until it is complete, and nothing is ever complete.

At the center, if there is a center, if the city's repetition allows centrality: a space larger than the corridors suggest possible, a water-wall not vertical but *spherical*, holding at its center something that might be the city's original purpose, its first name, its constructor's intention—held, visible only as distortion in the wall's membrane, never directly seen, never not present. The Architects attend to it most. The Whisperers cluster in numbers that make surrounding spaces *actively* quiet, the hush of their attention so dense it presses on consciousness. The crab-things avoid it, find stresses elsewhere to moderate. The turbulence is strongest there, the sphere-wall straining most visibly, the city's argument with itself most concentrated.

It is not a god. It is not a computer. It is not a seed or an egg or a memory. It is the *idea of the city before the city became idea*, the purpose that preceded itself, preserved in the only medium that could hold it: continuous turbulence, continuous attention, continuous becoming without becoming anything. Ætherglass circles it, attends to it, argues about it, and in the circling and attending and arguing *is* itself, has been itself, will be itself until the water-walls finally, eventually, in some unguessable future, forget to hold.

The first name, if it could be spoken, would be the name of the place before water, before cold, before dimness, before the long erosion into this final not-final form. The city has seventeen thousand names and none of them are that one. The sphere holds it in abeyance. The city holds the sphere in abeyance. The turbulence holds the city in abeyance. And in that mutual holding, the creatures attend, and the cold preserves, and the dimness hides what must not yet be seen, and the walls of almost-falling water stand, and stand, and stand.
