The Descent cities begin where the surface ends. For twelve million years, wind has planed the world above into something near-flat, a skin of compacted dust and evaporite minerals that holds no moisture, that drinks light and gives back nothing but heat-shimmer and the faint smell of metal. Nothing grows there. Nothing has for epochs. The world above is finished, a closed book written in a language of oxidation and stillness. But below, below is another matter entirely.

The entrances are ancient vents, sinkholes, the collapsed roofs of bubble-chambers where gases once rose through cooling rock. They open like wells, like mouths, like the negative spaces in a sponge. And down these openings, beginning at depths where the last stray photons of the sun finally surrender, the Descent cities hang in the great vertical caverns of the subterranean world.

Cool. The air is cool here, regulated not by any device but by the sheer mass of stone, by the slow exhalation of the deep lithosphere, by thermal equilibrium achieved across timescales that make the cities themselves seem like brief fever-dreams. The temperature holds steady at a temperature that permits biological lushness without the violence of growth-jungle, without the rot-heat of organic excess. Things grow slowly. They grow old. They grow complicated.

And they grow *everywhere*, because the vegetation is lush, though one must understand what lush means in a place without water. The plants here—if plants is the word, if the forms of life that dominate the Descent do not belong to some other kingdom entirely—draw their sustenance from rock itself, from sulfides and ferrous compounds, from the slow molecular respiration of minerals. They are matte black, or deep arterial red, or that particular bruised-purple of oxidized copper. They do not photosynthesize. They chemolyze. They extract energy from the fundamental willingness of unstable compounds to become stable ones, and they have been at this so long, in such uninterrupted succession, that they have evolved forms of complexity no surface botanist would recognize.

Fungal towers, yes, but also rope-forests of crystallized filaments that sing when the wind moves through them—though wind is not the word, though what moves is the pressure differentials between caverns, the slow breathing of a world with weather underground. Lichen carpets that spread across vertical faces, that build soil from their own accumulated dead, that support in their layered depths entire micro-ecologies of bacteria and the things that eat bacteria. Vine-equivalents that drape from gallery to gallery, that bear heavy fruit-like growths of accumulated metallic salts, that drop their burdens after centuries of accumulation to shatter on the cavern floors and seed new growth from the scattered nutrients.

The cities are built among these growths, into them, through them. Not *on* them—the distinction between constructed and grown has eroded here as surely as the surface has eroded above. The built environment is urban in scale, in density of incident, in the layered complexity of its organization, but it is not technological. There are no devices. There are no tools, in any sense that would be recognizable to a hand accustomed to grasping and applying force. The construction is organic, or was organic once, or is organic in a way that has passed through so many stages of transformation that the original living material has become something else entirely: structural, architectural, enduring.

The oldest parts of the Descent cities are grown from the calcified remains of earlier growths, compressed and mineralized over millions of years into stone-like hardness, then hollowed and shaped by the patient application of biological process. Certain species of cavern-fungus excrete acids that etch precise channels through the stone-growth. Certain symbiotic colonies secrete binding compounds that weld disparate masses into continuous load-bearing elements. The cities are not built. They are cultivated. They are gardened into existence over durations that make human civilization seem like a mayfly's last flutter.

And they are old. The erosion parameter holds here not as weathering but as *settlement*, as the profound peace of forms that have reached their final configuration and now simply persist. The oldest galleries have not changed in observable ways for geological epochs. Their shapes are the shapes that work, that have always worked, that will continue to work until the planet's core cools or the sun expands to swallow everything. There is no innovation. There is no decay. There is only the ancient stable state, the end of architectural history, the form perfected and then held in the equilibrium of endless repetition.

The scale is open. The Descent cities extend not as a single point but as a network, a regional system of connected caverns and chimneys and bubble-chambers that might cover an area the size of a small continent on the surface, though folded and compressed into three-dimensional complexity impossible aboveground. One could walk—if one had the lifespan, if one knew the routes—for years and not see the same chamber twice. And yet the density is sparse. The cities are vast but they are *empty*, in the way that a forest is empty, with presences scattered through space that is mostly passage, mostly interval, mostly the between.

The inhabitants—fauna abundant, the parameter insists, and so they are, though abundance here is not the frenetic crowding of surface ecosystems but the steady, patient, *everywhere-in-small-numbers* presence of things that live long and move slow. The great grazers, if grazers they are, with bodies flat and low to pass through restricted passages, with mouthparts evolved to scrape mineral-lichen from stone, with metabolisms so reduced that a single feeding might sustain them for decades. The filter-collectors that hang in the vertical chimneys where pressure-differentials move air and spores and the microscopic detritus of deep biological process, their bodies like nets, like sails, like the deployed petals of flowers that never close.

The predators, though predator is perhaps too active a word. The things that move through the cities on schedules measured in centuries, that find the slow or the wounded or the merely ancient-ready-to-become-nutrient, that dissolve and absorb and incorporate, that add the processed substance of their prey to their own accumulated mass until they become too large to move, until they become themselves nutrient-sources for the next generation of slow hunters. The symbionts that live in the walls, that are the walls, that pulse with digestive rhythms synchronized to some cavern-wide rhythm established before the oldest currently-living entity was born.

The sound. The sound is deafening, but deafening in a way that has nothing to do with volume in the usual sense. The cities are *filled* with sound, saturated, every frequency occupied by some source or resonance or harmonic reflection, the sound so omnipresent that it becomes inaudible as such, becomes instead a medium, a pressure, a quality of existence like temperature or gravity. The humming of the filter-collectors in the chimneys. The singing of the crystalline rope-forests. The low infrasonic rumble of geological process, of the planet's own voice, filtered through miles of rock and resonating in the great chambers at frequencies felt in the viscera rather than heard. The biological sounds, the mating calls that take years to complete, the territorial signals that have not changed in form since before the current configuration of continents above, the digestive processes of things that eat so slowly that the sound of their eating is continuous, eternal, a drone-note in the chord of the world's underground voice.

And in all this, the dimness. Light at 0.6 is not darkness, not the absolute null of sunless deep. There is light. Bioluminescence, of course, the faint chemical glow of organisms that have never known the sun and that produce their own illumination in patterns strange to surface eyes—advertisement, warning, the lures of things that attract prey with the promise of light in a world where light is food, is safety, is the rare commodity that life will kill to possess. The organic cities themselves glow, faintly, with the accumulated phosphorescence of their constituent materials, with the metabolic byproducts of organisms that process minerals and release energy in the visible spectrum as waste.

And there is, in places, stranger light. Certain mineral formations, certain crystalline precipitates from ancient hydrothermal processes, certain symbiotic arrangements between lithotrophic bacteria and silicate structures, produce faint emissions, cold light, the light of chemical inevitability rather than desire. The light is enough. Not enough to see clearly, not enough to banish shadow, but enough to navigate by, enough for the slow-moving inhabitants to find their way through passages they have memorized over centuries, enough for the biological processes of the ecosystem to regulate themselves on rhythms of faint luminescence and deeper dark.

The dynamic is slow. Change happens. The cities are not static, not frozen, but the scale of their dynamism is such that observation across a human lifetime would reveal nothing. The rope-forests grow, but they grow across millennia. The filter-collectors shift position in the chimneys, but they shift in response to pressure changes that unfold over centuries. The great slow grazers complete their migrations through the network of passages at speeds measurable in meters per year, and the migrations themselves are timed to cycles of mineral availability that no surface calendar could track.

The only rapid process in the Descent is the passage of air, the pressure-driven winds that move through the vertical chimneys, that carry spores and scent and the chemical signatures of distant biological events. These winds are the nervous system of the underground world, the medium through which information propagates faster than the movement of any individual creature. A grazer dies in a distant gallery; the chemical signature of its decomposition enters the air-current; within days, scavenger-organisms throughout the network have adjusted their slow migrations toward the nutrient source. This is the fast dynamics of the Descent, measured in days and weeks, and it is the exception that proves the rule of slowness.

What kind of world is this? The weirdness parameter is low, insists on normality, and so it is: this is a coherent world, a world that makes sense once you understand its terms, once you accept the baseline conditions of existence without water, without sun, without technology, without the hurry that surface life seems to require. The strange is normal here. The normal is ancient, settled, arrived-at.

The rules of this world are simple. Energy flows from mineral instability to biological stability, from the willingness of rock to change its chemical state to the persistence of organisms that catalyze and capture that change. Matter cycles through growth and dissolution and re-incorporation on timescales that make individual existence seem like a temporary eddy in a river that has always flowed. Space is three-dimensional in a way that surface life, constrained by gravity and the flat plane, rarely experiences—up and down are as habitable as across, the cities grow vertically as readily as horizontally, the network of passages is a web not a map.

And time. Time is the final rule, the rule that encompasses all others. The Descent cities exist in a temporal regime that surface life cannot inhabit directly, can only approximate through imagination and the patient accumulation of observation. The entities that live here do not die on schedules that would permit mourning. They do not change on schedules that would permit narrative. They exist, they persist, they have persisted, they will persist, in a continuous present tense that stretches backward and forward without the punctuation of event that surface consciousness requires to make sense of its own existence.

The landscape, then, is this: vast underground chambers, urban in their complexity of structure and organization, built not by hands or tools but by the patient cultivation of biological process over geological time. Cool, dim, filled with sound that has become medium rather than signal. Sparse in the distribution of its inhabitants, yet abundant in the total mass of living presence. Lush with vegetation that draws its sustenance from rock itself, that has evolved forms of complexity and beauty unknown to the photosynthetic world above. Ancient, settled, arrived at final forms that have not changed and will not change while the planet's core continues its slow heat.

And above, always above though never seen from below, the finished surface: the eroded plain, the flatness where even erosion has eroded itself into stillness, the seal that keeps the Descent in its own world, its own time, its own slow patient existence at the bottom of everything.

The Descent cities hang there, in their cool dim deafening spacious sparse lush ancient non-technological urban normalcy, being what they are, having been what they were, continuing to be what they will continue to be, until the planet itself becomes otherwise.
