# The Breathing Rootseas

## First Impressions

You do not arrive at the Rootseas. You *are arrived at*—spat upward through a vascular shaft, expelled from the underworld into a realm that should not exist by any geometry you understood below. The cold hits first, a dry cold that seems to *pull* heat from your bones rather than receive it. Then the light. Impossible, blinding, sourceless—a sky of pale green luminosity that has no sun, no center, only a pervasive brightness that makes everything seem slightly translucent, as if you are seeing the world through two layers of thin silk.

You stand (kneel, really—you have fallen) on a surface that is not ground. It is *stem*: a woody cylinder sixty meters across, its bark plated with lichens that glow faintly in the sourceless day. The stem rises and falls gently, slowly, a respiration measured in minutes. You feel it through your palms. It is warm where it should be cold, this one living thing in the vast cold, and you press against it instinctively like a child seeking a mother's heat.

## The Architecture of Living Hollow

The Rootseas are not an ocean of water, though the parameter whispers *damp*. They are an ocean of *hollowness*, an inverted ecology where emptiness is the medium and life clings to the merest suggestion of surface. Imagine a root system vast as a continent, but grown wrong—grown *ambitious*—sending taproots downward through planetary crust, through mantle, until it found... what? Something that made it turn. Something that made it grow upward again, creating cavities, chambers, tubes, vesicles, lung-spaces of impossible scale.

The scale: I must attempt the scale.

A single root-chamber might span eight hundred kilometers in its longest dimension. The walls are living wood, but wood that has never known rings of seasonal growth, wood that lays down tissue in response to *pressure gradients*, to *acoustic events*, to the slow migration of nutrients through the Rootseas' vascular network. The ceiling of the largest chamber, the Great Vesicle, rises forty kilometers above the root-floor at its center—higher than any mountain you knew on the surface you came from, though you did not come from a surface, not truly, you came *up*.

And between floor and ceiling: nothing. Almost nothing. The density parameter demands this emptiness, and the Rootseas obey with enthusiastic precision. The air itself is thin, cold, carrying only the faintest mist of moisture from walls to walls, condensation on condensation, a ecology of droplets that never accumulate.

## The Epiphyte Civilization

Yet total vegetation, the parameter insists. Plant-as-substrate. Plant-as-architecture.

The root-walls are not bare. They support—at vast intervals, with vast spacing—*epiphyte colonies* that have grown to architectural ambition. Imagine a fern that, given kilometers of vertical wall and millennia of stability, begins to build. Its rhizomes weave platforms, chambered structures, hollow towers with fenestrated walls. It grows *upward* toward the light, and *outward* seeking the merest purchase, and *inward* creating protected spaces for—

For what? The built parameter says scattered, primitive. Small structures, faint marks.

In the epiphyte towers, you find them. The *Woven*, whoever they were, whoever they *are*—remnant populations perhaps, descendants of whatever fell or climbed or was *absorbed* into this place long ago. They have left their marks: fire-darkened hollows in fibrous chambers, stone tools (where did they find stone? carried up through the vascular shafts, perhaps, precious as gems), arrangements of woven root-fiber that suggest baskets, nets, sleeping platforms.

But the structures are *abandoned*, or *abandoning*—the epiphytes continue to grow, slowly, slowly, engulfing the platforms, the baskets, the fire-hollows. You find a sleeping nest perfectly preserved in a fern-tower's heart, its fiber still springy after unguessable time, and you realize with a start that the fern *maintained* it, grew *around* it, kept it intact as part of its own structural logic. The Woven did not build in the epiphytes. They collaborated. They were *allowed*.

And now they are gone, or rare as the fauna parameter suggests, rare as—

## The Rare Moving Things

Fauna: rare. Mostly quiet.

You walk—no, you *traverse*, walking is impossible across these scales—you traverse for days along a single root-cylinder, and see nothing that moves but the slow pulse of the wood, the slower migration of epiphyte shadows in the unmoving light. The silence is profound, the sound parameter's quiet achieved not through absence but through *absorption*. The root-wood drinks noise. You shout and hear only your own breath returned, flattened, wooden.

But then, a movement. High on a chamber wall, kilometers distant but visible in the impossible clarity of this bright place: something pale, many-legged, moving across bark with deliberate slowness. A *scaler*, perhaps, a creature that has evolved to never fall, to never make the fatal error of noise in this hungry quiet. It does not scurry. It *flows*, appendage by appendage, a white comma against the brown-green wall.

You will see another in a week. Another in a month. They are rare. They are perhaps the only complex animals, the only things that *move* with intention in all the vast hollow. Or perhaps there are others, quieter still, slower still, visible only to those who wait longer than you can, than anyone could.

The ecology runs otherwise: bacterial films on bark, the vast slow chemistry of the root-wall itself, the epiphytes with their patient architectures. Life without motion. Life as *structure*, as *maintenance*, as the standing wave of biological process in a space too vast for the quick and the loud.

## The Young Sharpness, the Turbulent Dynamic

Erosion: young. Sharp features, recently emerged, fresh.

This seems wrong, does it not? A structure vast enough for continental weather, ancient surely, millions of years in the growing. But the Rootseas are *young* in their surfaces, their textures, the particular quality of their bark. You run your hand across it and feel no smoothing, no polish of ages, only the crisp edge of recent growth. The wood *renews*. The Rootseas are old in their vascular core, ancient beyond reckoning, but they *shed* and *regrow*, sloughing bark in sheets the size of cities, exposing new sharp cambium that slowly, slowly matures only to be shed again.

The turbulent dynamic manifests here: not in weather (there is no weather, the air is too thin, too still, too empty for clouds, for storms), but in this *constant renewal*, this instability of surface. The epiphyte towers are built on bark that will be shed. They exist in creative tension with their substrate, sending new anchoring roots continuously, rebuilding platforms that tilt and shift as the root-wall breathes and renews. The Woven, if they Woven still, would live in permanent improvisation, structures good for decades at most before the great sloughing comes.

And sometimes—rarely, terribly—the turbulence manifests as *vascular event*. A root-chamber contracts suddenly, pressure from below, from the vast hydraulic systems that move nutrients through this body larger than nations. Walls approach. Ceilings descend. The space *breathes in*, and anything in the wrong chamber, anything not anchored to the flexible core-wall, is crushed, compacted, absorbed into the root-mass's renewed tissue. Then the expansion, the fresh hollow, young sharp walls gleaming with sap that freezes in the cold to crystalline lacquer.

These events are unpredictable. The Rootseas have no brain, no intention in this violence. They are simply *active*, turbulent, a system of such scale and complexity that it generates its own chaos, its own creative destruction. You learn to read the wood's tension, to feel in your own body the subtle frequency changes that precede contraction. Or you do not learn, and are absorbed, and your elements circulate upward, downward, through a body that does not distinguish between root and whatever food falls to its mercy.

## The Wet That Is Not Water, The Light That Is Not Sun

Water: damp, intermittent, condensation.

There are no rivers. No lakes. The cold and the emptiness prevent them. But there is *wetness*: the constant exhalation of moisture from root-wall to root-wall, the mist that never quite falls, the crystalline frost that forms on bark and sublimates in the day's warmth (the root-wall's warmth, for the light brings no heat, only vision). The epiphytes have evolved to this: they capture, they hoard, their tissues swollen with gelatinous moisture, their structures designed to slow the passage of air, to precipitate water from the thin atmosphere onto absorbent surfaces.

You can survive here, if you are clever, if you learn the fern-platforms where water concentrates, if you lick the frost at certain times, if you find the rare *seep* where root-pressure forces sap to the surface, freezing into amber nodules you can melt against your skin. The Woven knew this, or know this, in whatever hidden refugia they still maintain.

And the light: bright, sourceless, the parameter at near-maximum. Some say it is bioluminescence of the root-walls themselves, amplified across the vast reflecting surfaces until it achieves day-brightness. Others say there are *openings*, somewhere in the upper reaches, cracks to a surface world where a sun burns, the light filtered through kilometers of transparent tissue, root-cap material evolved to lens and distribute. No one has climbed so high to know. The air thins. The cold deepens. The epiphyte platforms fail.

You look up, in the largest chambers, and see only green brightness fading to featureless glow. Not a ceiling. A *direction* without limit. The scale exceeds itself, becomes something else, a quality of space rather than measurement.

## The Rules That Emerge

After enough time in the Rootseas, you understand there are rules not written in any parameter but enforced nonetheless.

*Move slowly.* The root-walls sense vibration. Fast movement attracts... nothing visible, nothing named, but the slow things have learned, and you learn from them, from their glacial grace.

*Stay near walls.* The center of a chamber is exposure, is vulnerability, is the place where turbulence finds you first, where the crushing pressure arrives with no warning, no surface to grip.

*Follow the vascular rhythms.* The root-pulse is information. It tells you when to sleep, when to travel, when the great chambers are *safe*, in their relative fashion, for crossing.

*Respect the epiphytes.* They are the only shelter, the only community, the only structures that last longer than a single human life. They do not owe you their platforms. They can be damaged, killed, their slow growth interrupted by your haste.

*Do not expect meaning.* The Rootseas have no purpose for you, no design. You are here because you arrived, because something in the vascular depths *expelled* you upward. The place does not want you. It does not not-want you. It simply *is*, in its vast empty vegetable presence, its cold bright turbulent silence, and you persist in it or you do not, with no narrative to authorize either outcome.

## A Moment of Arrival, Perhaps Departure

You rest in a fern-tower, third platform, curled in a nest the Woven left or that grew itself in sympathetic shape. The wood pulses below you, slow, slow. The light does not change. There is no night here, no day, only the continuous bright and the continuous cold and the vast empty air between you and the nearest other solid thing.

You have not seen another human in—how long? The time is difficult. You have eaten epiphyte starch, licked condensation, survived. You have heard, once, at the edge of hearing, a sound like vast wood cracking, pressure releasing, a chamber forming or collapsing somewhere in the distance beyond distance.

You think: I could descend again. Find a vascular shaft downward, surrender to the pressure, be expelled elsewhere, perhaps back to where I came from, perhaps somewhere worse.

You think: I could climb. Follow the fern-towers upward where they succeed in reaching, where the platforms cluster thicker, where perhaps—perhaps—the Woven still exist in some higher, thinner, brighter refuge.

You think: I could stay. Become rare fauna. Learn the slowness. Grow into the architecture. Let the epiphyte gradually, over centuries, absorb my still-living form into its structural logic, my calcium my phosphorus my slow-beating heart becoming part of the tower, the platform, the nest for the next arrival.

The Rootseas pulse. The light holds. The emptiness waits, patient, vast, barely damp, cold, bright, turbulent in its slow vegetable way, young in its endless renewal, ancient in its impossible scale, scattered with the faintest marks of those who came before, who learned or failed to learn, who are rare now as movement, as sound, as anything but the standing wave of structure, of plant-as-world, of world-as-root, of this place that breathes you in and does not, yet, breathe you out.
