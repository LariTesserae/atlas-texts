The Khor are singing.

You cannot hear them, not at first. The air is too thin at these altitudes, the pressure too low for sound to travel as sound. But stand very still on the weathered lip of a transit gantry, let your boots settle into the pitted aluminum where a billion footsteps have polished the oxidation into something like velvet, and you begin to feel it in your teeth. A subsonic resonance, a hum that lives in the architecture itself, in the hollow bones of this place.

They call it Oth-Moal, or sometimes just The Expanse, or more often nothing at all—it's simply where they are, where they have been for eleven thousand years of recorded lineage, and perhaps three times that in the unwritten depths where the Founding Layers sit, compressed and half-melted into geological truth.

The Khor are not insects, though they are segmented. They are not machines, though they manufacture. They are not precisely fauna in any categorical sense that off-world taxonomists have successfully applied, though they move, consume, reproduce, die, and their biomass is abundant beyond counting—sparse in spatial distribution across the vastness, yes, but absolutely teeming in aggregate, a thin soup of biological potential spread across a continent's worth of impossible elevation.

For Oth-Moal is vertical before it is horizontal.

Imagine a world that was once, perhaps, more conventionally spherical. Then imagine it cracked open like an egg, not violently but slowly, geologically, the shell peeling back in great curved plates that rose and rose and *kept rising*, kilometers of continental shelf turned skyward, until the original surface became a dim memory at the root of everything, a warm low place they call the Drown (for reasons none now remember, there being no water to drown in, there never having been, the chemistry here running on silanes and metallic hydrogen compounds that behave something like aqueous chemistry but aren't, quite).

The habitable surface—if surface is the word—is a series of staggered shelves, terraced cliffs, hanging valleys and inverted ones, a Möbius topography where "up" and "across" have merged into a single directional quality. The elevation parameter is extreme not because you stand on a mountain but because you stand *within* a mountain that has become architecture, that has become *process*, the slow geological crack having met the slower geological patience of the Khor, who shaped what the world offered, who grew their industry into the very wounds of planetary formation.

The rock here—weathers. That is the strangeness of it, the established quality of erosion in a place that should not permit it. The atmosphere is thin, yes, but it moves with vast slow weather systems, thermal cells the size of small nations rising and falling across these vertical faces, and the atmosphere carries *reactants*. Not water vapor—never water—but silane mists, aerosolized metals, compounds that interact with the bedrock in ways analogous to oxidation on wetter worlds, producing rind, producing patina, producing the slow accretion of *history* in mineral form.

Everything in Oth-Moal is old. Everything is in its own state, having found an equilibrium between making and unmaking. The industrial infrastructure, the tech at 2.3, is not the bright new violence of expansion but the settled hum of maintenance, of *continuation*. The Khor do not build new cities. They tend the ones that exist, that have existed, that will exist. Their factories are geological features now, their engines so long running that they have developed—there is no other word—*personalities*, operational quirks that the attending Khor negotiate with rather than repair. A pump that runs backward on alternate days. A smelter that produces only when sung to in a particular register. A transit cable that carries passengers only when the weather is particular, not for safety but for something else, some preference or pattern that has emerged from eleven thousand years of continuous operation.

The light is normal, which is to say it comes from a sun, which is to say it falls through the thin atmosphere with a quality of absolute clarity that makes edges sharp enough to cut, that makes distances *wrong* because you can see too far, can see the next terrace up, the next shelf over, can see the Khor moving there if you have good eyes or good instruments, their bodies catching the light in segments of chitin and processed mineral, their carapaces in colors that shift through refraction rather than pigmentation, so that each one seems to carry its own weather, its own small aurora.

They are abundant, the Khor, but sparse in their distribution. A city—if city is the word—might contain only a few thousand individuals scattered across structures that could house millions. They do not aggregate for comfort. They aggregate only where the infrastructure demands their presence, where the singing requires harmonic density, where the great slow processes of their industry need tending. Between these nodes, the terraces walk empty kilometer after kilometer, the transit cables humming with only the wind for passengers, the great erosion-rinds growing in peace, accreting their histories.

And the weirdness—2.2 on a scale that already accommodates so much—lives in the *negotiation* between biological and mineral, in the way the Khor have become inseparable from their built environment, in the way the environment has become responsive, has developed what can only be called preferences. The Founding Layers, the compressed depths, contain Khor bodies mineralized into the very rock, and the rock there behaves differently, carries signals differently, seems in certain frequencies almost to *dream*. The current Khor do not mine those depths. They maintain them. They sing to them. They are waiting, perhaps, for something that emerges on timescales longer than their recorded history, longer than the geological crack that made this place, something that needs only patience and continuation and the absolute commitment to not starting over.

For there is a rule in Oth-Moal, unspoken because it need not be spoken, obvious because it is the condition of existence: *nothing is replaced*. Everything is repaired. Everything is extended. Everything is incorporated into the ongoing. A broken transit gantry is not removed but bridged, the new work growing like scar tissue from the old, the join visible, honored, becoming in time indistinguishable as the erosion-rinds grow over both, as the slow weather of this place makes all things equally historical, equally present, equally *now*.

The sound is mostly quiet. The singing is felt, not heard. The factories operate in frequencies below and above the auditory. The wind in the thin atmosphere makes a whisper that you feel in your lungs if you breathe it, if you are the sort of thing that breathes. Most visitors are not. Most visitors never come. Oth-Moal requires too much patience, offers no resources that can be extracted faster than they accrue, presents no strategic position in any conflict that moves at biological speeds.

But if you do come, if you do stand on that weathered gantry, let your boots settle into the pitted aluminum, feel the hum in your teeth, look out across the terraces and shelves and hanging valleys and the next terrace up where Khor move in their slow abundant sparse vastness, carrying their personal auroras through the absolutely clear light—you understand something. Not about them. They remain opaque, their biology too other, their timescale too extended, their purposes if they have purposes beyond continuation inscrutable.

No. You understand something about *staying*. About the radical act of not abandoning, not replacing, not restarting. About the architecture of commitment, how it weathers, how it accretes, how it sings in frequencies you cannot hear but can, if you are patient, if you are willing to become strange enough, if you let yourself be eroded into the shape of this place—begin, finally, to feel.

They are singing. They have always been singing. The song has no ending. That is the point. That is the whole of Oth-Moal, the whole of this warm dry vast vertical sparse industrial strange mostly-quiet slow place: the recognition that endings are a choice, that the other choice exists, that somewhere in the universe there are those who make it, who keep making it, who will be making it when the sun that lights these terraces has consumed itself and the Khor themselves, perhaps, have mineralized into the final Founding Layer, becoming the dream-rock, becoming the voice that future things—if they come, if they have the patience—might finally, in some unimaginable later frequency, actually hear.
