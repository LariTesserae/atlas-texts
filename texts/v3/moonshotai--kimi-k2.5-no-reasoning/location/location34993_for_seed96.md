The place is called the Drypool, though the name is a joke that outlived its humor, like a nickname that stuck to someone the world forgot.

Nothing here is dry, not really. Water sheets across every surface, not flowing but *held*, caught in the interstices of what was built. The construction—every inch of it, the totality of this high world—is of fired clay and stacked porcelain, biscuit-ware and salt-glaze and raku blackened in fires that burned centuries ago and have not burned since. It is primitive making: wheels turned by foot, kilns fueled by something that was once peat or compressed dung, tools of chipped flint and soft copper. Yet there is so *much* of it. Kiln cities stacked on kiln cities, a density of making that admits no gap, no bare substrate, no stone unturned to brick, no breath of air without its ceramic channel.

You are high up. The elevation presses at your temples, though the atmosphere is wet as a fever, condensing on every surface, running in sheets down walls that are themselves glazed to channel exactly this. The place extends—regional, open, you can see the curvature of the ceramic world as it rolls toward horizons filmed with steam and the particular bright gray of porcelain sky. But you cannot walk freely. The density is too complete. Corridors exist, roofed channels where water runs ankle-deep, their walls so densely tiled with relief sculptures—thousands of hands, millions of faces pressed in wet clay before firing, all identical, all slightly wrong in their repetition—that you must turn sideways in places, must press yourself through crowds of frozen figures that are not quite columns, not quite caryatids, not quite anything living enough to call fauna.

No plants. Nothing rooted. The water carries no duckweed, no film of algae greening its surface. The light—so bright, reflected from every glazed curve, every pooling meniscus—finds nothing organic to absorb it. Only mineral. Only fired earth. The wetness supports a kind of quasi-life, or so the rules of this world seem to suggest: in the deepest pools, in the places where water stands too still for too long, you find *bloom*. Not vegetation. Something else. Nacreous films that rise overnight, layer on micaceous layer, building small hollow forms—cups, vases, amphorae made without hands, precipitated from the saturated water like kidney stones or pearls. They are alive in the way crystals are alive, in the way a stalactite nurses its single drop. You do not touch them. The locals, if there are locals, do not touch them. Perhaps there are no locals. Perhaps the density of built environment only *simulates* habitation, the way a termite mound simulates architecture without intention.

The sound: mostly quiet. The water makes noise but it is a hush, a continuous sibilation that blends to silence in the ear's accommodation. Your footsteps on porcelain tile click and then do not click, absorbed by the wet, by the density of matter around you. Somewhere, always somewhere distant, you hear the *pock* of condensation falling into standing pools from the ceiling of whatever corridor you walk. The rhythm is geological in its slowness. Hours between drops, sometimes. Yet there are so many corridors, so many ceiling-joists sweating their gathered moisture, that the sound becomes continuous at a scale you cannot directly perceive—a statistical rain, a probability of dripping.

Erosion is young. The place has not learned patience. Edges are sharp where tiles meet, where relief hands have broken and show their biscuit cores, pale as bone marrow. The water is already working, already carrying away infinitesimal grains of fired clay, already rounding what was made angular. But slowly. The dynamic here is glacial, more than glacial—tectonic. The pools rise and fall across centuries, not seasons. A corridor that was passable may be waist-deep for three hundred years, then ankle-deep for two hundred more, and no force in the world could make this happen faster. The clay absorbs the violence of change and responds in geological tempo. Floods come, but they are slow floods, knee-deep walls of standing water that advance at walking pace, that take decades to fill a district, that leave no debris because there is nothing loose to carry, only the dissolution of edges, the softening of relief.

Weirdness: the faces. You have noticed them. They are everywhere, pressed into every surface, and they are all the same face. Not human. Not quite. Too broad across the cheekbones, the eyes set at an angle that suggests a skull meant for binocular vision in some other plane than the horizontal. The mouths are slightly open, as if caught in the moment before speech, or the moment after. And in certain lights—the particular bright diffusion when water clouds the air to walking fog—they seem to track you. Not to move, never that. But to have been *placed*, each one, at exactly the angle to meet your gaze as you pass. A density of attention. A packed witnessing.

You have not seen what made them. You will not. The tech does not admit it: simple wheels, simple kilns, hands that pressed wet clay. Yet there are too many. For hands alone, for the time that seems to have passed, the math does not resolve. Something else precipitates here, something that builds like the nacreous cups in the deep pools, layer by slow layer, intention without intention.

The atmosphere: you breathe water as much as air. Not drowning, not quite. The humidity is a second atmosphere, a liquid skin that presses at your lungs, that makes every breath a deliberate act, a negotiation with the wet world. You are always on the edge of hypoxia, the oxygen thinned by elevation, the water taking what little remains into solution. You move slowly. The rules demand it. Fast movement here is a kind of violence against the density, against the packed witnessing faces, against the slow accumulation that is the only process this place recognizes.

You come to a larger space—a plaza, though the word suggests openness and this is only a slightly less packed corridor, a chamber where the ceiling rises to thirty meters and the walls retreat to a width of perhaps twenty. Water stands here to your hips, perfectly still, perfectly reflective. The brightness redoubles, the glazed ceiling a sky that mirrors the water-sky below, and you stand between them unable to tell which is origin, which reflection. In the water, you see the faces continue, upside-down, pressing from below as they press from above. Their mouths are slightly open. The water fills them. Perhaps this is how they drink, century by century. Perhaps this is how they speak, releasing their held breath in the slow bubbles that rise, that break with a sound too quiet to hear, that contribute to the statistical rain, the continuous hush.

You do not know how long you have walked. Time moves strangely in the Drypool, compressed by the density, stretched by the slowness. You may have been here hours or years. The water is cool against your legs, always cool, the temperature of deep porcelain storage, of clay before firing, of the moment before transformation.

There are stages beyond this. You sense them. Places where the water deepens to swimming, to drowning, to pressures that would crush. Places where the density becomes absolute, where the packed construction admits no body, only the slow passage of water through microscopic channels. Places where the brightness amplifies to blindness, where the faces multiply to resolution beyond perception, where the quiet achieves a kind of loudness, a pressure on the eardrum that is sound's inverse. You are not there yet. You are here, in the plaza, between skies, held in the witness of mouths that do not close, in the wet mineral world that never dries, that was never dry, that built itself from water and fired water into this impossible permanence.

Walk carefully. The tiles are slick. The faces are watching. The next drop will fall, is falling, has always been falling, somewhere in the density of this packed and total place.
