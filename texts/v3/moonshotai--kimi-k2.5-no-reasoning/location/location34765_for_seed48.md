The Tepidarium, they call it, though no one remembers who named it or when. A basin perhaps sixty kilometers across, sunk between hills that were worn smooth long before anything human ever touched them. The erosion here has worked for epochs—water and wind and something else, some chemical patience—sculpting a landscape of settled forms that seem almost to have agreed upon their shapes, reached a treaty with entropy.

It is hot. Not the violent heat of eruption or forge, but a clinging, digestive warmth, the temperature of a body that has been running fever for a very long time. The air carries 1.8 of something—water, yes, but not water as falling or flowing, water as condition, as medium. It condenses on every surface, runs in thin sheets down walls that are already wet, has long since finished whatever chemical conversations it might have had with the stone and now simply *is*, a second atmosphere within the first, a skin of moisture over everything. You do not walk here so much as wade through humidity, through air that has weight, that presses against your eardrums, that pools in your lungs like warm broth.

And yet: no plants. Not one. The basin is mineral, fungal at most in the strange pale carpets that bloom in the dampest corners, that fan out in patterns too regular to be entirely natural, too irregular to be machined. The world here predates leaf or petal, or perhaps has simply moved beyond them. The erosion has had nothing organic to work with, nothing to nourish or be nourished by, and so it has made its own vocabulary of shapes: fluted columns where water has dripped for millennia, bowls and hollows where it has pooled and evaporated and pooled again, surfaces of volcanic glass that have been polished to a kind of dreaming by nothing more than particulate wind and patient chemistry.

But the basin is not empty. Far from it. This is the strange arithmetic of the place: density at 0.2, empty by any measure of volume, and yet—built at 2.2, urban, a settlement of some scale, and fauna at 2.6, overwhelming. The eye resolves this slowly. The buildings, if they are buildings, are not dense objects but *distributed* ones, hollow, skeletal, more void than wall. They rise from the basin floor in structures that seem to have grown rather than been constructed, though the regularity of their arrangements betrays intention—grids, radiations, the mathematics of city planning. But the walls are lace, are filament, are cage. What looks from a distance like solid mass reveals itself, as you approach, as scaffolding thin as bone, as membrane stretched in frames, as architecture that has taken the lesson of the air to heart and become mostly space itself.

The industrial technology is similarly *unfolded*. Engines run here, factories operate, but they have no density to compress against. The mechanisms are spread across kilometers, connected by cables thin as spider silk, by channels where fluids move in films rather than streams, by processes that happen in surfaces and boundaries rather than volumes. Electrification, yes, but the wires festoon the latticework like vines, carry potentials measured in whispers rather than shouts. A machine the size of a cathedral might accomplish the work of a terrestrial workshop, its pistons the diameter of threads, its pressures distributed across square kilometers of semipermeable membrane. Production scaled not by intensity but by extension, by the patient accumulation of infinitesimal processes across impossible areas.

And the fauna—this is where the arithmetic becomes almost unbearable. The animals, if animals they are, have adapted to the same dispersion, the same hollow strategy. They are overwhelming in number but not in mass. Insects, at first you think, but the scale is wrong, or right in a wrong way. Things that move through the latticework like blood through capillary beds, things that colonize the wet surfaces and the fungal films, things that have found the niche of *surface dweller* in a world that has abolished volume as primary territory. They are everywhere and nowhere concentrated, their sounds—mostly quiet, 1.0, but present—aggregate to a kind of breathing, the susurrus of countless tiny presences going about their tiny hungers. Predator and prey operate here at a scale that humans strain to perceive, mite-eating-mite in slow catastrophes played out across the factory walls, in the condensation channels, in the thin films of water that sheet down every surface and carry nutrients, carry offspring, carry information in chemical gradients too subtle for any nose but theirs.

The light is dim, 1.2, not darkness but a kind of exhausted brightness, as if the sun has done its work too long, as if the wet air has finally soaked up the last of its urgency. What illumination there is seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, the sky a uniform luminous gray, the wet surfaces reflecting and scattering until direction becomes meaningless, until shadow is only a slight variation in saturation, not a geometry but a mood. You cannot tell morning from evening. The temperature does not shift enough to mark time. The dynamic processes of the place—cycles, yes, but cycles so long and so slow that they read as stillness to anything faster-lived, biological pace measured in generations of bacteria or in the swelling and contraction of geological structures dreaming of metabolism.

And yet it is active, 1.8. The water moves, condenses, runs, pools, evaporates, condenses again. The factories operate, whatever their purpose, whatever they make in their diffuse and patient way. The fauna feeds and breeds and dies, overwhelming in its totality if invisible in any single moment. The fungal films spread and senesce and are consumed. The very architecture seems to grow, to repair itself, to slowly extend its lace across the basin in patterns that might, in centuries, double or halve its coverage. Nothing is still, but nothing moves quickly enough to catch the eye unprepared.

It is normal, 0.0 weirdness. This is the perhaps most terrible thing. There is nothing here that violates expectation once the parameters are understood. It is a world that has simply found different solutions to familiar problems, optimized for different constraints, evolved or designed or accreted into a configuration that is strange only by the measure of other worlds, not by its own internal logic. The heat makes sense given the humidity. The humidity makes sense given the mineral surface, the lack of vegetation to transpire it into control. The hollow architecture makes sense given the empty density, the pressure to spread, to become surface, to colonize space without mass. The diffuse industry makes sense given the diffuse resources, the absence of concentrated fuel, the availability of endless wet surfaces for chemical process, for thermal differentials measured in fractions of degrees across kilometers.

You could live here, if you were small enough, if you were slow enough, if you could learn to think in surfaces rather than volumes, in patience rather than urgency. Some do, perhaps. There are structures that seem designed for habitation, for observation, for the kind of consciousness that might want to watch this slow world and think its slow thoughts. They are hollowest of all, bubbles in the lattice, spaces preserved against the general tendency, maintained by some expenditure of effort or will or simply by the lack of any compelling reason for them to collapse.

The sound, mostly quiet, 1.0, is the sound of water moving in films, of countless tiny feet on wet surfaces, of distant machinery operating at thresholds of perception, of the air itself settling and shifting in thermal gradients too slight to feel. No voices, or voices so attenuated by the humidity, so absorbed by the endless wet surfaces, that they become part of the general texture, indistinguishable from the breathing of the place.

This is the Tepidarium. It has existed long enough for erosion to settle into agreement with it. It will exist, probably, until the heat source fails, until the water finds a deeper level to sink to, until some parameter shifts and the patient equilibrium breaks and something new begins the long process of becoming itself. Until then, it is what it is: wet, hot, empty, built, alive with overwhelming life that overwhelms only in aggregate, a city of surfaces, a factory of films, a world where volume is a luxury and time moves in the only currency that matters, which is the patience of minerals, the slow ambition of water, the endless present of a place that has found its form and does not hurry to change it.
