The Sunken Choir of Keth-Vorath

---

You approach it first by vibration rather than sight—a low thrum in the sternum, the kind that makes your teeth dream of music they cannot quite hear. Then the ridge falls away, and there it is: a bowl of city cupped in mountain hands, perhaps three hundred paces across at its widest, dense with something that moves and something that stays, and something else that does both at once depending on how you look at it.

The water is the first strangeness. Every surface weeps. The black stone of the old construction—basalt or something heavier, something that drinks light and holds it uneasily—runs with moisture that does not freeze despite the killing cold. The air is saturated, breath-visible, breath-heavy, each exhale joining the general condensation. Water beads on every edge, hangs in trembling globules from the undersides of things where gravity seems negotiable. It pools in the worn grooves of ancient streets, runs in rivulets down walls that were carved before iron came to this range, gathers in suspended curtains where the dense-packed structures have worn each other smooth with proximity. The stone itself seems to seep, a slow geological weeping, springs rising through porous foundations to emerge as tears from carved mouths, from the eyes of statues that may or may not have had eyes originally.

And cold. The cold is absolute, intellectual, a kind of stillness in the temperature that makes the wetness paradoxical, that keeps you waiting for ice that never arrives. Perhaps the water moves too fast to freeze, or perhaps something in the stone forbids it. Your fingers numb. Your lungs burn with the metallic taste of altitude. Two thousand meters up, perhaps more—the air thin enough that sound carries strangely, but here sound is already strange.

The density presses. The city was built small to begin with, and time has layered it upon itself. Original structures—low, domed, cyclopean in their masonry—support later constructions wedged into every available space, then later constructions upon those, until the vertical accumulation resembles less architecture than geology, a sedimentary record of need pressing against need. No alley runs straight for more than ten paces before turning, bottlenecking, opening into a pocket courtyard where laundry—ancient, mineral-stained, somehow still present—hangs in frozen drapery. The current inhabitants move through this in vast numbers, or perhaps the same inhabitants move vast numbers of times; the distinction blurs, here.

Because they are the second strangeness, after the water. The people—if people—of Keth-Vorath.

You cannot fix their number. This is not imprecision but impossibility. In any given courtyard, alcove, stairwell, you count twelve, twenty, forty forms moving with purpose, carrying water-skins, baskets of something wet and luminous, infants wrapped in oiled leather against the weeping atmosphere. But turn your head, and there are more in the space you just measured, or fewer, or the same forms in different arrangements, as if the act of observation stirs them into new configurations. They do not appear or disappear. They are simply *differently present* depending on how you attend. The density parameter is literal: many presences per volume, layered, saturating the available space in ways that make three-dimensional mapping inadequate.

They are short, most of them, built for this compressed architecture, heads slightly large, hands that seem to hold too many joints. Their tech is primitive in the precise sense: no metal visible except what has been carried in from outside, and that tarnishes immediately, blooms with impossible verdigris, is ritually cast into the central well at intervals. They work in stone, in water-cured hides, in something woven from the fibrous algae that grows in the perpetual damp of the lower levels—rope and fabric and rough paper that never truly dries. Their tools are obsidian, bone, wood impregnated with some mineral that hardens it almost to stone itself. They build with mortarless precision, each block dressed by endless patient abrasion, fitted until the water's constant pressure tests the seal and finds it sufficient.

And they are silent. Or nearly. This is the quiet that waits beneath the water-thrum, beneath the subsonic geological hum that first announced the place. The inhabitants of Keth-Vorath do not speak, or if they speak, the sounds fall below human hearing, into those frequencies that register as pressure rather than sound. You see them communicate—elaborate gesture-dialogues, quick exchange of postures, occasional touch that seems to transmit complex information through the simple fact of contact. But the streets, the crowded impossible streets, run with a hush that belongs to empty churches, to snow-covered fields at morning, to the moment after a held breath.

Except sometimes. Sometimes the silence breaks.

The third strangeness. The dynamic turbulence.

There is no weather pattern to Keth-Vorath, or rather, the weather is concentrated, internal, eruptive. Things change suddenly and locally and violently without warning to anyone but the inhabitants, who seem to navigate it with the ease of long practice. A street that ran north-south when you entered it now runs up-down, or spirals, or briefly encloses a volume that contains what you can only describe as weather—actual storm-system, cloud and lightning and sleet, compressed into a space no larger than a modest room, raging with the focused intensity of a tantrum while inhabitants walk calmly around its perimeter, carrying their baskets, tending their infants. Then it subsides, and the street is north-south again, or perhaps was always, and you are uncertain whether you witnessed something or imagined it in the thin air, the cold that works at cognition like water at stone.

The central well is worst, or best, or simply most itself. Shaft-sunk through the accumulated construction, perhaps two hundred meters to water that is not water but more concentrated wetness, a kind of liquid density. It draws the eye, the attention, the counting faculty. Stand at its edge and you will lose track of the inhabitants who surround you, or find that you have been counting the same one repeatedly, or that the well's perspective has become recursive, depths containing depths, each with its own ring of silent watchers. Things emerge from it: the luminosity that feeds their rough agriculture in terraced chambers where lichen-gardens carpet damp stone; the occasional solid object, tool-shaped or body-shaped, that is carried away with reverent haste; the weather itself, sometimes, compressed storms released upward to expend themselves against the weeping sky.

The fauna is abundant in the same mode as the population. They keep no animals visibly, yet animals are present—wet-furred things that pass through walls or perhaps through momentary gaps in attention, shelled creatures that cling to the weeping stone and are harvested for their nacre, something avian that flies in patterns too dense for single bodies, too coordinated for flocks, occupying the air in quantities that seem to exceed its volume until you look directly, and find only ordinary rain-wet wings beating against the cold. The ecology is wet-cold-abundant, life finding the interstices, thriving in the saturated atmosphere and the stone's constant seepage, layered upon itself like everything else in this dense small place.

The light, though. The light is absolute.

At this altitude, even clouded, even weeping, the sky illuminates with a clarity that belongs to desert, to pole, to places where the air's thinness lets through radiation unobstructed. But Keth-Vorath amplifies, or perhaps contains, light in ways that exceed its source. The black stone does not reflect but seems to re-emit, each water droplet becoming lens and lamp simultaneously, so that the city's density glows from within, every surface alive with its own soft luminescence, shadowless or many-shadowed depending on where you stand and how you breathe. The brightest hours have a quality of interiority, as if the light were remembering its source rather than receiving it, as if the whole place were a single eye adjusting to illumination that never varies yet never repeats.

And in this light, the erosion shows. The established balance, the long-existed weathering. Nothing here is new. Nothing has been new for durations that the inhabitants may not themselves remember, their primitive tech maintained by endless refinement of the already-perfect, their architecture settled into forms that need no alteration because they have become, finally, accurate. The water has worn what it will wear. The stone weeps what it will weep. The storms expend themselves in their pockets and alleys and well-shafts, and the city remains, small, dense, wet, cold, bright, strange, turbulent, silent, abundant with presences that are and are not there, carrying their baskets, tending their infants, moving through the luminous rain without a sound.

You came to map it. You will leave unable to say whether you were in one place or many, whether the inhabitants noticed you or included you or never knew you were there, whether the time you spent was hours or days or a single extended moment of attention, compressed and layered like the population itself. The ridge falls away behind you, and there is only the thrum in your sternum, the wet memory in your lungs, the faint taste of impossible minerals, and the persistent sense—auditory, almost—that somewhere just below hearing, the Sunken Choir continues its endless, silent, many-voiced song.

*Some who return build miniature Keth-Voraths in bottles, in enclosed gardens, in the condensation of their own sealed breath. They add water, wait, watch for the storms that sometimes come.*
