The city of Vitrifactum rises from a plain of thermal glass at the lip of a caldera, its elevation not dramatic—perhaps two thousand meters above some vanished sea level—but enough that the air carries a thinness, a quality of being *worked over* by distance and heat. The caldera itself is old, settled into its form like a wound that has learned to hold its shape. Its rim has been weathered for ten million years, not sharp now but rounded, maternal, a brow of stone above the city's sweating flank.

And the city sweats. That is the first thing. The water parameter here is not rivers or lakes—there is almost no standing water to speak of, no vegetation to require it—but rather a constant thermodynamic weeping. Hot springs thread through the city's foundation, not scenic pools but a subterranean vascular system, water forced through fissures in the volcanic bedrock by pressures the original settlers must have understood and exploited. The buildings—industrial, layered, organized in concentric rings around the caldera's inner slope—are clad in a ceramic the color of dried blood and old ivory, and these ceramics *breathe*. They exhale steam at every joint and seam. The water table sits mere meters below the surface, so close that basement levels must be continuously pumped, and even then the walls weep, the floors grow slick with condensate, the whole structure seems less built than *secreted*, something grown in conditions too wet for stone, too hot for ordinary architecture.

There is no plant life. None. The parameter insists on this so absolutely that its absence becomes almost audible, a negative space where green should be. In its place: mineral gardens, elaborate formations of deposited salts and silicates that grow in the thermal waters like mad coral. Aragonite fans. Travertine terraces in miniature, creeping up through cracks in the pavement. The "gardens" of wealthy precincts are arrangements of these accretions, tended by workers who understand pH and saturation, who seed supersaturated solutions with seed-crystals and grow formations over decades that look almost—almost—like something alive. But they are not alive. The city exists in a biological vacuum where even extremophile bacteria have been excluded, deliberately or accidentally, by the chemistry of the waters themselves. Perhaps too alkaline, too laden with metal salts. Perhaps engineered that way, once, to prevent something worse.

Yet the fauna parameter insists: abundant. And so there are animals, but animals of a particular kind. They arrive on thermal columns from the caldera's heart, where something—some process, some ecosystem sealed away from the surface world—generates flying things on the scale of kites or small aircraft. Call them vent-bats, though they are not bats, or ash-kites, though they do not truly fly in the way of birds. They are buoyant, gas-filled, translucent when the bright light passes through them. Their bodies are membranes stretched over internal cavities where hydrogen or methane or some lighter-than-air compound accumulates, heated perhaps by the city's eternal warmth. They drift up from the caldera floor on invisible thermals, hundreds of them, thousands, their numbers making up for the emptiness below. They graze on airborne particulates, on mineral dust, on the condensate that beads and evaporates in the bright air. They are silent. Utterly silent. Their movement makes no sound; they have no calls; the sound parameter demands this silence or near-silence, and the ash-kites comply. One sees them by shadow, by the occasional iridescent flash of membrane in the high bright, by the slight obstruction they make against the glare. They are beautiful and they are eerie and they are *wrong* in a way that satisfies the weirdness parameter—not dramatically wrong, not tentacles-and-void wrong, but subtly, insistently other.

The city's industry runs on this heat. That is the logic that holds the parameters together. The urban scale, the industrial technology, the hot wet constant of the place—it is a geothermal economy, not extractive but *participatory*, the city existing in symbiosis with the caldera's exhalations. Factories occupy the lower rings, where the steam is hottest, where turbines can be driven directly by the pressure escaping from below. The ceramic cladding is itself industrial product, manufactured from local silicates dissolved in the thermal waters, sprayed and fired in place by the heat that permeates everything. The city builds itself, in a sense, from the caldera's generosity or indifference, depending on your theology.

Higher rings, closer to the rim, the temperature moderates slightly—still hot, still weeping, but bearable for dwellings. Here the density parameter plays out: sparse, balanced with emptiness. The rings are wide, the buildings scattered with deliberate space between them, courtyards of thermal glass sand, the mineral gardens, the necessary voids where steam escapes upward rather than accumulating. The city feels uncrowded even where thousands live. The sound of it is machinery at the lower edge of hearing, turbines turning in sealed chambers, pumps circulating water through the buildings' veins, a subsonic rumble more felt than heard. No voices carry far in the thin bright air. No birdsong, of course. No wind in leaves. The ash-kites make their silent patrol. The silence is not empty but *full*, saturated with the absence of expected noise, the ear straining and finding only the body's own pulse, the city's low mechanical breath.

The light is almost too much. Brightness at 2.6 of 3, in a landscape without shade-giving trees, with buildings spaced too far to cast significant shadow at most hours, with a sky that seems to amplify rather than diffuse. The ceramic surfaces are slightly reflective, designed perhaps for this, bouncing glare into every corner. Workers and residents develop a stooped walk, eyes downcast, or wear hoods of woven mineral fiber, or—higher status—have their irises modified, darkened, given protective filters that make their gaze seem to recede into shadowed sockets. The ash-kites, seen against the sky, are dark holes in the brightness, their translucent bodies invisible, only their internal structures—veins, gas-sacs, the dim pulse of whatever circulatory process they possess—visible as ghost architecture within.

Erosion, established, settled-form: the city has existed long enough to have become part of the caldera's own weathering. Certain structures have collapsed and been built over, not cleared, forming hollow hills where new buildings attach like barnacles. The thermal waters have deposited minerals across generations of machinery, sealing old engines in layers of travertine, creating industrial fossils that are sometimes excavated for materials, sometimes venerated as evidence of the city's depth in time. The weathering is in balance now; the city loses to the caldera at roughly the rate it grows. A steady state. A kind of peace, or truce.

And active—dynamic—the ecosystem, if it can be called that, of city and caldera and ash-kites, moves. The thermal waters shift, new vents opening, old ones sealing, requiring constant adjustment of pumps and tunnels. The ash-kites have breeding cycles tied to pressure changes below, to some seasonality in the caldera's respiration that the surface dwellers do not fully understand but have learned to predict. There are storms, not of rain—rain is rare, the air too hot to hold much moisture, most water staying below or in the weeping structures—but of mineral dust, of ash from whatever deep fires still feed the system, of displaced ash-kites driven in dense silent flocks across the city's open spaces, their gas-sacs flashing in the too-bright light. After these storms, everything is coated, everything must be cleaned, and the cleaned material feeds back into the ceramic industry's crucibles.

What do the inhabitants want? What do they believe? The parameters do not specify, but the place suggests: they have made peace with a world that offers heat and water and nothing green, that supports abundant life only in this strange buoyant form, that surrounds them with industry made necessary by the very conditions that make it possible. They are practical, probably, but also perhaps religious in a direction hard for outsiders to follow—worshippers of the Vent, the Opening, the perpetual exhalation that requires no sacrifice because it takes constantly, indifferently, in the form of human labor, human attention, human lives spent in proximity to heat and pressure and the weeping of walls.

I imagine a ritual: once yearly, or once whenever the pressure demands, a procession to the caldera's true rim, where the ash-kites rise most thickly, where the bright light is unmediated and the silence absolute. There, certain individuals—aged, or sick, or willing, or condemned—step over. Not death, exactly, or not only death. The fall is long enough to see the shape of things, the city a ceramic band, the glass plain beyond, some other formations in the middle distance that suggest other cities, other vents, other forms this life might take. And then the heat, and the joining with whatever processes generate the ash-kites, or feed them, or simply exist in that space too deep for human presence. The city receives back, perhaps, in the form of pressure stabilized, of ash-kites prolific that season, of waters that weep a little less, a little more bearably, for a time.

The weirdness is not the weirdness of monsters but of *system*: a place that holds together, that functions, that supports abundant life and urban industry and a kind of beauty in its mineral gardens and silent fliers, yet holds these together without any of the usual agreements between living things and their places. No green. No sound. No familiar animal, only the gas-filled things from below. And yet not dead, not abandoned, not failed—active, settled, balanced in its own state, a hot wet silent bright sparse strange city weeping at the edge of a wound in the world that has learned, like the city, to hold its shape.
