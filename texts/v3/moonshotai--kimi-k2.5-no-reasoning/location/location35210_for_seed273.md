The city is called Threnod, and it rises from the sea in a way that makes you question whether "rises" is the right word—perhaps "accumulated," or "sedimented," or "grew like a callus." It is built entirely, every surface, every substrate, every ostensibly wild thing. There is no bedrock here, only the compressed foundations of earlier Threnods, the city built atop itself so many times that the original island—if there was an original island—has become archaeological rumor. What exists now is a vertical archipelago of constructed hills, artificial peaks rearing 2,400 meters above the acid-green shallows of the Surrounding, which is what the inhabitants call the sea, as if by refusing its proper name they might deny its persistent, patient existence at their feet.

The elevation is extreme but not dramatic. The city does not thrust; it accumulates. Its highest districts are reached not by sudden cliffs but by gradients so gradual you might climb for hours without noticing the accumulating pressure in your ears, the thinning of the damp warm air. The slope is engineered this way, has been engineered this way for millennia. The old civil engineers of Threnod—there is a guild, still active, called the Society for the Prevention of Suddenness—designed against the abrupt. Staircases are forbidden above 800 meters. Escalators, the industrial heritage of the city's nineteenth computational age, wind in long helical routes through the dense districts, their brass mechanisms continuously maintained, their treads worn concave by centuries of footfall.

You can hear them. The sound of Threnod is loud but specific: not the chaotic noise of organic cities, but the layered industrial drone of machinery in balance. The escalators hum at 60 hertz, a frequency chosen by some ancient acoustic committee for its supposed neutrality. Beneath this, the city breathes. The ventilation systems, kilometer-wide turbines buried in the constructed hills, draw the damp warm air from the Surrounding and push it upward through channels lined with copper sulfate to prevent the lush vegetation from rotting the machinery. The vegetation is everywhere, is *required* everywhere by the city's charter of atmospherics. It grew originally as vapor management, as living condensers in a place where water exists not as flowing abundance but as persistent damp, as sweat, as breath-fog, as the minute precipitation that falls upward from the heated lower districts to cool and descend again in the heights.

Every surface grows. The facades of the towers, which are not towers in the skyscraper sense but rather vertical hills of continuous construction, are terraced in geometries that maximize leaf-surface. Vines engineered in the biological manufactories of the 3400s root in the artificial substrate and extend tendrils toward the bright light, which is abundant but not direct. The city's upper reaches are clad in a material called whiteglass, not truly glass but a ceramic developed for its property of diffusing the intense tropical luminescence into something the plants can use without being destroyed. The result is a city that glows, that seems internally luminous, every leaf receiving its portion of scattered radiance.

The density is overwhelming until you learn to parse it. There are, the census estimates, forty million inhabitants, but "inhabitants" is a narrow category excluding the manufactured fauna, the maintenance organisms, the semi-autonomous constructions that occupy the interstitial volumes. Threnod is dense with presences. The streets—there are streets, though they wind in three dimensions through the constructed terrain—are narrow, often canopied by living bridges, by the aerial roots of epiphytes that have learned to use the city's architecture as forest. The fauna moves through this density with the confidence of evolution, which in Threnod means the confidence of long design. There are no pets, no livestock in the traditional sense. The animals are functional, are atmospheric, are aesthetic, are sometimes all three and sometimes something else the categories don't catch. The condenser-bats, mammalian in appearance but probably not mammalian in origin, hang in their millions from the ceilings of the lower transit vaults, their bodies modified to precipitate moisture from the saturated air, to produce the intermittent rain that falls in scheduled intervals through the city's irrigation architecture.

The sound of their urine, the polite term is "processing output," falling sixty meters into collection troughs, is part of the loudness, part of the industrial music.

The erosion is established, settled-form, long-existed. The city does not decay in the way organic things decay, nor in the way purely mechanical things break. It weathers. The whiteglass develops a particular patina, a microtopography of deposited minerals from the constant vapor, that the aesthetic committees have declared preferable to new material. There are specialists, highly respected, who accelerate this weathering in controlled ways, who introduce micro-erosive agents to achieve the balance that reads as authenticity. The city has been in its own state for so long that "new" and "old" have become relative to the point of meaninglessness. A structure built yesterday using techniques unchanged for four millennia is simultaneously contemporary and ancient. The industrial technology, which is substantial—electric grids, manufacturing nuclei, the vast desalination and mineral extraction operations that process the Surrounding—operates at a pace that makes it feel almost geological. Factories function for centuries without significant change. Production is continuous but not urgent, scaled but not accelerating. The city consumes what it needs, which is what it has always needed, which is what it will need until some unimaginable disruption.

That disruption is not imagined. The dynamic is slow, the cycles gradual. The city's climate systems pulse across decades, not seasons. The vegetation follows, the fauna follows, the human inhabitants follow or they leave. There are departure ports for those who cannot bear the patient pace, who require the stimulation of faster environments. Few use them. Threnod has a way of adjusting the expectations of its inhabitants, of training a temporal sensibility that finds the rapid world elsewhere unbearable.

The water is 1.4, damp, intermittent, present as potential more than as flow. It exists in the air, in the plants, in the condensate of the bats and the slow perspiration of the heated lower levels. There are rivers in Threnod, but they are narrow, artificial, more gesture than geography. They carry the processed output of the atmospheric systems to where it is needed, which is everywhere and nowhere, which is the condition of the damp. It never quite rains, except where it is scheduled to rain, and even then it is a soft precipitation, a suggestion of moisture, the city's way of reminding itself of what it has almost but not quite eliminated.

The weirdness is low but present, unusual in the manner of a hair found in food, something that cannot be fully integrated or fully rejected. There are places in Threnod where the long accumulation has produced effects unanticipated by any design committee, where the density of constructed history has generated a kind of spontaneous organization in the substrate itself. The lowest levels, the earliest foundations, are rumored to have become something other than architecture, have developed a slow kind of awareness from the accumulated electrical potentials of millennia of habitation. The Society for the Prevention of Suddenness does not comment on these rumors, which itself constitutes a kind of confirmation. There are maintenance workers who descend to these depths and return changed, not dramatically, not in ways that violate the city's slow dynamic, but changed. They speak less. They move with a certainty that seems borrowed from something older than the training manuals. They are tolerated, are given assignments that suit their altered disposition, are in some sense honored as the city's way of acknowledging what it cannot understand about itself.

I have been trying to describe Threnod for hours now, and the place keeps becoming more itself. The bright diffused light has shifted into the long afternoon phase, the whiteglass reddening slightly as it scatters the declining sun. The condenser-bats are stirring for their first processing cycle. Somewhere above, in the extreme elevation where the air is thin enough to require mechanical assistance, the continuous harvest of atmospheric moisture is beginning its descent through channels I will never see. The city sounds loud, sounds slow, sounds like something that has learned to sound like itself through longer practice than any organic thing could accumulate.

There is a bench, grown rather than built, a secretion of the older substrate-organisms into a form the aesthetic committees approved some eight centuries past. I will sit here. The damp warm air will condense on my skin. The vegetation will continue its patient conversion of diffused light into the oxygen I breathe, into the sugars that feed the fauna that feed cycles I participate in without understanding. The city will continue, is continuing, has always continued in this slow dense bright loud constructed way, this artificial extreme where nothing is natural and everything has become, through long existence, something that natural might aspire to mean.
