The city of Veth Orren does not sit upon the mountain. It *is* the mountain, or what the mountain has become after ten thousand years of patient, communal burrowing.

You approach from the lower valleys where the last stunted black pines cling to shale slopes, and at first you see only the mountain itself—a young peak still shedding its birth-skin, its flanks raw with scree and the glint of fresh-fractured basalt. The erosion parameter here reads as *newness*, not softness. Nothing has worn smooth. Every edge bites. The peak rises well above the snowline, but the snow does not lie evenly; it gathers in the city's catchment basins, the intentional hollows carved by hands that understood water as something to be courted, not commanded.

Then you see the mouths. Hundreds of them. Dark rectangles punched into the mountainside at every elevation, some no larger than a stretched armspan, others yawning wide enough for processions. They read as windows, as doors, but most often as *vents*—the city breathing, the city sweating. Around each mouth, the stone has blackened with soot and cooking grease, mineral stains from the eternal leaching of damp, the slow precipitation of human occupancy. The damp parameter lives here in the condensate that pearls on every interior surface, that forces the Vethoren to oil their leather wrappings and wax their woven reed cloaks with religious precision. Rain comes seldom but memorably, brief violent thrashings that the mountain swallows through its thousand channels, funneling water down to cisterns carved in the deeper shafts where the cold never lifts.

---

The scale of it takes days to comprehend.

You enter through a mid-level mouth, one of the old public passages, and the tunnel curves inward not toward a center but toward a *volume*—a three-dimensional honeycomb without plan, without master architect, accreted across generations by consensus and occasional violence. The corridors branch, split, dead-end, reconnect. Some shafts descend into the volcanic warmth; others climb toward chimneys that vent at the summit. The ceiling heights vary wildly: crouch passages where a laden farmer scrapes his bundle along stone, then sudden echoing galleries where whole neighborhoods hang suspended, platforms and rope bridges and cantilevered floors lashed together with sinew and the cured gut of the mountain's own beasts.

The density parameter manifests as intimacy without privacy. You hear your neighbors always—the scrape of their stone tools, their coughing in the cold dark, the rhythmic work of their existence. Families occupy cavities barely larger than their sleeping bodies, separated from the passage by hanging membranes of oiled skin. Storage hangs in nets from the ceiling. Drying herbs and fungi crust every available surface. Children move through the infrastructure like water through roots, knowing hundreds of paths opaque to any adult who hasn't lived their particular corner. The city *presses*. Its population numbers in the tens of thousands, perhaps more—no census has ever succeeded—and they exist in a space that would seem to violently reject such concentration.

Yet they have made peace with compression.

---

What they have not made, in the technological sense, are things.

The primitive parameter does not mean simplicity here. It means *material honesty*. The Vethoren work stone with stone, grinding harder against softer, patiently abrading channels and basins. They knap volcanic glass for cutting and scraping, not for weapons—the mountain provides; why hunt abroad? Their metal, what little exists, comes from cold-working surface copper found in the raw young rock, hammered into sheets and riveted, never smelted. Fire they manage carefully, in isolated hearths vented to chimneys that spider upward, consuming precious fuel: dried dung, rendered fat, the combustible bones of certain beasts. Light comes from lichens cultivated in the brighter shafts, phosphorescent colonies that paint corridors in pale green and sickly blue, supplemented by seal-fat lamps in the deeper reaches.

What they have built instead of *things* is *relationship*—with the mountain, with the fauna, with each other.

---

The beasts. The abundant fauna does not roam the slopes; it *inhabits*.

You will not see them immediately. They are acoustic phenomena first, or thermal ones. A warm exhalation where no vent should be. A low subsonic hum that resonates in your sternum, that the Vethoren have learned to produce with their own throats, a language of infrasound that carries through stone. The *veln* are the largest, mammalian in vague outline but with the segmented limb-structure of something crustacean, their bodies adapted to press through passages that would seem impossibly narrow. They live in the mountain's own labyrinth, feeding on lichens and the mineral-rich seepage from the Vethoren's waste channels, and they give: milk, hide, the fibrous sinew that binds the city's architecture, eventually themselves.

The relationship is not domestication. The Vethoren speak of it as *negotiation*, a slow interspecies contract written in reciprocal habit. The veln open certain passages; the Vethoren seal others. The veln provide; the Vethoren cultivate the lichen beds toward veln-preferred niches. Young veln are not *taken* but *offered*, emerging from the deep on certain lunar alignments, presenting themselves at known exchange cavities. The Vethoren do not question this. They accept.

Smaller fauna fills every exploitable niche: the eyeless *keth* that hunt insects in the damp margins, the *orrith*—lichen-eating beetles whose carapaces provide the amber translucent material for the city's rare windows, catching what light filters down from distant mouths or up from phosphorescent shafts. Fungal gardens in the warmer depths host their own biome, mites and nematodes and stranger things, protein cycles that complete without light.

---

The soundscape resolves only after days of listening.

Mostly quiet, yes, but *attended* quiet. The city has learned to dampen its impacts—the soft placement of stone-shod feet, the murmur of infraspeech, the veln's continuous subsonic song that you feel more than hear until you learn to listen with your body. Sudden sounds mean *emergency*: a collapse, a flooding shaft, a veln dispute, a death. The Vethoren move through their architecture with the deliberation of people who know every sound propagates, who have no doors to close against the acoustic intimacy of their existence.

What breaks the quiet, when it breaks, is weather.

The city generates its own, the scale parameter made atmospheric. Cold air sinks from the summit mouths, warm rises from the volcanic depths, and the collision generates currents, mists, occasional violent precipitation in the larger galleries. The Vethoren have mapped these patterns across generations, know which passages will be impassable in which conditions, which platforms ice over, which damp surfaces suddenly flow with condensate. They have a hundred words for the quality of cold air moving against warm stone, the particular silence before a pressure change, the smell of weather arriving through kilometers of mountain.

---

The normalcy of it, the low weirdness, is perhaps what finally disorients.

There is nothing inexplicable here, nothing that violates physical law, only the accumulated strangeness of a culture fully adapted to conditions most humans would find inimical. The Vethoren are not alien. They are *thoroughly*, *exquisitely* human, in a place that has demanded every ounce of that adaptability. They tell stories, sing, feud, love, die. They bury their dead in the deepest dry shafts, where the cold and the mineral chemistry preserve them, a necropolis of uncounted generations stacked in the stone like geological strata. They mark time not by sun but by the thermal pulse of the mountain's deep vents, by the veln's reproductive cycles, by the slow accretion of their own carvings on passage walls—counting systems, calendars, legal judgements, poetry, all scratched into basalt with harder stone, accumulating toward a literature that cannot be carried but only visited, walked through.

---

Leaving, if you leave, you re-emerge into a world that seems *thin*. Too spacious, too quiet in the wrong way, too lacking in pressure and presence and the continuous mutual regard of dense habitation. The mountain recedes behind you, apparently solid, apparently blank.

But you know now. You know the warmth of veln breath in a dark corridor, the green shimmer of living light, the weight of stone that is also home, the patient negotiation of survival in a place that offers everything and nothing, that takes as it gives, that has become—not despite its hostility but because of it—vast enough to hold a world.
