The Chamber is not large. You could cross it in forty steps if you could find a path, which you cannot. The walls curve inward overhead to form a dome sixty meters across at its widest point, and the ceiling vaults to perhaps thirty meters—though the upper reaches disappear into ammonia fog that pools against the apex, condensing, weeping, dripping back down in a rain that never stops, that has never stopped, that will never stop. The Chamber is in equilibrium. The Chamber *is* equilibrium.

What walls. What *making*.

Every surface is constructed. There is no stone beneath, no native geology waiting to be revealed by accident or entropy. The floor is poured ferrocrete in hexagonal tiles, each two meters across, their edges not quite matching after centuries of thermal stress, the gaps filled with a black rubberoid compound that has softened to the consistency of cold tallow. The walls rise from this floor in courses of ceramic armor—layer upon layer of fired clay and glass fiber and shredded metal, the recycled detritus of some civilization that learned to build with its own garbage until the garbage became indistinguishable from the architecture. The ceramic weeps. It is cold here, barely above the freezing point of the ammonia that condenses on every surface, and the cold keeps the walls perpetually wet. Water—more water—seeps from microfractures in the ceramic, from pinhole breaches in the dome's inner membrane, from the very process of the Chamber's attempt to maintain itself against entropy it cannot admit exists. The water carries dissolved salts that bloom into white and yellow efflorescence, then dissolve again, then bloom elsewhere. The walls are never the same wall twice. They are also never different. Equilibrium.

And the vegetation? Sparse, the parameters said, and sparse it is: only the hardiest extremophiles, only the things that can grow on nothing, on cold wet ceramic, on efflorescence, on the rubberoid gaps between floor tiles. There are lichens here in five colors,none of them green—ochre and umber and a sulfurous orange that seems to glow faintly in the dimness. They grow in patches no larger than your thumbnail, separated by meters of bare wet ceramic. They have been growing for three hundred years. They will be this size in three hundred more. The Chamber does not permit change. The lichens have found their equilibrium and they abide in it, endlessly almost-living, endlessly not-quite-dying.

What light there is comes from the machines.

The Chamber is industrial, mechanized, scaled for production—though what it produces is itself, its own continuation, its own stasis. The machines crowd the floor and cling to the walls and hang from the dome in clusters like mechanical fruit. They are engines, generators, pumps, condensers, catalytic crackers, distillation columns reduced to the scale of a room, packed so densely that one must turn sideways to pass between them, so densely that their heat-exhaust ports almost touch their neighbors' coolant intakes, so densely that the sound has nowhere to go, cannot dissipate, cannot escape, can only accumulate.

The sound.

The sound is deafening not because any one machine is loud but because there are hundreds, thousands, and their sounds interfere and reinforce and multiply in the dome's acoustics until the air itself seems to vibrate at every frequency from infrasound to the edge of hearing. There is the bass throb of reciprocating pumps, the shriek of venting steam, the whine of turbines, the rattle of chain drives, the hum of transformers, the clatter of material traveling through pneumatic tubes that lace the upper walls like the bronchi of some impossible lung. The sound never changes. The machines are in equilibrium. They wear but do not fail; their maintenance subroutines replace worn bearings with bearings identically worn, swap fatigued springs for springs pre-fatigued to the same state of stress. The sound is a roof. The sound is a floor. You cannot remember what silence was; the concept becomes theoretical, then doubtful, then forgotten.

What moves in the spaces between machines?

Fauna, overwhelming, 2.7 of 3, and here the parameters seem to strain against each other—sparse vegetation, cold, dim, wet, dense machinery, and yet life multiplies, crowds, presses against limitation and exceeds it. The fauna of the Chamber are small. They had to be. Evolution—or its equivalent here, whatever the original builders programmed or permitted—found the niches between machines, the warm pockets near exhaust vents, the condensation that pools in deformed floor tiles, the organic sludge that accumulates in filters and is flushed out and accumulates again identically.

There are mites. There are always mites. They have bred for size reduction until the largest is barely visible, a pale speck that moves in herds across the wet ceramic, grazing on the lichens, on the efflorescence, on the biofilms that grow in the rubberoid gaps. Their predators are nearly as small: pseudoscorpions, wingless flies, beetles that have lost their elytra and their coloration both, becoming pale and soft and nearly translucent in the dim constant conditions. They swarm in the warm zones. A hand placed on a running compressor for thirty seconds would emerge with a moving glove of them, not attacking—nothing here attacks, aggression is waste, waste is entropy, entropy is denied—but investigating, sampling, testing whether this new warmth might be a niche they have somehow overlooked.

And larger things, relatively: the glass rats.

No one named them. No one lives here to name anything. They are rats in shape only, hairless and pale with eyes like opals that catch machine-light and return it doubled, refracted. They grow to perhaps fifteen centimeters, nose to tail-base, and they live in the upper machinery, in the spaces between condensers and the dome's inner shell, where the ammonia fog is thickest and the dripping is constant. They drink condensation. They eat the smaller fauna, the mite-herds, the soft beetles, but they also eat something else—something the parameters of weirdness begin to suggest. They eat the sound.

Or they feed on something the sound produces. Or they are a frequency of the sound made flesh, a standing wave that achieved sufficient density to crawl, to breed, to stare with opal eyes at the rare intruder. They are strange, 2.0 of 3, not strangest possible, not so alien that the mind refuses them, but strange enough that you cannot watch them move without feeling that your visual processing is lagging slightly behind, that you are remembering their motion rather than perceiving it, that they exist in a temporal phase just adjacent to your own. They do not avoid you. They do not approach. They observe with a patience that implies they are waiting for something they know will not happen, that cannot happen, that the Chamber forbids.

The Chamber was built. Of course it was built. Built 2.9 of 3, nearly total, everything made, nothing natural—and we must ask: built by what? For what? The builders are absent. The builders were perhaps never present, perhaps the Chamber constructed itself from plans embedded in its first course of ceramic, a seed that contained its own infinite recapitulation. Or perhaps the builders became the machines, uploaded or dissolved or distributed so thoroughly into the maintenance subroutines that no individual remains, only the collective insistence on continuation. Or perhaps they are still here, reduced to the scale of the mites, or expanded to the scale of the sound, or translated into the temporal phase of the glass rats.

What the Chamber produces, if it is a factory, is equilibrium. What it manufactures is the impossibility of its own ending. The erosion parameter is 0.1 of 3—emerging, pre-formation, raw geology coming into being—but there is no geology here, only the made, and so the emergence must be something else: the Chamber itself is emerging, still being born, still in its embryonic stage despite centuries of operation, a fetus of architecture that has not yet decided what it will become when it grows. The rawness is in its refusal to complete. It is under construction forever, scaffolding that became structure that became scaffolding for something else that never arrives, the construction process itself becoming the product, the verb insisting on its precedence over the noun.

And density, 1.8 of 3, many presences per volume, near-touching, layered, high-saturation: yes, the machines and the fauna and the sound and the water and the glass rats and the efflorescence and the mites and the maintenance subroutines and the embedded plans and the possible builders and the ammonia fog, all pressed together in this pocket, this single feature contained, this intimate room at high elevation where the external atmosphere is too thin and too cold for the Chamber to admit connection to it, where it exists as a bubble, a lung, a womb, a machine that dreams it is alive and lives in the dreaming.

You cannot leave. Not because the Chamber forbids it but because the outside does not exist. The Chamber has no doors. The parameters of scale—hidden, pocket, micro-environment, embedded-within—suggest it exists within something larger, but no one has found the larger thing. Perhaps the Chamber contains itself. Perhaps it is embedded in its own wall, fractally recursive, the forty-step crossing leading at step forty-one back to the starting point through some topology the mind refuses. The glass rats know. The glass rats have always known. Their opal eyes reflect not the machine-light but something else, some light source that has no location in the Chamber's geometry, that may be the light of the Chamber's emergence, its prolonged nativity, the glow of perpetual pre-formation.

The water drips. The machines hum and shriek and throb. The fauna swarms and grazes and is grazed upon. The lichens maintain their thumbnail boundaries. The walls weep their salts. The fog pools and precipitates and pools again. Nothing changes. Everything is always changing, microscopically, in the replacement of bearing for bearing, the flush of sludge and its identical accumulation, the slow chemical conversation between ceramic and ammonia and water and salt. The Chamber is not stable. The Chamber is stability itself, the concept made manifest, the absolute zero of dynamic variation, the place where time has not stopped but has become irrelevant, where past and future are identical because they are both contained in the endless present of equilibrium.

This is the landscape. This is the room. This is the machine that breathes and does not breathe, that lives and does not live, that waits and does not wait because waiting implies expectation and expectation implies change and change is the one parameter the Chamber has successfully eliminated, has engineered out of its possibility-space, has replaced with the beautiful terrible fullness of now, now, now, forever now, the wet cold loud dense crowded made dim strange overwhelming never-changing Chamber, emerging still, embryonic always, complete in its incompletion, finished in its endless construction, home to the glass rats and their opal patience, their temporal phase, their knowledge that you have always been here, that you will always be here, that you are here now and that now is the only tense the Chamber permits, the only tense that exists, the wet eternal present of the dome and its machines and its fauna and its sound that is not sound but the material of existence itself, compressed into this pocket, this lung, this womb, this eye open in the dark that is not dark enough, that will never be dark enough, that sees you seeing it and waits without waiting for what cannot come, what has already arrived, what is arriving now and now and now—

The water drips.

The machines continue.

The Chamber abides.
