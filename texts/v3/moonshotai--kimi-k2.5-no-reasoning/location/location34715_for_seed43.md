The place is called the Belly. Nobody remembers who first gave it that name, or whether they meant it literally enough.

You enter through a throat of dressed stone—blocks the size of beetles, each one cut by hand from something harder than itself, fitted so the seams are only visible when the light slants certain ways. The air hits you first: warm, yes, but *swollen* warm, the heat of digestion, of fermentation, of something metabolic happening in the dark. It carries moisture too, not rain-wet but *exhale*-wet, the damp of a mouth held open too long, condensation beading on stone that has never seen sky and never needed to.

This is the below-place. Not a cave—caves have the honesty of hollowed rock. The Belly was *built down*, constructed by hands or tongues or intentions that worked from the surface inward, core-ward, making a vessel rather than finding one. The ceiling is low enough that a tall person learns to duck through certain arches, but the space never feels cramped. Dense, yes. Saturated. Every surface bears its neighbors, shares its boundaries, participates in continuity.

The walls are living calendars of accretion. Generations of builders—if they were builders, if they were generations—added strata to strata. Mud packed over stone packed over fired clay packed over something organic that has desiccated to the consistency of stale bread. The oldest layers, innermost and highest, have the established patience of things that have long since found their form. They do not erode so much as *settle further into themselves*, growing smoother, more certain, more *there*. Lower down, newer work shows the rougher fingerprints of its making, though "newer" might still mean centuries of the Belly's slow metabolism.

Vegetation sparse but *present*: not plants exactly, or not plants primarily. Things grow in the interstices, the seams between construction phases, rooting in mortar that was mixed with organic matter, drawing from the damp warm darkness. Fungal shelves like half-open books. Gelatinous colonies that pulse faintly, that might be colonies or might be single organisms sprawled across meters of wall. Vine-analogues that are more like slow liquid, creeping down from ceiling crevices, dripping, re-collecting, continuing their descent elsewhere. They do not photosynthesize—there is no sun here, and the normal light comes from sources the Belly generates for itself, bioluminescent patches that were bred or coaxed or *negotiated* into existence, soft amber-green glows that make shadows appear only when you move quickly enough to outpace their adjustment.

The fauna is abundant, and this is where the Belly becomes most itself, most *active*, most loud.

Insects, first and always, the base of everything. Not swarming—not that density of chaotic movement—but *present*, attending every surface. Beetles whose carapaces match the stone they traverse, visible only when they move. Moth-things that never approach the light sources, that navigate by the warmth gradients in the air, their wings soft as eyelids closing. Worms in the dampest mortar, visible only when a wall weeps, and the walls weep often, not from grief but from process, from the Belly's ongoing digestion of itself.

Then the hunters. Things with too many legs or the wrong number of joints, things that have grown into the architecture, that know the hollow spaces in walls as their territory. You hear them more than see them—a skittering overhead, a dry whisper in a seam too narrow for your cupped hand. They are not dangerous to you, not usually, but they are *competitive*, territorial, engaged in the constant negotiation of predator and prey that makes the Belly's darkness into something like an economy, like a marketplace of consumption and evasion.

Louder still: the builders, if they remain. Or their descendants. Or what they became.

You might call them people. They have hands, or hand-equivalents, and they work, or work-equivalents, but their technology never advanced beyond the simplest fabrications. Stone on stone. Clay shaped and fired in recesses that serve as kilns. Fibers twisted from the fungal shelves, from the gelatinous colonies, from their own sheddings. They move through the Belly's narrow passages and chambered spaces—cathedral-scale only in the occasional sudden openness, more often room-scale, grove-scale, the scale of intimacy and encounter—and they *make*, constantly make, adding to the Belly's density, packing more presence into each volume.

Their sound is the Belly's sound. Rhythmic impacts of stone on stone, not random strikes but patterned, *speaking*, a percussion language that carries through the construction and becomes part of the architecture itself. Chanting that is more breath than word, the exhalation-damp given rhythm, given *intention*. The wet sounds of feeding, of the gelatinous colonies being harvested, of the fungal shelves being read and then consumed. The crying of young, or what serves as young, a sound that continues until it doesn't, until it merges into the adult percussion or falls silent in a way that might mean acceptance or might mean something else.

They are unusual, the builders. Not quite what you expect from eyes evolved elsewhere. Their weirdness is moderate, present, not overwhelming—you can look at them, can almost categorize them, almost fit them into a frame of reference. But something in their proportion is wrong, or something in their movement, or something in the way they seem to *extend* into the walls, to participate in the architecture as much as inhabit it. They do not wear clothes so much as *grow* interfaces with their environment, secretions that harden, accretions that they carry with them, adding to the Belly even as they move through it.

The rules here are simple once you feel them:

Everything is made, and everything makes. The distinction between constructing and growing, between building and being, has dissolved long ago. To live in the Belly is to add to it. To cease adding is to become material for others' addition. Death here is not ending but *incorporation*, the body joining the walls, the nutrients joining the colonies, the pattern joining the percussion language that echoes through the warm damp dark.

The Belly processes. That is its nature, its function, its endless dynamic activity. It takes in—moisture from the occasional seepage from above, organic matter from the surface world that filters down through the throat, the constant production of its inhabitants—and it transforms, ferments, generates the warmth and the light and the conditions for further making.

It is small, the Belly. You could walk its full extent in a day, if you knew the paths, if the paths remained stable (they don't, quite, they shift with the accretion). But its density makes it *larger than itself*, each chamber holding more than should fit, each wall bearing more history than its thickness should allow. It is a pocket universe of making, a proof that total construction need not mean sterility, that the entirely built can become as complex, as active, as *alive* as anything that grew in open air.

You leave through the throat, if you leave, and the light outside—if there is an outside, if the throat leads to sky rather than to another belly—tastes wrong for days afterward. Too dry. Too *unworked*. You return, or you dream of returning, of the warm damp percussion, of the amber-green adjustment of shadows, of the way the walls weep and the builders add and the Belly continues its patient, established, endless digestion of itself into more of itself.

Or you don't leave. You begin, instead, to add your own strata. Your hands find stone harder than itself. Your breath joins the exhalation. Your weirdness becomes moderate, usual, almost unnoticeable. You become the sound, the process, the active dynamic of this small dense constructed warm damp world that has no substrate but itself, that is entirely made and entirely making, that holds in its chambered darkness a complete ecology of intention and accretion, waiting, working, welcoming.
