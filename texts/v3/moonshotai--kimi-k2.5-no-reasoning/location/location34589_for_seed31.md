The water has nowhere to go but up.

This is the first thing you understand about the Shelf: eleven thousand feet above any conventional sea, and still the wetness finds its way out of the rock, still it beads on every surface, still it gathers in the hollows of this young plateau as if the stone itself were wounded and weeping. The plateau emerged—this is the local word, *emerged*, as if the earth chose to show itself—only fourteen generations ago, thrust up by some subterranean violence so recent that the elder cliffs still bear the tear-marks of their own birthing, striations like frozen lightning where mineral seams were wrenched apart and flash-cooled in the sudden alpine air.

The stone is black. Not basalt—something greener, more tender, barely finished cooling in geological time. When the cloud-dim light catches it right, you can see the glassy interiors of half-formed crystals, still hungry to finish their growth, trapped in the interrupted moment of their making. The rock weeps. Every crevice runs with water that has never seen a river's mouth, never joined a sea. It condenses on the black faces, gathers in the sudden valleys, finds the lowest point of every wound in the terrain and makes of it a promise it cannot keep: a pool, a marshlet, a mirror that will evaporate by afternoon and reform by dusk, eternal recurrence without destination.

---

The town—*Vorru*, they call it, which means roughly *where we stopped because the crying stone would let us plant*—sits at the plateau's eastern tilting edge, where the black stone finally surrenders to something like soil. Not true soil, not the deep accumulation of millenniums. This is young ground. Volcanic grit, pulverized emergence-breccia, the first thin suggestions of organic matter from the sparse vegetation that has managed to colonize these surfaces. The Vorruans farm it anyway, or farm at it, scratching shallow basins in the weeping stone and filling them with gathered water, floating seed-strata on the surface tension, growing something between algae and grain that exists nowhere else—pale, translucent, needing no soil-depth because it draws its sustenance through water-film directly from the stone's own mineral weeping.

There are perhaps six hundred people in Vorru. The number has remained stable for as long as anyone counts. No children are born; no one dies, exactly, though people sometimes *complete*, walking out into the weeping wilderness and not returning, their empty dwellings left standing because materials are too precious to waste on demolition, too scarce to justify replacement. The population holds steady through some mechanism no one discusses, newcomers arriving from somewhere lower, somewhere else, appearing at the marsh-edges with water still running from their clothes and no memory of the ascent.

The buildings are low, broad, roofed in something between ceramic and glass that the Vorruans know how to make from certain condensations on specific cliff-faces—primitive tech, yes, but specific to this place, this chemistry, this impossible water-cycle. They stack flat stones in dry-wall constructions, seal the gaps with the ceramic-glass runoff, create enclosures that breathe without quite being permeable, that hold the wet warmth without drowning in it. Everything is layered: sleeping platforms above cooking spaces above gathering pits, each level slightly offset so the weeping walls drain through channels that the first builders carved and that no one has needed to repair, the channels self-maintaining through some property of the water itself, some mineral accretion that heals the stone it passes through.

---

The vegetation is lush, they say, but the word means something different here. No forests. No canopy. The plateau is too young for trees, too high for quick growth, too wet in the wrong ways—roots drown in the saturated stone, in the marsh-edges where water cannot drain away because there is no lower place for it to go. Instead: mats. Colonies. Things that grow flat against surfaces, that spread in sheets and ribbons, that have learned to photosynthesize through water-film and condensate-cloud and the strange diffused light that never quite achieves directness, trapped as it is between the perpetual cloud-sea below and the higher overcast that seems anchored to these altitudes.

There is a moss that grows in spirals, following the path of water runoff, each spiral a record of some stone's particular topography. There is a reed that has no hollow stem but grows solid, heavy, sinking roots into the black stone itself through cracks it seems to create by some pressure of its own growth. There are lichens that fruit in globular masses, pale blue-green, that the Vorruans harvest before they reach some critical ripeness and begin—we have seen this, the slow ones, the watchers—*releasing* something, some substance or organism or process that moves too fast or too slow to perceive, that leaves behind only an emptied husk and a patch of stone somehow more wet, more weeping, than before.

The vegetation clusters. The spaces between clusters are absolute. You can walk for hours across bare black stone, nothing but the sound of your own feet and the perpetual micro-sound of condensation, of weeping, of water finding its inevitable level at eleven thousand feet where no level should exist. Then: a marsh-edge, sudden, a half-acre of saturated grit and reed-mass and the spiraling moss, all of it humming with a biological frequency that makes your teeth ache, though no one can say if this is sound or some other sense misinterpreted. Then: nothing again. The sparse density of this world, the absolute balance with emptiness.

---

No animals. The Vorruans do not discuss this. The absence is not noted, not mourned, not questioned. There are no birds against the cloud-sea, no insects in the marsh-edges, no worms in the thin soil. The vegetation propagates by mechanisms no one has observed—spore-drift on the perpetual wet wind, perhaps, or some slower transfer through the stone's own water-chemistry. The Vorruans themselves eat the grain-algae, drink the weeping water, and do not ask what they are in a world without other animal presence. Their bodies have adapted, or been adapted, to this protein-geometry, this mineral nutrition. They are peaceful, slow-moving, given to long contemplation of water-patterns on stone.

The silence is not quite silence. It is the absence of biological sound, filled instead with the geological and meteorological: the micro-drip of condensation, the occasional crack of black stone adjusting to some deep thermal rhythm, the wind moving through reed-clusters with a frequency below hearing that you feel in your sternum, your teeth, the water in your own eyeballs. Sometimes, in the absolute stillness between wind-moments, you can hear the plateau itself: a subsonic note, continuous, the sound of eleven thousand feet of young stone still settling into its final shape, still weeping the water of its own emergence, still becoming whatever it will become when geological time finishes with the moment of its birthing.

---

The rules here are simple because they are unquestioned. The water goes up and comes down and never leaves. The population holds steady. The buildings stand and weep and are not repaired because repair is unnecessary. The vegetation grows in its clustered patterns and is harvested and grows again. Nothing changes because the system has found its equilibrium before it ever began, a frozen moment of young-world creation held in perpetual stasis, a landscape that emerged complete and has never learned to age.

The Vorruans do not ask what is below the cloud-sea. They do not ask what is above the overcast, if anything is. They do not ask where the newcomers come from or where the completed ones go. They tend the marsh-edges, gather the condensation, harvest the blue-green globes before their ripening release, and watch the black stone weep its water into a world that has nowhere lower for it to flow.

This is the Shelf. This is Vorru. This is what it means to live in the wet wound of a mountain that has not finished being born.
