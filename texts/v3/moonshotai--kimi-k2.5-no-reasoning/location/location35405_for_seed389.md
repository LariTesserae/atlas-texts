The place does not have a name that anyone speaks aloud. Names require breath held steady, and breath is too precious here.

---

You arrive through a series of switchbacks carved into something that is not yet fully stone. The road—a term used loosely, for it is more a *tendency* in the raw material, a suggestion that the dense, half-formed mass beneath your feet might prefer to be flat—climbs through what geologists, if any visited, would struggle to categorize. The rock is warm. Not geothermally warm, though that would be expected at this elevation where the air thins to a pale ghost of itself. This warmth is *manufactured*. Or perhaps *accumulated*. Heat pooled and pressed and tended until it became atmosphere, until it became character, until it became the fundamental quality that everything here must negotiate with.

The sky at this extreme elevation should be brutal, clarity without mercy. Instead it is hazy, thickened by effluent that does not rise high enough to escape, trapped by the bowl of greater peaks that ring this place like teeth in a half-closed mouth. The light filters through at 1.5 normal—sufficient to see, insufficient to trust what you see. Shadows fall at wrong angles. Structures cast double shadows, sometimes triple, as if light here moves through phases that physics elsewhere has not discovered.

---

The city—*city* is the word, though it fits as poorly as *road*—occupies a plateau that is still *becoming*. This is the erosion at 0.5 emerging, the pre-formation. The ground has not decided what it is. In places it is porous, vesicular, cooling from some state more liquid than solid. You can find bubbles frozen mid-birth, cavities where gases escaped and left thumbprint-sized voids. In other places it has begun to crystallize, to organize itself into columns that grow upward at the speed of centuries, visible only to those who have the patience to watch for decades. The inhabitants have this patience. Their technologies require it.

They built here, or built *into* here, or perhaps the building and the raw geology are indistinguishable now. The structures rise from the half-stone rather than upon it. Foundations sink into material that might, in a thousand years, flow around them like very slow water. The builders accepted this. Planned for it. There are buildings here tilted at fifteen degrees that have been tilting for four centuries, their inhabitants adjusting their floors with each generation, adding steps where gravity has made new requirements, abandoning rooms that have become walls.

The density is 2.4—near-touching, layered, saturated. But this density is not of people. The inhabitants are few, rare as the fauna score suggests (1.4). What crowds this space is *purpose*. Each structure, each half-formed corridor, each chamber excavated from cooling rock, is saturated with function. The city is a machine that has forgotten it is also a habitat. Or perhaps it never knew.

---

The industrial technology (2.5) operates on principles that seem, to outside observation, to require more input than output. Enormous engines, their housings grown from the same raw geology as the ground, consume heat and pressure and the potential energy of not-yet-stone. They produce: something. The production is not visible in goods. There are no warehouses, no transport systems for finished products. The city's industry produces *conditions*. Maintains the haze that traps heat at this impossible elevation. Keeps the half-formed ground in its useful intermediate state. Generates the strange acoustic properties that make this place mostly quiet (0.9) despite the constant operation.

For the engines are quiet. They move without vibration, or with vibrations that cancel themselves, that fall into frequencies the ear declines to hear. What sound exists is intermittent: the settling of cooling material, cracking like very slow lightning; the occasional release of pressure from some depth, a sigh that lasts minutes; and the water.

---

Water at 1.2—damp, intermittent, occasional. It exists here in violation. This elevation, this heat, this raw geology should not hold moisture. Yet it does, in ways that suggest intention rather than accident. Condensation forms on surfaces that have been cooled by processes the engines enforce. It gathers in the vesicular voids, in the half-formed bubbles, in channels carved by hands or by the patient work of liquid finding any downward path. There are rivers here, if the term can stretch to accommodate flows that move a meter per day, that evaporate before reaching any destination, that exist in sections like remembered water, like the idea of water made barely physical.

The inhabitants collect it. This is their primary activity, the labor around which all others organize. Condensation harvesters—delicate frameworks of material that is not metal, not ceramic, not anything with a familiar name—stand on every surface that the engines cool. They gather moisture that has been manufactured as much as captured, water produced as byproduct of the greater industrial purpose. The harvesters fill reservoirs that are themselves half-organic, membranes that swell visibly over weeks, that pulse with the slow rhythm of accumulation.

You may drink this water. It tastes of the stone it condenses upon, of the processes that made it possible. It does not taste of life. Nothing here tastes of life. The vegetation score is 0.1—no plant life, not even the memory of it. There is no pre-life here, no post-life, no frozen preservation of what once grew. This is a mineral world in the most absolute sense, a place where biology discovered it was not required and departed for more welcoming conditions. Or was prevented. The weirdness of 2.4 does not permit certainty on this point.

---

Yet there is ecology. The strangeness insists upon it. Not biology as you would recognize it, but a system of relations, of exchanges, of mutual dependence between the engines and the raw geology and the condensation and the inhabitants. Fungal-or-simpler does not apply, for there is nothing fungal, nothing that metabolizes, nothing that grows in any organic sense. Instead there are patterns. Energy-patterns, perhaps, or information-patterns, or something for which no category exists.

They appear in the heat-shimmer. They organize the haze into temporary geometries, structures of air and temperature that persist for hours before dissolving. The inhabitants interact with them. This is the true purpose of the industry, the true production: to cultivate these patterns, to provide conditions in which they might *become*, might grow more stable, more complex, more something that the inhabitants seem to need with an urgency that has no obvious explanation.

The patterns do not respond to observation. They respond to presence, to heat-signature, to the particular electromagnetic profile of a body maintaining itself at 37 degrees in an environment that would prefer 280. They gather, sometimes, around the living. They have never harmed anyone. They have never, in any record the inhabitants maintain, done anything at all except exist, and become, and occasionally achieve a density of organization that the engines then—somehow—incorporate, harvest, make use of in processes too slow to witness.

---

The inhabitants. They must be described, though description fails.

They are few, as noted. They are patient. They do not build new structures but *continue* existing ones, adding to the half-formed, the emerging, the not-yet-decided. Their technology is industrial but their relationship to it is almost agricultural. They tend the engines. They tend the condensation. They tend the raw geology, the cooling stone, the vesicular voids, the channels where water moves at geological speed. They are shepherds of slowness in a place where all change is gradual (dynamic 0.6), where cycles complete in centuries, where the weakest of gradients must be respected because nothing here responds to force.

They have no names that they share with visitors. They have no visitors. You are not here. This description is a violation, an imagining-in, a condensation of its own forming on the cool surface of a mind that has touched this place only in the geometry of its absence.

---

What emerges, finally, is a portrait of maintenance. The city—machine, habitat, geological event, industrial accident, living argument against necessity of biology—maintains itself at the edge of becoming. It produces conditions for its own continuation. The heat it traps allows the engines to operate; the engines produce the cooling that allows water to exist; the water allows the patterns, or the patterns allow something in the water, or both allow the inhabitants to continue their patient tending, which allows the engines, which allows the heat, which allows—

The cycle is not closed. Nothing here is closed. The plateau is still cooling, still deciding. In ten thousand years it will be stone, real stone, the kind that exists elsewhere without question. The engines will have exhausted their fuel, which is potential, which is the energy of *not yet decided*. The patterns will have achieved whatever stability they seek, or failed to, or achieved something else entirely. The water will have escaped, evaporated, found its way to some lower place where biology waits to incorporate it.

Or: the cooling will continue, and the engines will find new fuel, new potential, new *not-yet* to consume. The plateau will extend itself, grow, colonize the surrounding peaks with its warmth and its haze and its patient, patient inhabitants. The patterns will become something that requires a new word, something between computation and manifestation and slow, slow thought.

The place does not know. The place is not the kind of thing that knows. It is the kind of thing that continues, that maintains, that exists in the narrow corridor between states, *emerging*, never quite emerged, never quite to be, but present in its becoming with a density and strangeness and quiet industrial purpose that fills the available space entirely, to near-touching, to saturation, to the point where any additional presence—even the presence of description—must press against what is already there, must find its own narrow channel downward, must move at the speed that this place permits, which is the speed of water at 1.2 in an environment that should not permit it, which is the speed of cooling stone, which is the speed of becoming itself, patient, patient, without end.
