The Bells of Keth-Vorrum

---

There is a city built inside a single exhale—a place that exists because some enormous lung once pushed air through stone and never fully collapsed. You arrive at altitude, but not the altitude of mountains. This is *internal* height: a vaulted chamber, cathedral-wide but somehow intimate, pressing close with its ceiling lost in soft bioluminescence and its floor fallen away in stages to a depth no one has plumbed.

The erosion here has finished its work. What remains are shapes that have become their own fossils while still standing. Balconies of stone have eroded until they resemble half-melted candles, floor upon floor of them, each one a previous street level now reduced to lip and ledge. The city didn't fall—it *settled*, compressing like layered pastry, like a book whose pages have glued together into something you can only read by glancing at its edge. This is urban construction without tools, without technology, without even the concept of making. The "built" quality emerges from organic process accreted across time scales that don't register change. Coral architects. Fungal city planners. Bacterial engineers whose blueprints only made sense across ten million years.

The temperature holds at just above freezing, that precise cold where water refuses to commit to either state. Moisture beads on every surface, gathers in the thousand depressions of the eroded stonework, runs in thin seasonal threads down the walls to a central well-shaft that might be bottomless. The dampness doesn't rain from above—it *perspires* from the stone itself, from the breathing of the whole structure. Condensation beads, gathers, releases. You can track the slow pulse of it if you stand still long enough.

And you must stand still. The density is emptiness made almost violent. The space between presences yawns. What lives here moves rarely, and when it does, you sense it as a kind of embarrassment, an intrusion on the established quiet. The fauna—rare as it is—consists primarily of things that have forgotten they are animals. Gray moths with bodies like dried petals cling unmoving to walls for years at a stretch, metabolizing so slowly their life spans might outrun human history. Something like a salamander, something like a slug, occupies the water pockets and can be found coiled in the permanent condensation, skin pale as the stone it rests upon, visible only when it opens eyes the color of old ice to track movement it will never pursue. The most common creature is a kind of soft-shelled crab that filters the condensation, that has grown to the size of a human hand and developed carapaces so thin they seem like wet paper, so they flatten themselves into cracks and become invisible.

But the sound.

The sound is the city's voice, and it never stops speaking.

Wind moves through the erosion-sculpted cavities with the precision of instrument design. Each hollow, each tube worn through stone by water's patient violence, each narrow gap between fallen levels where air must squeeze itself—these are pipes, these are reeds, these are the holes of flutes carved by no hand. The sound is *loud*, yes, but more than loud: it is *distributed*, coming from everywhere at once, the whole space ringing with tones that shift gradually as the air pressure changes, as the condensation's slow pulse opens or closes certain passages. Sometimes a clear note holds for hours, a drone that seems to come from the foundation of the world itself. Sometimes harmonic clusters emerge, dissonances that feel accidental but recur too precisely for accident, as if the erosion patterns encode a music that water and wind finally learned to read.

The light source is the ceiling, or what functions as ceiling—a colony of something that produces soft amber illumination, a vast sheet of pale radiance that shifts in subtle waves, brightening in certain regions when the condensation increases, dimming elsewhere, so that shadows don't fall consistently but wander, pool, regather. The light is normal enough to see clearly, strange enough that distances become uncertain, that the furthest eroded balcony might be ten meters away or thirty, that the drop to the next visible level might be survivable or fatal, the eye cannot judge.

Vegetation exists in contradiction to the emptiness. It gathers in the lush mode, but concentrated, obsessive, as if all the life-distributed-through-volume of a forest has been drawn to specific nodes and forced to express itself there. Certain water pockets—those that hold year-round, that have carved basins deep enough to maintain liquid against the cold—support growths that might be a moss, might be a fern, might be something that never bothered with categories of plant. They spread in mats thick as thighs, deep green fading to black-green, and from them rise structures like stems, like fruiting bodies, soft and pale and warm to the touch, producing faint sweet smells that the cold mostly suppresses. These oases of lushness are small, room-sized, fiercely bounded. Between them: only stone, only the ringing emptiness, only the sound.

The strange quality emerges not from any single element but from their conjunction. This is a place that should not hold together. Urban without builders. Cold yet damp. Empty yet lush in pockets. Loud yet so still. Active in its processes yet ancient beyond operation. The wind-music changes and changes—the erosion continues at scales too small to see—the condensation moves in its slow respiratory rhythm—the light pulses like something almost alive—the rare fauna occasionally shifts, opens eyes, closes them again. But all of it feels *finished*. Not dead, not dying, but having arrived at a state so stable it has become weird by excess of equilibrium, by the uncanny quality of a system that found its answer and stopped asking questions.

Travelers who find the Bells—who descend through the shaft from some entrance in a mountain somewhere, or who emerge from water passages below, or who simply wake here without memory of approach—sometimes stay. There is nothing to eat but the pale fruiting bodies, which sustain without nourishing, which fill the stomach with something the body cannot quite refuse or assimilate. There is nothing to do but listen, but move between the lush nodes, but find ledges where the sound organizes itself into something almost pattern, almost melody. They erode into the city, these travelers. They become part of the density, part of the emptiness, found occasionally curled in dry niches, breathing slowed to the condensation's rhythm, eyes open to the wandering light, listening, still listening, having forgotten what they listened for but unable to stop.

The place is small. You could cross it, perhaps, in a day of careful climbing, though the levels' erosion makes such crossing uncertain, though distances deceive, though the sound disorients, though the cold slowly claims the unadapted. But it feels infinite in its compression, a world entire held in the space of a single held breath, a single note sustained so long it has become environment, become home, become the only music and the only silence, both at once, ringing on.
