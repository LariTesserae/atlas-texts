The city is called Keth-Vorath, though whether "city" is the right word depends on whether you consider a thing that has no outside to still be urban. There is no countryside. There is no wilderness pressing against walls, no horizon of green beyond the final foundry. Keth-Vorath is total, complete, finished, and it has been finished for long enough that the inhabitants have forgotten the vocabulary of the natural. Rain falls, but it falls from condensers in the ceiling of the world, which is to say from pipes in the roof of other buildings, which drain other condensers, in a recursion that lacks both a top and a bottom in any direction the eye can track. The water is damp, intermittent, irregular—sometimes a fine mist settles for weeks and nothing dries, and sometimes the pipes run dry for months and people learn to collect breath on cold glass, to lick the walls where condensation gathers in the cooler hollows between structures.

It exists at elevation, this place, though "elevation" means something different here. The streets rise and fall, stacked, terraced, helical, pierced through by vertical shafts where lifts clank in endless slow motion. But there is no ground they rise from. The foundations of the lowest buildings descend into fog, into murk, into levels where the gas-lamps (for they are gas-lamps, industrial, hissing, the light bright and yellow-white and hard-shadowed) gutter and fail, and below even that, into spaces where the maintenance crews do not go, where the strange fauna thickens and changes. Young the city is, in its geological sense—sharp edges, fresh construction, seams still visible between poured sections of its material, a substance something like concrete, something like ceramic, something like the bone of something enormous that was never alive in the ordinary fashion. The erosion is minimal. The corners are sharp. But the *weirdness*—the reality breakdown that scores through Keth-Vorath like veins of different ore through rock—that is old, or at least it is not young in the same way.

The fauna is abundant. This is perhaps the most striking thing, arriving from elsewhere (though there is no elsewhere, strictly speaking, but let us maintain the fiction of perspective). The city teems. Creatures move in the walls, in the spaces between the walls, in the conduits and channels and the endless ductwork. Some of them are recognizably organic—pale things with too many legs, flat things that slide along the condensation-filmed surfaces, soft-bodied fliers that navigate by the bright lamps and leave trails of phosphorescent slime. Others are harder to categorize. There are things that appear to be mechanisms until you see them breed, things that appear to be animals until you see them rust, things that occupy the visual field as smears or afterimages, that leave physical traces—scratches, excretions, nests built from stripped wire and compacted dust—without ever quite resolving into presence. The density of the city supports them. The density is extreme. Keth-Vorath is packed with itself, layer on layer, building grown into building, bridge and tunnel and passage and chamber, spaces that were halls become crawlspaces as new construction presses in from above and below and the sides, the city folding and refolding like a material under pressure. You can walk for hours and the architecture does not repeat, or it repeats but wrong, with details altered, proportions stretched, the same door in three different sizes in three adjacent walls, none leading to the same interior.

The sound is mostly quiet. This is the terrible thing. The industrial tech—the engines, the factories, the mechanized production that must exist somewhere to maintain this totality, to manufacture the replacement parts, the new growth, the endless material of the city—operates at frequencies below hearing, or above, or in some register that registers not as auditory phenomenon but as pressure, as wrongness, as the sense that your inner ear is detecting motion that your eyes cannot confirm. When you stand near the actual machines, the foundries and assembly halls (which are small, distributed, tucked into the interstices of the dense structure), you feel them more than hear them: a vibration in the sternum, a blurring of vision, a sense that your teeth are talking to each other in a language you never learned. Between these nodes, the quiet is profound. The fauna makes little sound. The condensation drips silently. The gas-lamps hiss, but it is a sound that occupies the auditory field without registering as event, like the sound of your own blood. You can hear someone breathing three rooms away through a closed door. You can hear, or think you hear, the subsonic operations of the city's maintenance systems, its slow digestion of itself, the gradual recycling of material from disused sectors into new construction.

The light is bright. The gas-lamps are numerous, aggressive, illuminating everything without warmth. Shadows are sharp, defined, present. But the light behaves strangely at the edges of perception, at the corners of corridors, at the limits of the open spaces (which are town-sized, regional, extending past the immediate in every direction, though "open" in Keth-Vorath always means relative, means a chamber larger than others, means a shaft rising ten stories before the next bridge crosses it, means a foundry floor where the ceiling is distant enough to suggest sky without ever delivering it). In these edges, the light bends. It pools where no source exists. It reflects from surfaces that should absorb it. There are places where your shadow moves independently, where it stands when you walk on, where it turns to face you with something like curiosity, or hunger, or the absence of either.

The weirdness—the reality breakdown—this is the truth of Keth-Vorath, more than any other parameter. The city is not quite consistent. Walk far enough in any direction and the rules change. Gravity shifts, subtly, a degree here, a degree there, so that after a long journey you find yourself leaning into a slope that your inner ear insists is level. Time moves differently in different districts. You can age years in what seems like hours, or emerge from a passage to find that your companion, who walked beside you, has become elderly, or has died and mummified, or has never existed and no one remembers them, though you carry their effects, their name written in a hand you recognize as your own. The material of the city—the ceramic-bone concrete—is permeable to certain states of mind. Intense desire, prolonged meditation, overwhelming fear: these can reshape it, open passages where none existed, seal others forever. The city is responsive in a way that suggests it is not quite inert, not quite material in the ordinary sense.

And it is slow, all of it, slow in its changes. The dynamic is glacial, patient, operating across durations that exceed individual lifetimes. A bridge may grow—literally grow, extrude material from its own substance—over decades, reaching finally to connect with a platform that has likewise been extending to meet it. Foundries come online over years, their subsonic hum gradually penetrating new districts, their emissions (what do they emit? not smoke, exactly; more like certainty, more like the conviction that production is occurring) slowly saturating the atmosphere. The condensation cycle, the intermittent moisture, operates on a scale of seasons that the inhabitants have learned to track without ever quite understanding. Wet decades. Dry decades. The city remembers them equally, accommodates both, the damp and the drought each producing their own architectures of adaptation, their own faunal shifts, their own patterns of behavior in the dense, packed, total population that occupies every volume, that lives in the walls and between them, that has never known outside and has developed, accordingly, a relationship to interiority that includes no longing for exterior, no sense of constraint, only the endless negotiation of proximity, of near-touching, of the layered and the saturated and the full.

What do they make here, in Keth-Vorath? It is difficult to say. The industrial tech is present, visible, operational, but its products are obscure, distributed, perhaps consumed in the production itself. There are no markets in the ordinary sense, no visible commerce. Material moves, is processed, emerges transformed, moves again. The inhabitants—those who are inhabitants, who are not fauna, though the distinction is not always clear—participate in this circulation without necessarily understanding their role in it. They maintain. They operate. They move through the bright-lit dense spaces of their total environment, their cool, elevated, young-sharp damp world, and they are mostly quiet, and they are patient, and they have learned not to ask what the city is for, or what it is becoming, or whether the reality breakdown that scores through its substance like a promise or a threat will one day complete itself, will one day render Keth-Vorath into something that can no longer sustain even the strange life that has adapted to its conditions.

Perhaps that is its purpose. Perhaps it is a machine for producing the weird, for generating reality breakdown as other cities generate trade or governance or culture. Perhaps it is young enough that this is not yet clear, that its final form is still emerging from the sharp-edged fresh-made material of its construction. Perhaps it will grow, will fill more volume, will increase its density until every point touches every other point simultaneously, until the distinction between locations becomes meaningless, until bright light and absolute quiet and the slow patient cycle of damp and less-damp become indistinguishable from each other and from the consciousness of the city itself, whatever that consciousness might be, whatever it might become, whatever it might already be becoming in the spaces below the gas-lamps, in the fog of the lower levels, in the silence between the subsonic pulses of its distributed industrial heart.

You could walk there, if you found an entrance. There are always entrances, the city is total but not closed, it admits, it swallows, it transforms. You would find the damp first, the condensation on the walls, the occasional drip from pipes above. Then the density, the spaces compressing around you, the sense of presence above and below and to all sides. Then the quiet, the profound quiet that is not peace but pressure, the quiet of something waiting, something patient, something operating on scales you cannot yet perceive. Then the light, bright, hard, revealing everything and explaining nothing. And then, gradually, the weirdness, the breakdown, the sense that your own presence in this place is altering it, is being altered by it, is becoming part of the slow dynamic, the gradual cycle, the patient process of Keth-Vorath becoming more itself, becoming more total, becoming more complete, becoming more dense with the accumulated presence of everything that has entered it and failed, or succeeded, or simply persisted, in the damp and the quiet and the bright hard light of its endless interior, its absolute construction, its total and completed and forever-emerging self.
