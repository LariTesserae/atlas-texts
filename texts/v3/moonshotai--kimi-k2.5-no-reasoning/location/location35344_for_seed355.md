The city of Vess sleeps beneath a skin of water, and has slept so long that sleep has become its nature, its architecture, its law.

You arrive—if arrival is possible here—at the edge of a basin so wide that its far rim dissolves into darkness. The darkness is not merely absence of light but a *presence*, a thick atmospheric substance that pools in the low places and clings to the wet stone like breath on glass. Temperature means something different in Vess. The air hangs at the threshold of fever, humid enough that your own body becomes a source of moisture, sweat indistinguishable from the ambient condensation that beads on every surface and runs in slow rivulets toward the drowned streets below.

The water parameter reads 2.4, and this manifests as saturation without depth—a city that has forgotten the distinction between liquid and air. The streets hold no standing lakes, no canals in the Venetian sense. Instead, water *occupies* everything at a molecular level. Stone weeps continuously. Mortar between bricks has softened to a fungal consistency, a perpetual dissolution that somehow never completes itself. The erosion parameter of 2.1 governs this: Vess is *established* in its decay, having reached equilibrium long ago. Things do not fall apart here. They *maintain* their falling-apart, a dynamic stability more enduring than any frozen monument.

The elevation stays low, the basin pressing against some geological floor that refuses further subsidence. And the scale opens outward—town-or-valley-size, yes, but with corridors that seem to extend past reasonable geometry, perspectives that suggest the city continues into the darkness beyond where any eye could follow, becoming perhaps another city, or the same city again, or the idea of city-ness itself.

---

Sparse vegetation at 0.9 manifests as *tolerance* rather than abundance. What grows here does so without chlorophyll's green arrogance. Pale rhizomatic networks thread through the waterlogged mortar, visible sometimes as ghost-filaments behind crumbling plaster, pale as nerve tissue. In the widest plazas, where buildings have completed their collapse into something like settled rubble, there grow structures that might be fungal or might be mineral—columnar, branching, the color of old teeth. They do not photosynthesize. They *condense*, drawing substance from the saturated atmosphere, building themselves from water and darkness and the calcium leaching from ancient bones. The built environment and the organic have achieved a negotiation so old that neither remembers which was the colonizer.

Because the built parameter reads 2.3—urban, settlement-scale, layered construction—and yet the tech parameter reads 0.4, pure nature, no devices, biological-only. This is Vess's central paradox, its defining genius. The city was built without tools, or rather: the *building* and the *growing* were never separated. The original inhabitants—if there were inhabitants, if this was ever *inhabited* in the usual sense—understood construction as a biological process. The buildings *grew* according to plans that might have been genetic rather than drawn. Load-bearing walls have grain like wood, crystalline inclusions like bone. Staircases descend in helices that mirror the cochlea. Window frames are ossified, petrified, something that was once responsive to stimulus and has since fixed in position, opening always onto the same angle of darkness.

The infrastructure-density manifests as *organism*. Water moves through capillary channels in the walls, pressure-driven, no pumps. The city's lowest levels stay wettest, wicking moisture downward, and this gradient drives slow processes—chemical exchange, the migration of mineral salts that form pale efflorescence on interior surfaces like the residue of thoughts. There are what might be streets, if you read them that way: corridors between structures, some broad enough for markets that never existed, others narrow as throat-passages. But there are no vehicles, no wear patterns from wheeled transport. Whatever moved through Vess moved *softly*.

---

The reality breakdown at 2.8 does not announce itself with spectacle. It operates at the level of consequence. Cause and effect have grown *polite* here, so attenuated in their relationship that observation becomes participation. Look too long at a particular wall and you may find—when you finally turn away—that you remember having built it, though your hands hold no such skill, though the mortar has been weeping for centuries before your birth. The darkness parameter of 0.2 is not merely low light but *uncertainty of source*: sometimes a faint luminescence rises from the wet stone itself, phosphorescent bacteria or some mineral excitation, and in these dim moments you can see your own shadow cast in multiple directions simultaneously, each shadow engaged in slightly different postures than your own.

Time moves strangely in active processes that outrun perception. The dynamic parameter reads 2.0—multiple processes, weather cycles, ecosystem in motion—and yet the fauna reads 0.2, no animal life. What is active, then? The water's slow percolation through geological time. The chemical negotiation between stone and atmosphere. The growth of those pale columnar structures, so gradual that a year's change requires a century's observation. The collapse that maintains itself, the erosion that balances its own equation. And something else: the city's own metabolism, its respiration, the way the darkness itself seems to *inhale* and *exhale* over periods that might be days or decades, the wet air growing momentarily dryer, the phosphorescence intensifying then subsiding, a rhythm without heartbeat, a cycle without sun.

Sound at 0.6—mostly quiet—is broken only by the *voice* of this metabolism. Water moving through capillaries makes no sound loud enough for human hearing, but gathered in sufficient mass, running down some interior channel in the buried levels, it produces a note so low it registers in the sternum rather than the ear. The columns, growing, sometimes *crack*, a sound like distant thunder that arrives without lightning, without storm. And occasionally, in the widest plazas, the darkness itself seems to emit a pressure, a presence felt as near-sound, the auditory equivalent of the color just before black.

---

The sparse density at 0.9—much space between presences—manifests as *sanctity of interval*. Each structure in Vess maintains its territory not through aggression but through *resonance field*, some quality of its saturated stone that repels too-near approach. Walk the streets and you will find the buildings spaced as if in conversation with each other, the distances between them precisely calibrated to their respective heights, their respective volumes of darkness-swallowed interior. To force yourself between structures that have not invited proximity produces wrongness: the water in the atmosphere seems to recede from your skin, leaving a dry envelope that itches with the memory of dissolution. The shadows grow more multiple, more contradictory. Your own footsteps cease to sync with your movement.

There are no animals. The fauna parameter insists on this, and Vess obeys absolutely. No insects stitch the air. No bats, real or metaphorical, trouble the upper darkness. No rats move in the lowest wet levels where organic debris might otherwise accumulate. The life that exists here is *otherwise*—the fungal-mineral columns, the rhizomatic nerves in the walls, perhaps something slower still that corresponds to the city's own metabolic rhythm. Predation has been abstracted into pure process: the water takes from the stone, the stone from the water, the darkness from both without diminishing either.

And yet: the reality breakdown permits *almost-life*. You will see, in certain windows, shapes that suggest observation—masses of shadow with the approximate outline of creaturehood, watching without eyes, present without persistence, there when glanced sidelong, dissolved when faced directly. These are not fauna. They are *symptoms* of the city's own awareness, if awareness is the word for a consciousness distributed across square kilometers of wet stone, operating on timescales that make glacier-flow seem hasty. The city *dreams* them, perhaps. Or you dream them, and the city borrows your dreaming, adds it to its own slow processing.

---

What are the rules of Vess?

First: saturation is identity. To enter is to begin dissolution, not of body immediately but of boundary. Your skin becomes more permeable to the wet atmosphere. Your memories begin to *diffuse*, the most recent first, so that you may remember your childhood with crystalline precision while unable to recall why you turned a particular corner five minutes before. This is not damage. It is *equilibrium*, the city's insistence that all concentrations eventually distribute.

Second: construction and growth are continuous with each other. Nothing is made here, only *cultivated*. If you wished to build—though the tech parameter forbids tools—you would need to plant, to tend, to wait. The walls would accrete. The openings would form according to need and patience. The city teaches this if you stay long enough to learn: that all making is slow gardening, all invention merely the discovery of what was already growing toward form.

Third: darkness is not empty but *full*, saturated with potential that the low light prevents from resolving into particularity. In brighter conditions, Vess might become merely ruin, merely wet dead city, a case study in failed biotech civilization. The darkness preserves its multiplicity, its superposition of states. The reality breakdown requires this cover, this refusal of complete observation.

Fourth: active process without biological pace. The city's metabolism continues whether witnessed or not, indifferent to animal timescales. You may sit in a plaza for a day and perceive nothing. Return after a season and find the columns have shifted their branching, the water-courses adjusted their routes, a wall that stood now become doorway, a doorway sealed with fresh efflorescence. The ecosystem moves on geological patience, human presence merely a brief warm disturbance in the saturated air.

Fifth: the absence of fauna is absolute but *negotiable*. Nothing with nerve, with muscle, with rapid metabolism can persist. Yet the city permits *visitorhood*—your own presence, if you accept the terms of slow dissolution, of memory-diffusion, of eventual surrender to the saturation that makes no distinction between your body's water and the atmospheric wet. Stay long enough and you might become *feature*, something the city grows around, incorporates, speaks through in its own slow voice. Not animal anymore. Not vegetable or mineral exactly. Something *architectural*, patient, wet to the core, dreaming in the darkness that pools and breathes and waits for nothing because waiting implies time's urgency, and Vess has long since forgotten urgency entirely.

I have tried to leave Vess several times. Each departure arrives at arrival. The basin's rim recedes as approached, or reveals itself as merely another district, lower than expected, wetter, more darkly saturated. The exit becomes entrance. The weeping stone welcomes. The phosphorescence, when it rises, shows my shadow in its multiple postures, and I recognize in some of them the posture of someone who has been here much longer than my memory admits, someone who helped *grow* this corridor, this particular angle of dissolution, this spot where the water pools just so against the pale mineral-fungal growth that might, if I wait long enough, begin to resemble something almost like intention, almost like welcome, almost like the city's own slow word for *home*.
