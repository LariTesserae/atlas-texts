The city of Vornath stretches across a shallow bowl of lowland, no part of it rising more than a man's height above any other, yet it covers enough ground that a swift walker needs most of a day to cross from edge to edge. Everything here is made. Every surface, every texture, every growing thing and flowing thing and living thing has been shaped by hands using tools of stone and bone and fired clay. There is no ground beneath the city. There is only more city, descending in layers of uncertain depth, foundations that are themselves buildings that are themselves foundations, a recursive architecture of intention that no one now living understands.

The heat is constant and specific, a wet pressing warmth that holds at the threshold of discomfort without ever quite crossing into suffering. It is always this temperature. The distinction between day and night exists only in the quality of the brightness, for the light never dims. Three suns—if they are suns—maintain their positions in a sky that has no identifiable blue, only a pale luminous shell that casts no shadows of consistent direction. The brightness is total, yet somehow gentle, a saturation that reveals everything and interrogates nothing.

Water moves through Vornath in channels carved through the made-ground, shallow troughs of glazed ceramic that bear a faint rose color, carrying fluid that emerges from seep-holes in the lower precincts and loses itself into absorption sinks at the basin's edges. The water is warm, nearly body-temperature, and carries a taste of copper and something vegetal that no one can name. It condenses on every surface in the cooler hours—that is, in the hours when the heat feels fractionally less aggressive—and drips from the eaves of structures back into the channels, a constant quiet recycling that keeps everything damp without ever quite flooding.

The vegetation is lush but strange, growing only from the made-ground, never in it. Great ceramic vessels, some as large as rooms, sit on rooftops and in courtyards, filled with prepared substrates of fired brick fragments and organic matter that might once have been food waste or might once have been something else entirely. From these vessels rise plants that do not correspond to any natural taxonomy: fronds of a translucent green that deepen to amber at their edges, heavy with moisture that they release in a constant slow weeping; vine-like growths that drape between structures on cables of woven fiber, bearing flowers that open and close but never seem to progress further in any cycle; stands of something like bamboo but with a texture more like cooled wax, pithy and pale, that can be split and used for the simplest tools.

The ecology is abundant with creatures that have learned, or been taught, or simply emerged, to live in this total architecture. Slender lizards with six limbs instead of four, their skin the grey-green of weathered copper, bask on warm ceramic and hunt the smaller creatures that cluster near moisture. There are beetles the size of thumbs whose carapaces are the same rose as the water channels, and they move in slow deliberate processions along routes that must be ancestral, generations deep, though no one has observed their young or their deaths. Birds—perhaps birds—perch on upper ledges and make sounds that might be calls or might be something else, something more like the city breathing through them. Their feathers have a synthetic sheen, colors that seem to come from structure rather than pigment, and they do not fly far, only from perch to perch, as if the air itself requires negotiation.

But here is the thing that makes Vornath what it is, the reality breakdown that no one speaks of directly but everyone navigates differently: the city is never changing, yet it is not fixed. Things do not move, yet they are not where they were. A corridor walked in the morning will still exist in the evening, will still connect the same two plazas, but the length of it will be different, or the angle, or the quality of the air within it. Doors lead where they led, but sometimes the distance between threshold and destination compresses or expands, and travelers learn to carry water and patience in equal measure. The water channels flow always in the same direction—there is a direction, always downward toward the absorption sinks—yet measurements of their flow rate produce different results at different times, without any detectable change in channel width or fluid depth.

The people of Vornath, whoever they are, whoever they were, have built their lives around this static instability. They do not keep records of distance, only of connection: this place is near that place, this route joins these points. They build with materials that tolerate ambiguity, fired ceramics that do not rot, stone that weathers to more stone, fibers that stiffen but hold their form. Their technology is primitive in the most specific sense: they use tools that tools could make, a constraint that seems self-imposed or perhaps self-emergent from the materials available. Knives of volcanic glass set in hardened resin handles. Hammers of dense ceramic with faces that can be replaced when they fracture. Needles of bone, polished by use against ceramic sharpening surfaces. They have no metals; or rather, metal exists in the city as traces, impurities in colored glazes, but no one extracts it, no one seems to recognize it as a category separate from stone.

The structures themselves are mostly single-story, spreading horizontally rather than vertically, as if the uncertainty of dimensional relationships makes height feel unsafe. Roofs are flat, used for the growing vessels, for sleeping in the cooler portions of the cycle, for gathering. Walls are thick, thermal mass to moderate the constant heat, and pierced with openings that have no doors, only flaps of woven material that hang and stir in the occasional movement of air. The architecture is repetitive—archaeologists from elsewhere would call it regimented—but the repetition is imperfect, each structure slightly different from its neighbors, as if copied from memory or from something half-seen.

In the center of the bowl, if center is a word that applies here, there is a larger open space, a plaza where the made-ground is patterned with tiles of different thermal properties, so that some areas are hotter to stand on and some cooler, a map of temperature that might correspond to something functional or might be decorative or might be a language no one now reads. At one edge of this plaza stands a structure larger than the others, more complex in its irregularity, and from its highest roof—there are several roof levels, nested and overlapping—emits a sound.

The sound is the only sound in Vornath that is not made by living things or by water. It is a low tone, barely audible at the plaza's edge, felt more than heard, and it has been sounding for as long as anyone remembers. It does not change. It does not pulse or vary. It is simply there, a boundary condition of the city's reality, and no one has ever identified its source or suggested it should stop. In this mostly quiet place, where the lizards move without scurrying and the water flows without rushing and the birds call in voices that seem to hold their breath between notes, the tone is the landscape's anchor, the fixed point against which all other stillness is measured.

The erosion parameter says young, and this is true in a way that haunts: the city shows little weathering, little wear, as if it has not existed long enough to have aged. Yet the depth of the made-ground, the layered architecture below, the established routes of the rose beetles and the nesting preferences of the six-limbed lizards, all suggest time accumulated. There is no resolution to this. The city is young and ancient, freshly made and long inhabited, a place where the normal entropies have been interrupted or redirected or were never invited to begin.

And the weirdness, the reality breakdown, expresses in moments that no one discusses afterward. A person walking alone enters a corridor and exits into a plaza they do not recognize, only to be told by others that this plaza has always been here, always connected to this corridor. A ceramic vessel is left overnight and in the morning contains a plant that was not there, that must have been growing for weeks to reach its present size, yet no one planted it and the vessel's contents show no disturbance. The three suns—if they are suns—maintain their triangular arrangement, yet their positions relative to features on the ground shift without any detectable movement, a parallax from no motion, a geometry that refuses to resolve into single-point perspective.

The people adapt to this as people adapt to anything that does not kill them quickly. They develop practices of verification, not of belief but of function: does this route still work? does this vessel still grow? does this sound still hold? They cultivate a social density that matches the spatial sparseness—communities spread across distance, connected by reliable routes, maintaining contact through the established channels that are themselves maintained because they are established. There is no central authority, no clear hierarchy, only the accumulated weight of what has proven sustainable in a place where sustainability itself is the miracle.

What emerges, in the description of this place, is not a dystopia or a utopia but something more like a habitable theorem: given these constraints, what life is possible? The answer is present in the slow movement of the rose beetles, the patient growth from the weeping fronds, the continuous tone from the central structure, the human figures moving between buildings with the calm of those who have never known urgency because urgency requires change, requires gradient, requires the possibility of things getting worse or better, and here the equilibrium is not comfortable but it is absolute, a frozen moment that somehow sustains its own duration without ever tipping into something else.

At the absorption sinks, where the water channels finally release their contents into the porous ceramic of the lowest zones, there are sometimes found objects that do not correspond to any known making: shapes of unfamiliar material, unweathered yet ancient in their strangeness, and these are left where they lie or are carefully covered with more ceramic or are simply ignored, depending on the temperament of whoever finds them. They are not tools. They are not raw material. They are punctuation in a text that has no sentences, only continuous present, and the people of Vornath have learned, at the primitive level that their technology defines, to let such punctuation pass without reading.
