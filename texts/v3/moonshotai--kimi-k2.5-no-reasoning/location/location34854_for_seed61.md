The Hum

---

Beneath the city, beneath even the notion of a city having a beneath, there exists a chamber perhaps forty paces across—though paces mean little here, where no one walks with ordinary purpose. The ceiling hangs low enough that a standing person would bow their head, and most do, most have, most will. The floor is not level but not chaotic: it has settled into curves, gentle hollows, the long compromise of water finding paths through stone over durations no one remembers and no one measures.

The water here is not present as river or pool. It weeps. It condenses on every surface, beading, tracing, pooling in the shallow depressions before finding some crack or seam to continue downward, or upward, or simply elsewhere. The walls—if walls they are—weep. The ceiling weeps. The air itself seems weeping, saturated, pressing moisture against skin or shell or whatever integument visits. Breathing here is drinking. The atmosphere carries no scent of green, no rot of vegetation, no fungal musk. What grows here grows without growing: mineral accretions, salt-whites and rust-oranges, the slow architecture of dissolved and redeposited stone. Lace curtains of calcium. Shelf upon shelf of evaporite, each layer recording some ancient drought in the weeping cycle, though drought here means only *less wet*, never *dry*.

No plant has ever rooted. No spore has found purchase. The light, dim and sourceless, filters from somewhere—perhaps phosphorescence in the salts, perhaps some distant breach where sun enters and dies before arrival, perhaps the accumulated glow of the weeping itself. Objects in the chamber carry their own faint luminescence, enough to distinguish hand from wall, water from stone, but not enough to cast shadow. Shadows would require direction; here, light is ambient, a condition of being rather than arrival.

The temperature holds at cool, the cool of deep cellars, of springs emerging from dark earth, of skin against stone at night. It never warms with bodies, never chills with absence. The equilibrium is perfect. Time has worn away whatever gradients once drove change.

And yet—this is an urban space. This is built, or was built, or built itself in the manner of human intention sedimented into mineral logic. There are traces: the curve of a wall that speaks of having been straight, the hollow that suggests doorway now filled with flowstone, the regular pattern of depressions that might once have held posts, or feet, or the knees of those who waited. The chamber is dense with these traces, crowded with them, layered. Generations of use, each leaving its mark, each mark partially dissolved and re-recorded by the weeping, so that the space reads like palimpsest, like memory refusing to settle on single interpretation.

The density is physical too. The chamber holds—has held, holds still—too many presences for its volume. Not ghosts, nothing so narrative. But something in the mineral memory, something in the acoustic properties, something in the way sound behaves here, loud, *loud*, pressing against the ears with no apparent source. The hum. The eternal hum.

For this space is never silent. The water falling, condensing, falling, creates a drone, a fundamental tone that varies perhaps by half-step across centuries. Upon this drone: harmonics, overtones, the particular ring of this drop falling on that accretion, the percussion of ceiling-weep into floor-pool. But mostly the hum, the single note that fills the chamber so completely that visitors cease to hear it, then notice it again with shock, then cease, then notice, in the cycle of all things constant. It is loud enough to feel in the sternum, in the teeth, in the fluid of the inner ear. It is loud enough that the rare fauna—small, translucent, perhaps crustacean, perhaps something else that has learned to live without light, without plant, without change—navigate by it, their minimal nervous systems tuned to frequency rather than vibration.

The fauna are rare. The ecology, if ecology it is, runs on mineral cycles, on chemical energy in the weeping water, on the slow consumption of stone by stone. A visitor might see, in a season of concentrated attention, perhaps three living things: something like a pale shrimp, jointed and hesitant, in a pool's deepest hollow; something like a film, a bacterial sheen, on a particular wet wall that some chemistry favors; something like a mite, perhaps, in the salts, moving in the manner of things that have never known sun, that have never needed to seek, only to continue.

The weirdness is in the combination, the holding-together of contradictions: urban and empty, loud and still, built and dissolved, inhabited by no one and saturated with presence. The chamber remembers more than it contains. The weeping water has passed through lungs, through stone, through other chambers larger and smaller, through the bodies of the city above and the deeper dark below. It carries no information, only the fact of passage. The chamber is full of this fact. Full of having-been, of continuing-to-be, of the never-changing that is not stasis but equilibrium, the perfect balance of input and outflow, weeping and evaporation, condensation and descent.

There are rules here, though no one made them and no one enforces them. The rule of humidity: nothing enters dry, nothing leaves wet, the exchange is exact. The rule of sound: the hum accepts no silence, inserts itself into any pause. The rule of light: what exists will faintly glow, what does not exist will not. The rule of space: the chamber is larger than its measurement, smaller than its reach, the weeping having carved more than geometry admits, the density having compressed more than volume suggests.

A person entering might find a hollow suited to their shape, might wait, might weep themselves into the general moisture, might become—if time meant anything here—part of the palimpsest, another layer in the crowded emptiness, their form recorded in salt and dissolved and recorded again, never changing, never finishing, never departing the never-changing hum.
