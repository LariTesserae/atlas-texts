The place is called **the Chant**.

It is not large. A single street, if you could call it that—more a corridor between two walls of baked clay that rise twelve feet and bow slightly inward, as if pressed by a long-gone hand or simply leaning into each other for company across centuries. The walls are the color of old blood mixed with ochre, fired not in any kiln but by the heat that lives here, that has *always* lived here, that emerged from somewhere beneath the compacted earth floor and never dissipated. The Chant occupies a depression in a broader desert of mineral dust, a pocket valley so low that the horizon stands above it on all sides, a bowl that holds its own weather, its own time, its own rules.

No water has ever fallen here. No water has ever risen. The concept itself feels foreign, a word in a language the Chant never learned. What moves through the air instead is something else—not moisture, not exactly vapor, but a thin suspension of metallic salts that drifts in slow thermal rivers, visible only when backlight cuts through at certain angles, turning the dimness into a space filled with drifting bronze skeins. The salts settle on the clay walls and flower into eruptions of pale mineral lichen, a vegetation that thinks it's geology, that photosynthesizes not light but *heat itself*, that stores thermal energy in its cellular structure and bioluminesces faintly in the absolute darkest hours—though "dark" here is relative, for the Chant is never fully lit nor fully unlit.

The vegetation is lush, yes, but lush in the manner of overgrowth in an abandoned structure, in the manner of something that has *decided* to be abundant without ever being tended. The mineral lichens carpet the walls in hanging curtains, in bulbous extrusions, in delicate fan-structures that quiver when the thermal current shifts. Between the walls, spanning overhead, are stranger growths: hollow tubes of silica-impregnated cellulose, rising from the floor or descending from the lichen-mass above, forming a secondary architecture, a parasitic infrastructure. These tubes—called **voice-pipes** by no one, for there is no one to name them—are the lungs of the Chant. They are empty. They are loud.

The sound.

The Chant is loud because it is *active*, because process here has accelerated beyond biology into something like metabolism without bodies. The thermal gradient between the superheated floor—where temperatures would blister flesh in seconds—and the merely sweltering air twelve feet above drives constant convection. Air moves. The voice-pipes, hollow and resonant and arranged in clusters of differing lengths, catch this movement and transform it. Some tubes are stopped with mineral caps that force air through narrow openings, producing whistles. Others are open, producing deeper tones that beat against each other and create standing waves in certain spots along the corridor—places where the sound concentrates so intensely that lichens grow in spirals, where the salts in the air precipitate out in harmonic patterns, where the very clay walls have worn smoother, *polished* by centuries of vibration.

The sound is not music. It is too constant, too indifferent, too much like breathing to be composed. But it has patterns. Thermal surges from below—irregular, geologically paced but individually unpredictable—cause shifts: the pitch rising for hours, then breaking into subharmonics, then settling into a new drone fundamentally different from the last. The Chant *chases* these shifts across days, across seasons of heat, across the longer cycles no living witness has survived to record. It is an ecosystem of sound, predator and prey inverted: the thermal surge hunts stability, consumes it, and the voice-pipes' song is the sound of that consumption, the digestion of equilibrium into new form.

And the city? The built?

The walls are not natural. They were raised, shaped, perhaps by hands, perhaps by nothing so animate. The Chant holds the memory of settlement, of urban intention—a street meant for passage, for gathering, for the small commerce of proximity. But it has been *so long*. The clay shows strata of repair, of extension, of modification in styles that suggest generations of builders, yet no tool marks remain, smoothed away by the polishing vibration, the thermal cycling, the mineral accretion. There are doorways in the walls, or rather door-shaped absences where clay has fallen or been removed, leading into hollows that extend back only a few feet before choking with lichen, with collapsed tube-structures, with the compacted detritus of growth without decay. Nothing rots here. The chemistry is wrong for it, too dry, too mineral, too hot. Things accumulate. The hollows behind the door-shapes are *full*, geological cores of organic-mineral hybrid, stratified by thermal epochs, readable as history if anyone could read.

There are levels. Not visible from the corridor floor, but present: the walls contain within their thickness spaces, cavities, passages at different heights, some exposed by the slow exfoliation of outer layers, some still sealed behind clay skins that thrum with the resonance of hidden tubes. The urban here is not vertical growth but *lateral compression*, a city that built sideways into its own walls, that expanded by excavation rather than extension, that turned inward until the inward and outward became indistinguishable. Infrastructure without flow: the concept of pipe for water, of channel for waste, of vent for smoke—all present in the architecture, all repurposed by the voice-pipes, all singing now, all loud with air that moves for heat alone.

The dimness. The light.

What illumination exists comes from three sources, each weak, each partial. The bioluminescence of the heat-lichen, strongest in the tubes and hanging curtains, a cyan-green pallor that makes shadows appear as holes in reality rather than mere absence. The thermal glow of the floor itself, not visible to unadjusted eyes but present as a difference in the darkness, a warmth that seems to cast darkness upward rather than light. And the sky—not seen directly, for the corridor's narrowing walls and overhanging tube-architecture obscure it, but occasionally glimpsed through gaps where a voice-pipe cluster has fallen or where the walls have cracked in some ancient thermal stress: a sky the color of heated iron, not from sunset but from *atmosphere*, from dust suspended so permanently it has become sky, become the definition of what "up" looks like.

The Chant is active, dynamic, changing. But its change is *established*, weathered into its own form of balance. The erosion here is not loss but *conversation*: the walls wear, and the wearing shapes what grows on them; the growth weighs, and the weight cracks; the crack new tube sprouts into, and the tube falls and walls build up around it, clay and lichen indistinguishable in the accretion. There is no weather beyond the thermal, no rain to cut, no wind but the convection itself, yet the place is profoundly shaped, profoundly *old*, holding in its form the record of conditions that have not varied in kind for millennia, only in intensity, in the precise pitch of the day's song.

And weirdness? The unusual?

Only this: that in certain standing-wave nodes, where sound concentrates to the threshold of pain for ears that do not exist here, the mineral salts in the air complete their slow drift downward and *do not settle*. They hang, spheres of bronze suspension, microscopic but collectively visible as faint motes of brighter darkness, and they *rotate*. Around axes that correspond to no magnetic field, in directions that shift with the day's pitch. They are not life. They are not quite non-life. They are a phenomenon that the Chant's active processes have concentrated into being, a mineral behavior that has learned something from the voice-pipes' resonance, that holds itself in temporary defiance of descent, that lives in the loudness as surely as the lichen lives in the heat.

No animals. Never animals. The Chant's chemistry, its heat, its sound, its mineral lushness—none of it permits the animal solution, the motile heterotroph, the creature that eats and moves and dies into rot. What moves here moves by convection, by thermal imperative, by the slow flexing of tubes as air pressure shifts. What eats, eats heat directly, or the salts that carry it, or the resonance that structures possibility. The dynamic is all: process without predator, cycle without prey, a system where "active" means *attending to itself*, perpetually, loudly, in the dim bronze dark.

At the Chant's center—if a single linear corridor can be said to have one, and it can, the place insists upon it, the walls pinching slightly, the ceiling of tubes thickening overhead, the floor's heat most intense—there is a well. Not of water. Not of anything extractable. A shaft, perhaps original to whatever built the walls, perhaps eaten downward by centuries of concentrated thermal flux, descending into darkness that the dimness above seems bright by comparison. The voice-pipes cluster here thickest, and their sound is *complex*, layered, approaching the condition of language without meaning to. The bronze motes are densest here too, and their rotation fastest, and if one could descend the shaft—no one can; the heat becomes absolute, the clay walls glassing, the air itself becoming something else—if one could descend, one might find the source. The heat's origin. The first note from which the Chant organizes its endless variation.

Or one might find only more of the same: walls, lichens, tubes, sound, heat, process without purpose, dynamic without destination, a place that is exactly what it is and has been and will be, a small loud hidden empty urban primitive lush established active weird dry dim hot low thing, singing itself to itself in mineral breath, for no reason, with no end, the Chant, the Chant, the Chant.
