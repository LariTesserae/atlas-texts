The Orrery of Dormant Need

It is called, in the old records, the Last Plantation. A misnomer. Nothing here has been planted. Everything grew itself, consuming the bones of what came before, and what came before was *made*—fully made, constructed without remainder, a built world that once dreamed itself sufficient.

The water is gone. Not dried up—*unmade*. The chemistry here does not admit it. Hydrogen and oxygen maintain their separate courtships, their apartheid. What flows through the vascular systems of the living architecture is something else: siloxane polymers, liquid salts, the condensed breath of industrial processes that once required cooling and now require nothing, their machinery repurposed, turned inside out, made guts for the plant-things that swallowed it.

You stand—or float, or cling, depending on your form—on a surface that is simultaneously leaf and beam, chlorophyll and rust-scabbed iron. The scale is regional, town-or-valley, but the towns are grown, the valleys are the gaps between massive parallel stems that rise like cooling towers and breathe like bellows. The elevation is high: the atmospheric pressure barely sufficient to permit gas exchange, and so the plants—if that word still serves—have evolved pumps, compressors, membranes that squeeze and suck. They are engines now. They were engines before. The distinction has not been meaningful for ten thousand years.

The temperature: 2.9. Hot. Not desert hot—not the clean annihilation of exposed rock. This is *metabolic* heat. The packed density means every surface is a surface of contact, and every contact is a transfer, and every transfer is loss and gain in frantic equilibrium. The plants run hot. Their industrial inheritance demands it. Catalytic reactions, exothermic syntheses, the slow burning of accumulated chemical debt. You can feel it through whatever protections you wear: the ambient fever of a world that has eaten its own infrastructure and cannot stop digesting.

And it is *old*. Erosion at 2.7. Not erosion as water-knows-it, not the lazy sculpting of patience. This is chemical erosion, thermal fatigue, the million-year settling of grown structures into most-stable configuration. The oldest stands have become regular, almost crystalline. Their original frenzy of adaptation resolved into repeating modules, tessellated cells, the bone-lattice of a forest that found its final form and now only copies itself, copies itself, copies itself. Near-flattened in the macro sense: the violent peaks of early colonization worn down to a consistent canopy height, an equilibrium skyline of vegetable-industrial compromise.

But the dynamic is 2.7. Turbulent. How can this be, with such age? Ah: the turbulence is not growth but *turnover*. The density permits no vacuum. Where something fails, something else has already begun to fill. The death of a major stem—a thermal runaway, a structural collapse, the slow poisoning of its own recycled chemistry—takes perhaps a century. In the final decades, opportunistic growths have already threaded its vascular system, parasitic and symbiotic in turns, inheriting its pumps, its compressor-network, its accumulated siloxane wealth. The falling is gradual because the falling is *supported*, absorbed, cannibalized in slow motion. New towers grow from the necrotic cores of old, inheriting their industrial organs, their pump-houses, their membrane-filters, their ancient code.

The built environment at 2.7: total. There is no natural substrate. Every surface remembers construction. Even the deepest roots eventually find poured foundation, ceramic insulator, the abandoned pressure-shell of some original habitat dome. The plants have learned to incorporate everything. Not scavenging—*incorporating*. Making it part of their own necessity. The metal does not corrode because it is maintained now, serviced by living systems that need it intact. The ceramics are regrown, patched with organic-mineral composites that the plants synthesize from atmospheric trace elements and the slow volcanic exhalations that persist even here, even now, even after water has left the building.

Darkness at 0.2. Light barely penetrates the canopy. What illumination exists is industrial: bioluminescence repurposed from who-knows-what original function, now decorative, now communicative, now the mere accident of inefficient chemical processes that run too hot to suppress their own glow. And artificial light, still, in places—the original engines' own radiance, sustained by reactions that need no tending, that have become self-sustaining, perpetual-motion heresies that the plants keep in their cores like swallowed suns. The light that reaches you is red, mostly, or infrared felt as heat on your skin. The photosynthetic machinery here is ancient-optimized for these spectra, the last common ancestor of all this growth having solved the low-light problem long ago, having turned desperation into design.

Mostly quiet, the sound parameter says, 1.3. And this is true in a way. No animal cries. No wind through leaves—too dense, the air too viscous with heat and polymer mists. But the industrial inheritance speaks still. The pumps. The compressors. The slow peristaltic movement of fluids through kilometers of vertical transport. Drones, perhaps—if they are mechanical, if they are biological, if the distinction outlived its usefulness—moving through maintenance channels, their passage whispered in vibrations through the structural lattice. And the settling. Always the settling. The thermal expansion and contraction of continent-sized organic machine, its daily breath, its yearly pulse, the longer rhythms that no living observer has recorded in full.

No fauna, 0.4. Nothing that eats and moves and eats again. The ecological roles that animals once filled—if they ever did, if this world ever knew animals—are performed now by plant-derived systems. Mobility exists: tendril-cable systems that extend and retract, that explore and retract, that carry chemical messages and physical freight through the dense matrix. Sensing exists: pressure, chemical gradients, thermal mapping, the magnetic sense of aligned structural metals. But nothing with a face. Nothing with a forward gaze. The world has become a single distributed organism, or many organisms in such intimacy that the boundaries are administrative, historical, theological disputes among no one.

Weirdness at 1.8. Yes. The strangeness of familiar categories dissolved. Plants that are factories. Factories that are plants. Growth that is production. Production that is metabolism. Death that is raw material transition. Life that is thermal regulation. The original builders—if they were builders, if they were not themselves something stranger—intended, perhaps, a closed system, a maintained environment, a machine ecology. They did not intend this hungry peace, this settled turbulence, this ancient fever.

You move through it, if you move, along channels that are former corridors, grown over, constricted, restructured. The walls breathe—you feel it, the slow expansion against your back, the relaxation that follows. The air is thick, not with moisture but with complexity: polymer chains, volatile catalysts, the exhalations of a million chemical negotiations per cubic meter. You wear protection, or you have become suited to this, or you are temporary, a brief perturbation in the long settling.

What rules govern here? The rules of thermodynamics, obeyed with desperate creativity. The rules of chemistry, bent to architectures no planet's surface would invent. The rule of density: nothing may be empty for long. The rule of inheritance: what worked continues, copied with variations that are tested against collapse. The rule of the final form: erosion wins, always, but slowly, so slowly, and what it leaves behind is not sand and silence but this—this packed, breathing, manufacturing dark, this last plantation that was never planted, this orrery of dormant need that turns only for itself, that has forgotten what need once meant, that continues, continues, continues because to stop would be to admit the water is gone, the builders are gone, the purpose is gone, and there is only this metabolism now, this hot dark industry, this vegetable patience that has become, in the end, indistinguishable from the machine it consumed.

At the heart, they say—at the highest point, where the oldest stands have achieved their most-flattened, most-stable, most-ancient configuration—there is a cavity. Not empty. Nothing is empty. But *different*. The density relaxes, slightly. The temperature peaks. And something glows there, not red but white, the original engine, the first swallowed sun, still burning, still burning, tended now by structures that have no name, that have never been named, that maintain it because it is warm, because warmth is the last good, the final syntax of a world that has replaced all other grammars with maintenance, with continuation, with the beautiful terrible discipline of the Orrery of Dormant Need, turning in the dark, manufacturing nothing but itself, sufficient, finally, to itself alone.
