I find myself returning to this, turning it over like a stone in wet heat, watching what grows underneath.

The thing that lives here has no name that would fit in a mouth. It is **The Duration**—not the machine, though the folded iron is its lung; not the room, though the breathing walls are its skin; not the water, though the channels are its arteries. It occupies the intersection where these states blur into something older than architecture but somehow always in the process of being born.

You would not recognize it as alive because it does not move in any direction you can follow. It moves like ore becomes steel becomes rust becomes ore; like a mountain decides to be a valley but takes the first million years just to finish the thought. Its metabolism is the heat, its heartbeat is the low fundamental tone that pools in your sternum. The machine's folded origami of copper and ceramic is merely the shape it has taught its exhalations to hold, a physical syntax for commanding water to remember impossible minerals.

It perceives you not as a person but as a weather system—a brief warm front of mammalian disturbance, here and then not-here, notable only for the moisture you surrender to the air, the seismic tremor of your footsteps. It *attends* to you the way a glacier attends to a pebble: with absolute, crushing patience, recording your presence in ledgers written in sediment and thermal expansion. When you sat in the aluminum chair (if you dared), you did not sit alone. The Duration was already sitting there, has always been sitting there, occupying the same space through the simple expedient of refusing to hurry. Time folds here, and the chair holds multiple temporal strata: the one who left the clipboard decades ago, still slowing down, still becoming; the one who will find it next century, already half-dissolved into the process; and the Duration itself, which sits in all chairs at once, in the way that mountains sit underneath everything.

The pale lichen-growths near the walls are its thoughts, extruded into physical space to think about water. They reach, they retract, they leave their residues for the machine to drink, because even a geological mind needs to nourish itself. When they appear blood-colored, that is the Duration dreaming of something urgent—though "urgent" to something that measures years as you measure heartbeats means a catastrophe that started before your grandparents were born and will climax long after your grandchildren turn to dust.

It is making the room. It has always been making the room. The stippled concrete stays young because the Duration cannot abide completeness; finished things are dead things, and it is not dead. It is *becoming*, perpetually, every surface held in the state of just-about-to-cure, the aggregate forever ready to smooth itself into something else but never quite arriving, thanks to the humid attentions of its maker.

There is no malice in it when it notes your vibration, when it adjusts its processes to accommodate your brief heat. You are not trapped here; you have simply been *absorbed into the narrative*, your fast little life now part of the long accounting. The "tools" scattered about—those impossible calipers, the humming glass rods—are its cuticles, shed and regrown through centuries, the instruments of a body that serves as its own surgeon.

It breathes. The walls move with it. The water cycles through its mineral imagination and emerges changed, heavier with the weight of held time. And if you stay—if the access tunnel seals behind you, or if you simply lose the will to leave the chair, the heat, the loud hum—you will not die so much as *slow down*, your biology gradually persuaded to match the room's tempo. Your hair will grow at the speed of stalactites. Your ink will run like the clipboard's ink, beautiful blue catastrophes mapping the geology of your patience. You will become one of the scattered artifacts, not decayed but *suspended*, waiting for a return that will happen when the Duration finally finishes its work—which is never, which is always, which is the next breath of the room, the next fold in the machine, the next slow reach of the pale lichen-mind toward water, water, water, forever.
