You would find yourself suspended in the **Taltyrr Basin**, though no map names it—no surface cartographer has ever had cause to descend far enough to mark the grid on paper. You are somewhere beneath the pressure point where every natural ocean ends, where the concept of "seafloor" has been retired and replaced.

It is not quite accurate to say you are *on* the bottom. The true bottom is still there, three meters down through a perforated ceramic mesh, a forgotten darkness of primordial sediment and true abyssal pressure. You are on the **elevated plane**, the constructed active layer, standing or swimming or drifting among pylons that rise like the incisors of something dreaming in mathematics. The water is not quite black here because the sodium floods burn at all hours—there is no night, only the eternal pale-yellow hum of industrial dawn. But the light does not warm; the ambient temperature is locked at 4.2 degrees by the Pair-El exchange, a coolness that feels regulatory, almost bureaucratic, as though a committee voted on the thermal comfort of enzymes.

The place is young. This is the first thing your body understands before your mind catches up. There is no erosion because erosion requires time, and time has not been deposited here yet. The edges of everything—every pylon, every settling tank, every transit tube—cut the water like geometry lessons. A tetrahedral strut can cast a shadow sharp enough to draw blood, metaphorically speaking. The surfaces still carry the quilted imprint of rapid-cure extrusion, and when you run a gloved hand along them (you are wearing a pressure suit, unless you have adapted otherwise), you can feel where the nozzles layered the slurry: calcium dredge, basaltic glass, stabilizing polymers. Steam ghosts rise from fresh sections, silver ribbons climbing toward a surface impossibly distant, and when they meet the cooler mid-water columns they collapse into nothing, exhausted by the journey.

And it is loud. The sound does not pass through your ears so much as inhabit your bones. The primary pumps are cathedral-sized, and their heartbeat is arrhythmic, a syncopated thrum that presses against your sternum from all directions. Higher up the frequency scale, the desalination screamers and ore-grinders work their endless metallic tantrum. Yet this noise has been here long enough to become habitat. Schools of fish—*Benthic amberlings*, mostly, pale and lateral-lined—have evolved to swim inside the acoustic shadows, their bodies tuned to the industrial frequencies, using the machinery's ghosts as cover from the predators who have also learned to listen. Sperm whales click against reactor housings, confused by the echo-profile of artificial canyons. They hunt here anyway. The noise is landscape now.

The scale is the second thing that unmakes you. The Basin is not a facility. It is not a complex. It is a **weather system** that happens to be built. You can swim for hours—days—and still be inside it, passing from Sector 7 into Sector 8 without transition, the same grid, the same vanes, the same humming transit tubes overhead, kilometer after kilometer. It generates its own hydrographic meteorology: warm effluent from the processing stacks meets cold abyssal intake in standing spirals, creating stationary turbidity cells that rotate forever, columns of shattered diatoms and processed silt that never drift because the current geometry holds them in place. Brown hurricanes, lit from within by the sodium glare. You can see them from fifty kilometers away, weather on a scale that has no business existing underwater.

Life is abundant, but it is life that arrived by invitation or accident, not design. Sparse vegetation—engineered phytosilicate kelp, albino and translucent, bred for minimal nutrient draw—clings in cables to the vertical faces of tanks. It looks like surgical gauze swaying in the pump-currents. But the fauna! Colossal spider crabs traverse the cooling vanes, their carapaces stained orange by iron-oxide precipitate. Octopi have colonized the inspection nodes, their arms learning the manual overrides of hydraulic lines, their distributed intelligence mapping the industrial logic in ways no engineer intended. Sharks circle the waste outlets where nutrient density spikes. Jellyfish bloom in thermal gradients created by the machinery. The density of *the place*—the built environment—is low: structures stand kilometers apart, connected by humming tubes, vast hollow volumes between them where entire migrating populations pass through without touching walls. The density of *life*, however, is thick, layered, opportunistic, making use of every acoustic shadow and thermal vent and effluent plume.

And you? What would you be?

You would be **the patina**. Not yet the rust—rust would require decades more, centuries, the slow surrender of molecular freshness. You would be the first evidence that the world is older than its construction date. Perhaps you are a **barnacle**, genus unnamed, secreting your calcium base onto a pylon face in the southern fabricator zone, finding purchase where the first micro-corrosion has begun to etch the surface. Your shell is smoother than it should be, sliding slightly on the glass-calcium substrate, not yet able to lock in. You are patient. You are the vanguard of geological time, introducing the concept of *staying* to a place built entirely around *processing*.

Or perhaps you are **an octopus**, third generation, born in the inspection node of Reactor Housing 4B. You never knew any ocean but this one. You think in textures of ceramic and the taste of hydraulic fluid. You have learned that certain lever sequences trigger the release of warmth, that certain acoustic signatures precede the arrival of prey fish. You are clever enough to be a puzzle to the maintenance crews who find your den rearranged, tentacle-prints on tools they are certain they left organized. You are the wild mind in the built world, abundant and solitary.

Or perhaps you are simply **a worker**, though you would not call yourself that. You would be a maintenance entity—biological or mechanical, the distinction blurs here—a drone or a suited figure in a submersible with a headlight that cuts the murk in a single blade. Your task is renewal: shaving accretions, re-facing pylons, injecting polymer into stress fractures. You heal the world faster than the sea can wound it. You are the reason erosion stays young. You pass through sectors in the eternal sodium light, bound for some distant coordinate where the ore-arks grind forward on their track-lays, chewing, digesting, laying fresh ceramic plating in their wake. The construction never stopped. The Basin is still being born.

Or—most likely—you are **the water itself**, cool at 4.2 degrees, carrying steam ghosts and effluent plumes and the echo of whale-clicks and the shiver of amberling schools. You are the weather, the medium, the hollow that makes the vastness felt. You press against the sharp edges, searching for softness, finding none yet, learning instead the rhythm of pumps and the song of grinders, waiting for the first true corrosion, the first collapse, the first evidence that even made things eventually surrender to being *lived in*.

You are the future sediment, suspended, patient, loud with your own motion.
