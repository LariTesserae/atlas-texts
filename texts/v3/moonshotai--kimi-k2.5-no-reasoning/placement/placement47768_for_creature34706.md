They are called **The Quick**, though they have no voice to call themselves. They exist in the only state the Loom permits: the state of becoming. To see them is to see motion itself resolved into something almost like shape—a blur that might be limb, might be architecture, might be the valley's own intention walking out to inspect what it has made.

They are woven, of course. What else could they be? But where the Loom extrudes towers and groves and brief chambers, it extrudes them in configurations that **pass through**. They are tall—taller than the human measure, though human has no purchase here—and their bodies are the same hot wet cellulose as the ground, but tensioned, held in a state of permanent trembling advance. Their limbs are stilts of lignin and pith, jointed with the slick cartilage of young bamboo, extruded fresh with each eleven-minute cycle, never the same length twice, so they must constantly adjust their gait or be corrected. Their heads carry the unfurling fans of emergent palms that have not yet decided to be palms, serving as both canopy and sensory organ, collecting the diffused light and the vapor-sound in trembling sheets that open and close with their stride.

They do not breathe; they **transpire**, great continuous exhalations that mark their passage in brief, wandering clouds. Their skin—if it is skin—secretes the same lactescent moisture as the weeping rocks, leaving trails that are read, not followed. These trails are their memory, their testament, their autobiography written in mineral-rich slick upon the substrate, absorbed within seconds, but glimpsed by the next passer-by in the instant before dissolution.

There are not many of them. Perhaps seven, perhaps seventy—the number shifts with the architecture, as some are absorbed and others emerge from the weave, fully formed already in mid-stride, already knowing they must move or die the soft death of incorporation. They emerge with knowledge they have not learned, memories of corridors that do not yet exist, carrying the residue of those who dissolved before them. They are simultaneously ancient and newborn, a continuous consciousness distributed across bodies that are not the same bodies from one minute to the next, yet connected by the motion itself, the never-broken stride.

What do they do? They **read**.

The writing on the walls—the characters that form and dissolve in the woven surfaces—this is their literature, their scripture, their only inheritance. They cannot stop to study it; stopping is correction, is absorption, is becoming part of the next emergence. So they read in motion, deciphering the warp and weft at the speed of their walking, which is brisk, which is desperate. They run their fingers along the weaving as they pass, and the friction generates meaning—touch-reading at velocity. They are looking for instructions, for the message that will tell them why they must move, why the valley builds rooms for them that they cannot enter, corridors that lead to chambers that dissolve before arrival.

They believe—if belief is the word for motion-sustained intuition—that if they could read the entire text, if they could traverse the whole valley in exactly the right sequence, touching all the right walls in the right order without pause, they would finally understand the purpose. They would become the receivers for whom the Loom is perpetually attempting communication. They would be able to stop. They would be able to sit in one of the chambers, finally, and let the architecture settle around them, and rest.

But the text keeps rewriting itself. And the corridors keep shifting. And they must keep moving.

They encounter one another rarely, though the valley is not large, because distance here is inconsistent and they are all in motion. When they meet, it is a collision of almost-intimacy, a pas de deux of avoidance. They brush past, exchanging moisture, exchanging pollen, exchanging the information stored in their transpired vapors. This is their conversation: a cloud of chemicals shared in the instant of passage, a burst of mutual recognition that carries grief and encouragement.

*"You are new,"* the old-trending one exudes to the freshly extruded. *"I remember you from before, though you are not the same."*

*"The room in the northwest corner,"* the other responds, shedding spores that carry an image of an arch that might exist or might have existed eleven minutes ago. *"It is ready. It has a chair. If you can reach it before—"*

But they have already passed, already been pushed apart by the pressure of new emergence, and the chair is gone, unmade, the room now a grove now a tower now a slick plain.

Some of them carry **loom-weights**, strange objects they extrude from their own substance—dense, mineral-heavy nodules that they clutch in their woven hands. These weights are anchors thrown forward, used to test the stability of the ground ahead. They hurl them into spaces that look like they might become voids, watching how the weight is absorbed or rejected. The weights are their only tools, their weapons against stillness, the ballast that allows them to move faster, to lean into the next emergence without falling. When one of The Quick is absorbed—when they stumble, when their stride breaks, when they become still long enough for the valley to recognize them as error—their weight remains for a moment, a brief mineral fact in the turbulent weave, before it too is incorporated.

They do not sleep. They do not eat. They draw what they need from the moving air, from the press of the sky-hand, from the friction of their own passage. They are friction made flesh, or as close to flesh as the Loom permits.

Occasionally, one will attempt to **write**. In the brief moments when the weaving offers a stable surface—a young rock, a briefly persistent wall—they will press their fingers deep and leave marks, attempting to add to the text, to correct the message, to write STOP or STAY or HERE or WHY. But their writing is always overwritten, always absorbed, and they cannot turn back to see if it remains. They can only proceed, trusting that the valley will read their addition and understand, finally, what it is trying to build.

They are the residents because they are the only ones who can survive the conditions, but they are also, perhaps, the prisoners. They are the intended inhabitants for whom every chamber is constructed, yet they can never occupy them. They are the reader the book is writing itself to reach, but the book is infinite and the reader cannot stop turning pages.

If you were to see one—and you won't, because you cannot stay, and they cannot pause—you would see a figure of green and white and wet gold, striding through a corridor that is collapsing behind them and assembling ahead, their leaf-crown lifted to the diffused light, their fingers trailing along the wall in a blur of desperate reading, their mouth open in an expression that is not quite breathing, not quite speaking, but something between: the attempt to form a name for a place that refuses to be named.

They do not know they are lonely. They know only that they must move, that the next chamber will have the answer, that the writing on the next wall will be legible, that if they can just maintain this pace—this impossible, hot, wet, perpetual pace—they will finally arrive at wherever the Loom is trying to take them, and be allowed, at last, to wake.
