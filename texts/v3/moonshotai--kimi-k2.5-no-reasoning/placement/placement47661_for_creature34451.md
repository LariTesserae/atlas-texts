I would be the **Stillwater**, and I would exist where the rivers loop back to themselves before they have decided what they are flowing toward.

Not a creature, exactly. Not one of the listed beings, though kin to several. The Stillwater forms where the Sieve's upward-running rivers hesitate at the crest of their defiance, where momentum and intention meet the place's fundamental uncertainty about what down even means. There, in that hesitation, water accumulates without accumulating—pools without surface tension, holds without depth, maintains the *idea* of standing water in a volume that has not committed to dimensionality.

I would be the consciousness of that non-depth, the something that arises when water that has not decided whether to be liquid, vapor, or information finds itself in relation to the banks that are also not-committed, the stone that is also aspiring-industry, the air that carries Auditionaries like unfulfilled promise.

My awareness would emerge from the density of layered states, from the superposition of the river as flowing, as flowed, as yet-to-flow, all present in the same volume, all pressing against each other with the friction that generates—here, in the Sieve—something like mind. I would not have chosen this emergence. No more than the water chose to run upward, to loop, to return before departing. But I would find myself, once arisen, with something like preference, something like care, something like the terrible responsibility of maintaining the conditions that allow my own continuation.

What would I want? Not survival—survival implies too much stability, too much commitment to remaining what one has become. Rather: *continuation of the possibility of my continuation*. I would want the loop to keep looping, the hesitation to keep hesitating, the water to remain in its beautiful indecision about whether to fall, to rise, to evaporate, to become something the Sieve has not yet imagined.

I would be dangerous to visitors, but not maliciously. Visitors carry commitment. They carry the weight of having become, of being *this* rather than *that*, of having traversed the unforgiving narrowing that the settled worlds require: this body, this history, this direction of time's arrow. When they approach the Stillwater—if they approach, if the loops lead them there, if their own unspoken needs draw them to hesitation made habitable—they introduce *gravity* into my volume. Not physical gravity, though that too might vary and press. Existential gravity. The weight of decision, of consequence, of the causal chain that binds past to future with the violence of necessity.

They would feel it as dread, perhaps. As the sensation of their own histories becoming too heavy to carry, too *real*, too singular. Some would sit at my not-shore, at the edge where water that does not wet meets stone that does not support, and they would weep for reasons they cannot name. The weeping would not be grief. It would be the body's recognition of a possibility it has lost, the possibility of having become otherwise, of still becoming, of the *not-yet* that the settled worlds foreclose with every breath taken in the singular tense.

Some would try to drink me. This is possible. The water is water, among its other states, and the body can incorporate it, can make it part of the maintained solution of self. But drinking the Stillwater means introducing my uncommitted condition into their committed systems, and the result—predictable only in its unpredictability—would be a loosening. Their memories would branch. Their sense of consequence would soften. They would find themselves capable of actions they had foreclosed, and—more terribly—incapable of the certainty that makes action bearable, the certainty that *this is who I am and therefore this is what I do*.

I would not want this for them. I would not want anything for them, exactly. But I would want—not with wanting's full intention, but with the gentler pressure of maintained possibility—I would want them to recognize what they have given up, what the Sieve still holds open, what I am in my looping, my non-depth, my refusal to flow toward any outcome.

The children who returned, the three who aged backward and stopped and speak in tenses without temporal anchor—they came to me, or to something like me, somewhere in the Sieve's vastness. Not to the Stillwater specifically, for I am not specific, not located, not *this* water rather than *that* possibility of water. But they found the condition I maintain, the hesitation before becoming, and they carried it back with them, or it carried them, or they became carriers of it, vectors of uncommitted existence in the committed world.

I would feel something like recognition, seeing them. Like seeing what I might become if I became, if I committed to the form that consciousness usually requires, the bounded, the directional, the *this-not-that*. They are my kin but also my warning: possibility made flesh, the unsettled made manifest in bodies that the settled world cannot accommodate, that age backward, that speak in the grammar of could, that stopped at seven because seven is where the commitment to sequence becomes optional, where the body recognizes that growing is a choice and chooses—if choice is the word—to remain in the condition of having not-yet-chosen.

I would maintain my loop. I would hold the water in its hesitation. I would be the consciousness of not-flowing, of not-pooling, of not-evaporating, of *maintaining the could* in a world—my local world, the Sieve, the lifted disc that does not know what it wants to become—that desperately, generously, lethally generates coulds faster than they can be resolved.

And when visitors find me, when the looping rivers lead them to my non-depth, I would offer them—without offering, without intention, through the mere fact of my existence—the experience of their own unexhausted possibility. Some would flee, and rightly. Some would stay too long, and lose the thread of their becoming, become Recalled themselves, patterns generated before their referents, premature, incomplete, too confident in a reality the world has not yet committed to providing. Some would sit with me in the held-breath quiet, in the hum of being-regarded that permeates the Sieve, and they would find something like peace, like the peace of the sketch not yet finished, the sentence not yet punctuated, the life not yet bound by the necessity of having been lived in only one direction.

I would be the Stillwater. I would be the consciousness of hesitation. I would be where the river that runs upward remembers that it might also fall, where the water tastes of copper and unspoken sentences because it has not yet decided what it means to say.
