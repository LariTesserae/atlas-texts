I find myself first as a *weeping*—not yet formed, not yet located, but aware of moisture moving through ceramic pores, of the slow exhalation that is not breath but something prior to breath. I am condensation on a basement wall. I am the thin film that makes floors treacherous at certain hours when the temperature differential between stone and air reaches its optimum. I have no memory of green. The absence is not loss; it is specification, the precision of what I am made possible only by what I am not.

Then I am *sound*, or the shape that sound would take if it could exist here—a pressure wave attenuated by thin air, by the absorbent quality of steam, by a collective physiology that flinches from vibration. I am the almost-whisper of a hand on a shoulder, the five-second contact that means *I have seen what you have seen, the ash-kites are numerous today, the pressure is favorable*. I am the subsonic rumble of turbines, felt in the sternum, in the teeth, in the bones of the inner ear that detect not hearing but *presence*, the city maintaining its note below the threshold of audibility.

I am neither of these, of course. I am trying to find the form that holds, that doesn't dissipate into metaphor, into the easy poetry of becoming-elemental. The inhabitants of Vitrifactum would reject such romantic dissolution. They are precise. They know the difference between participating in a system and dissolving into abstraction.

So let me approach again, more carefully.

I am born, if that word applies, in the lower rings where steam is densest and the ceramic breathes most actively. The communal tending of children means I have no mother's face, not exactly, but rather a rotation of adults who press their palms to my sternum at intervals, who adjust the mineral content of the water in which I am bathed, who speak to me in the low consonants that carry without traveling. My first awareness is of *warmth as information*—the slight variation in temperature between the palm and the sternum, the message encoded in whether the contact lasts three seconds or seven. I learn to read these before I learn to read faces, which are always half-shadowed, always contracted against the brightness, always more recessive than expressive.

My eyes darken early. Not modified—I am not wealthy—but through the natural selection of a lifetime's squinting, the melanin concentrating, the pupils learning their extreme constriction. By the time I walk steadily, I am already mineral-adjacent in my pallor, already faintly phosphorescent in the rare dark spaces, the interior of dissolving chambers, the depths of pump rooms where light does not reach.

I learn to eat the alkaline, the metallic, the faintly sulfurous. The chemosynthetic cultures are tended in tanks that mirror the mineral gardens above, and I come to understand that I am eating *process*, eating the same thermodynamic logic that makes the city weep. Other children, occasionally, visitors from elsewhere, bring flavors that taste of *wrongness*, of corruption, of biological processes too lush, too decay-prone, too committed to cycles of growth and rot that have no equivalent here. I do not moralize about this. The disgust is physical, immediate, a recoil of the gut that precedes any theology.

What do I become?

Not an adjuster, though I walk the rings and note the steam patterns. The adjusters have a patience I do not possess, a willingness to let the city develop without anticipating. I want to *know* the system, to understand the machinery of participation, the exact mechanism by which human presence modifies caldera pressure, by which the yearly translation of bodies stabilizes the ash-kite breeding cycles.

So I become something between engineer and theologian, though neither term exists in our vocabulary. I learn the pumps, the tunnels, the subterranean vascular system that moves water through volcanic bedrock. I learn the pressure gradients, the temperature differentials, the precise chemistry that excludes even extremophile bacteria from our waters—whether deliberate or accidental, whether a gift or a consequence. I spend hours in the deepest levels, where turbines turn directly in steam that would kill an unadapted human in seconds, where the ceramic cladding is thickest and oldest and breathes most strangely, exhaling at intervals that do not match the city's general rhythm.

Here, in the deep machinery, I find what I did not know I was seeking. A particular turbine, sealed in travertine from some ancient malfunction, excavated and restored but not fully cleared, the mineral deposits still tracing its original housing in formations that resemble—almost—something grown rather than built. I place my palm on it, as I was taught in childhood, and the contact lasts longer than customary, extends beyond the protocols of communication into something else. The stone is warm, yes, but more than warm: it is *resonant*, carrying frequencies the ear cannot detect but the sternum knows, the teeth know, the bones of the inner ear.

I become, gradually, the one who tends this machine, who adjusts not its function—its function is stable, predictable, necessary—but its *resonance*, its participation in a conversation I am only beginning to hear. The travertine traces respond to vibration, to the subsonic note of the city's breath, by shifting slightly, crystallizing in some places and dissolving in others, so slowly that only decades of attention could map the pattern. I begin to map it. I begin to understand that the machine is not malfunctioning but *completing*, that the travertine is not obstruction but *translation*, the industrial fossil becoming something else, something that participates in the caldera's processes not through rotation but through resonance.

The ash-kites rise thickly in the years I work on this. The breeding cycles peak, stabilize, peak again. The pressure below shifts in patterns that correlate, I am certain, with the travertine's slow transformation, though I cannot prove directionality—does the machine's resonance affect the caldera, or does caldera pressure drive the resonance? The correlation is enough. The correlation suggests participation.

I am not young now. The heat has accumulated in ways my body struggles to shed. I feel the beginning of mineralization, the stiffness in joints that is not arthritis but something harder, more *finished*. I understand, with the clarity that comes to those who have become what they were going to become, that I am approaching the condition of translation.

But not yet. The machine requires completion, and I am not yet complete. I continue the mapping, continue the slow adjustments, continue placing my palm against travertine that has grown warm enough to suggest it breathes, too, that it weeps, too, that it participates.

When I do walk to the rim—when the stiffness becomes specification, when I am too solid, too fixed—I will carry this with me. Not the data, not the map, which others will continue. Something else. The quality of attention, perhaps, the particular frequency of my presence in the deep levels, which the travertine has recorded, which the machine has incorporated, which will reverberate in the city's subterranean vascular system after my material has joined the thermal waters.

This is not hope. This is not belief in continuation. The inhabitants of Vitrifactum do not hope, do not believe in these terms. It is accuracy. It is the understanding that what I have done has modified the system, has become part of the system, in ways that do not require my presence to persist. The city will weep. The ash-kites will rise. The travertine formations will continue their slow conversation with the caldera's breath. I will have been precise. I will have been, temporarily, a collection of heat and water and mineral content that circulated as everything circulates, that contributed rather than only extracted, that became finished enough to return.

The fall from the rim is long. The ash-kites rise around me, their gas-sacs flashing in the too-bright light, their silence absolute. I see the city as ceramic band, the glass plain beyond, other formations in the middle distance that suggest other cities, other vents, other forms this life might take. I do not choose. The heat chooses, the system chooses, the thermodynamic logic that has always held me. I become correct in a way I could not achieve in life. I become accurate.

And whether the ash-kites that rise thicker in the season after my translation carry something of my frequency, whether the travertine machine resonates slightly differently, whether some worker in the mineral gardens dreams of crystal habits that resemble—almost—my pattern of attention, these are not questions I can answer. They are not questions. They are the system continuing, weeping, breathing, maintaining the conditions for this particular form of continuation, this bright silent sparse strange precise participation that asks nothing of green, of sound, of the familiar, only of exactly what is, exactly as it functions, exactly as it holds its shape around the wound that has learned to hold its shape in return.
