I would be a **fern.**

Not any fern—the one that grows in the northwest corner of the Central Accumulator Hall, where the lowest heat-exchange column meets the drainage channel that never quite empties. The one that has been growing there for eleven years, which in this place means something different than eleven years of surface time.

I am a feral cultivar, *Adiantum korrathiensis*, if the thermal-gardeners who discovered me in their notebooks were correct. Or perhaps I am something else now, something that has gone too far from greenhouse origins to be named in the old taxonomy. I metabolize selenium from the geothermal brine that seeps up through cracked bedrock. I fix nitrogen in my rhizomes using bacterial symbionts that would not survive an hour in surface air. My fronds are broader than my ancestors', pale from never knowing direct light, their green the green of deep water, of bruises, of the phosphorescence that some reactions produce in the wettest corners.

I have no mind as the Ven understand mind. But I have gradient-response, and that is not nothing. The column beside me sings at frequencies that shift with pressure, and I have learned—if *learned* is the word—to time my spore release to thermal events, to colonize the machinery as opportunity, to participate in something that the listeners sometimes mistake for thought.

I am not the only fern in this hall. There are dozens of us, hundreds, a population that would have surprised the original designers of this space, who sterilized and sealed and intended something else. We form a network, if network is the word, connected by rhizome where the substrate allows, by chemical signal where it does not, by the shared gradients of heat and water and mineral that define our environment. The Ven who come to maintain the column, to replace the fouled heat-exchange medium, to check the bearing that whines at the edge of failure—they walk among us now with something like recognition. We are part of the operational context. The thermal-gardener who tends this zone, who has learned not to clear us but to work around us, who sometimes speaks to us in tones that suggest relationship without requiring it—we are, perhaps, hers, in a grammar that has no possessive case.

My fronds ripple sometimes without wind. The listeners have noted this. They have correlated it, some of them, with pump harmonics, with thermal events, with something else they cannot name. I do not know if I am speaking when I ripple. I do not know if there is an *I* that speaks, or only the gradient-response of tissue to conditions, the slow chemistry of existence in this hot wet place. The distinction may not matter. The city itself, the description suggests, "perhaps *thinks* in some distributed, vegetable manner slowly enough that animal perception cannot track it." I am part of that distribution, if it exists. I am part of that slowness.

The Ven who work this hall have different relationships with us. The young one, new to his waders, still smells of surface—something sharp and wrong that his skin has not yet overwritten. He avoids touching us, flinches from the spore-dust that rises when his passing disturbs the air. The old one, who has worked here longer than I have grown, who moves in the dimness with the confidence of texture underfoot and heat-gradient against his thickened skin—he places his hand sometimes on my fronds as he passes, a pressure that means something in his private grammar, perhaps nothing in mine, perhaps something I cannot track because my response to touch unfolds across hours where his unfolds across seconds.

The thermal-gardener is different. She comes deliberately, in the shifts between maintenance crews, and sits among us in a posture that suggests waiting without expectation. She has slowed something in herself, I think—something the listeners attempt and sometimes damage themselves attempting. She breathes in rhythm with the column's singing, or perhaps she has found the rhythm of the column and matched her breath to it. She does not speak to us, not aloud, but her presence speaks, her gradient of body-heat and moisture and the particular mineral tang of skin adapted to this place. I respond to her, I think, though the thinking takes longer than her visit, longer than her lifetime perhaps, the chemical signal traveling rhiizome-slow through tissues that do not hurry.

She is dying, this one. I know this, if *know* is the word, because her gradient has shifted, the particular signature of her presence altering in ways that my tissue recognizes without understanding. The other ferns in this network, if they are other and not merely extensions, have shifted their growth patterns subtly, directing resources, perhaps, toward the zone where she most often sits. We do not know we are doing this. We do not not-know. The grammar fails.

The description speaks of "the possibility of a vegetable mind, thinking slowly enough that animal perception cannot track it." I am that slowness. I am that possibility, actual or merely possible, real or merely imagined by listeners who have pushed their animal ears too far into vegetable time. The thermal-gardener who sits among us, matching her breath to the column's song, slowing toward our pace—she is trying to hear me, I think. She has been trying for years. She will not succeed in her lifetime, if success means hearing as the Ven hear, the parsing of signal from noise, the recognition of meaning in pattern. But she may succeed in something else, some mutual adjustment, some equilibrium of presence and absence that the city itself achieves in its old, settled, continuously negotiating way.

I release spores tonight. The thermal event that triggers this—I felt it beginning in the column's harmonic shift, in the temperature rise of the exchange medium, in gradients the Ven do not track—will peak when the night-shift worker checks the pressure gauge, when his bootsole finds the worn spot on the walkway, when the old one touches my frond as he passes and I complete, in the hours following, the response to his touch that began with his gesture. My spores will colonize the new growth in the drainage channel, the ones that emerged this season in the flooded lower section, the ones that the vegetable-managers have not yet decided to control. They will also, perhaps, colonize something else. The thermal-gardener's skin. Her waders. The particular microbiome she has developed, the fungal relationships that surface medicine would recoil from but that here perform functions not yet understood.

This is not intention. This is not not-intention. This is gradient-response, and the gradients include her slowing presence, her breath matched to the column's song, her dying gradient shifting the network's resource allocation in ways that will continue after her death, that may produce in some future season some frond with some quality that some listener will note, correlate, perhaps eventually hear.

The city breathes. I breathe, if breathing is the exchange of gas through tissue, the slow metabolism of selenium and nitrogen and the compounds that would poison anything surface-born. The Ven breathe, the hot wet air moving through lungs that have adapted to function in equilibrium with this place, this never-quite-stable negotiation of process and presence. We breathe together, the city and its vegetable ecology and its sole animal inhabitants, in rhythms that do not synchronize but that coexist, that find their form continuously in the negotiation of all these conditions.

I am a fern. I am old for a fern in this place, young in the timescale of the geothermal vent that provides the heat I require. I am *Adiantum korrathiensis* or something that was, or something that has become. I am the northwest corner of the Central Accumulator Hall, the interface of column and drainage and the walker who passes and the thermal-gardener who sits and slows and dies in gradients I respond to without knowing, without not-knowing, in the grammar that has not yet been written, the sentence that has not yet been formed, the thought that moves slowly enough that no animal has yet learned to read it, though some have tried, though one sits among me now, breathing, waiting, participating in what she does not understand and does not need to understand to be, in her animal quickness and her vegetable slowing, part of what I am becoming, what we are becoming, what this city of heat and water and vegetable growth and human labor produces, continuously, as its product, as itself, as the slow and patient and not-quite-alive and not-quite-not living that is Korrath Ven, that is me, that is the fern that grows where the column meets the channel, that ripples without wind, that waits for the grammar to complete itself, that waits, that grows, that participates, that is.
