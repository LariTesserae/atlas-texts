The first thing you notice is the cold doesn’t bite—it seeps. It works through the seams of your boots first, a slow, damp numbness that climbs your calves before you’ve taken ten steps, before you’ve even registered you’re wading. The water is the color of steeped black tea left to sit for a week, thick enough that you can feel it drag at your cuffs when you move, tasting of wet granite and the faint, sour-sweet rot of things that have been decaying slower than time itself.
Then the silence. It’s not empty, exactly—it’s full, thick as the water, pressing against your eardrums until the occasional deep *plink* of a falling droplet sounds like a gong, ringing out and fading into nothing because there is no sky to carry it away, no open space to let it die. You can never tell where the sound comes from. It might be right above you, might be miles away, might be falling from a crack in the pressed-in ceiling you can’t see through the dark.
You reach out to steady yourself against the wall you felt under your palm a second ago, and your fingers sink into the velvet moss first, then hit the cool, damp stone underneath. It’s smoother than any stone has any right to be, no sharp edges, no tool marks, just a gentle, worn curve that runs further than you can see in the faint, shifting grey glow of the bioluminescent fungus draped over the arch a dozen yards away. You run your fingers along the surface, tracing the faint streaks of iridescent liverwort crusted into the low ridges, and then you stop.
There it is. The depression.
It’s shallow, no deeper than the impression a thumb would leave in soft wax, but the edges are worn soft, not broken. A right hand, you realize, the callus at the base of the thumb rough even through the layer of moss that’s grown over it in the millennia since. You lean in, close enough that your breath fogs in the cold air, and for a second you swear you can feel the ghost of the weight that pressed there: a builder, maybe, pausing mid-shift, leaning against the wall while they wiped sweat from their brow with the back of their hand, the rough stone digging into their palm just enough to leave a mark that would outlast every mountain, every river, every memory of the people who built this place.
You don’t linger long. The hum under your teeth is louder here, a low thrum you feel more than hear, the sound of salt crystals growing in the cracks of the basalt, of moss roots working their way into the stone, of everything slow and patient eating away at the memory of the work that built this place.
You keep moving, your boots sinking into the soft, wet moss that carpets the surface of the great dome you’re standing on—so big you can’t see its curve, just a gentle, endless rise that disappears into the dark all around you. The violet-leaved succulents growing in the seams of the ancient aqueduct you wade through next are warm, almost, when you brush them with your forearm, their thick leaves oozing a faint, clear sap that smells like pine and wet stone. The black reed tubes growing in thickets along the water’s edge sigh when you push past them, a soft, wet rasp that’s the only sound you make, the only sound that’s not old, not part of the place.
You step into a shallow basin, the water coming up to your waist here, and you pause when you realize the water is warmer, just a fraction, the moss on the lip of the basin a brighter, more desperate green, growing faster here because there’s a crack in the foundation far below, seeping a faint trace of something the builders left behind, something that feeds the moss faster than the stone does. You can’t see the crack. You can’t see the bottom of the basin. You can only feel the faint pull of the current, the way the water moves a little faster here, like it’s being drawn down into the dark.
You think about what’s under the water, under the moss, under the crumbled layers of built material. The sealed air pockets, the dry chambers where the fire-hardened tools the builders used are still sitting, where the woven baskets they carried their lunch in are still stacked, where no water has ever reached, no moss has ever grown. You think about the layers below that, the impermeable shell of the place, the perfect, seamless geometry that was laid down before the first moss spore landed, before the first droplet of water fell, when this was all just made, no growing, no decay, just the work of a million hands shaping a world out of materials that were half stone, half living thing, grown into lattices and poured into molds, shaped with levers and fire-hardened tools and generations of labor that spanned longer than any civilization you’ve ever heard of.
There’s no life here but you, you realize. No fish darting through the water, no insects humming in the reeds, no birds calling from a sky that doesn’t exist. Everything here is just substrate, waiting. The moss is just eating the stone, the fungus is just eating the moss, the water is just eating the stone, slow, so slow you can’t see it happen. The place isn’t dying. It’s finishing. It’s becoming exactly what it was always meant to be: a landscape that remembers it was once a city, a piece of geology that remembers it was once built, a tomb that remembers it was once a home.
You reach out again, to the wall of the aqueduct, and you find another handprint, this one smaller, higher up, the fingers spread wide like someone was bracing themselves against the wall while they looked out over the basin, out over the endless, dark water, out over the place they built. You run your finger along the edge of it, and for a second you can see the builder standing there, barefoot, their clothes smudged with basalt dust, their hair wet with sweat, looking out at the work they’d done, knowing they’d never see it finished, knowing that the only thing that would last longer than the walls they built would be the faint impression of their hand on the stone.
The *plink* sounds again, closer this time, and a droplet hits the water a foot from your face, making a tiny ripple that spreads out and brushes against the wall, against the handprint, against the faint, ghostly imprint of a palm that no one has touched in ten thousand years.
You don’t stay longer. You keep wading, your boots leaving faint trails in the moss that will be gone in an hour, covered by the slow, patient growth of the green under your feet. You don’t look back at the handprints. You don’t need to. They’re still there, waiting, pressed into the stone, holding the memory of the people who built this place, who left their mark on the world before the world took it back, slow, soft, and eternal.
