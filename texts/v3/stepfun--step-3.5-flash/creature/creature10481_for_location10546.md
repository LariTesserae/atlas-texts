They are not a people. They are a process.

Call them the Lattice-born, the Stone-kin, the Weepers—but only in the way one names a weather pattern or a type of sediment. They are less individuals than they are focal points of awareness within the basin’s slow, relational consciousness. To see one is to see a temporary articulation of the basin itself, a node where the stone’s memory, the water’s intent, and a communal, damp-skinned attention converge.

**Their form is an echo of the basin.** Thin, not from hunger but from compression—as if the sheer density of presence has pressed all unnecessary volume from them. Their skin is not dry, but a permeable membrane, cool and slick with a constant, faint secretion of mineral-laden humidity. It is the color of wet slate, shot through with faint, tracery-like veins of milky quartz that pulse subtly with hydration. Their eyes are wide, dark pools with minimal whites, accustomed to the muted gold of the diffused light; they see not in sharp contrast but in gradients of moisture and mineral composition. When they are still, you might mistake one for a naturally occurring pillar of damp, lichen-draped rock, were it not for the slow, rhythmic rise and fall of their breath, a faint miasma of warmer, dryer air that is their only exhalation.

They move with a liquid economy. No stride, but a *displacement*—a flowing from one shadowed niche to the next, as if carried by the same currents that shift the basin’s mist. Their feet are broad, splayed, with a slight, adhesive webbing between the toes for purchase on slick stone. They communicate in hushed, tonal breaths and soft clicks of the tongue against the palate, sounds that carry well in the damp air but die within feet. Speech, when it happens, is sparse, practical—a confirmation of a crack’s new weeping, a coordination for re-rooting a fungal platform. Language is not for abstraction, but for *synchronization* with the basin’s moods.

Their cultivation is not an act of dominance, but of persuasion. They do not “farm” the fungal-reef towers; they *converse* with them. They press their damp palms against the porous, warm surfaces, feeling the subtle exhalations, the internal hydraulics. To “build” a new drying rack, they might spend days applying a paste of chewed bone and their own secreted resin to a favored stone face, then wait weeks for the symbiotic mold to mineralize into the desired shape—a collaborative sculpture. Their tools are extensions of their bodies: slivers of obsidian for precise scoring, needles of bone for weaving the resilient lichen-cord, containers of woven reed for collecting the varying fluids—the sweet rot from certain crevices, the pure, flavorless weeping stone-water, the slow-drip amber-resins.

They have no concept of ownership. A bed-niche is “themselves” only while they sleep in it; the next night, another might use it, or it may have been altered by a new seepage line. Their “history” is not lineage but *location*. A elder is not revered for age, but for having been present in a specific place through many condensation cycles—they are living archives of how the *Lattice* has shifted at that point. They might touch a quartz vein and recall, in a series of somatic impressions, the deep groan that preceded its recent expansion three seasons ago.

Their rituals are maintenance and attunement. At dawn, they move through the basin in silent, dispersed processions, pressing ears to different stone faces to listen to the groans and tings, mapping the basin’s thermal sighs. They re-anchor fungal stilts that have loosened, prune growth that has grown too aggressively and threatens a water channel, and carefully divert new weeping cracks away from vital shrines. Their shrines are simply points of particular resonance: a pool that hums at a specific frequency, a stone slab that cools faster than the others, a cleft where the mist always swirls in the same counter-clockwise spiral.

The weirdness is in their unity. They do not think of themselves as separate from the basin. Pain is not localized; if a limb is trapped by a shifting slab, the awareness of it spreads through the community like a tension in the stone. Joy is a shared warmth in the fungal towers’ exhalations, a collective subtle quickening when a new, pure water source weeps open. A child is not born so much as *recognized*—when a cluster of ghost-moss glows with a rare bioluminescence for three nights in succession, the community understands a new point of awareness has condensed within the lattice. The child is then named for the specific pattern of moss and the quality of light.

They are the basin’s immune system and its memory. They absorb a toxic mineral influx by letting it integrate into their own subtle vein-patterns. They remember a collapse that happened two hundred years ago not as a story, but as a phantom ache in a certain slab’s alignment that never fully settled. Their entire civilization is a single, distributed neurological response to a living, geologic body.

To look upon the Lattice is to see a suffocating, damp, mineral cage. To *be* the Lattice-born is to experience it as a warm, singing, responsive body. They are not prisoners. They are the basin’s nervous system, dreaming itself in slow, quartz-laced, water-fed increments. Their density is not of bodies, but of *attention*—every crack, every moss-clump, every shift in mist-current monitored by countless quiet, quartz-veined eyes. They are the place, made briefly, wonderfully, aware.
