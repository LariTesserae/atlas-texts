The first thing you notice, if you stay long enough to stop noticing the mist and the hum, is that no one here is Separate. The Sinter-born do not live *on* the plateau; they are a slow, warm outgrowth of it, as much a part of its layered, breathing geology as the moss-carpets, the rusted pipelines, or the Lithos that lumbers somewhere in the far open mist.

They are tall, lean, and so pale they seem carved from the same dull, slick sintered stone as the tableland itself, their skin shot through with faint blue veins that glow faintly when they stand in the violet light of the stone-bleed fungi. Their hair is white, gray, or the pale washed-out violet of lichen, cropped close to their skulls to keep it from catching the damp. Along their jawlines, their cheeks, the tips of their fingers, sprout thin, soft sensory tendrils, fine as copper creeper roots, that twitch and curl to read mist density, air pressure shifts, the faint vibration of the stone’s expansion before a tectonic groan. Their eyes are milky, pupil-less, the color of wet slate, no use for harsh sun but able to pick out the faintest bioluminescent pulse in the darkest runoff sump. Most carry the faint, sharp tang of sulfur and overripe fruit in their skin, a residue of the ancient gases that leak from the rock’s pores—you only smell it if you press your face close, and by then you are already one of them, or you are already lost.

They do not build. They carve, and they tend. Their homes are hollowed directly into the rock face, smoothed with layers of cured moss-carpet to absorb the worst of the damp, their walls cut with shallow runnels that channel condensation into glazed clay urns mixed with ground stone dust. They live in the middle layers of the plateau-city, tucked between the oldest burrowed tenements (buried three levels down, where the geothermal heat is gentle enough to keep the cold damp from seeping into your bones) and the upper factory districts where the mist hangs thickest and the air reeks of processing stone. Older families claim the lower levels, closer to the plateau’s slow heartbeat, and the Path-Finders—those who trek the open, unbuilt stretches of the tableland to check stress fractures, harvest fungi, and leave offerings for the Lithos—hold the most respect, for they are the only ones who know how to read the mist well enough not to vanish into it.

Their lives are written into the plateau’s four hard rules, indistinguishable from the rules of the stone itself. They learn to tap sintered rock with hammers carved from fused seabed stone, listening for the hollow hollowness that means a stress fracture is about to split, before they patch it with moss poultices and clay mixed with rust-lander carapace dust. They follow the mist to find fresh water seeps, avoiding the slick moss patches that will send you sliding into a sagged canyon, and the dry, brittle stone that will crumble under your weight if the stone is contracting in the drier “day” light. They do not distinguish between the factories and the mountain: the ore-crushers, the pressure valves, the railways snaking into the plateau’s heart are as natural as the moss, as much a part of the sintering as the ancient seabed rock and the pipes fused to the cliff face. They work the factories, tending the runnels, unclogging the coolant lines, harvesting the trace minerals the stone yields, taking only what they need, no more—because they know the stone will take back what is taken greedily, swallowing tunnels, collapsing tenements, letting the sagged canyons widen until they eat a whole district whole.

The non-human inhabitants are not separate from them, either. The vast moss-carpets that stretch for miles across the open plateau are tended like crops: the Sinter-born prune them back from walkways, let them spread only where their roots can hold crumbling stone together, harvest their leathery fronds to cure into a chewy, nutrient-dense paste that is their staple food. The stone-bleeds—fungal growths that ooze from microfractures, pulsing soft violet at night—are their light sources, their medicine, their link to the dead: they press the soft flesh to their skin to draw out fever, they braid the dried fungal cords into ceremonial lanyards, and they believe the faint hum the stone-bleeds make is the quietest layer of the plateau’s layered silence. The copper-leaf creepers that scale factory walls, splitting rivets as they grow, are their cordage, their chisel handles, their anchors: they prune the creepers back when they threaten to crush pipelines, but never clear them entirely, for their roots hold the factory walls together against the stone’s constant, slow expansion.

The rare fauna is mostly small, quiet, adapted to the dark and the damp. In the sumps under the factories live colonies of blind, pale salamanders, their skin translucent enough to see the red pulse of their iron-rich blood, that feed on the mineral seepage staining the moss undersides rust-red. The Sinter-born leave them small offerings of cured moss paste, for the salamanders are the ones that keep the sumps from clogging with mineral scale that would flood the lower districts. They appear only at night, when the bioluminescence of the stone-bleeds is brightest, their tiny clicking calls the only sound in the sumps besides the drip of water.

The larger inhabitants are rarer, and mostly unseen. The six-legged rust-lander beetles, their carapaces the same dull gray as the sintered rock, trundle up factory walls at night to feed on the stone-bleed fungi, their shed carapace fragments collected by the Sinter-born for chisels and scraping tools, for the metal is too soft and rusts too fast in the damp. They do not harm people unless provoked, and the Sinter-born ward them off from their dwellings with smears of sulfur-rich rock dust, which the beetles detest. They say the swarms of beetles follow mineral blooms, so when a swarm moves across the plateau, the Path-Finders follow them to find new veins of extractable ore, new fresh water seeps hidden in the rock.

Then there are the Sighs, and the Lithos.

The Sighs are the human-shaped figures that condense in the mist in the lowest, oldest districts, made of water droplets and the compressed ancient gases that leak from the rock’s pores. They whisper in a language of grinding gears and dripping water, and the Sinter-born say they are the echoes of the first settlers, the engineers who dug the first tunnels into the plateau, who inhaled too much of the gas and were absorbed into the stone, into the mist. They are not ghosts, not exactly. They leave warnings, if you listen close: a whisper of “the runnel is cracked” will save you from a burst pipeline, a murmur of “the embankment is soft” will keep you from sliding into a canyon. The Sinter-born leave small libations of condensed water for them at the edges of the lower districts, never disturb the places where the Sighs are thickest, and sing the Old Song to them once a month in the central cavern, the low harmonic notes matching the plateau’s sub-audible Resonance, the vibration that hums in your teeth if you stay still long enough, that curdles in your gut with a bone-deep nostalgia for a mountain you never saw, a sharp, sunlit world that existed before the wind and the ice and the stone settled into its final, smooth form.

The Lithos is the thing you glimpse only out of the corner of your eye, a disturbance in the mist that moves so slow you cannot tell it is moving at all, a sound like grinding boulders that rolls across the plateau for minutes at a time, so low it rumbles in your chest more than your ears. It is huge, older than the plateau, a patchwork of ancient seabed rock, rusted factory machinery, fused pipes and old train tracks, covered in moss and copper creepers, its body so large it takes up half the open horizon when you see it. The Sinter-born say it is the plateau’s bone, the reason the stone breathes so slow, the thing that let the first settlers dig their tunnels without the whole tableland collapsing. They leave offerings for it on high outcroppings: polished memory stone, small sintered chisels, woven moss cords. They say if you respect the Lithos, it will not let the stone beneath your feet crumble; if you take more than you need, if you dig too deep, if you disturb the Sighs or the stone-bleeds, it will let the mist wrap around you until you walk in circles until you starve, or the stone opens up to swallow you whole.

There is no line between who lives here and what lives here. The rusted pipe stapled to the cliff face is a landform, the slag heap is a hill, the Sighs are ancestors, the Lithos is the mountain that decided to become a machine instead of staying sharp. The Sinter-born do not think of themselves as separate from any of it: when they die, they are laid on bare rock outcroppings, no burial, no marker, and the moss grows over them, the stone-bleeds ooze into their bones, and they become part of the plateau, part of the Old Song, part of the mist that sighs in the low districts. The weirdness is not a trick of the gas or the light. It is just the way things are, here: a world that has forgotten how to be a mountain, and decided instead to be a living, breathing thing of stone, steam, and memory, everything sintered together into a single, stubborn, gentle whole.
