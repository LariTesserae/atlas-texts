The first thing you know is the thrum. It is not a sound at first, not something your ears catch—it is a pressure in the marrow of your shins, a hum in the roots of your teeth, a vibration that buzzes in the lenses of your eyes before you even blink away the dark of the tunnel that led you here. It takes a full ten seconds for the sound to resolve, to layer over that bone-deep pulse: a tectonic, subsonic groan, steady as a clock’s tick, so unchanging you can’t tell if it’s loud or quiet, only that it is everywhere, seeping through the smooth, dark floor of compressed slag and vitrified waste under your boots, up through the soles of your feet, into your blood.

Your eyes adjust to the amber glow first, then the mist that curls around your ankles, warm and smelling of wet iron and burnt sugar, sweet and cloying from the engineered vegetation clinging to every surface. The central basin holds no water you recognize: the geothermal seep that pools at its center is the color of liquid mercury, thick and slow, warm enough to steam faintly when a stray droplet from the weeping bronze plates hits its surface. Those plates curve up along the basin’s edges, pitted and streaked with millennia of thermal cycling, their surfaces etched with faint, concentric rings like the growth lines of a tree—each ridge a year of heat and cold, expansion and contraction, scoured smooth by the constant, warm rain of mineral-heavy droplets that trace gleaming silver paths down their flanks into the mercury pool. The droplets do not ripple when they hit. Nothing ever ripples here. The surface of the seep is glass-still, the mist that rises from it hangs in the air unmoving except for the slow, lazy drift where a wisp of steam escapes from the fissures in the pipes that web the walls like metallic roots.

The walls are grown, not built: fused bricks of soot-blackened clay, shards of melted glass, and ridges of corrugated industrial metal, all bound together by a calcium-hardened biofilm that glows with a faint, sourceless pale green light, so faint you only notice it when you glance away from the amber sodium glow of the lamps strung along the vaulted ceiling, their fixtures crusted over with mineral deposits and dark, iridescent vines that climb toward the high, mist-shrouded roof. The vines do not sway. None of the plants do. The ferns that sprout from crevices in the wall have stems like armored cable, their fronds dusted with metallic pollen that glints like tiny ball bearings when a puff of steam rolls past, and the black, rubbery leaves of the ground-cover vines drink the condensing mist so efficiently you can watch the droplets vanish into their surfaces, no trace left behind. The lower walls are painted thick with cyanobacterial slime, electric blue and sulfur-yellow, scrubbing the heavy metal runoff from the weeping plates into the faint phosphorescence that bleeds into the biofilm’s green glow. Nothing grows here. Nothing rots. The slime has been exactly that thickness for ten thousand years, the leaves the exact same size, the pollen dusted on the fronds exactly the same as the day the last person who knew what this place was for walked out and sealed the door behind them.

Under the constant hiss of steam escaping the microfissures in the pipe-webs, the endless crystalline tinkling of water droplets hitting warm metal, and the deep, unending thrum of the machines below, life is sparse, quiet, and specialized. You hear the chrome silverfish before you see it, a tiny, high-pitched skitter almost lost under the chamber’s eternal symphony. It is the size of your thumb, carapace polished to a mirror shine that reflects the amber glow, legs articulated from tiny copper wires, blind, navigating only by the vibration of the thrum through the floor. It scuttles across a corroded control panel half-buried in the slime, its dials frozen at the same settings they’ve held for millennia, and vanishes into a crevice between the fused wall plates before you can blink, leaving no trace but the faint impression of its weight on the slime, which will fade back to smooth in ten thousand years, if it fades at all.

Ten minutes later, a blind, six-legged creature trundles past, so slow you can’t tell it is moving unless you stare at it for a full minute. Its shell is fused from old circuit boards, gold tracings faded to dull brown, its legs thick and rubbery like the fern stems, filtering nutrient-rich sludge from the edge of the basin. It leaves a faint, phosphorescent trail behind it that will never fade, because nothing fades here. You watch it move three inches in the time it takes you to breathe in, and you realize you will never see it again, not really—it will trundle off into the dark between the weeping plates, and the next time you spot it, it will be in a different spot, and you won’t be able to tell if you’re remembering wrong, if it’s a different creature, if it was ever there at all.

There is no wind here. No birds, no insects, no sound but the eternal, unchanging symphony of industry that no one remembers building for a purpose no one remembers. The sodium glow is perpetual twilight, blurring all shadows into soft, amber smudges, the air so thick with moisture your clothes are damp within minutes, the warmth stifling, like being wrapped in a blanket soaked in hot metal. You can yell, if you want, but the sound is swallowed instantly by the thrum, no echo, no bounce, as if the chamber is designed to erase all sound that isn’t its own.

The emptiness here is not of absence, but of function: this space was once filled with the great engines that now hum below the floor, and now there is nothing left to fill it but mist, and thrum, and the slow, unchanging pulse of a system that has forgotten what it’s supposed to do, but keeps doing it anyway. The mercury pool will never rise or fall. The steam will never stop hissing. The biofilm will never stop glowing. The thrum will never change pitch, never slow, never stop. A leaf that falls from a fern will land on the smooth slag floor and stay there forever, never rotting, never moving, until the biofilm creeps over it over centuries, until it is just a faint dark spot indistinguishable from the rest of the floor. The “tree ring” etchings on the bronze plates will grow one new ridge every year, a slow, silent record of time that no one will ever read.

You are standing in the mummified carcass of a god of industry, and its organs still beat. When you leave, the only proof you were ever here will be the faint, silver smudge of mercury on your palm, that will slowly fade, and the memory of the thrum in your bones, that will stay with you forever. The chamber will not notice you were gone.
