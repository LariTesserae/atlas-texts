Life in the Veiled Catacomb does not announce itself. It seeps, it hums, it takes root in the spaces between your thoughts before you even notice it is there.

The smallest lives are the first to greet you, clinging to the conductive sheen the condensation leaves on your skin. These are the veil-weavers: single-celled, photosynthetic bacteria that feed on the faint static charge of your waking thoughts, feeding it back as a soft, tingle on your skin when you are anxious, a faint golden glow on your arms when you daydream. When you wipe them off, they leave tracery of your half-formed ideas behind, which the root-rubber drinks in before the hour is out. They are in every droplet of drizzle, every wisp of spore cloud, the place’s most pervasive, quiet symbionts.

The spores themselves are not just genetic material, but half-formed memory. The pale cyan ones shed by the Spine’s leaf-panels carry the plant’s own deep, slow recollection: the crack of concrete giving way to its first root-tendrils, the taste of rust on its early leaves, the low thrum of the old factory’s machinery it absorbed as it grew. The amber ones from the spore-fans carry human memory: the whistle of a 7am shift, the laugh of a child hiding in a warehouse, the quiet grief of a worker laid off the day the factory closed. Breathe them in, and you will get a half-second flash of a stranger’s life before the Catacomb metabolizes the memory into nutrients for its own growth. Some spore clusters have floated here so long they have grown tiny, crude hearts that pulse in time with the Spine’s rhythm, holding a collective memory of a hundred people who loved the same person, or feared the same storm.

Drifting through the humid air above them are the Mist-Medusae: jellyfish-like aerial plankton no bigger than your thumbnail, their bells glowing so faint blue they are nearly invisible in the twilight. Their trailing threads are sticky with a biofilm that harvests both spores and stray thoughts. If one brushes your cheek, you will get a sudden, out-of-context flash of another person’s daydream: a trip to a sunlit beach, a recipe for apple pie, a fear of falling. They are the Catacomb’s pollinators, carrying both genetic material and narrative from one end of the grove to the other. They cluster thick around anyone who is feeling strong emotion: a crying visitor will have a dozen of them swirling around their head, their threads catching tears and carrying the memory of that grief out into the wider Catacomb. When they die, they dissolve into mist, their bioluminescence fading into the ambient glow, their harvest of thoughts and spores added to the Catacomb’s collective store.

To your left, glittering up a vertical leaf-wall, is the city of the Glass-Skitterers: communal insects with chitinous, lens-like bodies that focus the dim, sourceless light into pinpoints hot enough to melt tiny tunnels through the leaf-panels. Their cities are labyrinths of these tunnels, strung together with silk they spin from their spinnerets that hardens into clear, strong glass when exposed to ambient spores.
