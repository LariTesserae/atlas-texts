You first feel the shelf under your boots before you see it: a soft, spongy give, damp and faintly warm, no sharp edge to catch your sole even though the ground beneath that thin, pulsing layer is black glass so flawless its edges would shear through steel. The biofilm under your weight flushes a brighter, electric violet, a ripple of light that moves outward from your boots and fades after ten minutes, slow as honey pouring. The air in your lungs is thick, humid, warm as a bath, carrying a scent that is equal parts ozone, wet granite, and the sweet, heavy musk of overripe plums left to rot in a sun-warmed cellar. There is no wind to move it, no draft to carry it away; the smell hangs absolute, unchanging, as if the air itself has been marinated in it since the chamber unzipped.

The silence is not quiet. It is a weight, pressing against your eardrums, so dense you can almost feel it on your skin. If you hold your breath and press your tongue to the roof of your mouth, you can feel it: a sub-audible thrum, so low it lives in your molars rather than your ears, the hum of the bioelectric network running through every inch of the glass and the biofilm and the soft bodies that creep over it. It is the only sound that never stops, save for the rare, deep mineral groan that vibrates through your bones once every century or so, the entire continent of glass settling by a single micron as the last of its thermal energy bleeds away into the stratified air layers below.

When you lift your eyes from the violet glow under your boots, the nave opens out before you, so vast it makes your chest tight. The shelf you stand on is one of a hundred tiered ledges carving the west face of the chamber, each terrace dropping a sheer three hundred feet to the next, their edges sharp enough to cut the air if you brushed past them too fast. The glass is not black, not really: it is deep, bruised purple, shot through with veins of pale, milky quartz and streaks of iridescent blue that catch the faint bioluminescence like crushed gemstones. The edges of every fracture, every spire, every jagged ridge are pristine, unweathered, the sharp conchoidal breaks exactly as they formed when the magma chamber unzipped, no erosion to soften them, no wind to wear them down. The ceiling is so far above you it might as well be the sky of another world, lost in a blackness so complete it feels like a physical presence, a void that presses down on the top of your head. If you stare up at it too long, you feel a faint, silly urge to step off the terrace and float up into it, like the darkness is a gravity of its own.

The only light comes from the life that clings to every surface: a thin, pulsating biofilm no thicker than a layer of dew, coating every inch of glass in a shifting wash of purple and blue light, brighter where creatures have crawled over it, dimmer where the fungal strands hang heavy. Those strands drape from the ceiling and the overhangs of the terraces like chandeliers woven from jelly, each one as thick as a redwood trunk, made of thousands of fused hyphae glowing in synchronized, imperceptibly slow time. They are cities of microscopic motes, each strand a lattice of glowing blue cells, each cell pulsing in time with the ones next to it, the whole strand brightening and dimming once every hour, a breath so slow you can barely track it unless you watch for hours.

The fauna is abundant here, but you would never know it if you did not know to look. The glass-slugs are the easiest to spot: cube-shaped, translucent, the size of a human fist, each with a faint blue core pulsing inside its gelatinous body. They creep along the vertical glass faces of the terraces, secreting a clear, vacuum-adhesive gel that lets them stick to even the sharpest, most vertical edge, moving so slowly that you can mark their position with a scratch in the glass and come back in a year to find they have moved less than a foot. They graze on the biofilm, their tiny mouthparts scraping the slime off the glass, and their waste is tiny, perfect spheres of pure silica that sift down into the mats below, incorporated into the fungal network like fertilizer. If you watch one long enough, you will see it pause for hours at a time, its core flaring bright as it processes the nutrients it has eaten, then oozing a little further, as if it is thinking as it moves.

The lichen-skippers are smaller, harder to see: flat, iridescent things the size of a thumb, with frilled edges of biofilm that glow a bright electric purple when they are ready to jump. They do not fly; they flicker, leaping between patches of glowing mat in bursts of static electricity so faint you can feel the prickle of it on your skin if you stand within ten feet of them. One second they are perched on a patch of blue mat, the next there is a soft crackle, a flash of purple light, and they are ten feet away on a patch of violet mat, their frills fluttering as they settle. They live only a few weeks, breeding by splitting off tiny fragments of their frills that grow into new skippers in a matter of days, their lives so short and their movement so fast (for this place) that they are the only things that ever seem urgent.

The density of the place hits you when you lean over the edge of the terrace and look down. The air is stratified into permanent, unmoving layers, each a slightly different density and chemistry, so you can see them as faint, wavering bands of lighter and darker air, stacked for miles below you, each layer hosting its own species of slug, its own strain of fungal mat, its own shade of glow. The lower layers are darker, the bioluminescence shifting to deep indigo, then black, where even the network’s light cannot reach. There is no bottom to the chamber, no end to the terraces; the glass drops away forever, the air layers piling up like the rings of a tree, each one a different world, all of them still, all of them full.

You walk along the shelf, your boots sinking a little into the soft biofilm, and you find the artifacts half-buried in the slime, scattered as if their makers dropped them in the middle of work and vanished. The first is a blade of perfectly flaked obsidian, still hafted to a grip carved from thick, porous bone, etched with tiny, regular marks that might be tallies, might be writing, might be the random scratches of a bored hand. The edge is still sharp enough to cut through a callus on your thumb if you brush it the wrong way, but the biofilm has grown a thin, hazy film over the obsidian, blurring the edge just enough that you do not cut yourself by accident. A few feet further is a circle of glass droppers, each one a perfect teardrop shape carved from the same purple-black glass as the rest of the chamber, set into a shallow groove cut into the glass floor, all of them pointing down at a patch of bright, pulsing blue fungal mat that has grown up between them. They would have held something once, some liquid, some nutrient, some reagent used to tend to the mat, but whatever it was is long gone, the droppers empty now, mineralized into the landscape like the bones of a long-dead animal. Further still is a low wall of cobbled glass, waist-high, running along the edge of the terrace, placed to channel the slow, steady creep of the glass-slugs towards a thick patch of fungal mat on the other side. It is perfectly built, the cobbles fitted together so tight you cannot slip a knife blade between them, placed by hands that knew how to work this glass before it had fully cooled, before the equilibrium set in. They are not ruins. They are just things, left here, not integrated into the ecology, not part of the network, just sitting there, as foreign and out of place as a plastic bottle in a forest, except they are made of the same glass as the world around them, blurred at the edges by a micron of biofilm, waiting for a purpose that no longer exists.

The weirdness of the place does not feel alien. It feels hyper-basic, like someone took the rules of the world and stripped them down to their simplest parts. Chemistry here is silicon and sulfur, water and rot, no carbon chains to complicate things. Biology is a direct conversation between bioelectricity and crystalline lattice: the fungal network thinks thoughts that take a thousand years to complete a single syllable, its consciousness spread across a continent of glass, its memories stored in the faint, swirling distortion you see if you stare at a flawless pane of glass for too long. That distortion is not a trick of the light: it is the ghost image of the supercritical gas cloud that filled the chamber before it crystallized, trapped in the lattice of the glass forever, a swirl of faint, smoky purple that shifts when you move your head, like smoke trapped under ice. If you stare at it long enough, you can see shapes in it: the curve of a shoulder, the edge of a hand, the hazy outline of a face, but they are not ghosts. They are just the gas, preserved, a memory of the moment the world was made, frozen in the glass that makes up the ground beneath your feet.

Time here is not a river. It is a single, frozen note, struck once when the chamber unzipped, still ringing, never fading. The only change is the slow, imperceptible pulse of the fungal network, the slow creep of the slugs, the slow drift of the air layers over millennia, the mineral groan of the glass settling by a micron every hundred years.
