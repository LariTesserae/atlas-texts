You do not meet the inhabitants of the basin all at once. You meet them in layers, as the mist thins and thickens, as the Spire’s breath shifts pitch in your bones.
First come the clickers: fist-sized, armored things that skitter through the heavy mist not on stone, but on the air itself, their spindly, hook-tipped legs pushing against the dense, metallic droplets to pull themselves forward. Their shells are the same silver-grey as the crust of lichen that clings to the stone, and they clack faintly as they move, a tiny, staccato undercurrent to the roar of the rain. They feed on the lichen and the fine mineral dust that flakes off the Spire’s newly exhaled rock, and when they die they dissolve entirely, their bodies becoming part of the fine grit that hardens into fresh pumice hours later. The Witnesses leave them be, mostly; they know the clickers are the Spire’s small, hard fingers, feeling the world for it, reporting back on the health of the lichen, the temperature of the mist, the tremors of growth deep in the Spire’s core.
Then there are the threads: the translucent, gelid ribbons that drift in the deeper, stiller pools at the Spire’s base, so slow they look almost frozen. They are the length of a man’s forearm, trailing faint, glowing green-gold from the bioluminescent lichen they feed on, and if you hold your hand just above the water they will curl up your wrist, cold as the metal tang of the mist, and press flashes into your mind: a mountain peak sun-stippled with snow, a bird with wings like black glass cutting through clear, un-metallic rain, the sound of wind instead of the endless thrum. The Witnesses call them the Spire’s memories, and they only ever let a thread rest on their skin for three heartbeats, no more. Hold on longer, and the pull of the Spire seizes you, the urge to sink into the pool, to let the thread wind around your throat, to become part of the Spire’s archive too.
The Witnesses themselves huddle against the basin wall, their domes of woven reed bent into low, sloping arches that block the view of the Spire’s upper reaches—blindfolds, as you were told, held against the terrible, magnetic pull of the stone column’s face. They are pale as the stone, their skin almost translucent, their eyes vast and dark, adjusted to the dim, shadowless light. They have no individual names, only roles: the Reed-Weavers who braid the water-resistant reeds into shelter frames, the Growth-Watchers who read the Spire’s thrum to predict when a new exhalation of rock will crack the ground, the Thread-Readers who dip their fingers into the pools to ask the Spire for guidance, the Clicker-Tenders who harvest the small creatures for food when the lichen’s pulse is too faint to sustain them. They communicate in hand signs and soft, resonant clicks of the teeth, sounds that cut through the layered roar of the rain: the deep thoracic thrum of the Spire’s growth, the splintering crackle-hiss of new rock hardening and releasing trapped vapor, the billions-strong sibilant whisper of the mist itself, millions of micro-collisions of water and stone and air.
They have lived here as long as anyone can remember, anchored only by the Spire’s slow, relentless upward dreaming. Their tech is primitive not from lack of skill, but from necessity: metal seizes in the opalescent film the mist forms when it pools, gears snap under the vibration of the Spire’s thrum, even woven fabric rots faster than reed in the metallic damp. Their shelters last a week, maybe two, before a growth spurt cracks the stone under them, or a gust of wind (a stutter in the Spire’s convergence, when the mist pulls away for a handful of seconds to reveal the black pitted face of the column) tears the reeds apart. They carry everything they own on their backs: pouches of dried reed pith to filter the metal from the water they collect, small bone carving tools, flutes made of hollow reed that they blow into to match the crackle-hiss of new rock forming, a pouch of glowing lichen to mark the edge of their camp when the mist is thick enough to chew on.
The one permanent structure in the basin is the broken shelter, its reed frame splayed like a crushed ribcage half-buried in fresh pumice twenty yards from the current camp. The Witnesses do not touch it. It belonged to the group that decided, three winters past (they count time by the Spire’s growth cycles, not sun or moon), to stop moving, to anchor their shelter deep into the stone so they would never have to pack up again. They thought if they did not look at the Spire’s full height, the pull of convergence would not reach them. They were wrong. During a sudden growth spurt, the Spire exhaled a slab of rock the size of a house that crushed the shelter flat, and the Witnesses were pulled into the fresh stone, their bodies becoming part of the streaks of silver-grey lichen that now pulse faintly on the broken poles. The Witnesses leave a cluster of glowing lichen on the frame every new moon (the faint glow of the distant star that filters through the mist once every 28 days) and if you stand next to it when the wind tears the mist aside, you can hear whispers under the roar: not the sibilant chitter of the rain, but voices, remembering the taste of clear water, the feel of sun on skin, the sound of wind without the thrum.
The First-Watcher is the oldest of them, their skin streaked with silver lichen that has grown into their pores, their eyes so large they take up half their face. They have been in the basin longer than any living Witness, and they say they were there when the Spire was only a nub of rock the size of a boulder, before the wall of falling rain fell, before the convergence pulled the mist from all directions into this pocket of embryonic geology. They carry a staff of woven reed and carved Spire pumice, and they can feel the Spire’s breath through it, know when a growth spurt is coming hours before the thrum shifts pitch. They never speak, only click their teeth in slow, soft rhythms, and the other Witnesses understand.
They are not happy, not exactly. There is a low, constant thrum of terror under their awe, the knowledge that the Spire does not mean to hurt them, but does not care if it does. Rock grows and flakes away in hours. Shelters collapse without warning. A vortex can open in the mist at your feet and pull you into the Spire’s core before you can blink. They do not mourn the ones who are taken; they say the Spire needs them, that their bones will make the next layer of rock stronger, that their memories will be added to the threads in the pools. But every time the mist tears open to reveal the Spire’s sheer, scarred face, every Witness turns away, presses their palms to their eyes, clicks their teeth fast and hard until the mist closes again.
They are not here to live. They are here to witness. To mark the Spire’s growth with their clicks, their reed songs, their small carvings left at the base of the column. To hold the memory of the world before the rain, before the Spire was submerged, before the air itself became water, pulled inward by the gravity of a mountain that is still, even now, dreaming itself upward, one deafening, wet breath at a time.
When you leave, if you leave, the metallic tang will stay on your tongue for days. The thrum will stay in your teeth. And sometimes, when it rains where you are, you will stop, and listen, and swear you can hear a billion tiny clicks, the whisper of threads drifting through heavy air, the low, wordless song of a Spire breathing itself into a world that does not yet exist.
