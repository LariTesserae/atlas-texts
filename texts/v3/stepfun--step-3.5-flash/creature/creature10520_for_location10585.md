What lives here is not a collection of creatures, but a single, slow-breathing organism composed of mineral, muscle, and memory. The inhabitants are not *in* the Salt Lick; they are its symptoms, its extensions, its unfinished sentences.

**The Symbionts of the Great Pump:**  
Within the rhythmic, sighing piston of the central pump, life has taken root not as infestation, but as function. Colonies of electrogenic bacteria coat the cylinder walls, their metabolic pulses syncing with the salt-crystal growth that alternately jams and frees the piston. They are the pump’s nervous system. Worms threaded with conductive filaments tunnel through the silt in the sump, sensing pressure changes and relaying them in weak currents to the moss-wrapped control wires. The pump does not work *for* anyone. It works *because* of them. It is a heart that learned to beat from the rhythm of its own veins.

**The Salt-Goats:**  
They are not grazers so much as geological agents. Their molars are cartilage-backed plates of dentine, constantly worn and regrown by the abrasive salt-moss. As they chew, their saliva catalyzes the dissolution of certain trace minerals from the crust, which their gut bacteria then precipitate into tiny, heavy pellets—droppings that are little more than concentrated ore. Their path across the salt-flats is a trail of future artifacts. They are walking refineries, shaggy with a fleece that traps condensation and weeps brine. Their eyes are pale, adapted to the diffuse light, and they seem to perceive the world more through the vibration of their hooves on the hollow crust than through sight. They follow the slow, invisible gradients of salt and moisture, their migrations seeding new lichen colonies with their fleece and their waste.

**The Gear-Birds:**  
These are not mechanical birds. They are birds that grew *into* their tools. Their wing-joints are natural hinges of keratin and chitin, but the edges of their feathers are reinforced with shed, metallic-blue scales they harvest from old paint flakes. Their caliper-beaks are bone, honed to a precise gap by constant use. They do not *use* tools; they *are* a tool, a living specimen of the place’s logic. Their nests inside the boilers are not just shelters—they are foundries. The heat from residual geothermal vents within the brickwork softens the metal scales they collect, allowing them to be woven into the nest matrix. Their clatter is not the sound of machinery, but the sound of a species that evolved alongside the echo of industry, whose language is measured in the pitch of feather-on-rust.

**The Blindfish and the Web:**  
In the black brine canals, the blindfish are not solitary. They school in densities that make the water pulse with green light, their collective electrical field a single, shimmering entity. They are the nervous system of the water itself. And above, in the dark, the Condensation Spiders weave not just to catch water, but to map the exhalations of the world. Their conductive silk hums with each droplet’s impact, a rain of infinitesimal data. The web-patterns across a decaying pipe network form a living, analog sensor grid, detecting shifts in humidity, temperature, and mineral load—information that is tasted, not read, by the goats and sensed as pressure by the burrowing worms. The spiders themselves are translucent, their bellies full of captured light, and when they die, their silk remains, becoming part of the mineral lattice, a fossilized moment of flow.

**The Rust-Vine and the Salt-Moss:**  
The vegetation is the place’s own skin, constantly regenerating. The Rust-Vine does not merely parasitize metal; it *consumes the memory of the metal*. It draws down iron ions, reorganizing them into delicate, lace-like structures that replicate the grain of the original steel, but in an organic, ephemeral form. It is a mimic that forgets its model. The Salt-Moss, conversely, is a memory-keeper. It grows only on the oldest, most stable salt crusts, its grey tendrils weaving through crystal lattices, and in its tissues, it concentrates the microscopic debris of the place—a grain of coal slag, a fleck of glass, a bacterial spore—preserving the history of the surface in a living library. To eat the moss is to consume the Lick’s past.

**The Unseen:**  
And there are those that occupy the transitions. Fungal mats that thrive in the thin film where salt-saturated brine meets fresh condensation, metabolizing the tension. Swarms of iridescent bacteria on the water’s skin that, when disturbed by a goat’s drink, flare into brief, silent auroras—a collective startle response from a single-celled civilization. Micro-currents in the water, shaped by the buried ruins, that guide the blindfish in loops older than the buildings themselves.

This is the population: no chiefs, no societies, only processes. The Salt Lick is a single, sprawling metabolism. The goats are its gut, breaking down crust. The birds are its hands, pruning and shaping. The fish are its blood, circulating light and signal. The pump is its stubborn, dreaming heart. The spiders weave its consciousness—a web of sensory whispers. Everything is both worker and resource. Life does not *inhabit* the ruin; it *unwinds* it, very slowly, back into the basic grammar of stone and brine, translating steel into salt, heat into rhythm, memory into moss. They are not survivors. They are the rewrite.
