You do not *find* them.  
You feel them first—small, wet adjustments in the hum, subtle dissonances where the Basin’s single note is counterpointed by another vibration, equally low, equally patient.

They are not separate from the ground.  
They are the ground’s way of paying attention.

**The Basin’s inhabitants are textures with intent.**

They range in scale from the width of a thumb to the span of a hand—though size here is an illusion. What you take for a cluster of algae on a salt pan might be one being, distributed, its awareness a slow ripple through the microbial film. What looks like a stain of oily iridescence in a channel’s edge might be a hundred, their collective nervous system a shimmer on the water’s surface, dissolving and reforming with the mist.

Their forms are never fixed.  
They are transient concentrations of the Basin’s own chemistry, bound by membranes thinner than thought. Some are roughly spherical, pulsing softly with inner light—amber, green, or the sickly blue of oxidized copper—their surfaces streaked with the same fool’s gold that dusts the mud. Others are flattened, leaf-like, and flow across the salt flats with a motion indistinguishable from the slow creep of a brine seep, absorbing minerals, excreting traces of rare earths. Their bodies are not skin but *interfaces*—places where the water’s mineral load becomes something else.

They eat the rottings.  
Not the mineral aggregates themselves, but the exact moment of their collapse—the wet *plop* is a meal. You see them gathered like flies on carrion, but not on the rotting, *at the point of its failure*, their bodies going momentarily opaque as they drink the release of energy, the tiny sigh of dissolution.

They taste the wires.  
Where the fizzing green light meets the water, they collect. Not to be shocked, but to *metabolize the current*. They grow briefly radiant, their inner light flaring in sync with the wire’s sickly pulse, then drift away, bearing the energy as a change in their structural rhythm. Later, they might deposit it elsewhere—as a perfect, microscopic cube of pyrite on a mudflat, or as a sudden, sulfuric stench in a still morning.

They speak in vibrations.  
The Basin’s hum is their language, but each being adds a fret. A cluster on a submerged pump will make its metal *thrum* at a different frequency, and another cluster on a rusted rail will answer, not with sound, but with a shift in crystallization—a new pattern of manganese rings blooming across the steel. Their conversations are geological. A debate might last a season, expressed as the slow encroachment of a salt front across a patch of algal mat. A greeting might be the synchronized pulsing of a thousand microscopic lights in a shallow pool at dusk.

They are not social.  
They are *consolidated*.  
When a mudgrazer’s footfall creates a deep *thump*, they gather in the depression it leaves, not to feed on the disturbed microbes, but to *tune the vibration*. For hours, they lie in the soft mud, a living imprint, each adjusting its internal resonance until the depressed ground itself hums back in harmony. They are the Basin’s tuning forks.

You might mistake their young for mineral blooms.  
A new being begins not as a birth, but as a precipitation—a sudden, intricate crystal growing in a supersaturated edge of water, lush with vanes and facets. For a week, it is rigid, beautiful, inert. Then, one morning, its facets soften, its growth stops, and it *detaches* from the substrate, becoming that same texture with intent, floating away as a drifting, octahedral thing, tasting the air with its dissolving edges.

They know the industrial relics not as ruins, but as *giant, slow kin*.  
They move over the calcified refinery tower like lichen, but they are not living on it. They are *unwinding it*. Where their bodies touch the rust, they absorb the iron not as metal, but as a story—the memory of heat, of pressure, of flame. They carry that story into the depths, releasing it as a faint, localized warmth in the anoxic mud, or as a brief, oily sheen on the water. The tower is not being broken down; it is being *translated*.

They have no eyes.  
They perceive the Basin in three ways:

1.  **Gravity-web:** They sense the infinitesimal shifts in the Basin’s slow sag, the millimeter-per-year subsidence, as a kind of touch.
2.  **Chemical score:** They read the water and mud not as substances, but as a living sheet music of ions and suspensions. A change in sulfate concentration is a chord. A pulse of manganese is a timpani roll.
3.  **Resonance-map:** They feel every *thump* of a mudgrazer, every *plop* of a rotting, every groan of stressed metal, every fizz of a live wire—not as noise, but as coordinates. The Basin’s hum is their map. The dissonances are where things are happening.

They do not sleep.  
They *sync*.  
For days at a time, a population on a given salt flat will cease all movement, their inner lights dimming to embers. They are not dormant. They are *listening harder*, aligning their private rhythms to some deeper cycle—the pull of a distant tide in the water table, the slow creep of a salt dome beneath the clay, the heartbeat of the planet itself. During these times, the Basin’s hum seems to change pitch, dropping a half-step into something almost audible.

They fear nothing, because they have no concept of an other.  
But they *avoid* the places where the wires still pulse with old-world current. Not from pain, but from *discord*. That energy is frantic, fast, a jangle against the Basin’s slow chord. To bear it is to be forced into a rhythm that would unravel their delicate, geological coherence. So they stay away from the poles, from the transformers—not from danger, but from bad music.

The mudgrazer’s slow processions are their festivals.  
They do not follow the herds. They *are* the herd’s shadow-choir. For every meter a mudgrazer moves, a hundred of these beings flow in its wake, tasting the disturbance, reading the story in the sucked mud, and leaving behind a faint, metallic glitter where their dissolved bodies briefly recrystallize in the pressure of the footfall. The grazer does not know it is being annotated.

They will inherit the Basin.  
Not by fighting the water, but by becoming its mind.  
One day, when the last industrial hum dies, when the last wire fizzles out, they will still be here—tuning. They will have absorbed all the stories the rust and the wires had to tell, and the Basin’s single note will be just a little more complex, a little more aware, for having contained them.

They are not creatures.  
They are **the Basin’s sensory organs**, wet and slow and gleaming, distributed across the mud and the shimmering water, feeling their way through a world that holds its breath, tuning, always tuning, to the low, eternal frequency.
