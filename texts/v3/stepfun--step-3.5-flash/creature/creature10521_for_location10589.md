They are called the Followers of the Thin Path, though they have no name for themselves that translates. To name a thing is to give it a fixed shape; they believe the Basin and its people are only ever *becoming*.

They are not short nor tall, but of a lean, tensile build, forged by a life of constant, low-grade labor: carrying stone, weaving reed, watching water, tracking hooves. Their skin is the color of old bone or pale lichen, dusted with a permanent fine silt that never quite washes away. Their eyes are large, accustomed to the shadowless, polarized radiance; they see subtleties in the flat light we would miss—a shift in the sheen of wet gypsum, the faintest ripple in a pan’s false future.

Their movements are the Basin’s movements: deliberate, circular, conserving. There is no hurry here, only the pace of mist and stone. They walk with a slight, rolling gait, feet splayed on the polished quartzite, silent as the Glass-Frills. Their gestures are economical, hands often open, palms showing—a sign of non-threat, but also of receptivity to the air’s small sounds, theStone-Sighs felt in the marrow.

**They are people of the in-between.**

They live in the in-between of the pans—not where the water *is*, but where it *will be* or *was*. Their homes cluster on the gentle slopes above transient basins, positioned by generations of water-maps. A dwelling is not a fortress. Its walls are porous silt-reed, breathing with the dampness. Its door faces not the sunrise (there is none) but the direction of the last reliable mist-spray.

They are people of the in-between of the fauna migrations. One season, a clan’s elders will be the Echo-Bat readers, their children learning the silent click-language, their diet rich in bat-egg and lichen scraped from high caverns. A decade later, the bats will have folded away into the stone-memory, and that same clan will now be Pan-Hound trackers, their knowledge the location of hidden, deep-soak pans the hounds know. Your social role, your entire knowledge base, is not hereditary. It is *epidemic*—spreading through the clan as the animals spread. You follow the flow.

**They are masters of non-attachment, not from philosophy, but from survival.**

A child does not inherit a specific house. They inherit the *skill* to build one—to judge the tilting of a mesa face, to read the slow crystallization in the stone. Possession is a static sound in a world of quiet motion; it is considered a kind of deafness.

Their greatest art is the *Resonance Weaving*. Using the network of whisper-tubes—hollow, polished quartzite rods that carry vibration—small bands will sit in separate chambers and hum a single, evolving tone. The tubes carry the sound not as words, but as pure, physical wave. A change in pitch by one group ripples through the entire network, felt as a tremor in the stone. The composition is never written down. It lasts only as long as the hum, and its "meaning" is the collective, felt agreement of the moment: *the Dust-Striders are moving north, the eastern mesas are growing unstable, we must shift the kiln-site.*

**Their elders are not rulers, but seismographs.**

They are the ones who can lie on the tillite plain until the ghost-glacier pressure hums in their joints. They can taste the air and tell if the mist will be sour (bad for silt-reed) or sweet (good for glow-lichen). They remember the last time the Stone-Sighs came in a triple pulse—a sign, they say, of a deep fracture forming in the black plateau above. Their authority is not in command, but in *comparison*. They compare the current whisper of the bats to the last time the bats spoke this way. They compare the path of the Glass-Frills to the pattern of cracks in a drying pan. Their wisdom is a vast, internal database of correlations from a hundred unfolding cycles.

**They have no concept of "progress."**

To them, the Basin is perfect. Its constant change *is* its stasis. A wall deconstructed is not a loss; it is the wall completing its purpose and becoming something else. A pan that vanishes is not a tragedy; it is the pan returning to the stone’s thirst, its water now part of the humid air that will birth the next pan elsewhere. Their technology—the kilns, the reservoirs, the whisper-tubes—are not advancements. They are *agreements* with the current unfolding. A better kiln is not one that fires more boards, but one that uses less precious water in its clay-slurry, one that cools in harmony with the evening’s mist rhythm.

**Their sacred act is the Letting-Go.**

When a death comes, the body is wrapped in woven silt-reed and placed on a specially prepared *sag-pan*—a shallow depression dug in a place predicted to become water in the next cycle. As the mist-spray arrives, the water slowly dissolves the reed, loosens the silt, and the body’s minerals—bones, salts, the very memory of the flesh—are returned to the Basin’s circulatory system. There is no grave to mark, no permanent stone. The memory of the person lives only in the changed song, the altered migration pattern they influenced in life, the slight shift in consensus that now includes their absence.

**So, what lives there?**

A people without footprint. A civilization without cornerstone. A society that is more like a single, slow, communal breath—inhaling as the pans fill, exhaling as they dry, shifting position as the fauna tides turn.

They are the human equivalent of the transient pans: a brief, dark, reflecting surface on the face of the Basin, perfectly shaped by the stone beneath, holding the sky’s impossible light for a moment, and then, when the silt clogs or the stone sighs, letting it all go without a ripple, becoming just another part of the thirsty, remembering, unfolding earth. They do not live *in* the Sharded Basin. They live *as* the Sharded Basin—a temporary, thinking, hand-and-hope pattern in the ceaseless, quiet, creative work of letting be.
