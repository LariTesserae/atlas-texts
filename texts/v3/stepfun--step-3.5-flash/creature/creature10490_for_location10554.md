Elspeth wakes to the thrum in her molars first. It is 6:02 a.m. by the rusted clock bolted to her glass-brick wall, though the clock has been losing ten minutes a day since the last storm sheared half its cable bundle loose; time in the Basin is measured more by the drone’s pitch than by any ticking. The 47 Hz baseline hum of the geothermal taps and slurry pumps vibrates through the iron bedframe under her, through the bitumen-felt roof glazed to a toxic, shiny green by decades of acid drizzle, through the three feet of water flooding the lowest floor of her tier. She has had the tinnitus ring in her left ear since she was seven, when a steam valve blew half a mile away and popped her eardrum; she does not even notice it unless the drone drops for the daily Quiet Window, and then it roars loud enough to make her teeth ache.
Her boots are stuffed with glass-silt, ground volcanic paste she packed into the soles last winter when the mire turned so glutinous she lost a good pair of steel-toes to the slurry halfway to the southern canal. The silt hardens to a dull, conductive sheen when it dries, and keeps her from sinking to the knee in the quartz-and-peat mire that passes for soil here. She sews her drainage tenure permit into the lining of her coat, right next to the pocket where she keeps spare copper wire for the Sighing Cracks: the paper is worn so thin you can see the shadow of her ma’s signature on it, the same permit that has controlled sump-pump 7 on the southern canal for three generations. No one diggs deeper than knee-height without one, not unless they want to breach a pressurized aquifer or a buried waste vault full of century-old furnace slag.
She climbs down the gantry ladder to the third tier’s walkway, the iron grating slick with condensate dripping from the overhead steam pipes. The air tastes of copper sulphate and wet rust, thick enough to chew. The low stratus clouds sweat a constant warm drizzle that beads on the algae-filmed basalt pillars standing like cooled, melting candles across the canal, their surfaces glowing faint violet in the diffuse, wet-gauze light. The sun is just bleeding through the cloud deck, bleaching the rust-orange boulders and cement-grey rustcrete embankments to soft pastels. She nods to Marisol, who is checking her pipe-orchids growing out of a crack in the geothermal conduit that runs along the walkway: the orchids’ roots tap the coolant line running inside the pipe, their blooms glowing a faint, pulsing blue. Marisol’s coat is dotted with rust-moss, the iron-eating growth that turns boulders to porous red sponge, and when she waves, her greeting comes back three seconds later as a layered, whispering chorus from the Echo-Moss patch on her shoulder. Elspeth just nods back; shouting is wasted here, sound swallowed after three yards by the slurry and the drone.
Her sump-pump sits half-submerged in the canal, its iron housing pitted and re-welded so many times it looks like a crust of overlapping rust scales. She checks the sacrificial walls first: the hollow ceramic blocks lining the canal, filled with quicklime and charcoal, dissolved halfway down their height from neutralizing the acidic runoff seeping out of the mire. She marks the worn ones with chalk, makes a note to requisition new blocks from the maintenance crew next week. Next to the pump, the Sighing Crack sighs low and melodic as the barometric pressure drops with the coming dawn; she threads a strand of spare copper wire through the fissure, the same offering her ma and her da before her left, to keep the crack “content.” Locals say if you skip the offering, the crack will widen enough to swallow a gantry whole.
She checks the pump’s output, wipes the diesel sheen off the water level gauge, then kneels to inspect the rustcrete embankment for new holes. The six-legged scuttle-worms burrow through the slag aggregate, leaving pinprick holes that let slurry leak into the canal and raise the water level enough to scrape the bottom of the cargo barges. She finds three new ones, marks them with red chalk for the patching crew, and watches a blind, soft-bodied sludge-eel slip past in the water, its silver-grey body undulating slow. She does not reach for it; the eels are “aspects of the seepage,” the old taboos say, kill one and a bad spring will burst open somewhere upstream and wash out three tiers of housing. She has seen it happen once, when a drunk tourist from the lowlands speared an eel for fun: the next day a new hot spring burst through the main road, boiling three barges full of ore and taking out the west gantry. No one hunts here, not even the kids.
She spots a new bubble of warm water seeping out of the mire halfway between the pump and the main settlement, mineral-rich fluid steaming a little in the cool dawn air. She scrapes a little into a jar to test later, marks the spot with a stake: under the Law of Equipoise, new springs mean adjusting the sluice gates two miles downstream to keep the canal level steady, no fighting the seepage, just managing it. The Basin does not care if you build a pipe or a gantry; it will open a new spring to balance the scale, slow and grudging, always. You just have to adjust.
By dusk, the sodium-vapor afterglow from the high-tension grids humming at the basin’s edge licks the horizon pink and tarnished-silver. Elspeth is half-drenched from the warm drizzle, her coat smudged with rust and algae, when she stops by the Static Pool by the old wind-screw to toss a pebble in. The water sparks blue with St. Elmo’s fire for three full seconds, tiny lightning veins crawling across the surface before fizzling out. She crouches to scrape a little of the glittering salt residue that has crusted the pool’s edge into her pocket, to use for soldering later; it conducts better than any store-bought flux.
The communal bath is the converted boiler of a rusted locomotive half-sunk in the western canal, its boiler long ago gutted and lined with ceramic tile, its firebox stoked with scrap coal and slag. She pumps the hand whistle on its cab as she climbs in, and it emits a deep, waterlogged groan that rolls out over the mire, carrying for miles. A minute later, a second groan answers from the old freight engine half-sunk two canals over, then a third from the derelict passenger loco at the far edge of the settlement: the Basin’s Heartbeats, no one remembers when they started, they are just part of the sound now, part of the place.
Old Jeb is already in the bath, his left forearm covered in a patch of rust-moss he uses to seal pipe joints; it works better than any industrial sealant, he swears, because it grows into the cracks and eats the rust that would otherwise eat through the steel. “Barge master brought in new glass-brick from the old factory up the flexure,” he shouts over the drone, his words swallowed half as long as they leave his mouth. “Gonna patch the south gantry walkway, stop it swaying so bad when the taps kick on.”
The Quiet Window hits right as they climb out of the tub, the maintenance crews shutting down all machinery running at 47 Hz for an hour. The drone fades to a low murmur, so quiet Elspeth can hear the marsh reeds chiming in the damp wind, their silica-streaked stems clinking soft as glass. The violet algae on the basalt pillars across the canal pulses brighter in the dim, and for a minute, the whole place feels almost quiet, almost still. She sits on the gantry edge and dangles her feet over the canal, the water warm against her boots, and holds up the blue pipe-orchid bloom Marisol gave her. Its glow is faint, but steady, like the rest of the Basin.
No one talks about leaving, not really. The lowlands have sleek cities, clean air, no drone in your teeth, but Elspeth has heard the lowlanders complain about the silence, about how the ground never breathes under their feet, how nothing there changes slow enough to keep up with. Here, everything changes, but slow: the rust patterns bloom on the steel like rust-coloured ferns, the sacrificial walls dissolve a little every year, the Sighing Cracks sigh a new note when the pressure drops, the Heartbeats groan through the drizzle every evening. The place is not dying. It is stable in its slow corrosion, and they are part of its metabolism, just as much as the rust-moss and the sludge-eels and the drone of the taps.
She tucks the pipe-orchid into a crack in the gantry railing as she walks back to her tier, the drizzle warm on her face, the drone slowly swelling back to full pitch as the Quiet Window ends. Somewhere down the canal, a fog horn honks, low and long, and the reeds chime in response. She pumps her fist once on the railing, the glass-silt in her boots grounding her to the mire, and does not feel the need to look back.
