The first thing you feel is the thrum in your teeth, before you hear it: a low, sub-audible vibration hummed up through the soles of your boots from the glassy volcanic stone underfoot, sharp with the tang of ozone. The air is permanently cool, carrying a mist that seeps right through your clothes, damps your skin with something that tastes like metal and wet stone when you lick it off your wrist. The silt under your boots crunches like compacted ash, grey-white and damp in patches, stretching out to the bases of structures that rise like fused organ pipes and broken ziggurats, their surfaces shot through with rust-colored iron veins and lattices of tarnished copper that glow faintly under the flat, pearlescent light that filters through the permanent mist, no sharp shadows anywhere, nothing ever moving fast enough to cast one.

This is the City of Static Chitin, and it is alive. Not with the life you know—no cell division, no breath, no hunger, no death—but with a slow, looping, unblinking vitality that thrums through every vein of quartz and twist of copper in its shell. The hard, glassy fused stone of its towers and hillside factories is its chitin: the hard outer carapace of a thing that has been dreaming the same dream since the stone cooled, locked in a single eternal instant of not becoming.

The “creatures” that skitter and hum and drift across its surface are not residents, not fauna in any human sense. They are its idle thoughts: temporary, fragile patterns woven from the city’s own static metabolism, brief articulations of its frozen rules, no more separate from its body than a twitch of your finger is separate from you.

The first you will notice are the gear-wasps, distant at first, a swarm of interlocking brass gears the size of house cats hovering and clicking in precise, unison formation above the slow-clanking conveyor belt that snakes between the nearest towers. They do not eat, do not sleep, do not have any will of their own. They latch onto the belt’s moving stone slabs, their rotation syncing perfectly with the belt’s glacial pace for three hours, adding a tiny, almost imperceptible extra layer of motion to the city’s peristalsis, before they disassemble mid-air and fall into the silt as inert, cold brass scrap. They are not a colony; they are a single, brief thought of rotation, expressed through a hundred tiny brass bodies, and the next swarm will click into existence right where the last one fell, latch onto the exact same spot on the belt, rotate for exactly the same three hours, fall into the same scrap, forever.

Closer to you, at the edge of the nearest basin, the iridescent, mercury-thick pool is evaporating in the mist, leaving behind a crust of salt where salt-singers grow. They are intricate fractal lattices of crystalline mineral, rising from the silt as the pool dries, and as they reach their full height they vibrate, emitting a thin, pure harmonic whine that makes the quartz veins in the stone under your boots hum in sympathy. Each one hums a single note, holds it for twelve seconds, then crumbles into grey dust that blows back into the silt. They are the city’s half-remembered dreams, the quiet notes it hums to itself when it does not need to move, and the exact same crystal will grow in the exact same spot tomorrow, hum the exact same note, crumble at the exact same second, forever.

Above you, the so-called smokestacks of the hillside factories do not vent smoke. They emit slow, viscous clouds of ochre, violet, and sickly green gas that hang in the air like solidified breath before dissipating, and every few minutes, thin grey membranes unfurl from their rims: the chimney-bats, drifting silent on the thermal updrafts, casting no shadow, their forms made of cement-hardened fog. When they land on the rust-veined facade of the nearest ziggurat, they dissolve on contact, leaving behind a perfect, paper-thin layer of verdigris before they vanish entirely. They are the city’s slow, unfocused breaths, exhaling the excess static of its unchanging metabolism, the verdigris they leave is its skin shedding a layer it will never actually grow back, the same thin coat laid over the same tarnished copper forever.

Then the ground heaves under your boots, sudden and soft, and you stumble back as the silt in front of you rolls like liquid, a shifting mass of smooth black pebbles flowing up into a perfect three-foot pyramid, holding its shape for four heartbeats before collapsing flat into dormant, packed silt, no trace it ever moved. That is the gravel-herd: not a group of pebbles, but a single, brief thought of shape, the city shifting its weight, flexing a muscle it does not know it has, adjusting its stance in a way that will never change the way it stands. It will heave into the same pyramid in exactly seven hours, same number of pebbles, same shape, same four heartbeats of hold, forever.

There is no death here, because there was never birth. The gear-wasp that falls into the silt is not gone; it is waiting for the next cycle, when the same brass gears will click into existence in the same spot, latch onto the same slab, fall into the same scrap. The salt-singer that crumbles is not destroyed; its crystalline structure is already forming again in the same pool, same shape, same note. The gravel-herd’s pebbles are already aligning under the silt, waiting to heave again. There is no “self” to any of them, no singular identity: when a hundred gear-wasps swarm the belt, they are not a colony, they are a single complex thought of the city, expressed through brass; when salt-singers hum in a dozen pools across the plaza, they are not individual notes, they are a single slow chord the city sings to itself, each note a different pool, different crystal, different vibration.

The city’s organs are all around you, fossilized but still humming with their eternal purpose. The great bellows of tanned leather and braided wire hanging from the organ pipe towers pump in a slow, unvarying clack-hiss, its diaphragm breathing in and out no oxygen, no fuel, just the rhythm of its own dream. The meshing gears and fungal-looking pulleys visible through the vast arched windows of the hillside factories rise and fall in a cycle that has never stopped, never slowed, never needed power, because they are its muscles, flexing in their sleep. The conveyor belts carry unidentifiable nodules of ore in endless circles, not to be smelted or used, but because they are the city’s half-formed thoughts, bits of pattern that never quite coalesced, moving through its gut forever because it never finishes digesting its own dream.

You are not an observer here. You are a pattern, too, for the little while you stand in the plaza. Your breath fogs in the metallic air, hangs for ten seconds before dissipating, same as the salt-singers’ notes. The heat of your body warms the stone under your boots for 2.7 seconds before it equalizes, same as the chimney-bats’ verdigris. Your heartbeat slows to match the thrum of the planet’s core under the silt, same as the bellows’ rhythm. You are just another tiny, fleeting thought in the city’s endless chord, and when you leave, the pattern of your presence will dissolve back into the silt, and the city will never know you were there, any more than it knows the gear-wasps were there, any more than it knows it is dreaming.

It will keep humming the same note, flexing the same muscle, clicking the same gears, forever. This is not a dead city. This is a city caught in the single, perfect, unchanging instant of its own life, its hard chitin shell holding its dream in place, no beginning, no end, no change, only the endless, quiet, static hum of being exactly what it is, forever.
