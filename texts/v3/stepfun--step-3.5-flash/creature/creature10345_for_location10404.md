The thrum of a trillion growth-tips pushing against stone is never just the spiresedge, not once you learn to listen. Under it, layered like the rings of a fallen trunk, is the click of silkshell chitin scuttling over moss, the high, chattering shriek of glimmer-monkey troops raiding a fruit spire, the scrape of bone knives carving new steps into a pithy spiresedge sheath, the low, rattling call of a water tender warning a neighbor that a rain-front is moving in from the western marshes.
Those are the sounds of the Gullet-Walkers, the people who call this place home, and who are as much a part of its roaring, living engine as the spiresedge or the canopy cats.
They are built for the damp, for the dim green light, for the constant, soft sway of the bridges that tie the hamlet together. Their skin is the color of polished loam, streaked with indigo and purple dye pulled from the parasitic vines that sheathe the spires, the pigment worked into their skin so deeply it glows faintly when the cold fungal jars are lit at night. Their fingers are slightly webbed from decades of hauling water troughs and gripping wet vine ropes, and nearly every adult has a small, pale blue fungal patch on their fingertips from tending the compost beds, the soft growth as much a part of their hands as the calluses from pruning knives. The tattoos inked into their skin with vine and mushroom pigment are a map of their lives: a new shoot for a child born, a blooming flower for a fungal harvest festival, a cat’s paw print for a close call with a canopy cat, a fallen spire for a grandparent who passed. They glow faintly in the dark, so you can tell who is coming down a gallery before you see their face.
They wear clothes woven from spiresedge fiber, treated in sticky citrus resin so they shed rain and resist the constant mist, cut loose enough to move as they climb the living ladders that zigzag up the spires. Most wear a small pouch of dried glint beetle wings at their belts, to rattle when they walk through empty galleries, so they don’t startle a sleeping canopy cat prowling the high branches. Children wear necklaces of polished silkshell chitin and glowing fungal scraps, so their parents can spot them in the mist, and most grow up with a small silkshell colony living in the sheared leaf-sheath that serves as their bedroom window, the obsidian-backed creatures skittering over the resin to filter mold and microbe scum without any effort.
Every part of the Gullet’s fauna is woven into the Walkers’ daily lives, not as separate creatures, but as neighbors bound by the same unspoken rules of the ecosystem. Glimmer-monkey troops rule the canopy, their fur shifting from silver to emerald to deep purple with the humidity, their constant chattering the soundtrack of the upper galleries. They raid fruit spires when the season is right, and the Walkers never chase them far—instead, they leave out bowls of overripe, fermented fruit for the monkeys to find, a tithe to keep the troops from smashing their stored food caches. Mud-rollers, the amoebic blobs that engulf detritus, are welcomed outside every leaf door: families leave small piles of vegetable scrap for them to eat, and the Walkers have learned to read their slow, gliding trails—if a mud-roller path winds toward the lower galleries, it means a water leak is coming, and they can patch it before the floor turns to muck.
The larger fauna are less servants, more partners and threats, bound to the Gullet by the same balance the Walkers obey. Small, fuzzy spore pigs root through the lower galleries for fallen fruit and fungus, their snuffling as common a sound as the drip of water, kept in woven-vine arbors and butchered only when their numbers get too high for the fungal beds to support. Canopy cats, sleek, four-eyed hunters that move like smoke through the branches, are both protectors and danger. They keep spore rat numbers down, and will leave half-eaten kills on the porches of families that leave them regular offerings of sweet fruit wine. But they will take a wandering child who forgets to rattle their seed-pod warning, and no one blames the cat—only the parent who failed to teach their little one the rules.
Even the spiresedge itself is a member of the community, not just a building material. Each towering, bamboo-like spire is named, has a history, is tended by the same family for generations. The pruners of that family speak to the spire when they carve new galleries into its sheaths, leave small offerings of fruit juice at its base, never cut into its living pith core. They know its rhythms: which side puts out new, sweet leaves in the wet season, which hollow holds water for months without spoiling, which branch will hold a woven vine bridge if tensioned just right. When a spire grows too old and falls, the family doesn’t mourn—they harvest its hard, dried wood for tools and building materials, and plant new shoots from its seed clusters in its place, so the spire is still part of their home even when it lies broken on the mossy floor.
Their lives are tied to the Gullet’s constant, turbulent rhythm, no matter what they do. Water tenders patrol the log aqueducts, their webbed fingers feeling for leaks in the damp wood, leading colonies of silkshells behind them to clean the channels as they walk. Pruners climb to the highest branches, their bone knives glinting, carving new galleries and pruning away overgrowth before it crushes a home. Fungal growers tend the steaming compost beds, their fungal-patched fingers brushing the shelf-like browns and gelatinous blues to check for blight, their dreams sometimes filled with the fungi’s slow, deep memories of storms past and harvests to come.
When a new growth spurts through a gallery wall overnight, the whole neighborhood comes together with their knives and resin to prune it into a new window, or a shelf, or a set of steps, singing as they work so the new growth holds the song of the community in its fibers. When a rainstorm floods the lower levels, everyone carries their stored food and their spore piglets up to the higher galleries, shares their fermented fruit and dried mushroom with families who lost their homes, works together to weave new drainage channels into the spiresedge to divert future flood water. Once a year, when the cold blue fungi bloom all at once under the new moon, the whole hamlet climbs to the highest galleries to watch the whole Gullet glow pale green and blue, glint beetles flying in clouds above like living stars, glimmer monkeys silent in the branches, even the canopy cats sitting on high limbs to watch, their four eyes reflecting the light.
It is not a gentle life. A canopy cat takes a toddler every few years, a rainstorm washes away a whole section of gallery every decade, a blight can kill a spire in a season. But no one sees this as cruelty, or misfortune. It is just the Gullet breathing, growing, chewing, roaring. To be a Gullet-Walker is to be a moving part in that roar: to prune, to tend, to signal, to share, to return your body to the spires and the fungi and the mud-rollers when you die, so you can become part of the next thrum of growth-tips pushing against stone. You are never alone here. The silkshells skitter over your feet, the glint beetles hum around your head, the spiresedge sighs under your hands, the glimmer monkeys watch from the branches, the canopy cat’s four eyes glint from the dark, and the whole, wet, noisy, shamelessly alive place breathes with you.
