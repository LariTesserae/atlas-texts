You step onto the basin and the first thing you notice is that there is no give, no scatter of loose rock under your boots: the entire landscape is a single fused sheet of porous, frothy reticulite, one endless obsidian skin stretched over the high hollow of the dormant range. There are no discrete boulders, no patches of bare ground between features—every square inch is the same honeycombed, vesicular glass, solid and unbroken at your scale, even as it is cavernous and cracked at the scale of a water droplet. Your boots thud soft and dull against the fused froth, no clink, no crunch, because the erosion has already stripped away every loose shard, every unstable edge; what remains is the permanent, equilibrium core of the rock, as eroded as it will ever be, now and a thousand years from now.

The first thing that hits you is the sound. It is not noise made by living things, but the roar of the water itself, amplified a thousandfold by the packed, hollow glass: billions of condensation droplets forming and falling through the pore matrix every minute, a deafening static hiss like a waterfall sealed behind a foot of fused black stone. It is not a sound you hear so much as feel: it vibrates in your teeth, thrums in the bones of your sternum, hums through the soles of your boots into the rock itself. Layered under the hiss are the high, thin pings of micro-fractures stretching in the sun, the sharp squeal of glass expanding and contracting in its unchanging daily thermal cycle, and the low, steady drone of wind howling through the far-off caves and tunnels, the rock itself acting as organ pipes, their tones so low they rattle the water beads trapped in the glass capillaries under your fingertips. There is no silence here, no gap between presences: the acoustic field is so saturated your voice would be swallowed whole, and you would not be able to hear it even if you shouted.

You lift your head to the thin, sharp air, and the light hits you like a physical weight. The sun is harsh and direct, the atmosphere so clear at this elevation there is no haze to soften it, and the dark frothy glass scatters it a million different ways: every pit, every vesicle, every tiny fractured facet of the obsidian skin catches the light, so the entire basin shimmers with a complex, shifting glitter, like a dark sea dusted with crushed diamond. The spires—frozen bubbles, old vent pipes, hundreds of feet tall—cast razor-sharp shadows that never shift, never lengthen or shorten, because the angle of the sun is fixed by the mountain walls that ring the basin. The low-lying forschammerite basins, where the glass is most vesicular, are hazed with percolating mist that glows pale gold in the sun, while the cave mouths burn violet with condensed vapor, their interiors so dark you cannot see a foot past their thresholds, even as the cool, damp air seeps out to brush your cheeks. The pattern of light and shadow is eternal: frost forms on the cold glass faces at dawn in the exact same delicate, fern-like patterns every single day, and melts all at once an hour after sunrise, the hiss spiking loud for ten minutes as trillions of droplets weep from the pores into the matrix. Nothing changes. The light never wavers. The frost always melts in the same places. The rock is frozen, but its rhythm is unchanging.

The wetness is everywhere and nowhere. The glass is hygroscopic, breathing moisture straight from the thin, dry air, and ancient meteoric aquifers trapped during the last ice age seep along hairline fractures, fed only by that condensation cycle, no gravity-driven flow, no rivers, no lakes. If you run your hand over the black surface, it is cool, slightly damp, a faint sticky residue of silica and trace volcanic salts on your skin. If you hold very still you can see the strings of water beads trapped in the capillaries under the surface, trembling with every low drone from the organ pipes, some so small they are just glints, some large enough to see the curve of their surface. The air is dry and sharp in your lungs, save for the mist-hazed low basins, where it is thick and clammy, fine droplets clinging to your eyelashes and the fabric of your clothes. The water is too mineralized, too acidic, too saturated with silica for any living thing to survive in it: this is a pre-life, post-life world, chemistry that never crossed the threshold into biology, sterile and sealed.

There is no vegetation, no lichen, no trace of organic carbon anywhere. The only things that approach the look of life are the rare, faint weirdnesses that flicker at the edges of the rock’s perfect equilibrium. In the deepest, darkest fissures, where the seepage is constant and no sunlight reaches, silica-colloid vortices form transient, branching filaments, pulsing with faint blue-green chemiluminescence from redox reactions in trapped iron sulfides. They grow a fraction of an inch, then dissolve back into the mineral-rich water, repeating their cycle in no discernible pattern, flickering in the dark like the rock’s half-remembered dreams. They are not alive, but for a second when you stare into a fissure, you could swear they are breathing.

On the surfaces closest to the organ pipe caves, where the drone is loudest, crystalline growths of salt and sulfate have formed into resonant, ridged shapes—tiny fans, cones, ridged plates—that vibrate in perfect sympathy with the low tone. They feed on acoustic energy, warm to the touch when the drone hits, humming softly against your fingertip if you lean close. They are mineral organs, grown from the rock itself, shaped by the sound, no more alive than the glass they grow from, but they respond, they react, they carry the song of the cave out into the wider basin.

In the mist of the low basins, aerosolized colloids of clay and metal oxide drift in tiny, rotating lenses, catching the sunlight and focusing it into moving, pinprick stars that hang in the air for a few seconds before dispersing. They are barely weather, barely solid, but for a split second they glow bright enough to cast faint, wavering shadows on the black glass under your feet, before they pop into nothing, leaving no trace.

The whole basin is a single, vast mineral organ. It sweats with condensation, sings with the roar of water through its pores, shimmers with light in its endless, unchanging pattern. There is no room between presences because every pore is active, every surface vibrating, every crack wet. It is not constructed, but it is not untouched: it is in a state of perfect, intricate, eternal occupation, a closed loop of water cycling through glass, light and sound transduced into heat and vibration, dissipated back into the hiss that never fades. It is cool, bright, wet, loud, packed, open, and utterly alone, the only thing in its world that exists, the only thing that has ever existed, the only thing that will ever exist, save for the faint, flickering siphon filaments that almost change, almost live, before they dissolve back into the dark.
