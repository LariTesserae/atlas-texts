They call themselves the Sigh-Seed. They do not live *in* the caldera so much as they *listen* to it. They are not the masters of this place, but its most attentive tenants.

Look for them not in the wide, empty grey plains of stone and lichen, but at the seams—where the Weepers weep strongest, where the hot-spring steam condenses into a perpetual mist, where the peat gives way to a solid hummock. They are small, and they are many. Their average height is a hand’s breadth below a tall human, but they are broader in the shoulder and hip, built low and solid as the basalt boulders they navigate. Their skin is not skin in the common sense, but a complex, symbiotic epidermis: a living tapestry of non-toxic lichens, fine mosses, and translucent water-retaining filaments that shift color subtly with humidity and temperature. In the dim green light, they can seem like part of the wall themselves—a moss-bearded stone that shifts its weight.

Their faces are the most striking feature. They have no external ears; the sides of their heads are smooth, damp planes, dotted with minute, sensitive pits that register vibration and pressure differences in the saturated air. Their eyes are large, dark, and oval, with a nictitating membrane that sweeps away constant mist. They do not blink so much as *clear*. Their mouths are small, pursed things, for they speak little in the way of airborne sound. Their true speech is subsonic, felt in the chest and the roots of the trees—a series of rumbles, clicks, and hums that travel through the damp earth and stone better than through the thick, sound-muffling air. To an outsider, their "talk" is the deep basso of the place itself, a layer beneath the water-thrum.

Their culture is one of **deep patience and precise integration**. A settlement is not a construction but a *cultivation*. They spend decades, sometimes centuries, shaping a single micro-environment. They guide the slow, mineral-rich flow of a Weeper not by digging channels, but by painstakingly arranging porous stones and encouraging specific root-forms to grow in its path, gradually redirecting the ooze to nourish a patch of bioluminescent fungi they tend for light, or a certain spongy lichen they weave into their shelter-walls. Their dwellings are the camouflaged domes described, but inside, the air is warmer, regulated by a deliberately maintained draft from a nearby vent. The interior walls are alive with carefully farmed mold-fungi that regulate humidity and metabolize airborne spores.

Their technology is **biological and symbiotic**. They do not make sharp obsidian blades for hunting; they *harvest* the obsidian-fragments that naturally erode from the cliffs, then place them in the hottest, most mineral-rich vent pools for years. The constant thermal cycling and mineral saturation slowly frosts the glass into a terrifyingly sharp, serrated edge they call "teeth of the sleeping mountain." They use these for precise ritual carving, not warfare. Their primary tools are living: long, flexible vines trained over years to grow to a specific length and strength; a species of giant, carnivorous fungus whose sticky tentacles they "milk" of captured insects for protein and glue; beetles they encourage to burrow in precise patterns to aerate their food-storage root cellars.

They are **mycophagous and hydro-sensitive**. Their diet is a complex calendar of fungal fruiting cycles, amphibian egg-spawn harvested by moonlight from bromeliad pools, and the mineral-rich, gelatinous "knots" that form on the roots of certain mosses near the vents. They drink not from open water, but from the cleanest, coldest Weeps, testing them with their tongue-flicks for dissolved mineral signatures. Dehydration is their only true existential fear—a drying of the world. They see the slow, inward erosion of the caldera not as a threat, but as the mountain’s ultimate, gentle sigh. They believe that when the basin finally fills completely with sediment and life, becoming a bog that drowns even the vents, they will too, gracefully, be re-absorbed—their bodies becoming the richest peat, their listening becoming the wind in the new reeds.

They do not have a concept of "ownership." They have relationships. A particular hot spring is "grandmother-vent," slow and nurturing. A cluster of razor-sharp obsidian shards is "the old sentinel’s teeth," to be appeased with offerings of clean water. The giant shelf-fungi are "the shelves of memory," and their rings are read like histories. Their "art" is imperceptible to the casual eye: a slight twisting of a vine’s growth to create a harmonic hum when the wind blows; a pattern of different mosses on a cairn that only reveals itself after a week of rain; a carefully buried stone that, over a century of mineral staining, will bleed a slow, ochre line down a rock face—a message read by their grandchildren.

They are the whisper in the water-thrum, the shadow that blinks back at you from a mossy wall. They are not creatures of this damp world so much as its **conscience**—a small, stubborn, listening awareness that has learned to speak the language of stone and spore, and in doing so, has become another, quiet layer in the ceaseless, chittering conversation of the Caldera of Perpetual Damp. They are not the inhabitants. They are the **echo** that has learned to answer.
