The vibration starts in the soles of your boots before you feel it anywhere else: a low, steady thrum seeping up through the grated steel of the transit platform, settling in your molars, humming in the fillings you got on the surface before you signed on for the Grid. You grip the railing, and the dark steel shudders under your palm, synced to the distant pumped-storage turbine that’s the heartbeat of this whole basin.

The air (if you can call the thin, ozonic layer above the water that) is cool, sharp with the tang of overworked electrical systems, and smells like nothing else you’ve ever known: no salt, no rot, no green growing things, just treated, recycled air and the faint metallic bite of ionized water. You lean over the edge, and the water is so clear you can see the geometric shadow of the next grid node three klicks out: a sharp, dark rectangle stamped on the basalt plain below, its edges so true they don’t blur even at that distance. Between you and that shadow, there is nothing. No drifting seaweed, no random fish, no sunken debris, no dust motes catching the light. Just uniform, shadowless blue, fading to a green-gray haze at the edge of your vision, where the scale of the place stops being something you can see and starts being something imposed on you. This is the empty density the engineers brag about: 60% of the basin’s volume is just water, circulating, waiting, designed to prevent congestion, to keep the whole system breathing. Most of this world is void, and that is not a flaw—it is a feature.

Below, the ocean floor is no floor at all. The natural substrate was scraped flat and paved over with interlocking basalt-concrete slabs, each the size of a city block, laid with a precision that has survived only a century of engineered currents, no gaps, no weeds sprouting in the cracks, no sand, no muck, no sign anything natural ever existed here. Deep, straight trenches score the plain: transit corridors for autonomous sleds, racks for the pipelines that carry water and ore and algae slurry between nodes. From these trenches rise the nodes themselves: factory complexes, processing spires, habitation monoliths, all cast from the same dark, non-corroding alloy, their edges sharp enough to cut, their surfaces bare and smooth, unweathered, unmarked by anything but the occasional scrape from a passing maintenance drone.

The nearest node, half a klick out, is a dewatering plant. You watch a plume of brown, sediment-laden water gulp into its grated mouth, and count the seconds: 92, 93, 94, and a clear, heated jet erupts from the stack on its roof, rising in a controlled thermal column that wobbles and twists before dissipating into the blue. Next to it, an algae silo complex hums so low you feel it in your chest, its external membranes rippling with the movement of thousands of tons of cultured, fast-growing spirulina— the only “vegetation” for hundreds of klicks, sparse, functional, entirely contained, its light levels timed to the 28-hour production cycle that rules every part of the Grid. Right now, it’s mid-cycle: the silo’s LEDs are at peak brightness, and you can see the faint green glow of the Spirulina through the translucent membrane, moving in slow, engineered waves.

The fauna here is managed, engineered abundance, no wildness left. Below the dewatering plant, a school of gene-tweaked bioluminescent herring moves in perfect synchronization, their blue-green lights blinking in unison as invisible current-makers (small, unmarked devices tucked into the trench walls) funnel them toward a feeding pen clamped to the node’s base.
