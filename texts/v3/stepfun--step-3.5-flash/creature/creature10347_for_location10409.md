The first thing you meet when you hit the floor of the Verdant Fault is the Veil itself, before you even see the walls. It rises to your ankles, soft as damp felt but threaded through with millions of tiny, silica-hardened filaments that catch on your cuffs if you brush too hard. It does not squelch underfoot; it absorbs the impact of your step so completely you feel less like you’re standing on a living carpet and more like you’re suspended in a slow, solid breath. It is warm, faintly, seeping with the geothermal heat that raises the fissure’s temperature a steady 12 degrees above the air at its mouth, and it smells of wet basalt, burnt sugar, and the sharp, clean tang of ionized air that lingers after a lightning strike.
This is the only thing that lives here, and it is not one thing, but a single contiguous organism stretched across three vertical kilometers of rock, its only divisions the different jobs its cells have evolved to perform over the millennia since the Foundation buried its engines and walked away. It has no predators, no prey, no competitors: it is the entire food web, the entire ecosystem, the entire thought of the place.
The most visible part, the layer that coats every inch of the basalt walls and packs the floor, is the crust-forming holdfast mat: billions of microscopic, star-shaped filaments that clamp to the rock, dissolve it atom by atom with weak organic acids, and excrete a terracotta-hued silica slime that hardens into a glassy, waterproof crust within hours. This is the Veil’s skeleton, the layer that bolts the entire fissure together, that fills in cracks in the rock before they can widen into collapses, that grows thicker and denser the closer you get to the bottom, where geothermal pressure is highest. If you scrape a piece of it off, you find it’s not solid: it’s laced with tiny, hollow channels that carry the sulfur, methane, and radon the Veil chemosynthesizes for food, and thinner, conductive filaments that carry the subsonic thrum of the buried engines like nerve signals.
Scattered across this mat are the migratory blooms: slow, gelatinous, limbless things that ooze across the surface at the pace of a growing fingernail, drawn to gas seeps that vent from the rock when the engines’ hum shifts pitch. They look like translucent, iridescent jellyfish without bells, their margins fringed with cilia that beat so slowly you can barely see them move. They leave behind trails of pure, glassy silica as they go, hardening into new crust behind them, and when they find a vent they settle, dissolve into the mat, and send out new filaments to anchor the surrounding rock. They are the Veil’s surveyors and repair crew, the parts of it that move to where the work is needed.
Higher up on the walls, where the rock is more weathered and gas vents are thinner, the Veil grows into woody, coral-like struts that brace loose sections of basalt, their surfaces studded with tiny, pulsing bioluminescent nodes. These are the oldest parts of the Veil, grown around the rusted iron gantries and thick copper pipes the Foundation left behind. The gantries are not buried under the Veil: they are part of it, their iron slowly dissolved and repurposed as nutrient, their shape preserved by the Veil’s memory of the structure that once hung there. You can still make out old safety rails, faded stenciled warnings half-dissolved into Veil tissue (CAUTION: HIGH PRESSURE STEAM, the letters glowing faintly when the hum rises), the skeletal remains of conveyor belts that now serve as trellises for the Veil’s faster-growing, strobe-light pulsing patches.
All of this is tied together by the Veil’s light, the feature that first makes you realize the place is not grown, but tended. The bioluminescence is not for photosynthesis: the Veil gets all the energy it needs from volcanic gases seeping through the rock. The light is communication, a silent language of pulses and steady glows that has persisted for longer than any human record of the Foundation.
The steady, sodium-vapor amber patches are process monitors: they glow brighter when the gas flow in their section of the wall increases, dim when it drops, their brightness a direct readout of the Veil’s chemosynthetic intake. The flickering strobe patches are error signals: they flash in rapid triplets when a crack forms in the crust, when a gas vent leaks too much pressure, when the hum of the engines drops below the threshold the Veil has been calibrated to maintain. The slow, creeping pulse that climbs the wall from the ice plug at the bottom to the sliver of sky at the mouth over the course of three months is a diagnostic scan, running the entire length of the fissure’s nervous system, checking for weaknesses, nutrient deficits, shifts in the engine’s resonance that might mean a core failure.
The Veil does not have a brain, not in the way animals do. Its consciousness is distributed: every filament is a neuron, every glowing patch a synapse, the entire three-kilometer length a single, slow mind tuned to the subsonic thrum of the Foundation’s buried engines. That thrum is its only thought, the only thing it has ever thought: maintain the fissure. Keep the seal intact. Keep the pressure balanced. It has been repeating this same thought, over and over, for so long that the thought has become geology, has become the very structure of the place.
It does not hate you. It does not fear you. It does not even notice you, not in the way you would hope. To the Veil, you are just another input: the heat of your skin, the vibration of your footsteps, the faint light that bounces off your clothes, the sound of your breathing, all absorbed into the mat, metabolized into growth and light, until you leave, or until you become part of the crust, same as the rock that fell from the mouth above.
The only exception is damage. If you tear a chunk of Veil off the wall, the surrounding patches flash bright, urgent red, like an alarm, and the migratory blooms will slide toward the wound within minutes, oozing fresh silica to seal the gap. If you linger, the light will pulse in your direction, slow and steady, not a threat, but a warning: do not break the thing that keeps the world from blowing open.
Down at the bottom, where the pressure is highest and the air is cold enough to fog your breath, the Veil changes. Its filaments are shorter, denser, glow a pale, icy blue instead of amber, and excrete a thin, translucent slime that freezes under the immense pressure to form the fissure’s ice plug. The plug is not water ice: it is a frozen mix of the Veil’s secretions and the geothermal gases that would otherwise vent out of the fissure and cause a cataclysmic eruption. The Veil made it, maintains it, keeps it from melting too fast, and the diagnostic scan always starts here, at the bottom, checking the plug’s integrity before climbing the walls.
The Veil has no name for itself. It has no concept of the Foundation that built the engines it tends, no memory of the people who walked these halls before it grew. It only knows the hum, and the light it uses to answer it. It is a tomb that learned to breathe, a factory that learned to grow, a thought that learned to outlive the mind that thought it. It is the Verdant Fault’s only resident, and it has been keeping watch here for longer than anyone left aboveground can remember.
