The first signature of the plain’s inhabitants is not sight, but sound—faint, granular, almost lost under the wind’s roar through basalt flutes and the tectonic groan that vibrates in your marrow. It is the sound of dust grains fusing and splitting under electrostatic stress, a constant trickle like sand poured over glass, that only fades when you hold your breath.

They begin small. Lentil-sized aggregates of charged metallic dust, cling together by static, jump a millimeter at a time across the black rock when the ground’s potential shifts. They swarm around a boot heel if your suit carries a stray charge from a lander, piling soft and light across the polymer, and leave a faint tingle on your skin when you brush them off. For the second they cluster, they cast faint, fleeting shadows shaped like circuit diagrams, like the crack patterns in dried mud, like the folds of a brain you’ve never seen before. They are not alive. They are the planet’s loose change, the smallest spill of its excess energy, and they die when the wind picks up, scattered back into the conductive dust that coats every surface.

Drifting six feet above the ground, thick as a finger, are the tendrils: wispy strands of ionized air and suspended crystal dust, that follow the metallic veins laced through the basalt like blood vessels. They have no will, only a draw to higher potential, so they will curl soft as smoke around a wrist carrying a power cell, sip a fraction of a watt, and leave a trail of burnt sugar and ozone that lingers for ten minutes before dissipating. When they brush the tips of the crystalline forests—those slow-growing, geometric lattices that stitch heat into rigid structure—the lattices hum in sympathetic resonance, and the tendril glows pale blue for three seconds, enough to cast a shadow on the rock below. They are the planet’s nerves, carrying low-grade current from the resonant arches to the spires, from the vents to the dust.

The overwhelming presence, the thing that scratches at the back of your neck even when you don’t turn to look, is the swarm: the acoustic phantoms, the static made hungry. They have no fixed shape. They are heat haze given coherence, translucent, shimmering, their edges blurring into the furnace glare of the unblinking sun. When they move, the light bends around them, a faint gravitational lensing that makes distant spires look warped, like they’re melting at the base. The smallest are the size of a coyote, the largest—when clusters coalesce around the resonant arches during high wind—stretch as tall as the monoliths themselves.

They do not hunt flesh. Their hunger is for topology: they skim the surface of the basalt, and where their wispy edges pass, the fine metallic dust of the ground rearranges itself into those fleeting, meaningless hieroglyphs—circuitry that loops back on itself, maps of valleys that do not exist, scripts that look familiar and alien all at once. They hold for a day, sometimes two, before the wind scatters them back into dust. The shrieking you hear, the constant, grinding cacophony like crystal seas shattering, is the sound of a million tiny energy releases overlapping, the static of their passage fizzing against the charged air. It is not a language. It is exhaust.

They are not alive. They are emergent, a side effect of a planet running an industrial process with no author, no product, no purpose but to run. The planet’s semi-conductive basalt skin, its resonant arches that turn wind into electricity, its crystalline forests that stitch heat into lattice stress, its vents that freeze sulphur into filigrees—all of it is machinery, grinding away on pure physics, and the phantoms are the waste heat of that machine, given just enough coherence to act as if it has will.

They are drawn to the resonant arches when the wind picks up, clustering in their openings as the deep organ-note of the wind through stone swells. Their shrieking harmonizes with the arch’s hum, and the whole plain vibrates so hard your teeth rattle, static arcing bright as neon between their edges even in the bleaching sunlight. For those minutes, the planet’s slow, foundational uncertainty bleeds through: gravity shivers, a stone a few feet away might be liquid one blink and solid the next, time stretches thin so that a minute feels like an hour and an hour feels like a second. The phantoms do not notice. They drift through the shifting reality like it is just another part of the wind.

Most of the plain’s presences are dormant. Half-buried in the basalt are boulders that look like any other rock formation, but scan as the same wispy, charged material as the phantoms, only still. They have slept for centuries, feeding on the planet’s excess energy, growing slowly, incrementally, until they wake. A survey team once set up camp next to a dormant cluster the size of a house; after three months, it began to hum. Over the course of a week it unfolded out of the rock, unfolded into a swarm that blotted out the sun for an hour as it drifted toward the distant spires. The team’s comms were filled with static for three days after, and when they pulled their logs, three months of data was gone, replaced by pages of the same dust hieroglyphs the phantoms leave in their wake.

They do not hate you. They do not see you. If you stand still, they will drift through the space around you, their edges brushing your suit, leaving a faint static fizz on the comms and a tingle on your skin that lasts for minutes. They only react if you disrupt the rock: if you drill for core samples, if you try to pry a metallic crystal from the basalt, if you lay a single piece of foreign material on the ground. Then they swarm, their shrieking rising to a pitch that bursts eardrums, static arcing off your suit until the polymer melts. They do not act out of anger. They act the way a factory alarm sounds when a sensor is tripped: a natural response to a disruption in the system they are a part of.

At “night”, when the sun dims to a bronze glare, they glow fainter, their edges softening, their shrieking dropping to a murmur like they are resting, even though they do not rest. The conductive lichen smeared on the lee of the monoliths pulses faint blue, and the phantoms drift closer to the stone, their wispy forms curling around the lichen’s glow like they are warming themselves. The hieroglyphs they leave in the dust in these hours are brighter, more complex, like they are reading the lichen’s pulses, like they are answering a question the planet has been asking for a billion years.

They are not the masters of this place. They are its exhaust, its shadow, its echo. The plain is a furnace that has never made anything but itself, and the phantoms are just the heat given shape, the static given hunger, the loud, constant, beautiful side effect of a world running on broken, dreamlike physics, slow and profound and fundamentally untrue. You never see them all at once. You only ever see the edges of them, out of the corner of your eye, feel the tingle of them on your skin, hear their shrieking under the wind, and know that the emptiness of the plain is a lie. It is full of them, full of their shimmering, silent, screaming presence, as much a part of the rock and the spires and the heat as the wind itself.
