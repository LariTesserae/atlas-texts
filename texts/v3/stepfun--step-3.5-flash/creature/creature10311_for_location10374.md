They call themselves the Silicate Weavers, though to ears untuned to the low thrum of the planet’s core, they might be mistaken for the spires themselves: tall, slender, their skin streaked with the faint sourceless bioluminescence of the crystalline moss that crusts the plateau, their fine hair woven with braids of living quartz filament or clips of glowing moss that shift color with the slow pulse of the substrate. They are not masters of this place, nor its victims: they are its quietest, most careful curators, a sparse population of metabolically adapted humans (and the few who have slipped past the boundary of human into something softer, more attuned to vibration and mineral pulse) whose existence is a deliberate, fragile paradox—urban, patterned, technological, yet folded entirely into the world’s total, slow-living vegetation.
Their bodies are shaped by the expanse: their lungs are expanded to drink the thin, ozone-tanged air, their eyes shifted to see the infrared glow of the petrified forests’ living alloy canopies and the ultraviolet pulse of the moss’s quantum-entangled photosynthesis, their bones laced with microcrystalline deposits that let them feel sub-audible vibrations— the deep drone of the core, the distant thrum of a Gilded Grazer ten miles out, the shift of a gravity pocket under their feet—as clearly as a touch. Most wear gravikinesis bands grown, not forged, from cultured silicate-eating bacteria that arrange themselves into micro-circuitry over months, tuned to their own bio-rhythm; for some, especially the eldest, the bands are integrated into their wrists, the silver circuitry blurring into theirskin like a second layer of living metal. To them, the bands do not feel like technology: they feel like will, a quiet nudge of gravity to catch themselves if a spire shifts, to lift a heavy load of harvested moss, to glide a few inches over a gravity low in the mycelial walkways.
Daily life unfolds slow as the planet’s growth pulses. The Weavers live in clusters of a few hundred, spread thin across miles of cultivated spires: genetically programmed giant sequoia-analogues trained over centuries to grow into spiraling skyscrapers, their bark patterned with glowing circuitry that hums in tune with the resonance fountains at the center of each cluster. There is no construction here, only persuasion: Tenders, the most common caste, climb the spires on mycelial walkways strong as graphene, their fingertips brushing the bark to adjust growth patterns, nudge a leaning spire back to center with a flick of their gravikinesis band, prune living alloy canopies so the wind chimes through them in the right key. Resonance-keepers tend the fountains, their throats laced with small crystalline nodes that let them hum counter-frequencies if a sky-lash’s scream or a grazer’s thrum disrupts the fountains’ harmonic hum, the only sound the Weavers allow to rise above a whisper. Archivists vanish for weeks or years into the deep plant caverns, where time flows slower, returning with carvings in petrified wood that record mineral blooms, gravity shifts, the slow migration of the solitary grazer herds, their speech slower, more deliberate, weighted by decades of deep-time solitude. Biomass tenders walk the gelatinous pearlescent plains, moving slow as the sludge itself to collect nutrient-rich samples for medicine, for fertilizer, for the balm that heals a cut from a sharp shard of petrified wood in hours without scar.
Their children are small, their quartz-fine hair often braided with scraps of glowing moss, their gravikinesis bands still soft and pale, not yet fully tuned. They run and bounce on the mycelial walkways, play games of listening: who can identify a grazer’s thrum from 15 miles out, who can hear the faint click of a crystalline structure expanding in the cool air, who can spot a sky-lash’s iridescent wing before the rest of the group. They are raised by the whole cluster, taught early to read the substrate’s pulses, to know when a silicate rain is coming, when a gravity pocket will shift, when the 24-hour stillness is near. They are given their first band at ten, cultivated from a sample of their own bacteria grown in the energy well’s nutrient mist; when they can use it to lift a shard of petrified wood or adjust their own gravity to walk up a 5% gravity slope, they are counted adults, free to choose their caste, free to wander the expanse for as long as they like.
Their relationship with the fauna is one of quiet, mutual recognition, not ownership. They name each Gilded Grazer that passes through their territory, track their migrations across the ochre and grey plateau, leave piles of nutrient quartz dust for them to rub against their living metal carapaces to shed old, damaged plates. The sky-lashes nest on the upper spires of their towers, and in return for not being disturbed, their screaming thermals warn the Weavers of approaching silicate storms that would disrupt the fountains’ frequencies. Young archivists sometimes bond with a sky-lash, leaving offerings of quartz on its nest until it accepts them, then riding its iridescent wings across hundreds of miles of expanse to record the history of remote clusters, or warn distant communities of an impending gravity shift.
Once every thirty of their days (measured not by the washed-out sun, but by the slow brightening and dimming of the crystalline moss’s bioluminescence), the entire cluster pauses for 24 hours of absolute stillness: all tech is shut down, all growth is paused, no one moves or speaks. They sit on the crust of the moss, or on the edges of the glowing energy wells, and they listen. The sound is not silent: it is the planet’s loud, ancient, indifferent song: the sub-audible drone of the core’s friction, the creak of petrified forests growing a fraction of a millimeter a day, the distant thrum of a grazer’s step, the organ-like chime of the wind funneling through the spires’ glass leaves. The Weavers say that if you listen long enough, you can hear the slow, gravitational sigh of the erosion that pressed the ancient mountains into continent-sized tables of polished stone, the echoes of a time before there was any intelligence here to weave pattern into the cycle. At the end of the 24 hours, they hum a single low, sub-audible note together, a signal to the planet that they are still listening, still part of the cycle, not its masters. The sky-lashes answer with soft screams, the grazers thrum in response, the wind chimes through the spires, and the fountains hum back to life.
When a Weaver dies, their body is laid on the crystalline moss to be reclaimed by the substrate: within a week, their molecules are disassembled, redistributed into the moss, into the spires, into the grazers that pass through. The only thing that remains is their gravikinesis band, grown from their own bacteria, immune to the substrate’s reclamation. The Tenders weave it into the bark of the nearest spire, and its quiet hum becomes part of the fountains’ resonant tune, another note in the city’s song, another ghost of will woven into the slow, living rock.
They are few, spread thin across a world that wants for nothing but water, their spires only small glints of light in the dim perpetual twilight, their loudest sound not the hum of their technology, but the 24 hours of deliberate silence in which they let the planet speak. They do not know, yet, if the world is learning their language, or if they are only just learning to listen to its own.
