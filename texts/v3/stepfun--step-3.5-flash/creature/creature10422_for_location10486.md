You do not so much enter the space as unspool into it, the memory of damp coastal plain air still clinging to your lungs for a heartbeat before it dissolves into something thicker, sweeter—like breathing in the forest’s own slow exhale, loam and ferment and a faint, sharp ozone that lifts the fine hairs on your arms.
Your first step is tentative, braced for mud, for squelch, for muck coating the soles of your boots. Instead the sphagnum yields like a living memory foam, springy and damp and soft as a moss-lined cradle, rising back to fill the divot the second you lift your foot, no trace of your weight left behind. A fat condensation droplet falls from a low-hanging vine a few feet away, thuds against the moss at your feet with the soft, heavy sound of a heartbeat, and you blink, because you could have sworn you heard it hit your shoulder half a second earlier. Time here is syrupy, you realize, stretching and thinning at the edges, and the sound travels slower than the water.
You look up, and the scale of it hits you not all at once, but in layers. The pillars rise corkscrewing into the green-tinged haze of the vault, fused trunks of ancient mangrove kin so wide you could wrap your arms around only a fraction of their girth, their bark a mosaic of pale lichens that shift color when you blink, and violet bioluminescent fungi that pulse in time with the thud of the condensation droplets. You hear the click before you see the millipedes: a synchronized wave of armored bodies crawling up the nearest pillar, their segments chiming together in a metallic rhythm that vibrates in your molars before it reaches your ears, and the air in front of the bark shimmers like heat haze, the sound so tactile it hums through the soles of your shoes.
Between the pillars, the “urban” space yawns open, vast and unobstructed, the “empty” volume so deliberate it feels like architecture. Woven living bridges of supple vines stretch between the trunks, their surfaces dotted with iridescent, fist-sized beetles chewing neat, crescent-shaped holes in the woody growth, their wings beating a continuous papery shush that merges into a white-noise roar so loud you have to raise your voice to think, but it is not harsh. It is the sound of a million tiny looms weaving the city together, you realize: the beetles are not damaging the bridges, they are trimming them, keeping the living platforms flexible, their chewing patterns matching the grain of the vine exactly, like they have been doing this for longer than the pillars have stood.
You walk toward what looks like a plaza, where the moss floor is paved with flat, coin-sized leaves worn smooth as stone, and the “street” of hanging roots you are following drifts left, no matter which way you turn. You glance up to check the position of the slender tower you saw in the periphery, a single tree grown through the fungal vault, its canopy filtering the diffuse, non-directional daylight into dappled emerald glow—and it is suddenly directly in front of you, its trunk so close you could reach out and touch it. When you look straight at it, though, it slips back to the edge of your vision, like it is playing peekaboo. You laugh, soft, because there is no menace here, only the quiet, unshakable sense that the space is rearranging itself not to confuse you, but to show you something you were not looking for.
The slow, deliberate metabolism of the place unfolds around you as you stand still. A vine next to the pillar uncurls so slowly you can barely see it move, stretching out over ten full minutes to catch a falling condensation droplet, then re-coiling just as gradually to deposit the water into a tiny root nub at its base, feeding the cluster of violet fungi growing there. You blink, and when you look back the vine is already halfway to its resting position, like it was never extended at all. Above it, a shelf of solidified fungal mycelium darkens, explodes in a silent cloud of glittering silver spores that drift down to coat the moss at your feet, then lightens again five minutes later, like it just took a breath.
There is no separation between the “inhabitants” and the space here, no line between wildlife and infrastructure. The pastel, soft-bodied worms pulsing across the moss floor digest decaying leaf matter to fertilize the pale sphagnum, their movement parting the green mat like a keel through still water. The darting, silent things with too many legs that skitter away when you shift your weight eat the excess fungal growth that would otherwise weigh down the hanging bridges. The millipede hum is a frequency that triggers spore release in the fungi, the beetle wings stir the air to spread the spores further, the condensation carries nutrient dust from the vault down to the roots under your feet. There is no “wildlife,” only organelles, each part of the system doing exactly what it was grown to do, no planning, no decision, just emergent, communal intelligence humming under every surface.
The “empty” space is not empty at all. It is the circulatory system: the air between the pillars thick with drifting spore dust and nutrient particles that glow silver when the shaft of emerald light hits them, moving slowly from one end of the cathedral to the other like blood in a vein. The “deafening” sound is not noise; it is the place’s neural chatter, the sum of all its tiny, relentless processes, vibrating through the moss under your feet, shimmering in the air, wobbling the bioluminescent light so it ripples like water when the beetles beat their wings.
You brush past a curtain of trailing Spanish moss hanging between two pillars, and it hums against your arm, a soft static zap that makes the tiny violet spots stitched across its fronds light up, like it is greeting you. The air tastes of loam and sweet, fizzy ferment, like overripe berries left in the sun, and the ozone tang lingers on your tongue.
You stand still long enough to count the thud of the condensation droplets, and you notice they sync to the hum of the beetles, sync to the click of the millipedes, sync to the slow pulse of the worms under your feet, all tied to the slow, swelling rhythm of the fungal vault above. The place is breathing. Inhale: the violet fungi pulse brighter, the beetles beat their wings a little faster, a shaft of golden light filters through the tower canopy. Exhale: the light dims, a cloud of spores drifts down, the moss under your feet softens imperceptibly, as if the whole organism is leaning in to listen to you.
You turn to look for the entrance you came through, and it is gone. Not hidden, not moved, just… not there, like the place has decided you do not need to leave yet. The tower you saw earlier is now directly ahead, its roots curling out of the moss like a staircase leading up into its golden canopy, and you realize it has been there the whole time, you just were not meant to see it until now.
You do not feel like a visitor here. You feel like a cell that wandered into a body, and the body is still deciding if you are part of its metabolism, or just a passing nutrient. The moss under your feet yields a little more when you shift your weight, not from your boot’s pressure, but from a pre-emptive softening, as if it anticipated your movement a half-second before you made it.
You take another step, deeper into the nave, and the hum of the millipedes swells, the beetles beat their wings a little faster, and the whole cathedral breathes with you.
