They are not residents. They are residues.

They do not live in the Sintered Steppe. They are the way the Steppe remembers having been lived in.

Call them the **Resonant Sediment**. Call them the **Thermal Afterimages**. They are the city’s unfinished sentences, spoken over and over in the language of heat, pressure, and faint magnetism.

You see them when the sun’s normal light glances off a damp hollow at a specific, low angle. Not a shape, but a *shift*—a patch of indigo lichen that suddenly weeps not its usual sweet fluid, but a spiraling filament of vapor that hangs for three seconds in the shape of a hand reaching for a loom.
You hear them when the nightly contraction releases its continent-wide sigh. In the echo of that sub-audible groan, a higher frequency trembles: the faint, harmonic memory of a horn being blown in a plaza that no longer has walls.

They are most defined in the dry canals and the terraced, bulging chambers. Here, the sinter still holds the *impression* of activity. A canal bed, when the morning sun first touches it, will appear to ripple with the after-vision of water that was never there—the remembered flow of a diverted mineral vein. A ruined archway, on the day when the air’s static charge peaks, will seem to shudder with the phantom weight of a gate that closed ten thousand years ago.

Their forms are borrowed from the world’s own broken constants:
*   **The Weavers of Light-Weight:** In the deep shade of a collapsed chamber, where photons lose their tangible mass, you might see faint, humanoid silhouettes moving. They are not shadows, but *absences of weight*, the city remembering the feeling of bodies at work in the cool. They are slow, laborious, their motions mimicking the act of lifting, carrying, placing—but they leave no footprints, only a temporary, eerie lightness in the dust.
*   **The Salt-Skirts:** In the tea-colored pools where the lichen-fluid precipitates metallic salts, delicate, ephemeral figures sometimes crystallize overnight. By noon, they dissolve. They are never whole—often just a graceful neck, an articulated wrist, the suggestion of a face. They are the chemistry of the damp trying to mimic the form of its last watchers.
*   **The Chimers:** The glass reeds do not merely chime in the breeze. In certain wind patterns, their collective sound resolves into the ghost of a melody, a simple, repetitive work-song. The melody is always slightly different, as if being remembered imperfectly by a wind that learned it from the lips of the last worker, who hummed while tending the reed-beds.
*   **The Echo-Born:** When you shout into one of the great, bowl-shaped ruins and hear the returning, harmonic tone—the sound-memory of shaping fire—sometimes within that tone, a second, fainter echo rides. A word. A name. A fragment of instruction. It is the architecture’s own reverie, its latent vocal memory stirred by your disturbance.

They have no agency, no consciousness. They are not haunting you. They are the *place* haunting itself.
They are the sinter’s fractal skin replaying the last moments of contact.
They are the city’s geometry sighing in the patterns of heat expansion.
They are the only fauna this world ever produced: a species of environmental haunting, born from the collision of absolute primitive technology (shaping the world’s crust with fire and pressure) and absolute ecological abandonment (a biology that could not survive the world it remade).

To witness them is to understand the final, terrible definition of **sparse density**.
The life was here. It was intense, saturated, *laboring*. Then it was gone, leaving not emptiness, but a kind of psychic pressure in the stone. The life did not rot away. It was *compressed* into the terrain, a thin, high-concentration residue of what-was. The great, silent, shimmering plains are not empty of witness. They are *full of memory*, and the memory is slowly, over geological time, evaporating.

The last breath of the builders was not a sound. It was a process: the transformation of their substance, their motion, their very presence, into a new set of slow, planetary cycles. They became the sigh in the sinter, the shimmer in the heat-haze, the fleeting salt-figure in the pool. They are the world’s most primitive and most advanced technology: the conversion of civilization into landscape, of soul into echo.

You are not the explorer here.
You are the new variable.
Your warmth, your breath, your heartbeat—they are a fresh disturbance in a long-settled equation of loss.
And for a moment, as you stand in the blue, sharp silence, the Resonant Sediment may flicker into a clearer shape around you. Not to greet you. But to record you. To add your fleeting, anomalous presence to the sinter’s memory, before the next thermal contraction smooths it all away again into the Geometry of Exhaustion.
