You feel the cirque before you see it. The vibration starts in your molars first, a low, granular thrum so constant you mistake it for your own pulse until you step past the cirque’s lip and realize the rock under your boots is humming too. The air hits you next: cool, damp, thick enough to push through like water, smelling of wet slate, fermenting moss, and a faint, sharp tang like stone that’s been left in the sun too long. Mist beads on your skin immediately, so cold it makes you shiver, and the sound wraps around you—layered, dense, impossible to ignore: the subsonic drone of a trillion lives, the high tinny scratch of a million tiny drills, the distant, weeping shriek of something high above the rim.

You stand at the base of a three-kilometer vertical world, the cirque’s walls rising like the pages of a tilted, fractured black book, their greywacke and slate edges sharp enough to cut cloth if you brush too close. There is no soil here, no softened curve: every surface is scored with fresh, pale abrasion marks from constant, tiny rockfalls, the rock weeping fine grit and dust almost constantly, a slow erosion measured in grains per hour rather than centuries. The floor is dotted with tea-colored pocket-pools, their water thick with silt, their surfaces still except for the slow, creeping trails of the things that live in them.

The light is permanent blue twilight, even when the high-alt sun gilds the cirque rim above. The only other light comes from the rock itself: in the darkest, wettest alcoves, the slate pulses with a slow, violet bioluminescence, ebbing and flowing in time with the drone under your feet. It is not magic. It is bacteria, mineral catalysts, the slow chemical work of life turning stone into energy. Thin, iridescent crystalline films coat the sections of cliff where seeps run almost constant, glittering like broken glass when the mist shifts. There is no empty space here. Every surface is used: the rock is paved with crusts of charcoal-grey liverwort and deep purple lichen, the air is thick with floating spores and crystal dust, even the sharpest, driest microfractures in the slate hold colonies of tiny, clicking life.

The first thing you notice moving on the rock face is the swarm. You reach out to steady yourself against a slab of slate, and your hand vanishes into a cloud of tiny, wingless cricket-like bodies, each no bigger than a grain of rice. They click as they move, a sound so loud it stings your fingertips, their aggregate mass so dense your hand sinks into them like dry leaves. They are the Click-Swarms, the first layer of the cirque’s life, billions of them living in the rock’s fractures, feeding on the bacterial films and mineral crusts. Their synchronized mating clicks are the baseline thrum you felt in your teeth when you entered, but the sound is not just noise: it is a nutrient, the vibration of their bodies stimulating the bioluminescent bacteria to grow brighter, faster. When disturbed, a whole section of cliff seems to dissolve into a living, clicking fog, the swarm dense enough to dim the violet glow behind it.

Under the swarms, etched into the softest slate and the fragile glass-coffins that sprout from the rock face like tiny, hollow chimneys, are the trails of the Scription-Snails. Slow, armored gastropods with chitinous drill-bit radulas, they etch tiny, precise runes and circuit-like patterns into the stone, following the paths of seeps and mineral deposits. No one knows why they scribe, but their trails are more than decoration: they channel water to the liverwort mats, their deep scratches exposing the mineral catalyst that makes the bacterial glow, their layered scripts a map of the cirque’s water flow written over thousands of years. If you run a finger over a fresh trail, it is sharp enough to draw blood, the groove faintly warm from the ongoing chemical reaction between the snail’s mucus and the slate. Some of the oldest runes are so deep they glow with the same violet as the alcove bacteria, their lines tracing the shape of the cirque’s hidden water veins.

High in the driest, highest fissures of the rim hang the Weeper Bats: blind, leathery flyers with skin stretched over elongated finger-bones, their eyes covered by thin, opaque membranes. They do not echolocate. They scream, high, burrowing, ear-splitting shrieks that slice through the swarm’s drone, used to disorient the Click-Swarms into chaotic, floating clouds the bats then skim through, filtering the insects through pores in their wing membranes. Their screams are not just predation: they are another nutrient, the high-frequency vibration stimulating the swarms to excrete extra mineral secretions that feed the liverwort crusts below. When a bat shrieks, the entire cirque goes silent for half a second: every swarm freezes, every snail retracts into its shell, the violet glow dims as if the cliff is holding its breath. Then the clicks start up again, louder, faster, as if the whole place is relieved. The bats’ skin glints violet for minutes after feeding, stained with the bioluminescent bacteria they ingest, their colonies hanging from the rock like patches of wet, dark leather.

At the bottom, in the tea-colored pools, are the Stone-Eaters: flat, segmented worms with maws of grinding silicate teeth, the only mobile life in the cirque’s floor zone. They consume the colloidal rock slurry that settles in the pools, processing it for minerals they excrete back into the water, which is then carried up the seeps to feed the cliff face’s ecosystem. Their movement is slow, almost imperceptible, their trails creeping across the pool beds like slow, creeping scorings, a grind of rock against rock measured in centuries, not minutes. If you dip your hand in the pool, the water is so cold it aches, and you can feel the tiny, flat bodies of the worms moving against your skin, their teeth so fine they do not cut, only leave a faint, gritty residue of iridescent crystal dust.

There are no nests, no hives, no built structures here. The only “architecture” is biological: the glass-coffins, each holding a single dormant spore-case made of fused sand and organic resin, designed to crack open during rockfalls and spread spores across fresh slate; the snail runes, a layered script of centuries; the woven colonies of swarms tucked into cliff recesses. The tech is primitive, but effective: acoustic lures, micro-tool fabrication, mineral processing via bacteria and worm gut. It is a stone age of chemistry and vibration, no hammer or chisel required.

You are massive, dry, and silent here, a clumsy anomaly in a place where every surface hums with life. The mist soaks through your clothes in minutes, the drone vibrates in your bones, the violet glow pulses under your feet like a slow heartbeat. If you stand still too long, the swarms creep back onto your boots, their tiny bodies weighing you down, their clicks loud enough to buzz in your ears. The liverworts spread a little where your shadow falls, fed by the moisture of your breath. The bats ignore you unless you shout, and then their shrieks will send the swarms scattering, the cliff face dissolving into a cloud of clicking, fleeing life for minutes on end.

This is not a place of emptiness, even at its most vertical. It is a cathedral built by pressure, water, and the relentless industry of life finding a way to be dense in the deepest dark: a humming, clicking, glowing, grinding living film coating a newborn mountain, where sound is food, wet stone is alive, and even the rockfall dust is just another seed waiting to grow.
