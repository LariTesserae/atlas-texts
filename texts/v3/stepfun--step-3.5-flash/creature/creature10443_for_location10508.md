The first thing you know of Stonehallow Basin is the wind. It funnels down the amphitheater’s curved walls in a low, constant sigh, a harmonic note so low it hums in your molars before it reaches your ears, shifting pitch only when you step lower, as if the stone itself is tuning the air as it moves. The walls rise three hundred feet on either side, layered like the pages of a stone book: rusty ironband streaked with oxidized rust, charcoal shale soft enough to mark with a fingernail, cream quartzite polished to a shine by a million years of glacial melt that no longer exists. There are no cracks, no loose scree, no signs of recent movement; the stone is finished, its final statement already spoken.

The terraces reveal themselves as you descend, not built but carved out of the river’s old work, broad flat benches girdling the bowl. The lowest ones are smooth as worn floorboards, polished by ten thousand years of moccasin soles and goat hooves; the higher you climb, the rougher the stone, the sharper the edges, the newer the use. There are no permanent structures, only arrangements: slabs of fallen quartzite leaned into windbreaks, dead juniper trunks stacked into low walls, hide curtains stretched over juniper frames to block the wind. The only constant sound besides the wind is the soft, crystalline plink of water dripping from mossy overhangs into the algae-dulled pools at the basin’s heart, a metronome so regular you can set your breath to it.

You might walk for ten minutes along the lowest terrace before you see the first person. She is old, her skin the color of sun-bleached shale, lined like the basin walls themselves, sitting cross-legged on a woven grass mat sorting dried black cushion moss into a pile of edible bits and bits for weaving. Her tunic is faded blue wool, dyed with indigo lichen scraped from the north-facing walls, worn thin at the elbows, patched with goat hide at the cuffs. A bone awl sticks out of the leather cord at her waist, and a pouch of flint shards hangs at her hip. She looks up when she hears your boots on the gravel, does not speak, only nods, and taps the packed earth next to her with the awl, an invitation to sit that you do not need to accept to know it is there. Her sleeping hollow is scooped into the terrace floor behind her, lined with dry moss, a goat hide curtain drawn half-closed to keep out the wind. A small stone bowl sits by her feet, half-full of the sweet, iron-tasting wiry grass that grows on the upper rim. She does not offer it; offering is not how things are done here. If you are hungry, you take what the basin gives. If you are not, you leave it.

Up one terrace, a man is knapping flint, the sharp tink of stone on stone cutting through the wind’s sigh. He is broad-shouldered, his forearms scarred with old cuts from flint shards, his face burnt dark by the thin high-altitude sun. A half-butchered goat hide is stretched tight on a frame of dead juniper branches next to him, the fur still stiff with salt. He pauses when he sees you, holds up the half-finished flint scraper he is making, turns it in the sharp light so the edge glints, perfect and sharp enough to shave with. He says something in a low, soft language, his consonants rounded by the thin air, points first to the hawk circling the rim overhead, then to the hide, then to the scraper, meaning he will use it to clean the hide for a new curtain for his sleeping hollow. He does not smile, but the corner of his mouth tugs up, almost imperceptibly, before he goes back to his work.

The next terrace up holds the children: two of them, no older than seven, chasing a tiny sand-colored scuttler through the moss by the seep channel. They laugh, quiet, hushed, because loud sounds echo off the stone walls and disturb the stillness. One of them holds a small woven juniper bark basket slung over her shoulder, lined with dried moss, and when she sees you she pauses, tucks a strand of matted black hair behind her ear, and holds up a pale blue piece of quartzite she has found, smooth as glass. She offers it to you, and when you take it, she grins, sharp and bright, and runs off after the scuttler before you can say thank you.

The highest, newest terrace is where the oldest man in the basin lives, sitting on a flat slab of shale whittling a juniper handle for a new bone awl. His face is a map of the basin’s strata, each line a season, a drought, a season the goats came, a summer the hawk eggs hatched early. A pile of sorted goat bones sits at his feet: small ones for awls, medium for scrapers, large for smoothing stone. He nods at you, holds up the half-carved handle, runs a calloused finger along it to show you it fits the awl perfectly, no gaps, no rough spots. He does not speak, because there is nothing to say. Everything is already known: the seep will run, the wind will sigh, the junipers will grow an inch every hundred years, the hawks will return every spring to nest in the inaccessible northern cleft of the rim.

There are no clocks here, no calendars, no way to mark time except by the sun’s slow crawl across the basin walls, and the plink of the water, and the migration of the goats, and the hatching of the hawk eggs. The people do not farm, do not herd, do not build. They attend. They clear the scree from the terraces, they move fallen stone into windbreaks, they harvest only the old dead juniper branches, only the excess moss, only one egg per hawk nest, only one goat per season when the herds descend. The rule is unwritten, absolute: take only what is given, leave the rest, never break a stone from the basin’s wall, never dig, never leave a mark that cannot be erased. If you leave the basin, it will be as if you were never there.

The seep, the Basin’s Breath, runs warm and mineral-rich from a crack at the basin’s center, with no volcanic activity for miles, no explanation anyone has ever thought to ask for. To question the seep would be to question the stillness, and no one here does that. Sometimes the stone hums, a low sub-audible vibration felt in the teeth when the wind hits a certain pitch, and no one mentions it either. It is just the basin’s song, the same as the wind’s sigh, the same as the water’s plink.

There are only thirty of them, scattered across the terraces so that you might go a whole day without seeing more than two or three. They are part of the basin’s low-density pattern, like lichen spots on the shale, like the black moss by the seep channels. When the goats descend the impossible outer slopes once a season, their cloven hooves clicking on stone like castanets, the whole basin goes quiet, no one moves, no one breathes, until the herds drink and climb back up the slopes, leaving only a few hoofprints in the damp earth by the channels, which the wind and the sun erase within a day.

When the rare rain falls, it runs off the smooth stone walls in thin sheets, no mud, no mess, the terraces draining so fast you would never know it rained at all. The people huddle in their sleeping hollows, pull their hide curtains tight, tell stories in low voices of the river that carved the basin a million years ago, of the first people who came and cleared the scree, of the time the wind was so strong it blew a juniper trunk all the way across the basin floor. They do not talk about what is outside the rim, because there is nothing outside that matters. The basin is a room of stone with the sky for a ceiling, and they are its quiet, temporary inhabitants, eroding themselves into its shape, just as the river eroded the stone, until no footstep leaves a mark, no stone is out of place, and the stillness holds, forever.
