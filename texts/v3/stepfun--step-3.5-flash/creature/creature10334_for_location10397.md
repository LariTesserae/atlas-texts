The first life here is not dramatic, not obvious: it is the iridescent mineral lichens, smudges of crystalline fuzz clinging to the sintered plain like bruises on a stone god’s skin. They do not grow in seasons, but in pulses tied to the planet’s Quiet Tempest: every time the continent-wide, subsonic sigh vibrates through the lithic skin, they drink a little deeper of the gamma rays bleeding up from the deep core, and their glow brightens a shade—from the dull opalescence of old ash to the sharp, cold fire of a diamond catching light. They have no roots, only anchoring crystals that interlock with the plain’s composite of volcanic glass, precipitated metallic foam, and fossilized radio waves; they feed not just on gamma, but on the compressed moments sealed in the gray stratified layers below the surface, the trapped light of dead stars and the echoes of broadcasts from water-world civilizations that burned out a million years before the plateau was planed flat. If you crouch beside one for a week, you will feel faint pulses in your fingertips when you brush its surface: the memory of a child’s laugh, the hum of a monofilament strand being woven into the lattice, the flash of light the day the plain’s creators decided to flatten their world into something that would outlast time. They do not die; when a gravity storm shears across the plain or a crystal bloom spreads its geometric lace, shards of lichen scatter for miles, and each shard grows into a new patch, carrying half the memories of its parent. They are the plain’s marginalia, scribbling notes in the margins of its geologic book in light no human eye was built to see.

Rarer, slower, are the sinter-mounds: low, porous hills of the same metallic foam that makes up the plain’s deep layers, blind and mouthless, feeding only on the geothermal ions that drift up from the cooling core. They move a foot a year—so slow you can stare at one for an hour and see no shift, but over a lifetime they crawl across continents, their porous skins soaked in tiny, trapped bubbles of compressed time. Touch one, and a flash of sensory memory will sear behind your eyes: the feel of rain on a water-world cheek, the hum of a ship’s engine, the quiet rush of the day the creators walked away from their cities to become part of the plain. They do not communicate with sound, but when two mounds cross paths (once every ten thousand years, on average) they hum a subsonic note so low it adds to the Quiet Tempest, a shared note of acknowledgement between two beings whose lives are longer than the rise and fall of most civilizations. They are the plain’s bookmarks, marking the places where something important happened, where a moment was pressed into the sinter for eternity.
