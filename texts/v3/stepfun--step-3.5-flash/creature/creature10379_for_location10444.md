To enter the Basin of Sighing Stones is to step inside a living throat. The first assault is auditory: a stadium-roaring, bass-heavy white noise of water weeping from ten thousand porous pores in the black, boxy walls, a constant, thundering fundament of drip and flow that vibrates in your sternum. Above it, layered like a high-resolution symphony, comes the crackling static of millions of skitterers—thumb-sized, silvery, their paddle-wings whirring as they dart across the inch-deep mirror of the flagstone floor. The water is so clear it reflects the ice-lens sky above, its surface filmed with microbial mats that ripple in the wakes of the swarms. You could throw a stone from one wall to the other, yet the sound of your small impact rolls and bends as if the space were miles wide, bending around the standing stones and through the mist in eerie, deceptive echoes.

The skitterers are the basin’s most numerous breath. They graze constantly on the microbial slime, their tiny mandibles scraping the stone, and they move in perfect, synchronized waves for reasons no eye can fathom—suddenly, across the entire surface, ten million bodies will turn as one, a ripple of silver flashing in the dim light. They skate right past your boots without pause, ignoring you entirely unless you lunge or cast a sudden shadow; then they scatter like spilled mercury, only to re-form moments later. Anything of iron you might carry would rust to dust within hours here, the air corrosive to certain ores, but the skitterers’ bodies hold no such vulnerability; their carapaces gleam with a patina of metallic sand that drifts in the water, glittering against any current, pulled perhaps by the subtle magnetic fields still humming in the ancient pewter-like alloy of the builders.

Above the water, the mist hangs thick, cool, and motionless save for slow thermal drifts that swirl it into ghostly tendrils. Here float the miasma-drifters, moth-like and larger than the skitterers, their wings made of papery, translucent membrane that makes a soft *shush-shush* when they beat—a sound so perfectly meshed with the water’s roar that you cannot separate them without listening intently. They filter nutrients from the mist itself, their bodies dusted with pale bioluminescent spores harvested from the fungal mats that cling to the lower walls. The fungi, in turn, paint the stone in slow pulses of ghostly blue and gray, casting the mist in a depth that feels more underwater than aerial. The drifters drift through the light without hurry, their pathings weaving around the standing stones as if reading the faint magnetic traces the stones still emit.

Beneath the flagstones lies the basin’s aquifer, and in its dark, blind tunnels swim the tunnelers—eel-like, eyeless, their skin patterned like wet basalt. They communicate in resonant thrumming and clicks that vibrate up through the stone and water, a sub-audible pulse that can be felt in the teeth and bones. When you press your ear to the flagstones, you hear it: a slow, ancient conversation in a language of pressure and frequency, a rhythm that seems to keep time with the skitterers’ synchronized turns and the drifters’ wingbeats. They are the basin’s hidden heart, and their steady migration through the gravel beneath your feet is the reason the water level never rises or falls.

Life here is not all animal. In the deep, concave shadows of the standing stones—sculpted torsos of some forgotten machinery, their surfaces etched with geometric glyphs that look like circuit diagrams eroded by eons—grows the void-moss. It is rubbery and black, feeding not on light but on moisture drawn directly from the saturated air. It pulses faintly when the condensation runs thickest, a slow, dark rhythm hidden in the glare of the fungi. The stones themselves do not merely stand; they participate. They weep their constant, cold trickle of water through engineered channels, feeding the microbial mats and the skitterers that graze them. Their sub-audible hum—felt rather than heard—seems to regulate the pace of the tunnelers’ clicking, the flutter of the drifter wings, even the synchronized waves of the skitterers. It is a machine, still running, though its purpose is lost to time, and its operators are gone.

The balance is maintained by the basin’s silent hunters: falcon-shaped, with wings of something like translucent insect membrane that absorbs sound. They swoop without warning from the upper gloom, snatching skitterers from the water or snagging drifters from the mist, their movements so fluid they leave no disturbance in the air. They are the only life here that will acknowledge you, and only if you move suddenly or cast a shadow across their hunting path. Otherwise, you are as much a part of the stonework as the toppled archways and weathered torsos. The mist clings to your clothes, the drip soaks your shoulders, the hum thrums in your jaw—you are not an intruder, not really. You are just another surface for the mist to settle on, another shadow for the void-moss to grow in, another listener to the sighing of the stones.
