You step into the basin and the first thing you register is not sight, not sound, but vibration: a low, constant thrum that hums in the fillings of your molars, buzzes in the bones of your sternum, before your ears even catch the individual layers of noise pressing in. The cold is immediate, seeping through your jacket, your socks, the soles of your boots in a slow, steady damp that never dries, never warms—only the faint, sour warmth of thermal vents far in the distance ever cuts through the perpetual chill. The air tastes of wet crushed stone, sweet rot, the sharp bite of sulfur and ammonia, so thick with mist that it clings to your eyelashes, your throat, makes every breath feel like swallowing cold, wet wool.

Your first sound is a drip: a single drop falling from an unseen crack high on the basin wall, hitting a smooth boulder a dozen feet away with a thud so deep it rolls through the bowl like distant thunder, bouncing off the distant black walls until it fades into the constant, unending hum underneath. That hum is the baseline of the world: the ceaseless, rain-like rattle of a billion tiny legs clicking against stone, the high, metallic whine of singers so dense in the mist it feels like it’s drilling inside your skull, the low, wet scrape of larger shapes moving through shallow water, the faint creak of porous spires shifting under their own weight.

Your eyes adjust slowly to the gloom, picking out light only where life makes it: faint, sourceless glows seeping from thermal vents deep in the rock, sickly green and orange pulses spreading across the surface of every stone. There are no sharp edges here. Every boulder, every outcropping, every pebble is worn smooth, polished by millennia of tiny feet and constant condensation, so soft under your palm you’d almost think it’s wood if not for the cold, damp grit of mineral under the slimy film coating it.

That film is the base of everything. No plants, no fungi, no sun to power photosynthesis: the entire biome is built on these thin, pulsing microbial mats, chemosynthetic, feeding on the sulfur and ammonia dissolved in the air and water, glowing faintly as they metabolize. They coat every surface, thick enough in places to squelch under your boot, so prone to grazing that you can watch them spread, slow as a heartbeat, over a bare patch of rock in the time it takes you to shift your weight.

The grazers are the skitterers: billions of small, hard-shelled, multi-limbed creatures, no bigger than your thumbnail, their legs clicking so fast the sound blends into that constant, rain-like rattle. Their shells catch the faint glow in iridescent blues, matte blacks, pale amber, and they move in thick, flowing carpets over every rock, every pool edge, every surface, nipping constantly at the film under them. When you shift your weight, they scatter in a tiny, clicking wave, then flow back the second you’re still, unfazed, undeterred, as much a part of the stone as the mineral itself. They are the granivores, the detritivores, the base of every food chain here, and their noise is so constant you stop hearing it as separate after ten minutes, just another layer of the world’s thrum.

Floating just above the ground, bobbing in the damp mist, are the drifters: amorphous, bladder-like beings, some no bigger than a grape, some as large as your head, their semi-permeable skins glowing faintly where they’ve absorbed nutrients. They drift on pockets of internal gas, slow as bubbles, and when two collide they merge for a few seconds, a lumpy, glowing single shape, before pulling apart to exchange material, no aggression, no competition, just constant, slow churn of semi-individuals. They filter the mist and the shallow pools for dissolved nutrients, and when one drifts close enough to brush your wrist, its skin is cool and slick, like touching the inside of a fresh clamshell, faintly warm from the gas trapped inside.

The high, metallic whine that drills in your skull belongs to the singers: tiny, winged or finned particles that swarm in the mist and the shallow pools in shifting, iridescent clouds. They communicate in chirps and hums and clicks so complex it feels like language, and their swarms shift and move as one, as if guided by a single mind. When a cluster dives toward a pool, the whine spikes so sharp it makes your teeth ache, and a second later a slide-shape lumbers out of the shallows to answer: large, segmented, pale gray predators, their flesh slick with slime, moving with a slow, wet, muscular rhythm, sensing the world only through vibration. They engulf skitterers and drifters in a single, fleshy motion of their circular chitin mouths, leaving a trail of dissolved slime that the microbial films begin to colonize within minutes, their scrape a low, guttural base note that undercuts the skitterer rattle and singer whine. Slide-shapes do not hunt with speed, but with persistence: you can hear their scrape approaching minutes before you see them, their suction-cup lined underbellies making a faint, popping sound as they move over the smooth rock, and they do not react to you at all, used to the constant vibration of life around them, grazing as much as they hunt, leaving no waste, no uneaten bits, as every scrap of their prey is consumed by the films and skitterers within minutes of their passing.

In the distance, the low hills you saw on first entering resolve into hundreds of mound-weaver spires: low, porous, mineral-cemented towers grown, not built, by colonies of tubular organisms that secrete a sticky, sulfur-smelling adhesive to bind crushed rock and sediment into porous, hive-like structures. They are both nest and feeding apparatus, filter-feeding nutrients from the damp air, their surfaces coated in the same pulsing microbial film as the surrounding rock, crawling with symbionts: skitterers that live in their pores, drifters that tuck into their crevices, singers that nest in their hollow tops. When a faint current of air moves through the basin, the spires creak, a low, groaning sound that layers into the constant cacophony, and clouds of singers burst out of their hollows, whining as they swirl up into the mist.

You walk for hours, and the world never changes: only more smooth rock, more pulsing film, more skitterers clicking under your boots, more drifters bobbing in the mist, more singers swarming in the gloom. The distant walls of the basin are black and dripping, coated in the same glowing films, crawling with the same life, the distant hills just larger mounds, larger accumulations of the same worn, living stone. There is no edge, no shift in terrain, no quiet corner: the sound is so dense, so constant, that yelling is useless, your voice swallowed instantly by the layers of noise, echoes bouncing off the stone so fast you cannot tell where a sound originates. Silence is unthinkable here: even if you plug your ears, the vibration thrums in your skull, the drips thud in your throat, the skitterer rattle hums in your bones.

Time feels fluid here. You watch a patch of film spread over a bare rock, pulsing brighter as it catches a stray drip, and you cannot tell if minutes or hours have passed. A mound-weaver adds a quarter inch of height to its spire while you lean against it, listening to the faint hum of singers inside its pores. There is no day, no night, only the uniform gloom, broken only by the faint glow of vents, the pulse of bioluminescent films, the occasional flash of light when a swarm of singers mates or a slide-shape hunts.

There is no waste here, no decay, no quiet: every dead drifter, every shed skitterer shell, every scrap of leftover prey is consumed within minutes, turned into more film, more skitterers, more singers, more sound. The basin is a single, closed, breathing loop, a world where rock and flesh and sound have merged into a perfect, mineral balance, where life has filled every cubic centimeter of space, every niche, every silence, until there is no room for anything else but the endless, deafening, overwhelming chorus of its going on.
