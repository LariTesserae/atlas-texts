You first notice the give of the ground under your boots: not the crunch of dry grass or packed earth, but the soft, powdery resistance of wind-compacted silicate dust, fine as flour, that puffs up in faint red plumes with every step. The air is thin enough to make your breath come a little quick, sharp with ozone and the metallic tang of pulverized rock, no trace of moisture anywhere—no hum of unseen insects, no scent of damp leaf or rot, only the faint, sweet ghost of burnt sugar that lingers on the back of your tongue, woven into the dust from the ambermoss that carpets the gaps between the crystal spars.

You kneel to brush a patch of it, and the moss yields like soft, cured leather, springing back the second you lift your hand. Scrape a flake off with your glove and it leaves a faint, burnished gold smudge that won’t rub off for hours, warm from the UV it gulped from the white sun. If you press your ear to the ground, you can hear the slow, almost imperceptible fizz of the seep aquifers welling up from fractures in the black glass bedrock below: the only “running water” this world affords, a thick, chilled tar of liquid hydrocarbons tracing glistening dark veins a hundred meters long before sinking back into the porous stone, feeding the roots of the spars that spear up all around you.

They are the forest, fifty meters tall in the oldest growth, matte grey silicate polymer trunks frosted with tiny white crystals that refract the brutal sunlight into faint, shifting rainbows at their edges. Tap one with your knuckle and it chimes like a fine wine glass, the sound swallowed instantly by the dust and the stone itself, gone before it can carry three meters. Run your hand along the trunk and it is cool to the touch even at midday, gritty as unpolished quartz, and you feel a faint, subsonic thrum under your skin—so slow you can barely be sure it’s there, the pulse of a metabolism that builds one inch of trunk per year, lives for three thousand, and spends its final centuries slowly dissolving back into the bedrock, feeding the next generation of spars growing out of the seep veins nearby.

A soft clink of glass on glass draws your eye: a Gleaner, scuttling between two trunks, six articulated legs moving in a smooth, deliberate rhythm, its body armored in interlocking plates of the same black glass as the bedrock, glinting like polished obsidian in the sun. It pauses, tapping the side of a spar with a sensitive forelimb, and you feel the vibration in your teeth: subsonic, the same frequency it uses to locate the soft, nutrient-rich nodes in the crystal, before it leans down to rasp a patch of ambermoss with its pale yellow mandibles, gold flecks raining off into its mouth. It has a dusting of fine red silicate on its back from the last dust-sun, and it does not brush it off. It scuttles ten meters further, pauses to graze again, and you realize if you mark its position and return in an hour, it will have moved less than a meter. Everything here moves at the speed of stone.

You climb a low rise of bedrock to take in the scale of the place, and the forest stretches to every horizon, a sea of glass spears and flecks of gold, no end in sight. The white sun hangs high, so bright it bleaches all color from the dust and the spars, leaving only sharp, inky black shadows that stretch for dozens of meters. Far off, you can make out the faint, hazy line of the plateau’s edge, where the ground drops away into the mile-deep Slope of fine, wind-packed dust that flows slow as a lava flow toward the planet’s burning equator. A herd of Gleaners moves across the middle distance, hundreds of them forming a slow, dark ripple across the gold moss, so still you can barely tell they are migrating at all.

The wind shifts, carrying the faint coppery taste of silicate from the Slope, and you know a dust-sun is coming. Over the next seven days, the air grows thick with fine red dust, the light softening to a hazy rose-gold, the sun shrinking to a pale disc behind the veil. The spars glow softly from within, diffused by the haze, and the ambermoss dims, starved of the UV it needs to photosynthesize. Dust settles on every surface, a fine powder that fills in your footprints, coats the lenses of your suit, works its way into the seams of your gloves. Visibility shrinks to a hundred yards, and the only sounds are the soft sigh of the wind through the spars, and the occasional faint tink of a crystal fragment shedding from thermal stress, hitting the dust below. The world feels small, soft, wrapped in wool, and you realize a “forest fire” here is just a dust-sun that lasts long enough to starve a quadrant of ambermoss for a year, no flame, no smoke, just slow, quiet starvation.

A month later, the wind shifts again, sharp and cold enough to make your teeth tingle with electrostatic discharge: a crystal-gale, scouring the plateau clean. The haze lifts in hours, the air so clear you can see the individual frost flecks on the spars five miles away, and the crystals begin to sing—subsonic, a thrum you feel in your marrow more than hear, the sound of the stone vibrating as the wind strips the dust from its surface. The light is so bright it feels like it could cut, sharpening every edge of the spars, throwing rainbows so bright you have to squint to look at them. Dust motes dance in the air like tiny stars, and for a few hours the world feels like it is made of nothing but light and glass and the slow, deep breath of the wind.

You have heard stories of the Sunderer, the limbless burrower of the Slope, but you never expect to feel it coming. The wind dies first, absolutely, not a breath stirring, and the thrum of the spars cuts out so fast your ears ring. The ground vibrates under your boots, faint at first, then stronger, and a line of dust ahead of you rises up like a slow-motion wave. The bedrock splits open, a glass-lined trench ten meters wide, swallowing three grazing Gleaners and a young, 20-year-old spar whole, before the ground shifts back into place, the trench already starting to fill with dust, soft and slow, like a wound healing. By the time the next dust-sun rolls in, no one will know the trench was ever there.

You sit back down on a patch of ambermoss, which springs up warm under you, and you realize there is no line here between the living and the dead, the biology and the geology. The spars are the planet’s bones, growing slow out of the rock, and when they fall they dissolve back into the bedrock, their ions pulled up by the next generation of spars, the next generation of Gleaners, the next generation of ambermoss. The dust under your boots is made of a million fallen spars, a million years of crystal and stone, turned to dust, turned to life, turned to stone again. The big, close moon hangs low on the horizon now, a pale white disc bright enough to cast sharp shadows even at night, and you know its tidal pull is tugging at the bedrock right now, making the seeps well up a little faster in the dark, feeding the crystals, feeding the world, so slow no one but the stone itself could ever measure the change.

The only sounds are the wind, the faint tink of a crystal fragment falling, the distant, almost imperceptible scuttle of the Gleaner herd three miles away. The world is so quiet you can hear your own heartbeat, the slow thrum of your own blood, and beneath it, the deep, slow pulse of the planet itself, breathing slow under a white sun, a forest of glass and gold that has been here for ten million years, and will be here for ten million more, and you are just a blink, a fast, soft thing passing through its slow, bright, quiet dark.
