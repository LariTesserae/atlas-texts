The first thing you notice is that you don’t land. You fall *in*—the air thickens before your boots hit the scree, dense with powdered copper and iron that catches in your throat and coats your tongue with the sharp, electric taste of a licked battery. The golden slant of sun from the plateau’s mouth above slicks down the obsidian walls in thick honeyed columns, turning the perpetual dust-haze to a soft, bruised purple where it bleeds into the violet hum of the phosphor tubes embedded in the rock far below. The first sound you register is not a noise, but a thrum in your molars, the sub-audible vibration of geothermal pumps and high-tension current running through the crack’s stone veins before your ears can pick up the distant hiss of steam or the skitter of tiny legs on hot metal.
This is the Adit of Forges, and nothing here lives so much as it persists, woven into the crack’s slow, industrial metabolism.
The people move like they are part of the rock themselves. Their faces are smudged with permanent streaks of metallic dust, their eyes glazed with a faint violet sheen from years under the strobing phosphor light, their gear stitched and riveted from the very materials of the crevice: aprons of repurposed asbestos-cement panels, gloves lined with ground slag to insulate against static, goggles polished from shards of fresh obsidian held on with braided copper wire that doubles as a power tap for their handheld grinders. Many carry faint, hairline trails of clear quartz in their skin, sealed into old burns or cuts by the crack’s slow, unceasing healing. One woman working the loading dock has a hook of fused steel embedded in her left palm, a modification she got after a mini-landslide crushed her old tool harness; the rock has grown half over the base of the hook in the two months since, and she has to chip the new quartz away every week to keep it from locking in place.
They are not separate from the place’s non-biological life—they are its caretakers, and its cells, in equal measure.
Rock-lice swarm in thick, papery-skittering clouds over every warm metal surface: the steam pipes running the length of the loading dock, the rivets holding the catwalk railings to the obsidian, the side of the gantry crane rumbling along its cliff-face rails. They are six-legged, centimeter-wide creatures of hardened slag and iron filings, grinding rust off the pipes and excreting a fine, hyper-conductive powder the workers sweep into woven wire baskets to patch short circuits in the crack’s tangle of power lines. A misstep kicks up a cloud of the stuff, and you will get a static arc that jumps a full meter, enough to blow the hair off your arms if you don’t ground yourself fast.
Below the loading dock, the black crude sludge river oozes slow, smelling of burnt plastic and deep-earth ozone. Agile, ribbon-like sludge-eels glide through it, their polished black ceramic bodies slipping between the potholes of congealed pitch, sensor pits on their snouts twitching at thermal variances and chemical signatures. Originally pipeline inspection drones dragged down from the plateau mine when the crack opened, they now act as the river’s caretakers: flick up to grab loose scrap iron dropped by workers, carry it to the intake valves of the ore processors, and shunt blockages of re-crystallizing pitch out of the main flow. Workers wade into the sludge in heavy, slag-lined boots to prod loose eels that get snagged on old rivets, muttering to them like they’re stubborn livestock.
Strung between the high-tension lines strung across the upper tiers of the crack, cable-spiders anchor their tripodal bodies to the cables, weaving their torsos from braided copper filaments harvested from scrap heaps, feeding on stray current that would otherwise blow out transformers. Their bodies glow with faint, random arcs of blue electricity that fizz and pop when plumes of condensed mineral vapor from the smokestacks drift past. Workers tap the lines gently with insulated poles to prompt them to shunt down a few feet, freeing up current for the gantry cranes hauling fist-sized, pulsing energy cells and raw ore blocks along the rails. Some workers raise hatchling spiders from eggs laid in the warm, dry alcoves of the upper walls, taming them to perch on their shoulders and warn them of incoming power surges with a faint, high whine.
The most unsettling residents are the Dust-whales: vast, slow vortices of concentrated metal dust and copper filings that swim through the upper haze, their shapes vague and submarine-like, their passage so silent you only notice them when the phosphor tubes flicker in sequence and a fine shower of glittering precipitate rains down from the walls. They are not solid, not quite—they are weather, and mood, and a form of airborne intelligence, the old-timers say. When one dissipates, it leaves behind a stable, humming cloud of its constituent particles that hangs in one spot for hours, vibrating at a frequency so low you feel it in your sternum. Workers pause all their work when one passes, holding up scraps of rock-salt to the cloud to catch the half-glimpsed shapes inside: flashes of the quake that opened the crack, the faces of the first settlers who dragged the first phosphor tube down into the dark, the shape of the thing that breathes at the crack’s lowest, hottest core.
You see the crack’s rules written into every inch of the place. A wrench left in a crevice three weeks ago is now half-encased in growing clear quartz, its handle sticking out to hold a worker’s lunch pail; leave any break in the rock, any scrap of metal, any spilled drop of sludge, and the crack will slowly pull it into its body, re-crystallizing it, reabsorbing it into its mineral skin. A new kid who tried to sweep a pile of metal dust near a high-tension line last cycle blew the front of his hair off with a static arc so strong it blackened his eyebrows; now he wears a frayed wool scarf wrapped tight over his face at all shifts, and all the older workers carry their tools wrapped in coarse asbestos to avoid the same. The upper tiers, where the sun reaches, are coated in a patina of processed dust and industrial grime, their walls rough and pocked; the lower you descend, the warmer the rock under your boots, the cleaner the air, the brighter the phosphor tubes, until you reach the lowest levels where the rock is still soft, still red-hot, still being born, and time moves so slow you can see the crystal grow if you stare long enough.
The pulse comes on the fourth day of your stay. For the first three days, the lights dim a little more each shift, the hum in your teeth grows louder, the catwalks creep millimeters as pressure redistributes through the rock, and the workers brace every loose pipe and stack with extra slag blocks. When it hits, the whole crack shivers hard enough to knock you to your knees if you’re not holding on. Avalanches of crystal scree cascade down the upper walls, clinking like glass bells as they hit the piles below, and the phosphor tubes strobe so fast the whole world flickers between gold and violet. Geysers of superheated steam blow in sequence up and down the crack, coating every surface in a glittering sheen of mineral frost that glows in the strobing light before sublimating away entirely by the end of the day. The whole thing lasts three seconds, then fades, and the hum settles back to its normal low thrum. The workers cheer, even the old ones with the quartz in their skin, and pass around cups of fermented sludge-brew filtered through rock-salt, tasting of smoked honey and burnt plastic. The kid who feeds the eels holds up a handful of the crystal shards that fell during the pulse, still warm enough to steam in the cool air, and says the deep divers say if you press these to the rock at the lowest level, you can feel the crack’s breath—slow, hot, and so deep it takes a full week to exhale.
That breath is the only thing that is not part of the place’s frantic, grinding metabolism. It is the ancient, slow heart of the crack, the reminder that the layered ziggurats, the humming wires, the skittering rock-lice, the people with their dust-smudged faces and quartz-veined skin, are all just a thin, noisy skin stretched over something unimaginably older, hotter, and slower. When you stand on a catwalk at the end of the shift, watching the last Dust-whale drift up toward the plateau’s mouth, its dust tail glinting gold in the sun, you can feel the hum of it in your bones, and you realize you are not just in the crack anymore. You are part of its breath, too.
