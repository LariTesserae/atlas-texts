You stand, and the first thing that registers beyond the vast, eroded horizon is the quiet settling against the soles of your boots. Not the soft squelch of soil, but the faint, dry skritch of gravel shifting under your weight, a sound so quiet it’s almost a sensation in your shins before it reaches your ears. That is the first sign of the plateau’s inhabitants, before you see them at all.

The most immediate are the Silica Skitters, no larger than a fingernail, their chitin plates fused from the very quartz that dusts the gravel plains. They dart in silent, flickering flocks across the stone mosaic, their movements not quite walking, not quite teleporting—vibrational shifts from point to point, so fast the eye barely catches the flash of their faceted eyes, each facet a tiny prism catching the cobalt light and throwing back sparks as sharp as the Weep-Stones’ glitter. They do not flee from you. They pause, cluster on the nearest pitted sandstone outcrop, their thousand tiny eyes tilted toward your warmth, their bodies emitting a sub-audible thrum you feel first in the fillings of your molars, then in the bones of your face. When the wind sighs through the stone corridors miles away, their quartz shells vibrate in sympathy, chiming like a billion tiny wind-bells, a sound so faint it merges with the wind itself, part of the plateau’s constant, quiet song. They are the plateau’s silica couriers: their shells pick up trace obsidian dust from the Spires’ bases as they graze on litho-moss, and when they molt, or die, their chitin is broken down by the mycelial Root-Runners network that threads every inch of the gravel subsoil, the silica carried deep to patch the microfractures spiderwebbing the Spires’ obsidian flanks. You watch one skitter pause to nibble at a patch of litho-moss clinging to a crevice: the moss glows a faint, warm rust-orange as it absorbs the infrared wavelengths invisible to your eyes, and the skitter’s shell flares bright blue for a split second where it touches the glowing growth, as if drinking in more than just sustenance. They are not just feeding, you realize. They are tending.

Lower to the ground, the Gyre-Crawlers ooze in slow, deliberate lines across the red clay, their armoured bodies matte as dried blood, leaving behind trails of iridescent slime that harden within the hour into fragile, hair-thin filaments of glass. They do not crawl randomly. Their paths follow the faint, warm vibrations seeping up from the Spires’ deep foundations, and their glass trails are not waste: they wick the cool mist released by the Weep-Stones toward the shaded rock faces where litho-moss grows, and their conductive filaments carry the faint electrical current of the Root-Runners’ network across the gravel, linking the scattered Weep-Stones and dune patterns into a single, planet-wide circuit. When the wind picks up, the hardened trails chime, high and fragile, like a spiderweb strung with glass beads, a sound that fades as quickly as the wind dies down. If you kneel to look close, the hardened trails are not smooth: they are etched with tiny, regular ridges, like the traces of a circuit board grown instead of printed, carrying data you cannot read, or moisture, or instructions from the Spires’ sub-audible hum. The Crawlers pause when you lean in, their eyestalks tilting toward you, exuding a fresh line of slime that curves around your boot, a fragile glass ring that will harden and break before the wind dies down, a tiny, temporary marker that you were here, recorded in their slow, deliberate chemistry.

The only above-ground sign of the Root-Runners is the occasional silvery, vein-thin tendril that breaches the gravel for a few inches, glowing with the same faint infrared pulse as the litho-moss, before withering back into the dark.
