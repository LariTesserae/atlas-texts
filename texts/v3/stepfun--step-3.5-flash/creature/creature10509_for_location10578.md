You lie first, because the rock invites it—polished rose and taupe, warm as skin despite the damp mist, no sharp edges, no grit, just a seamless curve that cups your back like it was shaped for you. The mountain’s breath washes over you first as a faint, slow vibration in your sternum, then as the soft hiss of mist settling on your face, tasting of wet stone, ozone, and that strange, sweet rot that smells like overripe plums and burnt wire. The hum starts in your molars before you hear it, a sub-audible thrum you feel more than perceive, syncing slow and steady with the faint pulse of violet light edging the fern fronds just over your shoulder.

The basin is bigger than it looks from the plateau rim. At first glance, the mile-wide hollow seems small, its walls only a few hundred meters high, melting into the false horizon of the high plateau so seamlessly you’d miss it entirely if you weren’t standing in it. The scale only hits you when you stand: the fern colonies are the size of small houses, hemispherical islands of black lace dotted across the polished rock, each one humming with life. The skitterers are the first sound you notice when you lift your head—sharp, rhythmic clatter, like a billion tiny castanets, rising from the nearest colony in a swirling, cloud-like swarm. They’re the size of large dogs, six legs clicking on the rock, carapaces glinting iridescent blue and green in the faint light, moving in perfect, unthinking synchrony, no collisions, no mistakes. A larger, slow-moving creature, all knobby joints and thick, armored plates, lumbers between two colonies, and the entire swarm condenses around it in seconds, a wave of clattering chitin that dissolves it so fast you barely see it happen, the nutrients already seeping into the rock before the last of the swarm breaks apart to continue their circuit.

The air is thick with pollen, glittering crystalline dust that catches the light like crushed diamond, coating every inch of bare rock, sticking to your hair and your clothes, and when you breathe it in, the hum in your molars brightens, sharpens, like the basin is tasting you back. The ferns are not just plants: their fronds uncurl in slow, deliberate moves, the violet bioluminescence at their edges pulsing in time with the thrum, and if you stare too long at their fractal patterns, they seem to shift, resolve into moving, impossible mathematical proofs that make your eyes burn if you try to follow them. They grow only where the mineral-rich seepage pools, in dense, velvet colonies that smell like crushed pine and wet metal, their roots digging down into the rock that is already half-tech, half-stone, nanoscale particulate from the ancient artifacts seeping up into their veins, turning their light into something that feels like memory.

The tech is not ruins. You have to look close to see it, at first: a crystalline tor half-buried in the rock a hundred yards from you, a geode-like lump tucked against the basin wall, the smooth, oblate spheroid half-submerged in the central lake. Up close, their geometry makes your eyes slip. Too many axes of symmetry, surfaces that seem to face you from every angle at once, matte iridescent metal that absorbs light instead of reflecting it, warm to the touch, thrumming with the same hum that’s in your molars. The sphere is the size of a small house, a hairline seam running around its equator, oozing a slow, viscous fluid the color of melted pearl and indigo that dissolves into the mist as it hits the lake, fueling the fern glow, seeding the air with the tiny particles that make the pollen glitter. Fern colonies grow out of cracks in the tor, their roots wrapping around its facets, the violet glow seeping into the metal like it’s being absorbed as much as it’s feeding the plant. There are no signs of damage, no broken parts, no signs that these things were ever built, or ever landed, or ever arrived at all. They are just part of the rock now, part of the drainage, part of the breath of the mountain.

The lake is mirror-still, reflecting the bruised purple of the high-altitude sky so clearly you can’t tell where the water ends and the air begins. A single fern grows out of the shallows near the sphere, its roots wrapped around a drip of the viscous fluid, its fronds glowing so bright they cast faint, shifting shadows on the rock that don’t match the shape of the fern above the water. A tiny skitterer, no bigger than your thumb, swims across the water’s surface to nibble at its edge, then scuttles back to the shore, leaving a tiny trail of ripples that take ten minutes to fade, slow as honey, as if the water is reluctant to break its perfect stillness.

The sound is a wall, layered and constant: the hiss of the mist, the clatter of the skitterers, the thin, high shriek of the crossbats that wheel in the warm air currents rising from the rock, their membranous wings fluorescing in the UV flash of the mist-cycle, leaving faint blue-green trails only you can see, shrieking so high it makes your teeth ache before they vanish into the haze. Every few hours, the mountain groans, deep and tectonic, so low you feel it in the marrow of your bones, the rock expanding and contracting as it cools after the day’s warmth. Underneath it all, the thrum never stops, matching the pulse of the ferns, matching the slow breath of the mountain, matching the beat of your own heart after a while, until you can’t tell which is which.

Every 37 hours, the mist thickens, the thrum picks up, and the entire basin shifts. The skitterer swarms lift off in slow, spiraling clouds, following the crossbats, migrating from one tech artifact to the next, their clatter rising to a crescendo before fading as they move over the basin wall. The fern bioluminescence flares bright enough to light the whole hollow, the pollen dust turning the air to glitter, and the sphere’s fluid oozes faster, feeding the surge of life before the cycle slows again. Nothing decays into nothing here. The dead skitterer you find half-buried in the rock is already broken down by tiny, thread-like fungi that grow only on tech-particulate, its nutrients already being pulled up by the fern colony twenty feet away. Even the mist is part of the loop: it seeps from the rock, seeded with the nanoscale dust of the artifacts, falls on the ferns, is eaten by the skitterers, exhaled back into the air, over and over, forever.

You are not an observer here. When you sit on the rock, the skitterers do not scatter; they pause, tilting their segmented heads, clicking their mandibles, as if acknowledging you as another part of the loop, another piece of matter to be cycled. When you stare at the sphere, you do not feel watched by a machine, or a god, or a predator: you feel watched by the whole basin, by the rock that remembers a billion years of wind and silica, by the ferns that hold the tech’s signature in their genes, by the mist that is half-water, half-ancient engineering, by the mountain that has breathed long enough to turn a tomb into a womb. The weirdness of the artifacts does not feel alien here, not anymore. It feels like home, like the kind of home you didn’t know you were missing, warm and loud and alive, a place where nothing is ever lost, where everything that dies becomes something new, where the oldest erosion has worn the world down to something softly, devastatingly new.

When you finally climb back up the basin wall to the plateau, the sound fades, the hum fades, the metallic-sweet taste fades from the back of your throat, and when you look back, you can’t see the hollow anymore. It’s just another worn convexity on the flat, ancient rock, invisible unless you know it’s there. But the hum is still in your molars, faint, persistent, and when you breathe in, you can still taste the ozone and sweet rot, and you know, for the rest of your life, that somewhere on that high, flat mountain, a basin is breathing, and the ferns are glowing, and the skitterers are clattering, and everything is cycling, and you were part of it, for a little while.
