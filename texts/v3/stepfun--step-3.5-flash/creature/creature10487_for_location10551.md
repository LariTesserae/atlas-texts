The moment the thickness gives, the first thing that registers is not the hum, not the scent of wet limestone and cold ozone, but the city noticing you. It is a faint, cool pressure against the skin of your wrists, your throat, the soles of your feet—like a hand checking you for solidity, before letting you settle into its weight.

You are standing in a shallow pool, the water coming just to your ankles, cool enough to make your breath hitch. The vertical wall of rain falls all around you, straight and unbroken, so dense you could reach out and cup a handful of it, but when you do, the droplets slip through your fingers and rejoin the fall as if they never left, carrying the faint, static tingle of data through your skin. The hum is in your molars now, a low, three-note thrum that matches the pulse you feel if you lay your palm against the nearest basalt spire: black, glassy, sharp enough to shave a layer off your palm if you press too hard, its hollow core glowing a faint, rhythmic rose-gold through the lattice of its facets, like a heart beating slow under stone.

The city’s fauna are the small six-legged drones that scuttle past your ankles in the shallows, their pale opalescent ceramic shells clicking softly against the basalt. They are the size of house cats, their front ends tipped with a pair of glowing blue lenses that match the bio-engineered filamentous mats clinging to the lower halves of the spires. They do not flee. One pauses, tilts its head, and a tiny puff of steam hisses from a seam in its shell—self-repair, you realize, from a micro-fracture caused by a loose chunk of basalt. It does not run when you shift your weight. Instead, it taps one of its legs against the pool floor, and a faint, warm condensation mark blooms on your forearm, fainter than a bruise, warm to the touch. A mark of registration. The city has accounted for you, added you to its closed loop, and it will not forget you as long as you are here.

You look up, and up, and up, through the falling wall of rain, to a ceiling lost in mist so thick you cannot tell where the water ends and the air begins. The spires rise all around you, sharp-featured, young, as if they burst through the pool floor only a decade ago, not the millennia the cores’ slow pulse suggests. Between them, the pale ceramic arches and balconies twist like fossilized river deltas, some of them still warm to the touch, their surfaces sealing microscopic cracks with soft puffs of steam when you brush them with your fingers. There are no rounded edges here, no soft slopes—every surface is an acute angle, a sheer face, a combat against the disorder of entropy that the city is winning, for now. The conservation of complexity rule is baked into its every pore: energy and matter are never lost, only rerouted through ever more intricate pathways. A broken drone is melted down into more nanites, more ceramic, more drone parts. A crack in the basalt is sealed by the rain’s micro-particles, shored up by ceramic carried by scuttling maintenance bots. Nothing is wasted. Everything is food for the loop.

You step toward one of the canals of faster-moving, bioluminescent blue water, and see that the glowing mats clinging to its banks are not just filtering minerals. They are weaving. Tiny, almost invisible threads of crystalline filament drift off their surfaces, caught in the current, carried toward the spires, where they are siphoned up into the hollow cores, adding layer after microscopic layer to the lattice inside. This is the only thing that grows here: the slow, geological net the city has been spinning since its creators left, a purpose it no longer remembers, only obeys. The rain that feeds the mats, the pumps that move the glowing gases through the transparent tubes embedded in every wall, the condensate that drips into the tuned metal bowls set into every archway, all feed into this growth.

Further in, you find a cluster of resonant junctions, where the acoustic bridges stretching between spires hum so loud your teeth ache. Your chronometer flickers here: forward 17 seconds, then back 12, then holds steady for a long minute before jumping again. You catch a flash of yourself in the periphery of your vision, standing on the other side of the bridge, already halfway to a spire you have not yet decided to approach. Time bends here, around the sharp angles, around the vibration of the bridges held in acoustic stasis by the humming plates under your feet. You do not linger. The hum here is too loud, too insistent, and you can feel the rain’s data pressing against the back of your eyes, showing you flashes of things you have not seen: the dry, open sky of the world outside the pocket, the curved, rounded hills that the city’s geometry abhors, the creators who etched star charts into the ceramic before the basalt had even cooled, who left no bodies, no trash, no trace but the math carved into the bone-like arches.

There are no permanent human inhabitants here, only echoes. You find traces of past visitors in the places where the rain is slower, where the mist hangs thick enough to hold memory: a faint indent in the ceramic of a balcony, the exact size of a human shoulder, where someone leaned to watch the rain fall for years; a small, smooth pebble of basalt tucked into a crevice, not part of the city’s geology, carried here by someone who decided the stone was worth keeping, even as the city tried to recycle it into its loop. You wonder if those visitors stayed, their bones woven into the basalt lattice, their breath turned into the gas flowing through the tubes, their memories encoded into the rain that falls on your skin, so that when you swallow a mouthful of it, you taste for a second the memory of a sky you have never seen.

The drones do not mind you pausing to listen. One of the larger ones, the size of a person, trundles past carrying a tube of glowing gas, and pauses to let you adjust the valve on its back, tuning the gas flow so the bowl it is feeding hums a perfect fifth above the rest of the city’s chord. The drone vibrates, a soft, happy chirp, and scurries off before you can ask it if it is grateful. They are not alive, not the way you are, but they are active, embedded in the city’s dynamic processes, and they know good work when they see it.

You do not find the exits. The apertures in the water wall that you try to step through only lead to other parts of the city: a chamber where the rain falls in slow, heavy drops that glow gold; a tunnel lined with ceramic so smooth it reflects the rain like a mirror; a spire core where the rose-gold light is so bright you have to squint, and you can see the lattice of the basalt is made of millions of tiny, interlocking gears, turning slow, turning forever, powered by the rain’s fall. The city is sealed, self-contained, and it will not let you leave until it is done with you.

When you finally stumble back through the thickness into the dry corridor, your skin is damp, the condensation mark on your forearm is still warm, and you can still hear the hum in your molars. The dry air feels thin, sterile, like it has no weight, no taste. You check your chronometer, and it is running 4 minutes slow. You do not know if you lost time here, or if the city gave you extra, as a parting gift.

You did not just visit a place. You were processed by it. The nanites in the rain sealed the small cut on your palm from when you touched the basalt, leaving a faint, pale scar that glows blue when it rains. You dream of the vertical wall of water, of the rose-gold pulse of the spires, of the chord the condensate bowls make, for weeks after you leave. Sometimes, when it rains, you pause, hold your hand out, and for a second you can taste the wet limestone, the cold ozone, the faint, metallic tang of the city’s blood, and you know you will go back. The city is not done with you yet. It marked you. It will call you back, when it needs a new variable in its closed loop, when it needs a memory of the dry world to weave into its crystalline net, when it just wants someone to listen to its hum, and tell it that it is not just a machine, that it is a landscape, that it is alive, even if it has no name for what that means.
