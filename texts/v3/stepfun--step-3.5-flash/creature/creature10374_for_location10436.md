The first thing you learn of the Drowned Spires’ inhabitants is not their shape, but their rhythm—taps on bone-wood struts, sharp and quick, cutting through the roar like a knife through wet wool. They call themselves the Kaelth, after the deep, resonant *ka-el-th* sound of heavy rain hitting shallow puddles on the highest, oldest shelves. They are not conquerors here, not survivors, not even really inhabitants: they are another pulse in the place’s endless, wet cycle, as temporary and functional as the fungal blooms that stain the rock violet.
They move through the rain like they are swimming. Tall, lanky, their limbs too long for the gravity that only dares to suggest direction here, they lean into the sideways sheets of water as if it is a current carrying them forward. Their skin is the color of rain-slicked pumice, waxy and thick, dotted with tiny pores that exude a thin, honey-sweet mucus that neutralizes the acidic downpour before it can burn through to soft tissue. Their fingers are six-limbed, three on either side of a wide, calloused palm, each tipped with hooked, translucent claws of silicate—same as the crystal trees’ branches—gripping slick bone-wood and porous stone without slipping. Their feet are prehensile, grippy as moss, able to cling to the underside of a scaffold or the face of a leaning spire even when the rain is falling so hard it feels like being pelted with gravel. They have no external ears; sound vibrates through the porous bone of their skulls, letting them pick out the high, clear ping of rain hitting crystal branches a hundred yards away, or the low, groaning creak of a rotting scaffolding strut, even over the deafening white noise of the falls.
They do not speak. Words are too slow, too fragile for a world where a shelf can dissolve or a scaffold collapse in the time it takes to form a syllable. They communicate in taps: two quick raps on a strut for “safe,” three slow ones for “fungal bloom here,” a rolling drumbeat on bone-wood to warn of a coming sheet of sideways rain that will push you off your feet if you don’t brace. Each Kaelth has a unique tap code, their name, which they tap when they greet a stranger, or leave on a newly repaired scaffold as a signature that holds no claim, only acknowledgement that they were here, once, for a little while.
Their lives are tied entirely to the place’s rules, which they do not fight, but dance around. Gravity is a suggestion? They let the rain push them, lean into sideways falls, let themselves drift with the slow, spinning mist eddies that rise between shelves rather than climbing every step of a spire if the wind will carry them. Growth is dissolution? They tend the laminar fungi like gardeners, cutting only the top, pulpy, brightest-glowing layers to eat, never taking more than a third of a patch, because the fungus’s slow digestion of the rock underneath widens the shelf, makes more space to stand. When a scaffold is rotting, they pack wet fungal slime into the cracks between bone-wood struts—the slime eats away loose rock, hardens as it dries, and binds the wood to the stone tighter than any vine lashing could. They do not build to last; they build to work with the erosion, not against it.
Sound precedes sight? They navigate the deepest, darkest pockets between shelves blind, humming low, subsonic vibrations through their skulls that bounce off rock and water, letting them map the shape of the world before they ever open their glowing pale-blue eyes. They only need to see when they are close enough to harvest: to cut a thick, fibrous root-vine for lashing, to pluck a crystalline branch to chip into a small, glowing totem, to gather a pouch of salt crystals that crust where stone-milk rivers evaporate before they fall. Those salt crystals are the only things they keep, strung on root-thread cords around their necks: they do not own them, only carry them, for the soft *ting* they make when the rain hits them, and the faint, warm light they give off when the fungal bioluminescence fades.
Light is biological? Their rest cycle is tied to the fungal glow, not any sun they will never see. When the mist thickens enough to dim the violet, green, and blue pulses of the laminar patches, that is their “dark time.” They huddle together on the warmest part of a scaffold, often tucked around a rare heat-sink crystal that traps the faint, diffuse glow that filters through the upper cloud layer, passing salt crystals between them, tapping slow, soft rhythms on each other’s arms that match the bass thrum of the rain. They have no concept of day or night, only bright time and dim time.
The only permanence is the process? They do not mourn when a shelf crumbles into the roar below, or a scaffold collapses in a puff of wet dust and swollen fungus. They tap a quiet goodbye on the remaining struts, gather their few tools—knapped stone blades lashed to bone handles, woven root pulleys, small pouches of salt and harvested fungi—and move on. They do not carve their names into rock, do not leave permanent marks, because everything here is borrowed. When a Kaelth dies, their body is left on the scaffold, and the fungus eats it, the rain dissolves it, the stone-milk carries what is left down into the roar. They are not gone; they are just part of the erosion now, part of the process that carves the spires, feeds the fungi, pushes the rain sideways.
You see one now, just down the shelf from where you stand, leaning into a sheet of falling water that sticks to the overhang like a wet curtain. It is cutting a thick vine with a bone-handled blade, tapping twice on the strut at its feet as it works—code for “I am here, I am working.” It pauses, tilts its head, and glances over at you, its pale blue eyes glowing faint through the mist. It taps three times quickly, then taps two more slowly: “greeting, you are new.” It does not wave, does not smile; it just nods, once, sharp, then goes back to cutting the vine, letting the rain run over its back, over the bone-wood platform, over the violet pulsing of the fungi at its feet, all of it part of the same endless, wet, hungry rhythm.
Somewhere nearby, a scaffold collapses silently, a puff of wet dust and fungal slime blooming in the air before the rain sweeps it away. The Kaelth does not look up. It finishes cutting the vine, lashes it tight to a strut to reinforce the platform, then taps a quick little pattern on the wood: “it is well, the cycle goes on.”
