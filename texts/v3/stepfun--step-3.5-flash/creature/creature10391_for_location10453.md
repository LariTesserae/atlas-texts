You swing a leg over the saddle and drop to the rock, and the first thing that hits you is the air—thin, sharp, so dry it rasps the back of your throat, tasting of crushed iron and baked dust even at three thousand meters up, where the sky is so pale blue it looks almost bleached white. The heat is immediate, a dry forty-two degrees that bleeds through your clothes before you’ve even adjusted your pack, sweat beading on your forehead and vanishing before you can brush it away.

You look up, and for a second you lose your breath again, not from the elevation but from the scale. The Shattered Mesas stretch out in every direction, a labyrinth of rust-red sedimentary stone sliced into a thousand vertical strips: slot canyons no wider than your shoulders, knife-edge ridges that taper to a point before dropping into nothing, isolated towers of sandstone that rise hundreds of meters out of the plateau like stone fists. You can see a hundred kilometers out to the hazy horizon, but the view is fractured, broken, by canyon after canyon after canyon, so that even when you can see forever, you feel boxed in, enclosed by rock on all sides.

You shift your weight, and your boot grits against packed sandstone—no loose scree, no shifting gravel, every inch of rock here settled, anchored, as if the land finished deciding how it wanted to be shaped ten thousand years ago and hasn’t budged since. To your left, the narrow shelf you’re standing on drops two hundred meters straight down into a slot canyon so narrow the sun only reaches the bottom for an hour a day. To your right, a sheer wall of layered orange and cream siltstone rises a hundred meters, the gap between it and your shelf so narrow you could almost jump it, if you were stupid enough to try. There is no empty ground here; every square meter is either a cliff face, a fissure, or a precarious perch, the whole region saturated with verticality.

You kneel to run a finger over the rock at your feet, and your nail catches on something black, crusty, rough as fired ceramic. It’s the Stone Moss, spilled across the stone like ink, petrified as it grows, fusing to the rock so tightly you can’t pry a flake off. It adds a millimeter of density every decade, slow as erosion, slow as memory. You spot a tiny glyph carved into the sandstone half-buried under the moss—one of the Engineer-Monks’ geometric signs, half-erased, the moss slowly swallowing the writing whole. As you watch, a fist-sized beetle trundles out from under a flake of the moss: a Lithovore, its carapace the exact color of rusted iron, mandibles whirring soft as grinding sand as it chews into a soft layer of siltstone, digesting the mineral-bacteria living in the rock grain. It doesn’t glance at you, doesn’t pause, used to things far too large to notice it. Its scuttle is the sound of sand grinding together, quiet enough to miss if you’re not paying attention.

You straighten up, and your eye catches a flicker of movement on the wall across the cleft. At first you think it’s just a patch of discolored sandstone, then it blinks: a pale, vertical slit of a pupil, set in skin the exact shade of the orange rock it’s pressed against. A Cliff-Drake, perfectly still, waiting for a moth or a grasshopper to wander too close. It can hold that pose for days, you know, blending into the stone so well you could walk past it three times and never see it. You hold your breath, and for a second you feel a low rumble in your chest, subsonic, so quiet you can barely hear it over the wind. It’s the drake’s call, felt more than heard, the same low, steady buzz you’ll feel in your teeth later from the Great Spire. It flicks a forked tongue, loses interest in you, and goes back to being a rock.

The sun crests the eastern mesa, and suddenly the canyons below you start to fill with fog—not the soft, wet fog of lowlands, but the Ghost Miasma, cold, dense, mineral-heavy, welling up from deep fissures in the earth. It clings to the stone like a second skin, so thick you can’t see the bottom of the slot canyon ten meters below, and it smells of wet iron and deep, cold rock. This is the only dampness the surface ever knows: rain falls less than once a year, in violent, twenty-minute thunderstorms that evaporate or vanish into the porous rock within hours, but the miasma comes from the deep, a ghost of water that haunts the canyons until mid-morning, when the heat burns it away. You watch it creep up the shelves, swallowing the sharp edges of the cliffs, turning the whole world soft and grey, then the sun hits full, and it evaporates as fast as it came, leaving the stone slick with condensation, tiny beads of water beading on the surface of the Stone Moss and vanishing before you can touch them.

Down in the deepest, most shaded cleft where the mist lingers longest, you spot a stand of Canyon-Willows, their white, smooth bark glowing like bleached bone in the dim light, their tiny grey scale leaves folded tight against moisture loss. They’re so gnarled and twisted they look less like trees and more than old, bent figures waiting for something. If you had been here three days ago, after the last rare rain, you’d have seen the Root-Walkers blooming next to them: thick, leathery rhizomes that snake through the rock cracks, following the hidden water veins underground, sprouting clusters of tiny copper-colored flowers that only last a single day before curling up and vanishing back into the stone. You can’t see the flowers now, but if you dig at the soil at the base of a willow, you’ll find the thick, leathery roots, and maybe a few shriveled copper petals, proof they were ever here at all.

A soft whoosh of air makes you look up, and you see a Sky-Fisher gliding between two mesas a kilometer away, its manta-ray wings spread wide, riding the thermals without moving a muscle. It dips into the last wisp of Ghost Miasma, filtering microscopic organisms from the mist, its passage so silent you didn’t hear it coming, didn’t hear it go, until it disappears over the next fin of rock, leaving no trace but the faint displacement of air. It rules the heights, you know, no predators up where the air is too thin for anything else, no need to ever touch the stone.

You follow the shelf around a bend, and find the first of the Engineer-Monks’ leavings: a Stone-Bellied Furnace, half-swallowed by the cliff face, its door sealed with welded stone blocks, no handle, no visible crack. The Monks built these five hundred years ago, to “read the stone” and tap the planet’s geothermal heart, but they never finished their work, or the work finished them. You press your palm to the cold stone of the door, and you can feel a faint, steady heat pulsing through it, like the furnace is holding a breath that’s lasted centuries. Nearby, a stretch of the Monks’ aqueduct carves a perfect, polished channel through the top of the mesa, its lines so precise you could set a level on them and not see a wobble. Most of it is broken, chunks of stone fallen out into the canyon below, but every few meters a thin trickle of mineral-sweet water seeps through a crack in the stone, running down to feed the pocket springs hidden in the fissures. Carved into the wall above the aqueduct are the Monks’ glyphs, dense geometric spirals and diagrams of thermodynamic cycles, half-buried under Stone Moss. You stare at one depicting a heat cycle for too long, and the lines seem to shift, the spiral unwinding into a picture of the Great Spire, then a pocket spring, then one of the Burrowers’ perfect circular boreholes, then looping back to a spiral again. When you blink, it’s just a glyph again, but the edges of it are wet, even in the full sun, as if the stone is weeping.

You find a wider stretch of shelf, and there it is, across the central canyon: the Great Spire, a needle of fused black rock and metal thrusting a kilometer up out of the central mesa, so tall its top disappears into the atmospheric haze. It is the only part of the Monks’ work that still half-functions, a geothermal tap drawing heat from the planet’s core, feeding it to nothing, nowhere, just humming. Even from ten kilometers away, you can feel its pulse in your teeth, a steady, low thrum that vibrates through the rock under your boots, the same frequency as the Cliff-Drakes’ calls, the same frequency as the thump of the Burrowers far below. No one knows why it still hums. No one knows if the Monks intended it to, or if it just decided to keep going, on its own.

You spend the afternoon picking your way along the shelves, following the trickle of water from the aqueduct down into a cleft so narrow you have to turn sideways to squeeze through. At the bottom is a pocket spring, a small pool of water so saturated with dissolved minerals it’s almost syrup, its surface covered in a shifting, iridescent microbial veil that glows faint gold in the dim light. The Ghost Miasma is trapped here, never burning off, the air cool and wet against your sun-warmed skin. A Canyon-Willow grows right next to the pool, its white bark streaked with orange mineral stains, and you press your hand to the rock wall next to it, and you feel it: a deep, slow thump, felt through the stone, so slow it’s almost a heartbeat. The Burrowers. You’ll never see them, never hear them, only feel them, moving through the wet, dark fissures far below, leaving their perfect, smooth circular boreholes in the soft stone walls as proof they exist. They are the only things that live deeper than the Monks ever dug.

As the sun dips low, painting the mesas the color of dried blood, the wind picks up, whispering through the canyons in layered, shifting tones, like voices half-remembered, like the stone itself is talking. The drip of condensation from the overhangs starts up, each plink echoing off the canyon walls, a slow liquid clock that counts centuries instead of minutes. You sit on the edge of the shelf, your legs dangling over the drop, and for a second you can’t tell what’s alive and what isn’t. The Stone Moss is growing, so slow you can’t see it, but it is. The Spire is humming, steady, unbroken. The Cliff-Drakes are waiting on the walls, the Sky-Fishers are riding the thermals, the Burrowers are thumping in the dark. The rain will come once a year, violent and brief, then vanish, the Ghost Miasma will well up at dawn tomorrow, the copper flowers will bloom
