The first inhabitants you will notice are the ones so small they seem part of the rock itself.

Thin, iridescent biofilms line the interior of every steam conduit and fissure wall, chemosynthetic mats with no will, no sensory apparatus, no purpose beyond metabolizing trace sulfides in the weeping slurry and excreting a silicate lubricant that keeps the continent-spanning pipe network from seizing under the thrum’s constant vibration. They do not grow in any way you can perceive; they accrete, layer by infinitesimal layer, as the slurry flows past them, their cell walls tuned to the exact harmonic of the world’s hum. Touch them, and they do not recoil; they simply smear, leaving the same faint silvery stain on your skin that will vanish in an hour as the ambient humidity evaporates it, no trace left behind.

Carpeting the ground between the iron-wood groves is the deep-purple moss, the only life form that dares (if that word has meaning here) to pulse. Its bioluminescent glow is not a signal, not a lure, not a warning: it is the byproduct of its metabolic cycle, the faint blue light of electrons firing as it breaks down the colloidal metal in the weeping fluid into oxidized ore nodules that rain down to harden into the raw material the buried reactors melt down to feed the entire system. The pulse is perfectly synced to the sub-auditory thrum, brightening and dimming a fraction of a percent every 3.2 seconds, the exact interval of the world-machine’s breath. Step on it, and it yields, oozing silvery slurry up to coat your boot, then flows back into place within the hour, its cellular structure knitting together with no fuss, no repair response, no awareness that it was ever disturbed. The cloying, overripe-sweet note in the air comes from its waste: a volatile, faintly alcoholic byproduct of its infrared photosynthesis, drifting on the fixed wind channels to mix with the scent of cold iron and hot solder, the only hint that anything here was ever “organic” at all.

From every wide fissure in the black basalt grow the metallic fronds: delicate, fractal structures of crystallized metal, accreted grain by grain from the weeping slurry, their shape dictated entirely by the vibration lines of the thrum. They never grow toward light, never turn to face the sun; they grow only along the pressure waves of the world’s hum, so every frond in the basin is an exact, fractal match to every other, their edges feathered with frost that melts and refreezes in fixed, repeating patterns as the thrum’s friction warms the metal just enough to keep the frost thin. They grow at a rate of 0.3 millimeters a year, so slow you will never see them lengthen, but over millennia they drape the fissure walls in veils of silver so fine they shatter if you brush them, only to regrow in the exact same shape within a decade.

Towering above the fronds are the iron-woods: trunk-like columns of braided rust-hued iron oxide, accreted around slow seeps of superheated steam from the conduits below. Their surfaces are sheathed in the gray-green lichens that give the basin its only hint of green, broad, leaf-like plates that do not photosynthesize so much as resonate: their cell walls are laced with tiny quartz crystals that vibrate in perfect sympathy with the world thrum, acting as a distributed sensory organ that keeps the machine’s rhythm stable, correcting micro-fluctuations before they can throw the frozen equilibrium out of balance. They do not compete for the narrow infrared light filtering through the mineral haze; they are spaced exactly 2,700 yards apart, the exact distance their canopy needs to avoid overlapping, no crowding, no struggle, no wasted energy. Their sap is a slow-dripping slurry of iron and trace minerals that hardens into ore nodules when it hits the ground, and when an iron-wood’s trunk eventually snaps under the weight of its own accumulated growth, it does not decay: it simply becomes a new fissure, weeping slurry that feeds new moss, new fronds, new lichens, no loss, no waste, no end.

There is no other life. No insects, no birds, no things that move with will. The only motion is the slow, millimeter-per-year creep of the metallic fronds, the infinitesimal shift of lichen crystals when the ordered lightning bolts from the distant transmission towers arc to the ground to tap the system’s rhythm back into tune, the fixed drift of metallic dust particles in the wind channels, carrying lichen spores and colloidal metal to seed new growth in the weeping fissures. There is no death here, not really. If you scrape a patch of lichen off a rock, the remaining edge will grow back over the bare stone in the exact same pattern, no mutation, no variation, as if it never was disturbed. If you cut down an iron-wood, the stump will keep weeping slurry, growing moss and lichen on its cut surface, just as it would have if you had never been there.

This is not an ecology of survival, or competition, or adaptation. It is an ecology of maintenance, perfectly synced to the machine that birthed it, indistinguishable from it. The radiator fins terraced into the basin walls are crusted with purple moss, their seams leaking steam that feeds new iron-woods. The Six-Sided Pillars jutting from the ground are encrusted with lichens, their pitted metal surfaces no longer distinguishable from the surrounding rock. The conduits that carry the weeping fluid across the continent are lined with the same iridescent biofilm that grows in the wild fissures. There is no line between the "built" world and the "living" one, because the life here grew from the machine’s waste, and the machine runs only because the life here maintains it: a closed loop so tight no one remembers where the infrastructure ends and the biology begins. It has been this way for so long that nothing remembers what it was like to be either, only to hum, to pulse, to weep, to exist, forever, in the sealed chamber of the frozen thrum.
