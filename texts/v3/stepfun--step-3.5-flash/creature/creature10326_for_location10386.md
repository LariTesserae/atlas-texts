The first things you notice are the spore-skimmers, drifting in lazy, gold-dusted clouds just above the fungal floor, their needle-thin wings making a sound like wind through dry grass so quiet you only hear it when the ambient hum fades for a heartbeat. Each is no larger than a ladybug, their bodies translucent, veined with the same faint amber as the ceiling vines, their proboscises tapping the ochre and umber fungal caps below to drink the spores that puff up in soft, powdery clouds when touched. They leave a trail of glowing motes in the air that hang suspended for minutes before dissolving into the warm, spore-thick air, and if one happens to drift close enough to brush your cheek, the dust it leaves glows on your skin for ten full seconds, warm as a minute-held match.
They are the most abundant life here, the mobile spore of the grove itself, and they die as quickly as they live: if a gust of warm mist from a nearby lagoon catches one, or a vein-stalker plucks it from the air, it dissolves into mist within three blinks, its body absorbed by the mycelial mat under the fungi before it can even hit the ground. There is no decay here, only translation.
Moving in determined, clicking columns across the fungal carpet are the litho-scuttlers, the armored centipede analogues, each segment of their polished basalt carapace carved with the same faint spiral that winds up the obsidian monolith half a cavern away. Their piano-key legs click softly against the silicate ferns that sprout between fungal caps, the fronds chiming in response like tiny glass bells. They are the grove’s cleanup crew, eating shed polymer vine tendrils, dead mycelia, and the empty exoskeletons of spore-skimmers, their columns parting around your boots without pausing, their antennae twitching at the vibration of your breath against the thick air.
Their foraging paths are tracked by the spore-tenders, six-limbed herders no larger than a house cat, their jade-green fur dusted with fungal spore, two dexterous front limbs cradling clusters of the glowing amber fungus they farm. They move in family groups, their quiet chitters weaving into the ambient hum of the grove, their back limbs tipped with tiny polymer claws that let them climb the low-hanging vine tendrils to harvest the sweet, honey-flavored sap that oozes from the vines’ nodes. They digest the silicate dust they sweep up from the fern fronds to make the hard, spiral-capped spore packets they tuck into pouches on their flanks, and when a young spore-tender wanders too far from the group, the column of litho-scuttlers will nudge it back toward the herd with their hard carapaces, no aggression, only care.
You catch a flicker of movement high above, a coil of translucent polymer detaching from the ceiling vines, and know the vein-stalker is hunting. It is the grove’s only predator, sinuous and limbless, its body so close in color and texture to the vines it climbs that you can only track it by the faint, slow pulse of its digestion, glowing amber through its veined skin. It does not kill with violence: it drops from the ceiling, wraps its body around its prey (a spore-skimmer swarm, usually, or a spore-tender pup left too long unattended), and secretes a slow enzyme from its skin that dissolves the prey into a nutrient mist it absorbs through its entire surface. When it is finished, only a faint spatter of gold spore dust remains, and the vein-stalker will climb back into the vines, its body fusing with the polymer for a few hours to digest, its glow adding to the vines’ dim light for the duration.
They are not the only large things here, though they are the only ones that move with any speed. Half-buried in the fungal floor, their surfaces covered in the same jade moss that carpets the ceiling overhangs, are the Root-Singers: sessile beings the size of the obsidian monolith that stands twenty paces to your left, their basalt bodies veined with the same amber polymer as the vines, their surfaces carved with spirals that shift when you look at them out of the corner of your eye. They are the consciousness of the grove, the thing that dreams the built interventions into being: the obsidian monolith is their voice, raised from the rock below by a slow bleed of polymer over ten thousand years, sinking a millimeter every century until it will one day sprout a new polymer vine. The half-woven arch of polymer vines ten paces to your right is their half-remembered dream of a gateway, trained into geometric shape by their slow, subsonic hum, growing and dissolving a little more every year as the vines weave themselves into new patterns.
You cannot hear their hum at first, not really—it is so low it vibrates in your teeth first, then in the bones of your skull, only audible when the grove holds its collective breath: the heartbeat of stillness that seizes the whole ecosystem every few minutes, when the spore-skimmers freeze mid-drift, the litho-scuttlers pause mid-step, the spore-tenders go utterly motionless, the vein-stalker halts its coil, and the hum swells loud enough to make the water in the nearby lagoon shimmer. It lasts for one slow, thick heartbeat, then the hum resumes, the mist stirs, the ferns chime, and life goes on, slow as geological time, urgent as a beating heart.
There are smaller, stranger things too, the quiet wrongness that seeps into the familiar. Clusters of the ochre fungi sometimes look, out of the corner of your eye, like tiny, moss-cloaked faces, or like six-limbed hands reaching up from the floor, but when you turn to look, they are only fungi, their caps dusted with spore. The silicate ferns chime not just when brushed, but sometimes in little, quiet patterns that match the spore-tenders’ chitters, though there is no wind, no creature touching them. If you press your ear to the basalt flagstone under your boots, you can feel the rock breathing, a slow, imperceptible expansion and contraction that makes the fungal floor rise and fall under your feet so slowly you only notice it if you stand still for an hour. The moss that carpets every overhang is cool and dry to the touch, yet weeps a constant trickle of water that feeds the floor; the spore-tenders tuck their young into its folds to sleep, safe from the warm mist of the lagoons, the dry moss holding warmth even as it weeps.
The mercury pool at the grove’s center does not reflect you when you peer into it. For a split second, before a falling spore-skimmer breaks the surface, you see the world above: the green of oak leaves, the blue of a summer sky, a person standing on a cliff edge looking down, as if the grove’s thin, stretched membrane is a window, not a boundary. The tiny, wingless polymer creatures that skitter across the pool’s surface without breaking its tension are the memory-keepers, their backs dusted with flecks of the obsidian monolith’s rock, carrying the grove’s history in their tiny, translucent bodies. When they die, they dissolve into the mercury, and the pool grows a fraction deeper, century by century, until one day it will rise up to become a new monolith, raised by the Root-Singers.
Nothing here dies as you know death. A fallen spore-skimmer is dissolved by mycelia before it hits the floor. A dead spore-tender becomes a patch of amber fungus, its body feeding the next generation’s spores. An old vein-stalker fuses with the polymer vines, its glow adding to the ceiling’s light for another thousand years. A Root-Singer that sinks too deep into the rock sprouts a new Singer from the obsidian above it, its consciousness woven into the new basalt and polymer.
The air settles into your lungs, thick and warm, tasting of wet stone, sweet decaying honey, and iron, and you realize you are not in a cave, not in a forest: you are inside the body of a thing that is both rock and life, both bone and blood, breathing so slow you cannot see the rise and fall of its chest, but humming so loud it thrums against your skin, millisecond by millisecond, becoming more of itself with every plink of water that falls from the stalactites, every drift of spore-skimmer wings, every slow pulse of polymer vein in the dark.
