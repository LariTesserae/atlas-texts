You feel the plateau before you see it.

The first thing is not the wind, not exactly—it is the infrasound, a sub-audible thrum that lives in your sternum, vibrates the fillings in your teeth, makes the fine hairs on your arms stand straight up with static. You have to clench your jaw to stop it from rattling, to keep from biting through your tongue when the first gust of the Scrape hits: a wall of high, screaming grit, so loud it drowns out your own heartbeat, so abrasive it sandblasts the skin on your cheeks raw in minutes if you are not wrapped tight in lichen-fur and oiled leather.

When you lift your head, the light is so sharp it makes your eyes water. The air is thin, dry as old bone, no haze to soften the edges of the world: shadows are absolute, star-black cuts against the pale, lavender-tinged sheen of the fused obsidian regolith. The rock is shot through with milky conchoidal fractures, and when the sun hits them just right, the whole plateau blazes with internal rainbows—faint, shifting arcs of color that crawl across the rock faces for ten minutes at dawn and dusk, so bright you have to squint, so quiet you could miss them if you are not looking. The air vibrates with the static of the wind, and if you hold your hand out, you can feel the faint, constant tingle of charge against your skin, like the air is holding its breath before a storm that never breaks.

The ground under your boots is springy, a sintered crust of compacted grit and frozen brine, baked hard by the wind over millennia, but brittle enough that a heavy step cracks it like thin ice. Through the fissures oozes a slow, constant weep of salt and methane clathrate, freezing instantly into spires of white, porous ice: the breath-towers. At night, when the temperature plummets, they grow, piling up into structures as tall as cathedrals, lacy and fragile, their walls honeycombed with holes that sing high, thin flutes when the wind catches them. By noon, they are gone, sublimated into mist, leaving nothing behind but a faint halo of darker, damper rock, and a little extra salt in the crust. The lichens eat that salt. They are the only greenless plants here, crusts of black, rust, and ash that cling to the rock in patches too faint to notice from a stride away, endolithic tendrils weaving through the obsidian, digesting mineral with slow, acidic sweat. Their productivity is unimpressive by lowland standards—they grow a millimeter a century—but they cover millions of square miles, a slow, crusty stain eating the rock into dust, turning the plateau, grain by grain, into the only soil it will ever have.

You have to hunker behind a ventifact ridge to get out of the worst of the Scrape, and it is there you first notice the Skitterers.

They are no bigger than your thumb, their chitinous shells plated with chips of the local lavender rock, fluted grooves carved into their carapaces that match the wind-carved ridges of the stone around them. They do not walk. They vibrate. Specialized organs in their thoraxes hum in sympathy with the wind’s pressure waves, lifting them a millimeter above the rock, letting them surf the gusts, leaping meters between fins and boulders with no visible propulsion, their movements so fast they look like teleportation. You hear nothing of them at first—their skittering is utterly inaudible beneath the wind’s scream—but you feel a faint tickle on the back of your hand, and look down to see one perched on your knuckle, its tiny, many-faceted eyes scanning your skin, its legs tingling against your leather glove like a cat’s purr. If you move too fast, it flicks itself three meters to the left and vanishes behind a rock, leaving only the faintest hum in the stone where it stood. If you wait, still, you will see a dozen more, then a hundred, skittering up the face of a breath-tower as it grows at dusk, their shells glinting like tiny chips of obsidian, the whole tower humming a little higher when they land, a collective vibration so faint you can only feel it through the rock under your palms.

Larger shapes move at the edge of your vision next: the Basin-Stalkers. Low-slung, broad-shouldered quadrupeds, their fur matted with lichen dust so perfect they blend into the rock like a stain. Their sweeping, fan-like ears are not for hearing, but for cancelling: they can phase-lock the membranes to the wind’s dominant frequency, carving a cone of near-silence around their heads, muffling the Scrape to a dull roar, letting them hear the faint, inaudible skitter of the Skitterers over the rocks. You catch the scent of them first—yeast and wet iron, the smell of the lichens they roll in to camouflage themselves—then the tips of their ears peeking over a ventifact ridge, then their whole body, motionless, watching you. Their small, dark eyes are fixed on you, their jaws working slowly, and through the hum of their ear-cancellation, you can hear the faint crunch of lichen under their teeth. They do not hunt you. They hunt the Skitterers, picking them off the rock when the wind dies, or when they find a pocket of acoustic calm. They are curious, though, and will stand and watch you for hours, motionless as the stone they stand on, until you step too close. Then they swivel their ears back, and the wind slams into you so hard it almost knocks you off your feet, and they are gone, vanished into the rock like a trick of the light.

The most profound thing you will ever see out here, though, is the Drift-Whales.

You find them in the deepest, most wind-sheltered basins, where the brine seeps pool into shallow, bitter lakes that never freeze, even in the coldest part of the plateau’s long night. They are aero-aquatic, their gelatinous, translucent bodies filled with lighter-than-air methane siphoned from the seepage, letting them drift a meter above the lake surface, trailing kilometer-long, feathery tendrils that sweep back and forth, filtering microbes from the moist air. They are the size of a lowland bus, their bodies streaked with faint blue veins that pulse slow as a heartbeat, and they have no eyes, no mouth, only a sensory patch on their underside that picks up vibration. You cannot hear them at all—their communication is infrasound, pulses that harmonize with the mountain’s base drone, felt as a shift in the pressure of the air rather than a sound. Stand still long enough, and you will feel a faint thrum in your chest that is not the wind, not the mountain settling: it is their song, slow, low, a language of pressure that has not changed in a million years.

If you are lucky, you will see a pod of them at dusk, five or six of them drifting over the brine lake, their tendrils tangled together like old friends gossiping. They do not flee when they sense you—their vibrational senses register you as too small, too insignificant, a grain of grit on the wind. They only twitch when the organ-pipe booms roll across the plateau, sudden cannon-like reports as pressure equalizes in hidden caverns, the mountain’s thunder in a cloudless sky. When the boom hits, they dip lower, their tendrils brushing the brine surface, and the hum of their song shifts, just a little, like they are bowing their heads.

The wind is sovereign here, after all. Nothing moves fast against it. Nothing grows fast enough to notice. The only construction is its own: the parallel stone fins that stand in rows like the ribs of a dead giant, the ventifacts carved with perfect, fluted grooves aligned to the prevailing wind, the accidental cathedral formed by two fins spaced just so that the wind whistles a pure, sustained chord through the gap, a note so clear it can be heard ten kilometers away. Lowland peoples make pilgrimages to this place, hiking for weeks across the glassy rock in their insulated suits, to sit in the cathedral and let the note clear their heads of the noise of the world below. No one built it. No one maintains it. It is a temple made of friction, a song made of wind, older than any bone, older than any memory.

You will not find silence here. Even if you stuff your ears with wax, even if you find the quietest crevice between two rock fins, you will feel the mountain sighing under its feet, the slow, seismic groan of the continent-sized slab of rock shifting a fraction of a millimeter on its permafrost cushion, a sound so slow it is a harmonic of the wind, the foundation note to all the chaos. You will feel the Skitterers humming through the rock, the Drift-Whales singing their pressure songs, the wind scraping at the stone, the breath-tower ice singing its high, thin flute as it melts.

Time out here is not measured in hours or years. It is measured in the growth of a breath-tower, the slow grind of a rock fin, the thousand-year drift of a Drift-Whale pod across the plateau. If you leave a marker here, a stack of ventifact rock, it will be gone in a year, sandblasted to dust, or knocked over by a gust, or swallowed by lichen. If you come back in ten years, the shape of the fins will be a little different, a new breath-tower will be growing in a fissure that did not exist before, a Drift-Whale will have died in a new basin, its body dissolving into the brine to feed a patch of lichen that will glow a little brighter than the rest for a century.

The space between presences is not empty. It is packed with sound, with dust, with static, with the ghosts of wind-carved shapes that have already been sanded away, with the vibration of a world that does not speak, but sings. You are not in a quiet place. You are in a place where the very air is solid noise, and life has learned to dance on its waves, to hide in its pockets, to sing its hymns in a language of pressure and friction, under a sky that never rains, only booms.
