They are called the Stewards, though they have no name for themselves beyond the singular, whispered syllable for “here.” They do not live in the Halcyon Steppes; they are a function of it. Their bodies are adapted to the damp chill and the thin air—lean and long-limbed, with pale, damp skin that never seems to sweat or chill. Their eyes are large, accustomed to the pearl-grey light, and they move with a plant-like slowness, as if grown from the very moss they tend.

Their culture is a closed loop of observation and infinitesimal adjustment. They wear garments of felted moss-fiber and woven reed, dyed with the same violet and bottle-green as the life they cultivate. In the eternal mist, these clothes never dry, and they wear the faint, clean scent of wet stone as a perfume. They speak in hushed, sibilant tones that do not carry, their language composed more of gesture and shared gaze than of words. A certain tilt of the head toward a specific crack, a linger of fingers over a patch of moss, a deliberate sequence of steps across a flagged path—these are their full and complete sentences. Myth, history, and law are not told; they are embedded in the angle of an aqueduct’s carved channel and the spacing of the terraced plots.

They are born, age, and die within the network of walls and courtyards, and when one dies, the body is wrapped in moss-cloth and placed in a shallow depression between two great slabs, to be reabsorbed by the damp, sterile rock. There is no grave, no marker; the place simply remembers the shape for a time, then forgets. No Steward recalls a predecessor. Time is not a line but a constant, accumulated present.

Their technology is entirely biological and geological. Tools are shaped from the sharp, black glass—scrapers, awls, delicate probes for testing moss-root depth. They know the precise weight and balance of each fragment. Cordage is made from the fibrous inner bark of the succulent plants. They trade in water-rights and sun-angles (the brief, diffuse moments when the overcast sky thins), not as commodities, but as shared calibrations for their silent, collective task.

They do not farm the moss; they converse with it. Each small, lush island is a tended thought. They know every individual cushion’s preference for shade or the slow, greedy bleed of a particular fissure. They clear away the occasional pale, sickly growth and encourage the vibrant violet with a whisper of crushed orange-bloom. The tiny explosions of color are not for beauty—they are signals, read like a language. A bloom’s intensity and hue tell them of mineral content in the weeping water, of a crack’s depth, of a coming shift so slow it will take a generation to perceive. The Stewards adjust their aqueducts and redistribute their plots in response, not to a forecast, but to a present tense that has already changed.

Sometimes, a pool’s surface will develop a hairline crack, or a silt of microscopic rock-dust will cloud its perfection. The Stewards will gather without sound, and for days, they will perform a silent ballet: siphoning the water with reed tubes into temporary moss-lined basins, cleaning the depression with polished glass scrapers until the dark rock gleams, then returning the water drop by drop, watching until the mirror is absolute again. The purpose is not utilitarian—the pools hold no drinking water. Their stillness is the still point upon which the entire silent world turns. To let a pool cloud or crack would be to allow a thought to become a nightmare. Their entire existence is the act of keeping the dream unchanging.

They do not look at the sheer drop to the cloud-sea. To look *out* would be to admit the existence of an “elsewhere,” a concept that would unravel the perfect, closed logic of the cracks and the pools. Their universe is the warmth under their bare feet, the mist on their faces, the slow pulse of water in stone. They are the consciousness of the Halcyon Steppes—a consciousness that asks no questions, seeks no horizon, and whose only prayer is the endless, quiet maintenance of a world that already, and eternally, is.
