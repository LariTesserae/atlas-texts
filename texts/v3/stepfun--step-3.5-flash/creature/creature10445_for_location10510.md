You first notice the silence before you notice anything else. It is not empty: it has a weight, a soft, granular texture that coats the back of your throat and hums in the fillings of your teeth, thick with low-frequency vibrations you feel in your shins before you register them as sound. It is the sound of a newborn planet cooling, slow and deep, slower than a heartbeat, so slow you only notice it when you go still.

Your boots sink a little into the young basalt, still warm from the geothermal vents that seethe under the high plateau. Patches of the rock are still glassy, cooled obsidian shards that glint sharp and black under the diffused, prismatic light, cut by thin veins of opal that glow pale pearlescent where the sun hits them, like the ground is stitched with threads of diluted milk. The air smells of hot stone and sharp ozone, undercut with a faint, sweet, green scent that comes from the moss, not from water, not from any plant you recognize. There is no smell of rain, no damp earth, only that sharp, sweet, mineral tang, warm against your skin.

The forest rises around you before you realize it is a forest at all. The Silica Bowers are not trees, but great, cathedral-scale mounds and arches of consolidated lithophytic moss, layered thousands of years deep, held rigid by silica filaments that feel halfway between soft felt and solid rock when you press your hand to them. They range in pewter and deep, saturated emerald, the older growths dark with age, the newer patches bright, almost iridescent where fresh steam condenses on their surfaces. The arches twist and lean into each other, crowding the plain, their surfaces dotted with waxy, translucent succulents that funnel the rare condensed mist (born of geothermal steam, not rain) into tiny, opalescent internal bladders, each one holding a bead of water that will last them for months. The light filters through the bower canopies in soft, smudged greens and blues, no sharp shadows anywhere, because the silica dust in the air diffuses every beam of sunlight into a gentle, hazed glow that makes the distant landscape look like it’s viewed through a faintly diffraction-grated lens. The horizon glows faintly prismatic, pink and gold and blue at sunrise, pale silver at noon, deep violet at dusk, no hard line between ground and sky.

The first sign of the forest’s life is the drift of Dustwings: a cross between mayfly and silicate shard, their wings layered sheets of iridescent mica that catch the light so they flicker like tiny, floating fragments of rainbow. They make no sound as they fly, their wings vibrating the air at a frequency just outside the range of human hearing, so you only feel the faint tickle of them against your cheek if they pass close. They feed on the moss spores, which puff up in soft, silent clouds when they land, leaving tiny dents in the moss crust that heal in a few hours.

Basking on a nearby obsidian outcropping is a Glassback skink, its scales interlocking plates of clear, horny silicate that refract the sunlight into a scatter of tiny rainbows across the basalt when it moves. Its blood pulses faint, pale blue under its transparent skin, and it blinks at you with nictitating membranes before turning its head to face the direction of a faint, subaudible click: a Vent mole, blind and sleek, burrowing through the moss bed under your boots. You feel the micro-seismic pops of its digging in your ankles first, tiny ticks that are the only distinct, non-hummed sound in this quiet world, before you hear the faint fizz of its claws breaking through the fungal filaments of the moss. The food web here is tight, compressed, every niche filled: the moles break up the moss to spread its spores, the skinks eat the dustwings, the Architects harvest mineral-rich vapor from the vents, no waste, no hunger, just endless, slow cycling.

It is only when you round a tall bower arch that you see the Architects’ city, woven so seamlessly into the forest you almost miss it. The Architects are eusocial, termite-like creatures no larger than your thumb, their chitin the same iridescent pearlescent as the opal veins in the ground. They do not build with tools: they secrete a powerful, gentle organic acid that etches soft, young basalt into smooth, rounded chambers, no sharp edges anywhere, their “construction” grown as much as carved. The city is a dense cluster of these etched mounds, connected by arched tunnels, layered over thousands of years: collapsed old chambers at the base, new ones etched above, each level grooved with complex, resonant patterns that tune the slow seismic hum of the planet’s core, so the mounds hum softly against the rock they’re carved into, like a cat purring. They weave the silica moss into the grooves of their chambers, so the structures are half stone, half living forest, no line between the built and the grown. You see a swarm of them working the side of a fresh basalt outcrop, their tiny claws clicking against the rock as they etch a new chamber, moving in perfect, smooth synchronicity, as if they are all dancing to the same rhythm you can only feel in your bones.

The strangeness of the place seeps into you slowly, like the warm air. The silica in the air and on the ground gilds everything in a faint, constant prismatic glow, so even the dark basalt looks soft, edged with silver where the light hits it. The silence is not an absence, but a saturation: every sound that has ever been made here lingers, caught in the silver-pale patches of Echo-Moss that grow in the grooves of the Architect mounds and the crevices of the bower arches. If you brush your hand against a patch of it, you hear a delayed, faint echo: the distant hum of dustwings swarming at dawn three days prior, the tiny fizz of a mole burrowing an hour ago, the soft, harmonic hum of the Architects tuning their mounds at dusk. They use the moss as natural memory recorders, you realize: the grooves carved into the basalt hold the echoes of the colony’s history, every new chamber etched holding the sound of the day it was made, layered over thousands of years of slow, quiet life.

There is no wind here, no rain, no water at all, save for the mist that condenses from the geothermal steam that vents constantly from cracks in the basalt. The cycles are slow, measured in years, not days: every few months a pulse of warmer, steam-richer air rolls out of the vents, and the whole place seems to inhale. The silica moss flushes a deep, vivid forest green, the succulents’ bladders plump with mist, the dustwings swarm in thick, glowing clouds that fill the bower arches, the Architects pour out of their tunnels by the thousand, etching new chambers, moving in perfect sync, their low harmonic hum rising to match the deeper thrum of the planet’s core. When the pulse fades, they all return to their slow work, the moss fades back to pewter, the silence settles back in, thick and warm, holding all the echoes of the swarm, waiting for the next pulse, the next breath.

Standing there, under a bower arch, your hand pressed to the smooth, cool surface of an Architect mound, the faint hum of the planet in your bones, the echo of a dustwing swarm whispering out of the moss under your fingertips, you realize there is no line here between city and forest, between rock and life, between the built and the grown. This is a place that was never built, only grown, slowly, over millennia, out of stone and silence and the slow, steady pulse of a newborn planet, every part of it tuned to the same deep, quiet rhythm, every part of it holding the memory of every sound it has ever made.
