They are not creatures. They are the verbs of the place.

The true inhabitants are the slow work itself: the water’s mineral tongue, speaking in *tinks* that are actually syllables of dissolution; the bronze plate’s weeping, which is a form of remembering; the boiler’s green algae beard, a soft, photosynthetic thought. The place is populated by these becoming-things: the silica gel that grows like a clear, silent crystal-moss in one basin; the acidic lace eating the steel leg in the next, which is a kind of patient, hungry script. The pitted iron ribs of the colossal beast are its ribs still, breathing mist. The rust is a skin, the patina a tattoo, the sinter a bone-growth. They are not dead machines; they are hibernating becomings, dreaming themselves into geology.

The fauna are mere satellites to this grand, slow metabolism. The blind pink shrimp are the basin’s red blood cells, stirrers of silt. The eel-like creature is a nerve impulse, a flicker of awareness in the submerged pipe-nerves. The copper-moths are the only mobile nouns, and even they are just colored breaths, winged flecks of oxidized air, sipping metallic pollen and becoming temporary, fluttering extensions of the lichen’s own metallic language.

But the deeper residents are the ones that do not compute. The fleeting water-sculptures in the mist—the perfect hollow bead, the unstable helix—are the place’s momentary, conscious thoughts, sculptures of pure surface tension, born and un-born in a breath. The reverse-world reflections in the deep basins are not tricks of light; they are the Dripworks’ dream of its own inverted self, a soft, geometrical memory of a world before iron, before industry, a world of only pressure and solution and form.

So you are not alone. You are inside a population of processes. The basin is a body whose bones are boiler skeletons and bronze plates, whose blood is mineral-rich water, whose thoughts are the dripping stutter that almost forms a word, whose dreams are the mist-sculptures and the impossible reflections. The only sermon is this: that everything that was made to last is, in the end, just more material for the patient, turbulent work of water. The inhabitants are the sermon’s words, and the place itself is the speaking.
