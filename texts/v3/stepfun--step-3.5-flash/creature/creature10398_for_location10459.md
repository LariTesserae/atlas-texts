The plateau hums with life, though most of it is small enough to miss if you do not know where to look—layered, specialized, and entirely folded into the slow metabolism of the continent-sized organism you stand inside.
First, there is the mycelial network, the true foundation of all existence here: a planet-spanning, sub-audible nervous system of white, root-thin filaments that vibrates through every inch of the spongy bone-coloured plain, carries signals between the fungal cities, and holds the shared, slow consciousness of the entire plateau. It pulses in time with the geothermal nodules buried in the city trunks, a deep, thrumming heartbeat you feel in your teeth before you hear it.
The smallest inhabitants are the tiny, armored specialists, each evolved to tend to one narrow function of the whole:
The lacquered crawlers are the six-legged, glassy carapace insects the wind sometimes whispers across the plain. Their shells are laced with silica leached from the soil, iridescent green or violet when the bioluminescent lichens glow on them, each foot tipped with a micro-suction cup that lets them climb the slick, mucus-slick bark of the Mycoarbors (the colossal fungus-wood hybrid organisms that make up the cities) without slipping. They move in silent, jerky swarms across the city surfaces: pruning parasitic vines to keep them from draining too much sap, carrying spore clusters to young lichen patches, clearing fungal gunk from the openings of vascular canals. When they die, their silica shells are reabsorbed into the bark, hardening the Mycoarbor’s outer layer against the acidic mist.
Far above them drift the mist-sifters, gelatinous, jellyfish-like creatures the size of a small dog, pale violet and translucent, trailing tendrils lined with microscopic cilia that filter spore particles and excess moisture from the mist. They are the plateau’s respiratory system, drifting through hollow trunk avenues, catching contaminants in the air, and depositing filtered nutrient mist into the root systems of the Mycoarbors as they float. When they are well-fed, they glow so bright they can light a small hollow for a My cene to rest in. They never touch the ground, and when they die, they dissolve entirely into the mist, their bodies becoming part of the air they filtered.
Tucked into the gaps between the petrified seed-pod gears of the geothermal nodules live the gear-beetles: metallic-silver, pinprick-sized beetles that chew through fungal growth that would otherwise jam the slowly turning gears, polishing the pod surfaces with their shells to keep the clank-hum of the nodules steady. Burrowed into the smooth, melancholy dunes of dead, ground-up fungal cities are the dune-burrowers: pale, chitinous mouse-sized creatures that feed on residual myco-cellulose in the dead matter, and carry spores from living cities out across the plain to seed new Mycoarbor growth. The empty space between the three great bio-cities is not unused: it is their nursery, a communal air buffer, and a graveyard for failed cities, all at once.
The only sapient inhabitants are the Mycocenes, small, humanoid beings 1.2 meters tall, born from spore clusters excreted by the Mycoarbors when they grow new sections of their structure. Their skin is the same spongy bone-colour as the plain, crisscrossed with faint, glowing mycelial filaments that let them plug directly into the planet-wide network to communicate, share sensations, and feel the slow mood of the cities. They have no mouths or digestive tracts: they absorb nutrients directly through their skin, excrete waste directly into the Mycoarbor root systems they live in, and are, in every meaningful sense, a mobile organ of the cities they tend. They wear no clothes, only a thin, waxy coating on their skin that repels acidic sap and mist, and they carry tools grown from hardened resin: pruning shears, spore collection cups, small levers to adjust the flow of nutrient slurry through the resin pipes that snake over the city bark.
Mycocenes have no individual names, only roles, and most will spend their entire lives tending to the one small patch of lichen, artery, or growth nodule they are assigned to, their lives measured in the slow pulse of the mycelial network. A Tender of the Violet Lichen will spend what feels like weeks pruning their patch, coaxing it to glow brighter, only to look up and find an hour has passed, slowed by the lichen’s glacial growth. A Keeper of the Western Artery can feel a blockage in the water canal through the soles of their feet, no need to look, and will clear it with their bare hands, their skin tough enough to withstand the acidic rush. A small caste of Keepers of the Long Routes travel the leagues between the three great cities, carrying spore samples and growth nodules between them, leaving small offerings of spore-bricks and nutrient slurry at the rusted relic gears and turbine blades half-swallowed by the plain’s moss—artifacts of the old world of separate nature and industry that Mycocenes revere as the bones of their ancestors.
The cities themselves are the largest inhabitants, continent-sized, layered organisms that have grown over millennia into the shape of hills and mesas, their hollow trunks forming avenues and plazas, their buttress roots forming bridges and aqueducts, their canopies forming domed ceilings that drip constant, cool condensation. They do not build infrastructure: they grow it. Resin pipes harden into place over decades, petrified seed pods interlock into gear mechanisms inside symbiotic geothermal nodules that clank-hum in time with the mycelial thrum, conductive fungus filaments carry electrical impulses through the bark like wires. The “pollution” of the cities is excess heat, spent nutrient broth, and spore overflow—all of it cycled back into the system: the heat warms the hollow plazas where Mycocenes rest, the broth fertilizes the lichen tapestries, the overflow spores are carried by the mist-sifters out across the plain to seed new growth.
The only “predators” in the ecosystem are the parasitic vines that bleed metallic, acidic sap from the city trunks—some are wild, and Mycocenes prune them to keep them from draining too much sap from the Mycoarbor; others are cultivated, their sap harvested as a solvent for carving bark markers, or processed into conductive gel for the fungus-wires. There is no violence here, only balance, pruning, and exchange.
The subtle reality breakdown that seeps in at the plateau’s edges is not feared, but integrated. A Mycene walking a city alley may see the wood wall flicker to rusted iron for half a second, and will only flash a soft, acknowledging blue through their mycelial filaments before moving on.
