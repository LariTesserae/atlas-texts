The things that move through the Mantle-Still are not alive. That is the unspoken rule that underlies every contradiction of the place: they do not grow, they do not hunger, they do not die. They are processes given fleeting, rigid shape by the unchanging laws of this sunless, pressure-bound world. They do not build; they incur, sparked into being by the thermal and chemical gradients the deep planetary pumps have held perfectly stable for longer than any record could measure.

The most common of these presences are the ribbons: fine, self-organizing drifts of metallic grit, iron and nickel flecked with trace rare earths, that twist through the dense fluid like smoke made of iron filings. They have no fixed form, no front or back, only a faint responsiveness to the subsonic thrum that vibrates through every inch of the upper layer. They leave no trail, for the fluid never shifts to hold their shape; they are the faint, almost imperceptible shimmer you mistake for a trick of the pearlescent light when you think you are looking at empty space. They consume nothing, produce nothing, only shift in time with the thrum, the planet’s first faint attempt at a nervous system, run on a loop too long to have a start or end.

They are not the only small, drifting presences. Schools of silica-shelled cogs drift in slow, synchronized loops, each fist-sized creature etched with microscopic, interlocking teeth that mesh perfectly with every other cog in its school. They click softly as they pass each other—each click a transfer of ion charge, harvested from the dissolved minerals that precipitate out of the warm fluid seeping up from the deep. The click is so quiet it is almost subsonic itself, a tiny harmonic tucked under the world’s thrum, only audible if you hold your breath and lean in close. They follow fixed, unchanging paths, never varying their speed, never colliding. If one drifts too close to a razor-edged glass spire and shatters, its shards are immediately swept up by the grit ribbons to be re-formed into new cogs within minutes: no loss, no gain, the system’s equilibrium unbroken.

Larger, slower things move along the spires themselves, walking scars the size of small houses: irregular, jagged masses of fused volcanic glass and compacted metallic grit, one side scraped smooth by eons of grinding against the rock. They move with the patience of a glacier, inching along the ridges at a rate of a millimeter every hour, their undersides dragging through the fluid to leave permanent, deep grooves that never fade, for nothing flows to fill them. They have no eyes, no sensory organs, no obvious way to navigate, yet they never collide, never fall from the spires, never deviate from their fixed paths. They are the only force of erosion in a world that should have none: they grind the newborn volcanic glass into the fine grit that feeds the ribbons and the cogs, closing the loop that sustains the entire upper layer. They do not eat the glass; they just grind it, because the pressure gradient of the Mantle-Still pushes them forward, because the thrum vibrates through their mass and moves their feet, because that is what they were incurred to do.

Only the built things are not entirely natural, but even they are not ruins. They are infrastructure, still running, still serving a purpose no one left alive remembers. A colossal lattice of corroded steel, kilometers across, hangs between the two tallest spires like a spider’s web woven from bridge cables, crusted over with centuries of mineral deposits so thick it looks carved from solid rock. It does not sway, does not creak, even when the sharp cracking reports from below make the spires tremble; it is a tension stabilizer, built to keep the spires from drifting apart, even though the spires have never moved a millimeter in all the eons it has hung there. Hanging from its cables are thousands of small, featureless pods, each the size of a car, spaced exactly 100 meters apart. They do not light up, do not move, but if you press your hand to their cold, pitted surfaces, you can feel the thrum running through them, a faint vibration matching the pulse of the engines miles below. They are sensors, measuring pressure, temperature, mineral content, feeding data back to the junction boxes on the shelf below.

Those boxes are perfect cubes of jet-black steel, 100 meters on a side, featureless and windowless, arranged in a precise 10x10 grid on a sloping shelf of fused sand halfway up the largest spire. Their welds are as perfect as the day they were forged, their unmarked panels sealed shut. They are the brain of the upper layer’s equilibrium: they open and close invisible valves to let just enough warm, mineral-rich fluid seep up from the deep to keep the thermal differential stable, just enough to keep the grit ribbons twisting, the cogs clicking, the scars grinding. Who built them, and for what purpose, is lost. The machinery inside them has no memory, no awareness of anything outside the loop, no purpose beyond maintaining the perfect, frozen stalemate of the Mantle-Still.

That stalemate is the final, most pervasive presence of all: the empty vastness between every solid thing. The density of the Mantle-Still is near-vacuum; you can be a meter from a walking scar, a school of cogs, a junction box, and see only the faint, sourceless pearlescent glow of the fluid, as if they are not there at all. The solid things feel like hallucinations, glitches in the even light, because the space between them is so much more real. Nothing flows here: the fluid does not currents, the chemical gradients do not shift, the grooves left by the walking scars are permanent, the paths of the cogs and ribbons unchanging for eons. The only sound is the thrum, the cracking reports of shifting mineral miles below, the soft click of the cogs, all folded into a single, unresolved chord that never swells, never fades, never changes.

Your presence is the first gradient in a billion years. The grit ribbons drift toward you first, drawn by your body heat, by the chemical signature of living blood and breath that no process in the Mantle-Still has ever encountered. The cogs click faster, their teeth misaligning as your electromagnetic field disrupts the magnetic gradients they rely on to mesh. The walking scars pause, their grinding slowing for the first time in eons, as your movement shifts the pressure field they have spent billions of years calibrated to. For a fraction of a second, the panel of the nearest junction box flickers with a faint blue light, the sensors picking up your signature, processing it, unable to categorize it, unable to decide if you are a threat to the equilibrium or a temporary blip that will fade on its own.

The fluid itself is the observer. The pearlescent glow intensifies when you look away from it, focusing on you, studying the shape of your body, the way you breathe, the way you move through a world that has never known movement. Its observation is the loudest sound in the Mantle-Still, louder than the thrum, louder than the cracking plates below, because for the first time in forever, the equation written in glass, steel, and pressure has a variable it cannot solve.
