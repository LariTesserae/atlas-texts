The stone under your soles gives, just barely—no sharp, unyielding thud of bedrock, but the soft, firm give of a cured rind. You scrape a boot toe against it, and a thin, papery fleck of iridescent purple flakes away, leaving a dark smudge of silt and salt on the rubber. It’s not rock. It’s a skin the planet has grown over its own bones: layer upon layer of precipitated halite, silt cemented by mineral seep, the fossilized, pressed-flat remains of microbial mats that bloomed when this world was young, when the sun was bright enough to pierce the water and the surface wasn’t sealed under miles of ice. The current has worn it so smooth there are no cracks to catch your step, no fractures to turn your ankle. You could walk for weeks, the slope so gradual you’d sink only a meter deeper into the dark for every seven days of travel, never noticing the descent until the light above dimmed to nothing.

The water is thick. Not quite syrup, not quite jelly, but dense enough that when you lift a hand to brush a fleck of crust off your wrist, the fluid trails after your fingers like cold honey, taking three full heartbeats to slip back into the current. A drop hits your lip, and you taste brine first, sharp and stinging, then the iron tang of dissolved basalt, then the faint, sour rot of hydrogen sulfide that clings to the back of your throat. There are no bubbles rising fast here; any gas venting from the deep wobbles upward slow, heavy, like they’re pushing through a crowd, and burst with a soft, felt pop against your cheek that you feel more than hear.

You hold your hand up in front of your face, and it’s a dim smudge, no sharp edges, no shadow cast against the greenish-grey murk. The only light comes from the crusts underfoot, and it’s not for seeing. The violent purple patches, the sickly yellow stains, glow faintly from the waste of the chemosynthetic bacteria that live in them, a bioluminescence so dim it has no purpose but to exist, like the hum of a machine that’s run so long no one remembers what it was built to do. There’s no horizon, no sky above, just a slow, uniform darkening as you tilt your head up, the greenish-grey fading to a lighter, milky grey where the last photons from the distant, ice-blinded sun die after traveling fathoms through the saturated water, then absolute black where the deep begins.

The first life you notice is the Filter-Krill: six-legged, blind, their matte, mother-of-pearl shells glinting faintly in the crust-light as they move in slow, stately processions along the basalt. They don’t scatter when you step closer; they have no concept of predator, no need to run, only to sieve the water for the particulate organics that drift down from the crusts above. Their filamented antennae wave slow, tasting the current, and when they brush your ankles, you feel the faint, soft click of their tiny claws against the crust underfoot, a sound so quiet it’s more a vibration in your shins than a noise in your ears. They part around your legs like you’re another outcrop of basalt, indifferent.

A Gulper drifts past ten meters out, a soft, pulsating bell of translucent membrane so pale it’s almost invisible against the murk. You can see the faint, slow pulse of its interior, the way it opens that inward-curving mouth and ingests a full cubic meter of the dense fluid at once. For half a second, the water in front of it clears, a temporary tunnel where the thick, particle-heavy brine is sucked away, and you can see the faint glint of krill shells, microbial mats, mineral flecks swirling inside its belly, drawn toward the empty center. Then it closes its mouth, and the tunnel collapses, the water rushing back in to fill the space, and it drifts on, slow, tireless, consuming, no mind, no purpose, only metabolism. There are no predators here, only consumers and the consumed, all energy drawn not from a distant sun, but from the planet’s own searing heart.

You think it’s silent at first, but then you feel the sound. The deep, tectonic thrum of the world’s core, traveling up through the basalt, through the soles of your feet, up your spine, humming in your molars. It’s so low it’s not a sound at all, just a vibration, the planet’s heartbeat, slow as mountain growth, steady as erosion. Closer, you feel the faint pressure pulses of the hydrothermal vents kilometers away, the hiss and bubble of superheated water and gas erupting through fissures in the rock, a sound like a million slow sighs, felt in your chest more than heard. There’s no wind, no waves, no roar of surf, only this thrum, these soft pulses, the faint click of krill claws, the soft pop of rising gas bubbles.

You walk a hundred meters, and the smooth basalt gives way to a tessellated pool, one of the erosion-carved basins that pockmark the shelf. Each pool is a perfect hexagon, its walls smooth as glass, carved by the endless current over millennia. This one is stained deep purple by a thick crust of chemosynthetic bacteria, the water inside so saturated with their waste it glows faintly even in the dim light. Another nearby is sickly-bright yellow, where a new vent seep has just broken through the basalt below, feeding a new colony of bacteria already spreading, eating the sulfur as it rises, turning the rock yellow as they go. Further off, you see the Plain of Glass, a field of interlocking obsidian shards, each one smooth as a worry stone, no sharp edges left, worn down by the same endless current that planed the rest of the basalt. The obsidian is so dark it drinks the light, and it looks like holes punched through the world, leading down into the absolute black of the abyss.

Half a kilometer out, you find the River of Brine, a permanent channel of hypersaline water cutting a slow, deep groove in the basalt. The water here is thicker, heavier, and when you dip a hand in, it stings the small cut on your palm from when you scraped it on the rock, the salt so concentrated it burns. The Filter-Krill that live in the river are paler than the ones on the plain, their shells washed out by the high salt, and they move even slower, buoyed by the dense fluid. You watch them for minutes, and notice they’ve arranged their discarded, molted shells in a loose curve along the edge of the channel, guiding the current to eddy inward, pulling more particulate organics toward them.
