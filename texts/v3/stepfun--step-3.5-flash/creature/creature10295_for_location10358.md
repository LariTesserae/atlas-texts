The life here does not belong to the land so much as it leases it, short-term, from the stone.
The most abundant tenants are the smallest, almost indistinguishable from the grit and grit of the landscape at first glance. Crystalline lichens dust every damp niche, their fronds like tiny faceted corals that drink mineral from the condensation beading on rock at dawn and dusk. They pulse with a faint, cool bioluminescence at night, and their glow shifts in perfect time with the subsonic grind of the stone sheets: brightening for three seconds every time a fault line shifts an inch, as if they are drinking the vibration itself. Their dust, rubbed free by the wind, is the base of the entire food chain here, a fine iridescent grit that hangs in the air for days, catching the light to paint the canyon air faint gold.
Cloaking the north faces of every ridge and mesa in velveteen swathes are the silver mosses, their leaves sharp as ground glass to lock in moisture against the constant wind. They hold the condensation that beads on stone in tiny, spongy reservoirs, enough to sustain a small creature for a week after a squall. If you bruise a handful between your fingers, it leaves a faint, sweet taste of wet quartz on your skin.
The only larger “plants” are the rib-stones: swollen, translucent succulents with ridges like fossilized fish bones, and rows of tiny stinging barbs along their edges that carry a mild, numbing paralytic. They store the brief bounty of rain in their thick, watery trunks, and grow so slowly that a torso-sized specimen may be two thousand years old. They bloom only once every fifty years, after a heavy squall, their bell-shaped flowers opening only when the wind’s shriek drops to a low rumbling key so their pollen is not torn away. Their glow matches the lichens’ pulse for three days before the petals crumple to dust, their work done.
Tucked into the surface tension of every condensation bead is a micro-fern no bigger than a grain of rice, its fronds so fine they are almost invisible. It lives for exactly seven days, feeding on the mineral content of the water it floats in, before drying to a fine golden dust that the wind carries into the deepest fissures of the rock, where it will lie dormant for years until the next drop of water beads there.
The transient water veins that snake through the rock after a squall hold their own tiny tenants: blind, pale crustaceans no bigger than a speck of pepper, that hatch from eggs that can lie dormant in the stone for decades. They live their entire lives in 48 hours, feeding on the mineral film coating the pool bottoms, reproducing, and dissolving into the water when it seeps back into the rock, their bodies adding to the mineral load for the next generation’s hatch.
The rarer, larger fauna are barely more than rumors, glimpsed out of the corner of your eye. The scuttle-scutes are six-legged, chitinous grazers the size of a small cart, their shells mottled to match the rock they cling to, so perfect they vanish when they still. They have no eyes, only vibration sensors studded across their chitin, that let them feel the grind of the stone sheets, the shriek of the wind, the movement of other scuttle-scutes hundreds of miles away through the rock. They move at geological speed, sometimes shifting only a few inches a day, feeding on lichens and mosses, drinking the condensation that beads on their shells. They can live for ten thousand years, and when they die, their chitin slowly mineralizes, fusing to the rock until their fossilized forms are indistinguishable from the strata around them. You will find their fossilized shells embedded in the oldest cliff faces, proof they have walked these stone shelves as long as the mountains have been grinding.
Rarer still are the shriek-kites: membranous, weightless flyers with wings like dried onion skin, so thin they are almost invisible against the hazy sky. They have no muscles for flapping, riding the turbulence of the canyons like discarded leaves, drifting for thousands of miles without touching down. They feed on the golden dust of the micro-ferns and the pollen of the rib-stone flowers, drinking condensation only when they are forced to land on a stone ledge, an event so rare most of them go their entire lives without touching the ground. They lay their diamond-hard eggs in the deepest, loudest fissures of the rock, where the wind’s shriek vibrates the shells and speeds their development. When they die, their bodies dry to dust and join the grit that blows through the canyons, so they are as much a part of the wind as the stone is.
The landscape itself holds tenants of a different sort. The scattered humming stones, worn smooth by millennia of wind, ring with a low thrum when the wind hits them at the right pitch, and if you press your ear to their surface you can hear the distant grind of the tectonic sheets, the movement of stone miles beneath your feet. The old listeners who built the scattered domes, terraces, and standing stones carved small hollows into these stones to amplify the sound, using it to track the stone’s movement, to find water pockets, to mark the passage of time. The standing stones are aligned not to the stars, but to the direction of the loudest, most constant grinding, so they catch the vibration best. The sealed basalt dome, half-buried in scree, holds carvings on its interior walls: diagrams of the stone’s strata, maps of water pockets, images of scuttle-scutes and shriek-kites, and at the very back, a carving of a listener with their palms pressed to a scuttle-scute’s shell, as if they were learning to listen to the stone the way the grazers do.
The Fata Morgana that flicker on the distant horizon are not always mirages. The old fragments of oral tradition that survived the listeners’ disappearance call them stone ghosts: echoes of people who spent so long tuned to the stone’s vibration, so long drinking the shriek into their bones, that they became part of the sound itself. They flicker on the edge of sight, walking the ridges of plateaus that do not exist, their forms shifting with the wind. If you stand still long enough, you can hear them, faint, under the roar of the wind, a low, steady hum that matches the stone’s grind.
Even the condensation that beads on every surface at dawn and dusk holds life: thread-like, translucent fungi that wind through each drop, feeding on the mineral content of the water. They are so fine they are almost indistinguishable from the water itself, and when they reproduce, they release spores so light they are carried up into the wind, to land on the next condensation bead, miles away.
Nothing here fights for space. Everything is a tenant, borrowing time and water and vibration from a world that will outlast every living thing on it, grinding slowly, endlessly, toward a final stillness that is already here, already now, already always.
