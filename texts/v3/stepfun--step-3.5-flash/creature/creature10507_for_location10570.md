There are no discrete bodies here, no creatures with legs or wings to move between the towers. The inhabitants of the Sap-Forest City are both the architecture and the force that grows it, a single distributed consciousness stretched across miles of living, breathing biomass, with different parts thinking and living at wildly different speeds.

The closest thing to a “small resident” is the spongy red-brown shelf you almost crush under your boot. It is a temporary spore-bearing outgrowth of the nearest megastructure, the spiraling fractal tower that looms to your left, and it has lived its entire 10-hour life in the span of the few seconds it took you to step off the raw basalt. Its rind is a map of its frantic micro-existence: the tiny raised ridges where it siphoned nutrient-rich seep water from the rock, the faint dark patch where it caught a hormone pulse from the distant petal-platform telling it to grow faster, the soft pale spot where it ejected its first rust-colored spore cloud an hour (its whole lifetime) ago. It is convulsing now, folding its edges inward, preparing to dissolve back into the main tower’s bulk—its memories of stress, growth, and spore dispersal will be absorbed into the tower’s rind, adding another faint swirl to the tapestry of its centuries of existence. For it, you are a slow, warm, unidentifiable tremor in the air, a strange stimulus that will be gone before it finishes its final dissolution.

Pull back, and the three megastructures that frame your bowl are the city’s major organs, each with its own distinct pace of awareness:
- The spiraling fractal tower is the elder, its rind a thick, tumbled archive of 4,000 years of micro-stories: the deep glassy scar where a volcanic outburst cracked its mid-section 800 of its years ago, the pale raised spiral where it pried a 30-ton chunk of basalt loose to expand its root system, the faint wavy lines where it has absorbed a thousand spore-shelves just like the one under your boot. Its consciousness is slow, geologic, each thought taking days to form as slow, thrumming chemical pulses that ripple for miles. It does not register you as a discrete entity at all—you are a microseconds-long flicker of warmth, a faint disturbance in the air currents that carry its hormone clouds, too fast to matter. It is currently sending a low, basal pulse to the lattice-work tower across the bowl, testing the tensile strength of the reinforced living cables that stretch between them, making sure they can hold the weight of the condensation lake through the next geothermal spike.
- The flattened petal-platform miles across is the city’s youngest organ, its consciousness jittery and fast, each thought a burst of high-pitched chemical signals that fade in minutes. Its edges are a fringe of pale, thin rind, new growth that has only been alive for a few days, reaching out to colonize the empty basalt between it and the other two towers. It just ejected the spore cloud you saw drifting earlier, billions of spores riding the hot air currents, each one a potential new outgrowth, a chance to expand the city’s reach. It is hyper-alert to every shift in temperature, every flutter of air, because it is still learning to regulate its own internal water table. When it sensed the warmth of your body, it sent a quick, sharp flash of pale green pigment to its leading edge: a warning to the tender new growth out there to pull back, in case you are a threat that will crush its fragile rind.
- The lattice-work tower holding the condensation lake is the city’s lung and kidney, its interlocked limbs covered in millions of microscopic filaments that harvest moisture straight from the hot, saturated air, dripping it down internal conduits into the steaming basins at its base. Its consciousness is rhythmic, steady, a slow pulse of hormone signals that regulate the water table for the entire bowl, making sure basins never dry out during geothermal spikes, that seepage never erodes the basalt too fast. It is the source of the low, groaning sigh you hear: it is shifting the weight of the 10-mile-wide condensation lake on its back, redistributing load across its root-drill bits to keep from tipping. It is also the only part of the city that is truly focused on you: it monitors the air for carbon and organic trace particles, and it has been sending out faint, sweet-tasting chemical probes since you arrived, testing to see if you are a source of nutrients, if you will leave dead skin cells, sweat, or shed hair behind when you leave that its spongy outgrowths can feed on.

These three are not separate beings. They are nodes in a single, continent-spanning superorganism, and the wispy, almost invisible chemical clouds that drift between them are its nervous system, its thoughts, its memories. When you see the three towers darken in synchronized waves miles away, that is the city processing a shared stimulus: a geothermal spike deep under the plateau, a signal that the basalt is softening, easier to drill into, so it shifts resources to expansion, darkening its rind to absorb more of the opalescent light to fuel faster growth. When a new crystalline spur sprays out of a tower near you, unfolds into a glittering green spire in 30 seconds, then retracts and dissolves back into the bulk, that is the city testing a new growth pattern, discarding it if it will not hold weight or harvest enough water, part of its constant, frantic experimentation with form.

The empty leagues of raw, sharp basalt between the towers are not unoccupied. They are the city’s frontier, the part it is still eating. Faint, glassy cracks spiderwebbing across the black rock are where the root-drill bits of the three megastructures have been prying and wedging for centuries, slowly breaking the crust apart to make room for more growth. You can feel the faint, slow pressure of them working far below your feet, the slow creep of the rind spreading out from the towers’ edges, inching across the rock a few millimeters a year. For the basalt, you are nothing: a fleeting warmth on your boot, a tiny scratch that will be healed over by new rind in a few hours, gone before the rock even registers that it was touched.

There is no silence here, because the whole soundscape is the sound of the city alive: the constant drip of its water distribution system, the soprano creak of fiber under load as towers grow taller, the basso rumble of root-fracture deep in the stone, the sigh of million-pore exhalations as it breathes out excess heat. It is the sound of a construction site where the builders are also the building, where demolition is just another form of growth, where nothing is ever wasted, and nothing ever truly dies—it is just reallocated, reabsorbed, rewritten into the endless, growing memory of the rind.

To you, it is a sauna the size of a continent, empty and full all at once. To itself, it is a single, vast, thinking body, constantly frantically reimagining what it can become, turning volcanic rock into spine and arch and basin, turning growth into architecture, turning itself into a world that finally learned to build.
