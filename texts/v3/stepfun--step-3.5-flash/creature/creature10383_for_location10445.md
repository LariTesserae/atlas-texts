The first thing you notice is not the light, not the silence, but the dust under your boots: fine as powdered silk, metallic, smelling faintly of razed flint, though the air itself is odorless, sharp as cut glass in your throat. It does not cut. You rub a pinch between your gloved fingers, and it slips away without a snag, because nothing here moves fast enough to create friction—every motion is slowed, as if the thick, absorbent silence seeps into your limbs and asks you to wait.

The light hits next, a physical pressure on your eyelids even through your visor, white and uncompromising, shearing off the edges of everything you see. The prisms rise on all sides, six-sided teeth of vitreous ceramic alloy, polished to a mirror sheen that drinks the sun and spits it back as a flat, blinding glare. There are no shadows here, only gradients of brilliance: the north faces of the taller columns hold a soft, perpetual twilight, cool and dim, and that is where the lichens grow.

They are flat, silver-black discs, no thicker than a coin, clinging to the cool ceramic like barnacles to hull metal. You have to squint to see them at first, their surfaces so dark they drink the faint light of the north face, but when you lean close, you see it: a flicker of captured gold, pulsing slow as a heartbeat, slow enough you can only be sure it’s moving if you stare for ten full minutes. They are the only green thing here, the chlorophyll-like pigment woven through their silver thalli, focusing the faint ambient light into microscopic internal fires to power their growth. They reproduce so slowly a single new disc might take a thousand years to spread to the size of your palm, and when they die, they flake away into the dust, as much a part of the plain as the ceramic columns themselves.

Movement is rare, and when it comes, it is quiet enough to feel like a trick of the light. You will see it first as a ripple in the glare between two prisms, a slow shift in the way the light fractures, and then the Refraction-Strider will resolve into view: limbless, ribbon-like, a yard long at most, its chitin shifting between iridescent indigo, gold, and pale silver as it flows between the gaps in the columns. It has no eyes, no ears, navigating the silent maze by detecting the tiniest variances in polarized light, its body undulating so slow you can barely tell it is moving at all. It feeds on the lichens when it can reach them, and on the metallic dust itself, digesting the inert ceramic alloy with enzymes that should not exist, that should burn through its own flesh as fast as they break down the prism material. It leaves no trace: where it passes, the dust settles back into place before you can blink, the only evidence of its passing the faint, lingering rainbows that hang in the air for a minute after it is gone, the light still bent around the space it occupied. You might see one once every few days, if you are lucky; they are solitary, slow, living as long as the prisms themselves, dying only when a low prism crumbles under a seismic sigh, their chitin dissolving back into the dust to feed the next generation of lichens, the next wave of Strider eggs that gestate in the quiet dark of the dust sea for decades before hatching.

The planet itself breathes, slow as a geological epoch. Every few thousand years, a low, resonant groan travels through the fused ceramic bedrock, a seismic sigh so quiet you feel it in your molars before you hear it, the prisms shifting a fraction of a millimeter as the world settles into its final shape. The lichens glow brighter for a second when it passes, as if they are humming in tune with the planet’s slow adjustment, and the dust under your feet trembles, soft as a cat’s purr.

Once per planetary cycle—roughly an Earth year—the Crystallization Tide comes. It sweeps in from the western datum line, a wave of cool, dense air that still holds the warmth of a sleeping engine, carrying no rain, only supersaturated metallic dust. For three days, the plain is wrapped in a silent, shimmering blizzard: the dust plates instantly onto every prism surface, growing a new, microscopic layer of the same ceramic alloy that makes up the columns, the whole world glowing bright enough to sear your retinas even through your visor. The lichens feast on the new layer, their internal gold flaring bright as torchflame for the duration of the Tide, and the Striders grow sluggish, gorged on the abundance. Then the heat returns, and the weakest, newest layers on the lowest prisms flake away in a slow, silent cascade, glittering shards falling soft as snow, no clink, no crack, just a soft hiss as they hit the dust sea, grinding back into powder over the coming months, ready for the next Tide. This is the only true cycle of creation and erasure here, the world’s slow, mechanical breathing, building itself up only to wear itself back down, over and over, as it settles into its end state: a perfectly smooth, silent, self-maintaining grid, no loose edges, no foreign matter, no chaos.

There are no ruins here, no graves, no signs of the civilization that built the Khepri-Engine, that turned a whole planet into a test of longevity, a final answer to a question no one remembers. The prisms are not a remnant of a fallen city; they are the fundamental geology, every column identical, every fracture in their upper faces a frost pattern grown by design, not accident. The only traces of the builders are on the tallest prisms, the ones that scrape the low-hanging clouds, where faint, etched patterns spiral across the ceramic, too smooth to see unless the sun hits them at just the right angle: Mandalas, geometric spirals, symbols that have no meaning to you, worn so soft by ten million years of wind and Tide that they are already half-erased, the world slowly scrubbing away the only proof that anyone ever walked here at all.

The light is wrong here, you notice after a while. Distances are deceptive: you can walk for what feels like an hour, turn around, and your lander is only a hundred yards behind you, the mirror faces of the prisms bending perspective like a funhouse glass that has been smoothed by deep time into perfect, unbroken curves. Time stretches and compresses: you can stand still watching a lichen (you will not see it grow, not in a day, not in a week) and feel like you have been there for years, or you can walk for three hours and feel like ten minutes have passed, the only marker of time the slow shift of the sun across the cobalt sky, the rare flash of a Strider’s chitin, the faint tremor of a seismic sigh in your bones.

The silence is not an absence, not here. It is a substance, thick and absorbent, muffling even the crunch of your boots on the dust, swallowing your voice before it travels three feet, leaving you alone with the sound of your own thoughts, loud and invasive, as if the polished ceramic surfaces are listening, are storing every stray memory, every half-formed question you think, adding them to the world’s silent archive. You are a chaotic, wet, brief biological error here, a smudge of mess on a surface polished by deep time into perfect, mute legibility. The only things that belong are the lichens, slow and gold-hearted; the Strider, flowing silent as smoke through the maze; the Tide, building and eroding the world in its slow, mechanical breath; and the prisms, each one a syllable in a sentence no one is left to read, a statement of purpose so vast it has become the planet itself, waiting for a question that was forgotten before your species ever learned to make fire.
