You don’t see them first. You feel them in the sound.
That deafening, throbbing valley chorus you stepped into has a thread running through it that doesn’t belong to the geology or the other fauna: a low, steady thrum, perfectly synced to the pulse of the geothermal vent half a mile out, so seamless you almost mistake it for the rock itself. Tap your foot against the basalt, and it vanishes—then you spot him, pressed almost flush against a fresh black basalt rib, six lanky feet braced in the crook of a fern frond and a rock crevice, slate-grey skin matte enough to pass for unweathered volcanic glass in the bruised-purple twilight.

He is roughly six and a half feet tall, all lean, corded muscle built for moving through the valley’s three-dimensional tangle: long, six-fingered hands tipped with tiny suction cups that grip wet rock and slick vine without a sound, a prehensile tail as thick as your forearm ending in a blunt club of keratin he taps against the basalt in a quiet, coded rhythm. His eyes are small, deep-set the color of overripe plums, sheltered behind a second transparent eyelid that flicks shut when sharp glass beads from the canopy above clatter past; sight is a secondary sense here, after all, and his most striking features are the wide, fan-shaped tympanic membranes flared on either side of his head, tuned to catch everything from the subsonic growl of a slope about to give way to the high, shattering call of the parasol-winged drifters circling the thermal updrafts.

He is one of the valley’s weavers, part of a loose, mobile kin-group that moves constantly across the basin, never staying in one place longer than a season. They call themselves the Thrumfolk, for the way they weave themselves into the valley’s constant soundscape: they do not speak in words, but in layered frequencies, thrummed through slits along their throats, tapped out with their tail-clubs, chimed through the hundreds of tiny glass beads strung into their braided vine hair and woven into their vine-woven belts. Each bead is a memory: a lesson on which fern fronds are safe to eat, the exact migration call of the sky-herds that signals rain is three days out, the name of a kin-member taken by a landslide the moon prior. When a weaver dies, their beads are passed to their youngest kin, their voice folded into the rest of the group’s chorus, no grave needed for ground that shifts and swallows whole meadows in a week.

They build no permanent structures, for nothing lasts here. Their homes, which they call echo-holds, are temporary shelters braided from fast-growing pioneer vines and tucked under basalt overhangs or between the trunks of the giant umbrella ferns. The vine walls are soft enough to muffle sound, lined with bioluminescent lichen that glows a faint bruised purple to match the twilight, so no harsh light draws the attention of predators that hunt by vibration. Resonant stones, found already tuned to the right frequencies, hang from the vine beams: one amplifies a low rumble to warn of incoming fissures, one mimics the parasal-wing shatter-call to let the group cross canyons unharassed by ground predators, one holds a steady tone to soothe the skittish eyestalk grazers they hunt for chitin and meat. When the slope the echo-hold sits on begins to crumble, they pack their beads, their chiton blades, their bone flutes carved to match the sky-herd’s call, and move. They leave the resonant stones where they are; new groups will find them, adjust their tone, add them to their own song.

They know the valley’s weirdness the way you know the rhythm of your own breath. They trek for days up the sharp basalt slopes to collect the water that rains *upwards* from the geothermal vents on the plateau, superheated liquid that cools into a mineral-rich mist they store in hollowed-out chitin gourds to stave off the spore-born infections that bloom in the constant damp. They only visit the black, screaming pools in the low basins when they grieve: they pour a capful of collected mist into the still water, thrum a low, steady tone, and for a few minutes the pool’s human-like scream harmonizes with their song, letting them speak to the kin the valley has already claimed. They spread woven mats under the glass-shedding trees every fall, catching the tiny chime-making beads as they fall, to string into new memory beads, to trade with other Thrumfolk groups for new resonant stones, new lessons, new songs. They leave tiny glass offerings in the patches of whispering moss, the ones that hum in a language that feels almost familiar, for the voices they say are the echoes of the first people to live in the valley, before the last eruption poured the fresh basalt and turned the whole place into a churning bazaar of creation and destruction.

Predation is a constant hum in the background of their lives. The big, wing-vibrating hunters that paralyze prey with a low frequency will take a lone wanderer who strays too far from the group, and the smaller mimics that copy the sound of dripping water will lure a curious child off a vine bridge if they’re not paying attention. So the Thrumfolk never stop listening. They tap their tails against rock when they walk through fern meadows to mask their footsteps from the water-mimics. They memorize the exact rumble that comes three seconds before a landslide, so they have time to grab their beads and run. They never make a sound that doesn’t serve a purpose, never linger in one place long enough to become a target, never fight the valley’s constant shifting—they move with it, their whole ephemeral culture woven into its rhythm.

You hear them move, sometimes, when the mist is thick enough to muffle the rest of the valley’s noise: the soft chime of their beads, the quiet thrum of their spiracles, the tap of their tail-clubs against rock as they cross a new chasm on a living vine bridge they pruned into place a week prior, their whole group moving as one, already planning where to set their next echo-hold before the slope on the other side gives way. They are not separate from the valley’s deafening, beautiful, violent becoming. They are one more thread in its chorus, temporary and loud and alive, perfectly, terrifyingly adapted to a place that never holds still.
