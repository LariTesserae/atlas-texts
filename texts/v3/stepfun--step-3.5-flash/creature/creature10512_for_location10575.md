You don’t find the Riven Bowl. It finds you: a narrow crack in the side of a sun-baked mesa, no wider than your shoulders, breathing warm, humid air out into the desert like a sleeping thing exhaling. You squeeze through before you think better of it, the rough mesa rock scraping your shoulders, and the second you cross through, the world inverts.

The dry, sharp air of the desert is gone, replaced by thick, warm air that clings to your skin like a second layer, smelling of wet ochre clay, sweet fermenting fern, and the sharp, briny tang of salt. Under your boots is black, glassy obsidian, cool at first then warming fast from the heat seeping up through the stone, worn so smooth by eons of erosion it feels like river-worn glass under your soles. It’s scored with a million tiny marks: the crescent gouges of hooves, the thin parallel scratches of tunneling claws, the deep, wide grooves where dragging wooden sleds have worn tracks into the stone over thousands of years, a palimpsest of habitation so dense you can barely see unmarked glass anywhere at all.

You look up first, and the scale hits you before you can parse it. The Bowl’s ceiling is a tangled, porous lattice of fused stalactites, so ancient they’ve melted into a single uneven roof, dotted with clusters of Breath-Ferns: trunkless, towering masses of iridescent blue fronds that sway slow in the mist, each exhale a soft, sighing release of oxygen that rustles their fronds like crumpled tissue paper. The walls rise in layered bands like a soggy lasagna: thick, wet ochre clay strata streaked with mineral stains, shelves of glinting crystalline salt that catch the faint blue-grey twilight and throw it back in pale shards, and vast spongy mats of leathery moss that drink the mist and glow faint bioluminescent green lower down, where the geothermic seep from the Bowl’s base thickens the air to near-fog.

The sound hits you next, all at once, layered so dense it presses against your eardrums. Under it all is the base hum: a million tiny metallic Moth wings flitting through the mist, their drone so constant you stop noticing it after ten seconds, like the sound of your own blood in your ears. Over that, the endless click and scrape of chitin: Drill-Worms tunneling under the clay, Mantis-Crabs shifting on the salt shelves, Lace-Wings fluttering as they filter-feed mist particles from the air. And under all of that, the deep, thrumming rumble of a Glass-Back herd moving on the far side of the Bowl, their hooves clicking on the obsidian so fast it blends into a single heartbeat you can feel through the soles of your boots.

The Kind are the first living things you see clearly, clustered on a mid-level clay shelf a hundred yards away. Their huts are grown, not built: woven from the fibrous stalks of giant ferns, mortared with paste of clay and crushed beetle-shell lime, their roofs thatched with dense carpet-moss that glows faint green even in the twilight. Rope bridges made of braided moss fiber creak as they move between the huts, and spiral staircases carved into the soft clay wind up the wall to higher fern groves and down to the mist-choked lower levels. Their skin is dusted with iridescent blue Breath-Fern pollen, their hair braided with salt-vine crystals that glint when they move, their clothes dyed deep ochre and russet with wall clay. A cluster of herders guide a small herd of juvenile Glass-Backs up a clay ledge, the little quadrupeds’ backs still soft and matte, their hooves clicking sharp and fast as they follow the shrill trill of the herders’ flaked obsidian whistles. A child runs past the edge of the shelf, bare feet slapping on the obsidian, holding a woven moss basket full of salt-vine nodules, and stops to stare at you for a long second before grinning and holding out a crisp, salty nodule to you, her fingers stained red with ochre.

You don’t realize the mist tide is thickening until the air grows heavier against your cheeks, damp and warm, and the Moths’ hum rises to a higher, frantic pitch as they swarm between the fern fronds to feed on the minerals the ferns release with the mist. The herd thrum grows louder, and you look up to see thousands of Glass-Backs moving as one, their obsidian backs catching the faint light and glinting like a shifting sea of black glass as they migrate up to the higher fern groves to feed. A Sky-Rat glides past overhead, its six limbs stretched, its fur iridescent blue like the ferns, a plump Moth clutched in its mouth, and for a half-second you see the whole Bowl reflected in its belly: the layered walls, the glowing lower moss, the tiny Kind huts, the moving herd, all doubled and warped in the smooth black glass under your feet.

There is no way to take it all in. The Bowl is only a kilometer across at its widest—you could walk the entire perimeter in a day, if nothing slowed you down—but every clay ledge holds its own clan of Kind, every fern grove its own sacred history, every cart track its own story of who dragged what, when, why. There is no sun, no moon, no calendars, no writing: time is measured in Breath-Fern growth rings, in the depth of the cart ruts worn into the obsidian, in the oral stories the Kind tell around their evening fires, tales that stretch back further than anyone can count, passed down from throat to throat in the dark, warm air.

Death here is not an end, just a transfer. Ten feet from you, a Glass-Back calf that stumbled and broke its leg ten minutes prior is already half-buried under the scurrying bodies of Gleaners: furred, many-legged scavengers that strip a carcass to nothing in minutes. By the time the herd moves past, the only mark left will be a faint damp spot where the moss will grow thicker and sweeter in a day, fed by the calf’s bones. Nothing is wasted. Everything is part of the loop.

You stand there for a long time, watching the Kind settle in for the mist tide, watching the Moths swirl like living silver between the fern fronds, watching the herd thrum fade to a distant rumble as the animals reach the upper groves. You are not in a cavern. You are inside the fossilized heart of a world, and it is beating: the thrum of hooves on glass, the hum of a million wings, the sigh of ferns, the click of chitin, all woven into a single steady rhythm that has been playing, unchanging, for time out of mind. The desert here is not a desert of dryness, but a desert of form: every surface has been smoothed, every edge worn away, but life has retextured it with a thousand frantic, overlapping layers of grazing, building, hunting, dying, and living again. The scale is hidden, but the weight of it presses on your chest: you are small, and temporary, and exactly where you are supposed to be.
