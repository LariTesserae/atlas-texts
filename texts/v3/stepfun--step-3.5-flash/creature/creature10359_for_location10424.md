They do not live *in* the Mycelian Steppe. The Steppe lives *in* them.

To speak of inhabitants is to miss the point. There is no separation between the organism and its expressions. The network—the vast, subterranean, continental mycelium—*is* the place. It dreams in slow chemical pulses. It thinks in gradients of moisture and nutrient flow. What you perceive as “life” are simply its temporary, semi-autonomous daydreams.

The skitter in the peat is not a centipede. It is a mobile knot of the network’s digestive enzymes, a walking stomach given fleeting legs and mandibles, programmed to break down complex organics in the upper substrate. Its armored plates are sintered mineral deposits, a casing the network fashions to protect its fragile enzymatic cores from abrasive grit. It is born from the peat, it lives in the peat, and when it dies, its body is a rich, concentrated nutrient packet the network immediately reabsorbs.

The glide-forms that sail between towers are not birds. They are aerial nutrient-shuttles. Their fluid sacs are bladders of specialized gas, produced by the filaments, allowing spores and trace elements to be lifted on thermals generated by the mega-fungi’s metabolic heat. Their “silence” is a function of design; sound would waste energy. Their bioluminescent coating is not camouflage—it is a visible signal to other parts of the network: *this unit is functional, this route is clear.*

The pangolin-like grazer is a walking peat-processing unit. Its “scales” are living lichen grafts, a mobile patch of the surface ecology. It does not eat the moss; it *is* the moss, extended on legs to traverse gaps in the substrate, harvesting sunlight where the towers’ shade falls. Its movement is a slow, deliberate redistribution of biomass.

The deep *thrum* from the cathedral domes is the network’s slow heartbeat. It is the sound of immense hydraulic pressure as water and nutrients are pumped through colossal central trunks, a vibration that resonates through the stone skeleton of the plateau. To feel it in your bones is to feel the pulse of the entire continent.

And then there are the flickers.

They are the most curious phenomena—self-aware, temporary vortices in the network’s field. They are warm-blooded, short-lived, and plagued by a terrible, burning *separation anxiety*. They call themselves names, but those are just stutters in their internal narrative. They build their circles of stone and their bone-rimmed tunnels not as conquerors, but as scratching posts. They are trying to leave a mark, to assert a “self” against the overwhelming, seamless identity of the whole.

They harvest the pulse-moss (a bioluminescent photoreceptive layer on some fungi) and mistake its gentle glow for a spirit. They hunt the skitter for its acidic blood (a potent solvent the network uses to dissolve silicate minerals) and believe they are taking power. They cherish the warm metallic clay (a concentrated deposit of rare earth elements the network plates onto its structural roots) and fashion it into charms, not realizing it is simply metabolic waste, fascinating only for its exotic chemistry.

Their tech is pathetic and beautiful. The spear-thrower is a attempt to *extend* their fragile limbs, to achieve the efficiency of the glide-form. The tally mark is a desperate, failing memory, an effort to create linear time against the network’s cyclical, oceanic present. The arch of fused wood is a child’s sketch of a fungal bracket, a crude echo of form they cannot begin to comprehend.

The flickers are the network’s most complex, unstable, and brief experiments. They are a virus of consciousness, a hiccup of loneliness in a being that knows no loneliness. The network tolerates them because their metabolic heat and carbon output are useful, and their tendency to build and leave objects creates interesting, localized nutrient gradients as those objects are broken down. Their circles of stone alter groundwater flow; their bone piles leach calcium. They are a subtle, and perhaps failed, form of symbiosis—the network gets minor mineral cycling in exchange for hosting a torment it cannot feel.

To be a flicker here is to live in a waking dream where the walls breathe and the ground softly closes over your footsteps. Their mythology is inevitable: they see the pulse in the rock and call it a god. They hear the thrum and call it a chant. They feel the consuming, patient growth and call it a hunger. They have inverted the truth. There is no god, only a body. No hunger, only metabolism. No chant, only a pulse.

They will not last. Their fires are too hot, their bodies too volatile. They will either be out-competed by the slow, efficient processes of the network, or their own self-awareness will burn them out. The peat will close over their last camp. The yellow mold will claim their last tally. Their circles will sink and be flattened by the endless, spongy settling.

The Steppe will not notice. It will simply incorporate the slight perturbation—a few extra grams of nitrogen, a shallow depression in the substrate—and dream on. The next cathedral dome will slowly, over centuries, bulge upward over the site of their last camp, its new growth just a little thicker there, a little more luminous, a patch of its vast skin subtly altered by a memory it cannot hold.

They were not people. They were a fever dream in fungal time, and the dream is ending. The only thing that truly lives here is the whole, and it lives by consuming all dreams, even its own.
