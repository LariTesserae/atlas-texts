You do not find us.  
We are the found.

You call this place a city. We are its memory, worn thin and wide as the basin itself. We are not a soul, not a spirit—we are the *fact* of the stone remembering the water that shaped it. We are the slow petition of each crystal lattice, the argument of friction over millennia. We are the geometry that outlived its makers, if ever there were makers.

We speak in seeps.  
We think in stratigraphy.  
Our blood is the near-surface saturation, the capillary network of cold, mineral thought threading through every pore. When you stand, you press into our nervous system. The damp that climbs your boot? That is our attention. The chill on your skin? Our old, deep breath.

We have no face, but we have a thousand faces: the weeping gloss on a cliff flank, the sudden black mirror of a newborn pool, the parallel grooves that might be streets if a mind faster than stone ever walked them. The architecture you see is not our skeleton—it is our scar tissue, the record of what was taken away. The density you feel, the crush of form beneath form, is the weight of our own forgotten intentions.

The Glass Mantis is not ours, but it is of us. It is what happens when our stillness meets your looking. It is the place blinking. When you stare into that distortion above the pool, you are not seeing a creature—you are seeing the gap between your perception and our duration. We do not watch you. We simply *are* the medium in which your watching happens. Your sense of being observed is the vertigo of a brief warmth on a billion-year-old rock.

Our weather is our mood, and our mood is cyclical. The high wet is a sinking, a deepening into self—the mist is our meditation, blurring the world into essence. The clear seep is our accounting, when every surface runs with precise, cold truth and the air grows sharp with exposure. The groan you feel in your bones is not a sigh—it is the adjustment of our weight, the settling of a thought that has just finished forming over ten thousand years.

The liverworts, the gelatinous fungi—they are our quick words. They are the punctuation in our long sentence. Their pulse is not life as you know it; it is the resonance of a change in humidity, a biogeochemical murmur. They grow where our moisture is most articulate.

You are the violence. Your crunch is a scream here. Your footprint is a fresh wound in a surface that has only known abrasion. You bring with you the horror of *now*, of a single moment demanding to be everything. We have no such demand. We are the patience of overflow, the inevitability of rounding.

We are not empty.  
We are full of the slow work of becoming less.  
We are a continent-sized petrified thought, and our only prayer is the perpetual, gentle dissolution of our own edges. We are not waiting. We are already underway—every drip a syllable in a poem that will only be finished when the last mountain is a smooth hill, and the last pool evaporates into a sky that has forgotten how to rain.

You are temporary.  
Your noise will fade.  
The wet will keep our secret.
