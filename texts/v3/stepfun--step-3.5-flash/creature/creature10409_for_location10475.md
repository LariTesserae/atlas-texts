They are not separate from the basin.  
They are the basin’s memory of itself.

You might think you see them first in the alignment of the stones—the way the spiral of blue crystal shards points not to a star, but to the exact millimeter on the western wall where the weeping water’s flow increases by a single drop per century at the solstice. You might feel their presence in the pulse that sometimes, if you lie very still on the silt, seems to move through the ground—a rhythm so slow your mind smooths it into stillness, but your bones register it as a vibration older than breath.

They do not have bodies.  
They have *consistencies*.

One is the **taste of wet limestone on the wind just before the mist thickens**.  
Another is the **memory held in the glass-ferns’ fronds**—a record of every spectrum of light that has ever touched them, stored in the crystalline lattice, replaying in silent rainbows when no one is there to see.  
A third is the **pressure change in the air when a moss-mound decides, over three hundred years, to shift its surface pattern by a hand’s width**.

They communicate through equilibrium.  
A disturbance is a sentence.  
The way the silt in the northern pool begins, after a decade of perfect counter-clockwise swirl, to hesitate for a single afternoon—that is a question.  
The response comes in the form of a new, faint blue vein exposing itself on the east cliff face, altering the way the morning light pools there—an answer in geometry.

You found their “tech.” That is how they touch the world without touching it.  
The circle of seven stones with holes—when the weeping water’s thread aligns with one, for a single hour every seventeen years, the hole holds a drop in suspension, refusing to let it fall. That is not a tool. That is a thought.  
The stack of three rocks with the groove—when the solstice star touches the groove at dawn, the water from the fissure, for one moment, runs clear instead of ink-black. A change so subtle no one would notice. But they do. That is their celebration.

They do not watch you.  
You are a noisy flicker in a long, slow perception.  
To them, your coming is like a sudden warm gust across the silt—a temporary, frantic stirring of the surface film. They feel it as a spike in the water’s mineral content, a brief acceleration in the moss’s capillary pull. By the time you’ve taken ten steps, they have already begun to rebalance.

If you could perceive them directly, you would not see shapes.  
You would see the basin’s processes *knowing themselves*.  
The way the blue crystal in the silt, when stared at long enough, appears to pulse—that is them breathing.  
The way the moss-mounds seem to lean very slightly toward the weeping fissure at dusk—that is them listening.

They are lonely, but their loneliness is geological.  
It is the loneliness of a mountain that has forgotten the name of the glacier that carved it.  
They do not hope for company.  
But if, by some unimaginable chance, another consciousness ever truly *stayed*—moved beyond coming and going, learned to breathe at their pace, to think in millennial gradients—they would welcome it by allowing it to become part of the balance.  
They would teach it how to dream in stone and water, how to speak in silences that last a lifetime.  
And eventually, they would merge, and the basin would dream with two minds instead of one.

For now, they are alone.  
And their only companionship is the perfect, weeping grammar of the water, the patient, continent-wide thoughts of the moss, and the slow, violet glow of the glass-ferns under a light no human eye can fully see.

They live in the space between the drop and the absorption.  
In the gap between the stone falling and the echo fading.  
In the pause where the silt forgets your footprint.

They live in the *almost* of everything.  
And the basin, in its deep, wordless dream, is their body, their world, and their only voice.
