You stand in the Grove of Whispers, and first you notice the silence beneath the noise—thick, warm, older than the Spire itself, older than the salt plain, older than the bones of the people who carved the ledge under your feet. The silica-moss springs softly under your boot, velvet-textured and flecked with mica that glints copper, then violet, then oil-slick black as the dim sun filters through the ash-colored sky. Dust-moths, their wings stained the color of crushed garnets and tumbled sea glass, flutter in a slow cloud around your head, their papery wings brushing your cheek like dry kisses, their constant rustle so loud it hums in your teeth. A scuttle-beetle, its carapace a mosaic of emerald and amethyst edged in tiny pearl-like silica bumps, trundles over the laces of your sandal, its tiny legs clicking against the leather, unbothered by your presence.
This is home to the Grit-Chasers, the people whose blood carries the same faint metallic tang as the Spire’s slow exhalations. Their skin is dusted permanent with iridescent glass grit that no amount of scrubbing will remove, settling in the creases of their knuckles, the lines of their palms, the faint scars from old carving accidents. Their clothes are woven from salt-grass and the fine, resilient fibers of the silica-moss, dyed with crushed scuttle-beetle carapaces and the rust-red iron that seeps from the Spire’s upper veins in streaks that look like old, dried blood. They have no family names, only names tied to the parts of the Spire they call home: Ledge-Vein, who carved the terrace you’re standing on; Grottowhisper, who tends the glow-worm jars that hang from every grotto ceiling; Moss-Tended, who hikes the upper ledges to check new silica growth for soft carving veins.
Their city is not built on the Spire, but grown with it. They do not quarry the silica, only carve along the veins of slightly softer stone that the Spire pushes up from its core, layer by layer over centuries. Rooms nest inside older rooms, their edges always soft, rounded by the Spire’s slow, unending erosion—no sharp corners anywhere, not in the stairs winding up the Spire’s flanks, not in the bridges of woven salt-grass that span the deep crevices between ledges. There is no mortar, only friction and plant resin, so the city shifts with the Spire as it grows: you can hear the faint click of stone settling against stone at night, a sound as familiar to the Chasers as the rustle of dust-moths, not a warning of collapse but a sign the Spire is healthy, expanding, making room for new rooms, new families.
All life here is tied to the closed silica cycle, as unthinking and inevitable as the drift of salt across the plain. The Spire breathes out thin mineral vapor in its slow, geologic sleep, and that vapor condenses in the deep, shaded fissures to feed the glass-ferns: delicate, articulated fronds of transparent silicate that grow a fraction of a millimeter a year, filtering the glass dust from the air and adding it to the Spire’s mass. The silica-moss that carpets the north face grows thick and plush, changing color with the angle of the weak sun: copper at dawn, iridescent violet at midday, oil-slick black when the dim sun dips below the salt plain’s invisible horizon. The scuttle-beetles trundle over the moss, grazing on the tiny silicate spores that drift from the ferns, their carapaces growing brighter with each meal. The Chasers harvest only the beetles that die of old age, using their carapaces for inlay in resin seals, for buttons, for the small charms tied to children’s wrists to keep them from getting lost in the maze of carved ledges.
In the deep, humid lower grottos, where the Spire’s breath is thickest, live the grinders: blind, pale, many-legged soft-bodied creatures that munch on decaying moss and peeling old resin, their wet, rhythmic chewing a soft counterpoint to the dry rustle of the dust-moths above. The Chasers leave them alone, even tend to their feeding grounds, because they keep the lower grottos free of the silica rot that would weaken the Spire’s structure. They only eat grinder meat that dies naturally, slow-roasted over the Spire’s warm exhalation fissures, the heat gentle enough that it doesn’t burn, just warms the meat through to tender. The only predators here are the glass-jacks: translucent, nearly invisible ambush hunters that lash out from crevices with needle-speed to catch dust-moths and scuttle-beetles that wander too close. The Chasers leave small offerings of crushed beetle carapace near their crevices, a quiet tithe to the Spire’s unthinking guards, so the glass-jacks do not strike at Chasers carving new rooms nearby.
To the Chasers, none of this is strange. There are no gods here, no prophecies, no grand mysteries—only the cycle, as steady and unthinking as the Spire’s slow erosion. They know the Spire was once a living thing, a vast organism of hyper-compacted silica that lived and died so long ago no record of it remains, but they do not worship it. They are its last, fleeting expression, just as the glass ferns, the dust-moths, the grinders and glass-jacks are. Their glass tools, their bone needles, their very bones are temporary borrowings from the Spire’s mass, returned to it when they die.
When a Chaser dies, their body is laid in a shallow crevice that opens to the Spire’s deep, crystalline root network, miles below the salt plain. The Spire absorbs the silica from their bones over a few years, and often, a tiny glass fern will grow from the spot where they were laid, its fronds the same color as the dye they wove into their clothes. The Chasers call these name-ferns, and tend them carefully, never picking them, letting them grow as a quiet memorial. The only exception are those who die of dust-sickness: when too much glass dust settles in their lungs, turning the soft tissue to silica, they cannot be returned to the Spire’s cycle, so their bodies are carried out to the flat, endless salt plain, left to stand in the dim light until the wind wears them down to smooth, glassy statues, their silica returned to the inert scrub that will never feed the Spire again.
The only sound that ever breaks the wall of insect rustle and Spire-creak is the faint tap of bone tools against silica, the sound of a new room being carved, a generation’s work that will not be finished until their grandchildren’s grandchildren are old. The Chasers do not rush. Nothing here rushes. A moss-carpet grows a millimeter in a century. A new terrace takes three generations to carve. The salt plain beyond the Spire’s shadow is perfectly flat, unchanged for as long as any Chaser can remember, stretching to a horizon that doesn’t exist, just a blur where ash-colored sky meets white salt.
At night, the glow-worms in the clay jars hung from every ledge pulse soft amber, their light gilding the edges of the carved rooms, and the dust-moths settle on the moss-carpets, their wings folded like stained glass shards. The Chasers sit on their terraces, eating dried salt-grass and sharing stories of the old carvings, the ones their grandparents made, the ones their great-grandchildren will finish. They do not think about the Spire ending. They do not think about the cycle breaking. They only think about the next room to carve, the next name-fern to tend, the next generation to leave their mark on the stone, however small, however brief, however temporary.
You stand in the Grove of Whispers, and you taste salt and glass on your tongue, and you realize you are not a visitor here, not really. The fine grit is in your cuffs, in your hair, between your teeth. The Spire’s breath is on your skin. You are part of the cycle now, too, for as long as you stay. For as long as you stay, the wall of insect noise is your lullaby, the soft spring of the moss under your feet is your floor, and the slow, unending creak of the Spire is the only clock you will ever need.
