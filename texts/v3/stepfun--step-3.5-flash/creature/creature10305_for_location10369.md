The first inhabitants are the ones you’d miss entirely if you didn’t look down: the microbial mats that coat every damp patch of ash and the slow, silty bottoms of the seep pools. They are not the dull green scum of temperate wetlands, but iridescent, layered things, shifting from opaline white where silica saturation is highest to deep rust where iron seeps bleed into the ash. At dawn and dusk, they pulse in time with the caldera’s sub-sonic thrum, glowing faintly through their gelatinous layers like light through stained glass. They are the base of every food chain here, and their constant, slow reproduction feeds everything else: the brine-skimmers graze on their surface films, the silica worms burrow through their deeper layers, and their decaying cells feed the glass-sedge roots that punch through the ash to tap the mineral seeps.
Thin as sewing thread and translucent as raw quartz, the silica worms wriggle through the damp top layer of ash in the spaces between seep paths, almost invisible until the sun hits them at the right angle, when their bodies flash silver and blue. They feed on the lower layers of the microbial mats, and their constant burrowing turns the ash into a soft, spongy carpet that holds moisture for hours after a seep dries. Larger creatures eat them: the rock-creepers, small, armadillo-like things with porous pumice plates that blend perfectly with the ash, crack them open with silica-tipped mandibles when they burrow for lichen roots. The worms leave behind tiny, twisted glass trails when they die, their bodies mineralizing almost instantly in the silica-saturated air, adding fine glitter to the ash that sticks to your boots and skin if you kneel to look at a pool.
The glass-sedge, the tall, hollow stalks that chime in the rare, thin breeze that sweeps the basin, are not just plants: they are miniature habitats. Their hollow stems are lined with a waxy, water-resistant silica, so the warm seeps that run down their sides don’t dissolve them, and their tips hold clusters of plump, translucent fruits that store mineral-heavy water so concentrated it will make your tongue tingle if you sip it. The silicate moths live in these stems: their wings are made of the same thin, fused quartz as the sedge, so when they flutter from stalk to stalk to drink the fruit nectar (which they tap with crystalline proboscises that never wear down), they make a high, soft chime that blends perfectly with the sedge’s low song. Their wings hold a faint bioluminescent dust that shifts from pale yellow to electric blue when they fly, so a swarm around a sedge grove looks like a cluster of floating fireflies made of glass. The mist-fins, the basin’s only larger fliers, nest in the densest sedge groves, tucking their refracting wing-membranes between the stalks to hide from the midday heat.
The brine-skimmers are the jewels of the seep pools, flat as coins and translucent as skimmed ice, their eight spun-glass legs splayed wide to distribute their weight across the warm, mineral-thick water’s surface tension. They skate in slow, silent loops across the pools, feeding on the microbial mats with tiny, frilled mouthparts that filter out excess silica, which they excrete as perfect, tiny opal beads that sink to the pool bottoms. Over years, these beads build up in layers, forming the soft, porous base that the caldera’s silica gardens accrete on so quickly. When the sun hits them at the right angle, their bodies glow a soft aqua, pulsing in time with the thrum, and if a group clusters together, the whole surface of a pool looks like it’s breathing light. They are tiny enough to pick up on your fingertip, and they will sit there, their legs curled gently, for minutes at a time, as if curious, before skittering off to rejoin their swarm.
The rock-creepers, the sedge’s ground-dwelling counterparts, are the size of a large cat, with long, low bodies covered in rough, grey pumice plates that absorb moisture directly from the air and damp ash, so they almost never need to drink from pools. Their feet have tiny, backward-facing silica claws that let them climb the smooth obsidian walls as easily as they walk the ash floor, and they spend most of the day tucked into cracks in the rock to escape the midday heat, their bodies going dormant, their bioluminescent eye-spots (set into the sides of their heads, so they can see while burrowed) dimming to almost nothing. At dusk, they emerge to graze on the lichen-plumes, the soft, pendulous electric-blue and sulphur-yellow growths that hang from boulders and seep fans, absorbing moisture directly from the humid air. They also eat silica worms and the fallen sedge fruits, and their droppings are fine, mineral-rich pellets that feed the microbial mats, closing the loop. When the thrum from the spire shifts—every three or four days, a low, rolling change in frequency that you feel in your teeth before you hear it—they all climb to the highest points of the caldera walls and sit, motionless, for hours, their plates vibrating softly, as if they are absorbing the signal. No one knows what they do with it, but after these events, the lichen grows in brighter, denser patches around their perches.
The mist-fins are the most ghostly of the basin’s inhabitants, not quite bird, not quite insect: their bodies are small, opalescent, and nearly eyeless, their long, narrow wings stretched between thin, glass-like rods that refract light so sharply they seem to bend the air around them. They do not fly so much as glide, riding the thermals that rise off the warm rock and the tiny convection currents from the seeps, and they make no sound at all when they move, their wings so light they don’t even disturb the ash when they land low. They nest in the dense glass-sedge groves, tucking their long, narrow bodies between the stalks, and they feed exclusively on silicate moths, snatching them out of the air with tiny, clawed tips on their wing rods. They have a strange, internal organ that condenses water directly from the humid air, so they never need to drink from the pools, and they are most active at dawn and dusk, when the caldera’s weird shadow-play is at its most extreme: their refracting wings make them look like they have three or four shadows at once, stretching long and short and curving around boulders independent of the sun’s position. They are drawn to the silica monoliths, and you will often see them perched on their smooth, shadowless surfaces at dawn, their bodies glowing faintly as the sun comes over the caldera rim, as if they are listening to the thrum that hums loudest there.
The Half-Spire, half-submerged in the turquoise pool at the basin’s low point, is not alive in any way we would recognize, but it is the heart of the basin’s system, and every creature here is attuned to it. The brine-skimmers cluster thicker around its base than anywhere else in the pools, their bioluminescence pulsing in time with the hum that vibrates out of its honeycomb cells. The mist-fins will fly circles around it for hours at a time, their wings refracting the light that filters down through the water into the honeycomb, making the spire glow with shifting rainbows that look almost like a signal. The rock-creepers will sometimes make the long climb down to the pool’s edge to sit on the warm rock around it, motionless, for hours, as if charging themselves on its frequency.
There are no predators here, no competition, no waste. Everything feeds everything else, in a tight, closed loop held in the same uneasy balance as the caldera’s heat and wet, its sharp rock and soft life. The only disruptions are the rare, dry wind that sweeps the basin, shaking the glass-sedge stalks into a loud, chiming song that makes the moths swarm and the mist-fins take flight in glittering clouds. For a few minutes, the whole basin is loud, full of light and sound, before the wind dies down, the seeps resume their steady *plink… plink…*, and the thrum hums on, quiet and constant—the sound of the place thinking, and its tiny, strange inhabitants going about their lives in its shadow.
