You step off the sloping ramp onto the mosaic of slick mud and algae-slick stone, and the first thing you notice is how the damp doesn’t just sit on your skin—it seeps through it. The heat is a soft, wet weight, the kind that makes the hair on your arms curl and the fabric of your sleeves cling to your wrists even when you stand still. The smell hits you first: wet clay cut with the sharp tang of ozone, underlaid with that faint, overripe mango sweetness that comes and goes with no warning, like the atrium is breathing out a memory of a fruit that never grew here.
The silence is so thick you can almost taste it, broken only by the slow, asynchronous concerto of drips: a distant plink from a ceiling lost in the dark overhead, a soft shhh as a bead rolls off a frond and vanishes into the peaty soil, the faint glug of water weeping from a pipe into its stone basin. There’s no hum of insects, no rustle of small creatures in the undergrowth, no skitter of claws on stone—fauna here is so scarce it feels like an oversight, a line left blank in the world’s design. The only life you can see at first is the vegetation: the cathedral stands of translucent, smoke-green fronds, their broad leaves dripping constant, warm tears; the pulsing, phosphorescent moss that glows a slow, sleepy amber, brightening and dimming so gradually you can’t catch it moving unless you look away for ten minutes and look back; the thick, copper-barked vines that climb the pale stone walls in scattered, braided ropes, their bark warm to the touch like it’s carrying a faint, harmless current.
But if you stay still for long enough, the small things emerge.
The crystalline fungi that engulf the iron pillars holding up the corroded, ghostly caterpillar of walkways don’t just look like glass—they ring like it, if you tap them gently with a knuckle, and tiny blue sparks jump from the point of impact, the same cold blue-white that flickers from the half-buried junction boxes scattered across the stone walls. Those boxes don’t look like they’re part of the natural rock, but if you reach out to touch one, your fingers pass through the stone around it like it’s smoke, and the only thing you feel is a faint, static prickle, like the memory of electricity, long gone.
The giant turbine half-submerged in the southern mud is covered in moss so thick its blades are completely hidden, but if you brush a frond aside, you can see the faint, rusted ghost of a nameplate on its side, only two letters still legible: HV. No other markings, no indication of what it was meant to power, what system it was part of. The copper vines have grown into its cracks, their bright, unworn copper insides (if you scrape a flake of bark off) fused to the rusted metal, so if you tug on one, the whole turbine creaks, a low, slow sound like it’s shifting in its sleep. The mud around it is full of tiny, eyeless, translucent worms the size of rice grains, so slow you can only tell they’re moving if you stare at a handful of mud for an hour, their pale bodies drifting through the peat, eating nothing anyone can name, leaving no trace.
The water that weeps from the thick pipes emerging from the walls flows slightly uphill: you can see the tiny bubbles drifting upward against the stone, climbing ten feet to vanish into a fissure in the ceiling. A single blue forget-me-not grows out of that fissure, no soil, no light but the faint blue glow that seems to come from the stone itself, pulsing in time with the moss, never wilting, never blooming, just existing. If you collect that water in a flask, it’s just warm, mineral-heavy, tasteless—until you leave the flask open in the atrium, and the water slowly rises in it, against gravity, spilling over the rim no matter how much you pour out, like the place refuses to let its water be taken away.
The shadows here don’t obey the rules. The amber glow from the fronds should cast warm, soft shadows, but your shadow is sharp, cool blue, lit by a source you can’t find. If you move, your shadow lags half a second behind you, like it’s made of something heavier, slower than you are. The scent nostalgia hits you out of nowhere sometimes: the smell of cold iron, of printer paper, of the linoleum floor of a school hallway you haven’t thought of in twenty years, no source, just a faint drift on the slow, invisible air currents that move through the space like quiet eddies.
The bricked-up archway on the western wall is the only place the copper vines won’t grow. They stop a foot away from it, their braided bark curling back like they’re avoiding something. The stone around the arch is stained dark, not with mineral streaks, but with a faint, baked-on soot that never washes away in the constant condensation, even though there’s never been fire here. If you press your ear to the brick, you don’t hear anything, but the smell of printer paper is strongest here, like there’s a desk just on the other side, papers spread out on it, waiting for someone to come back who never will.
The erosion here is young—crisp, sharp stone edges, no rounding from wind or water, like this entire amphitheater was carved and exposed only yesterday, in the span of geological time. But the processes inside it are ancient, glacial. The moss has grown a foot thick over the turbine’s blades in what must have been a century, the crystalline fungi have already engulfed half the iron walkway pillars, the frond grove on the eastern side has spread ten feet in the last fifty years. Nothing rushes. The turbine rusts a molecule at a time. The moss pulses, slow as a heartbeat. The vines contract minutely in the heat, so slow you can’t see it happening, only notice they’re a little shorter at the end of a long, unmeasured day.
This place is a tomb and a womb, all at once. The artifacts scattered through it are the bones of a world that never was, an engine room built for a purpose no one left behind a record of, now half-digested by the quiet, luminous jungle that’s grown over it. There are no graves, no markers of death, just the slow, patient work of growth taking back what was made. If you sit on one of the low, moss-covered stone tiers that rise like theater seats from the center of the amphitheater, you can feel the deep, sub-audible hum of the planet’s heat in your bones, the same hum that vibrates through the stone walls. You can listen to the drips, slow and off-kilter, try and fail to find their rhythm. You can watch a new frond unfurl, so slow you only see the difference if you blink twice. You can feel, for a second, like you’re not alone, that there’s someone just out of sight, behind the bricked-up arch, or under the mud, or in the dark of the vaulted ceiling—until you blink, and it’s just you, and the moss, and the slow, patient dripping of water that sometimes flows uphill, for no reason anyone can explain.
