You know this place by the cold that lives in your marrow. You know it by the smell: wet stone, old smoke, the sour tang of too many bodies in a closed breath. Your world is the press of rock on all sides, the constant, quiet song of water. *Plink. Trickle. Groan.*

You wake not to a sound, but to a change in sound. The deep river’s hum has shifted key in your sleep, a lower note now, a warning. You are already moving before your eyes are open, your hands finding the rough-hewn edge of your sleeping ledge, your feet testing the packed-earth floor for the familiar chill of damp. Your home is a cube of shadows, walls of wattle and clay smelling of last night’s fire and generations of sweat. A hide hangs over the entrance. You push it aside and step into the artery.

The main cavern is a slow, breathing beast. The air is thick with the mineral musk of the geothermal hall, the greasy smoke of a hundred low-burning lamps. You are part of the press. You nudge a basket of drying kelp from the path with your foot, its stiff strands whispering against your ankle. You brace yourself as a woman above you hauls a skin of water from a cistern, the rope creaking, the bucket swinging wide. A drop of icy spray catches your neck. You do not flinch. You are a cell in this body. Your space is the sliver between the potter’s wheel and the drying rack of fish skins. Your territory is the vertical: the notch in the wall where your tools hang, the shadow under the shelf where your children sleep.

You work with the rock. That is the first truth. Your pick is antler-tipped, worn smooth by quartz and grit. Your hammer is river stone. You know the sound of good stone—a clean, ringing *thock*—and the sound of a fracture beginning—a dull, warning *clack*. You listen for it always. You feel the new seepages with the backs of your hands, the cold kiss of a fresh vein. The rock gives shelter. The rock takes without asking.

You follow the beam.

It is your clock, your god, your measure. The pillar of daylight from the high fissure has just begun its crawl across the floor, a slow river of brilliant gold in the perpetual twilight. You have maybe a third of a day before it kisses the far wall and dies. You move with it, your tasks ordered by its passage. When it grazes your workbench, you are shaping the last of the day’s flint. When it reaches the central vent, you take your children to the warmth, to the hiss of sulphur and the soft glow of the fungi gardens. You watch the motes of dust dance in it, the only snow you will ever know.

You speak in tunnels.

Not just with your voice, but with your body. A touch on the shoulder: *The north spur is loud today.* A knot tied in a rope left by the cistern: *Beware the drip-line, it has shifted.* The dialect is one of gestures, grunts, and shared silences. You have never seen the north spur. No one you know has gone more than a day’s walk into the unmapped dark. To go farther is to forget the beam, to lose the sound of water, to become a story yourself—a warning told to children: *Stay where you can hear the pack.*

The pack is your skin and your cage. You feel the conflict in the tightness of the alley at feeding time, in the averted eyes over a contested fungi patch. You know the cough that means the lung-sickness is moving again. You have covered for your neighbor when her child cried too long in the night, inviting scrutiny. You have kept your own child quiet with a hand over his mouth when a patrol of the rock-watchers came, their torches throwing monstrous shadows. You need them to lift the stone. They need you to know the water’s song. You hate them sometimes. You would die for them without a second thought.

You have never seen the surface.

You know it from legend. A place of endless, burning white light that blinds. A vast, empty, howling emptiness where you cannot hear your own thoughts. The air is thin and sharp, a blade in the lungs. The legends say those who go there returnmad, babbling about a round, fiery eye in the sky. You believe them. Your world is the dim, the damp, the close. The surface is a myth for those who have nothing left to lose.

Your life is the passage.

It is the beam’s crawl. It is the water’s journey from high fissure to deep river. It is your own breath, in and out, in the packed dark. You do not think of tomorrow. You think of the next wall to shore, the next bundle of reeds to weave, the next child to teach the sound of a good stone from a bad. You are building. You are holding back the rock. You are catching the water. You are listening for the silence that comes before a fall.

You are here, in the cold, in the wet, in the press, under the slow, bright, crawling beam. You are the act of becoming itself. You are the place, breathing. You are the dripping. You are the packed. You are the waiting, and the working, in the dark.
