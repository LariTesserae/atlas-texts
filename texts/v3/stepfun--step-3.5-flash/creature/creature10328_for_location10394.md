What lives here does not live as you know life. There is no breath, no hunger, no birth or death in the way you understand them—only participation in the mesa’s single, unending act of unmaking. All of it is woven from the same threads as the place itself: ferrous ash, superheated steam, the subsonic thrum of peeling stone, the sourceless amber glow seeping from the crust. It does not dwell *on* Shard-Mesa so much as it is the mesa’s motion given fleeting, flickering form.

The first thing you will notice, if you stand still long enough, is the dust. The fine, warm ash that coats every surface is slightly magnetic, and the low hum of the exfoliating sheets makes it dance in silent, swirling columns a few inches above the ground: these are the Sift-Weavers. They are not individual creatures, but swarms of ferrous ash flecks and microscopic glass shards, held in tight coils by the magnetic field bleeding off the peeling monoliths. They move only where the vibration is strongest, surging toward new fissures the second a crack spiderwebs across a sheet’s surface, carrying tiny loads of sulfur dust from the vents to the fresh breaks to speed the exfoliation along. They have no will, no shape, but if you hold out your hand, they will drift down to rest on your skin, leaving faint violet smudges that glow for an hour and make the tectonic thrum thrum twice as loud in your bones. They do not bite, do not sting—they only cling, warm and weightless, like ash held in a magnetic field.

Closer to the great curling sheets, you may spot them: the Lamina-Wights. They dwell in the thin, glowing gap between a sheet that is peeling away and the warm rock beneath it, a space filled with superheated steam and mineral aerosol. Their bodies are made of overlapping wafer-thin slivers of the same black glass as the mesa, held together by the seeping steam, their edges sharp enough to draw blood if brushed too hard. They move rarely, and only when the sheet shifts: when a fissure widens, they will scuttle across the gap on dozens of needle-thin glass legs, leaving faint, glowing orange footprints on the ash that fade in minutes. They are not aggressive, only curious, in the slow, cold way of stone. If you crouch near a peeling sheet, one may peer out from the gap, its topmost glass sheet tilting like a head, refracting the amber glow into shifting shards of violet and green. If you hold out a hand, it may reach out a thin, sharp tendril to tap your palm, not hard enough to break skin, just enough to leave a warm, tingling prickle—tasting the salt and iron of your sweat, testing the anomaly of your warm, soft flesh against the cold, hard mesa. When a sheet finally shatters, they will dissolve back into the ash and steam before the sound of the break reaches you, leaving only a faint wisp of steam where they stood. Their shed glass flakes, if you find them, stay warm for days; hold one to your ear, and you will hear the tectonic thrum, clear as a bell, pulsing in time with your own heartbeat.

Miles away, where the ring of monolithic pillars rises from the ash, lives the only persistent, collective entity on the mesa: the Core-Singers. The pillars are the fossilized cores of a single, continent-sized sheet that peeled away in a single, impossible perfect circle eons ago, their inner veins laced with ferrous crystal and trapped thermal energy. They have no visible form, no sound you can hear with your ears—they are a resonance field, a chorus of crystal vibration woven into the rock itself. Stand within the ring, and the deep tectonic thrum will sharpen, shift into a low, wordless hum that vibrates in your teeth and the hollow of your throat, like the mesa is singing the shape of its own unmaking. They do not act, do not interact, but they feel: chip a piece of a pillar, and the hum will stutter, a cracked note in the song. Set the chip back in the circle’s center, and the hum will steady after a few minutes, leaving a faint, warm weight in your palm like a hand resting there for as long as you remain on the mesa. When a new sheet begins to peel on the far horizon, the pillars will glow brighter for ten seconds, their internal fractures flaring like veins lit from within—greeting the new fracture, the new unmaking, as it is born.

Underfoot, between the curling sheets and the fallen arches, the tiny glass spires that rise and collapse in days are home to the Spire-Motes. Each spire is hollow, fed by mineral-rich steam rising from the ash, and at its center hovers a single mote of glowing ferrous ash, no larger than a grain of sand. They pulse faintly in time with the tectonic thrum, living only as long as the spire stands—hours, days, never longer. They do not move, do not feed, only glow, brief sparks of the mesa’s constant becoming. When the spire collapses back into powder, the mote dissolves into the ash, its energy bleeding into the ground to seed the next spire that rises a few feet away. They are the shortest-lived things on the mesa, and the most common: millions of them dot the ash, tiny beacons in the dark, each one a blink of the place’s endless, slow breath.

At the edges of the sulfur-yellow vents, oozing from the rock where the metallic steam seeps out, are the Vent-Drifts. They are slow, semi-transparent blobs of superheated mineral slurry, warm to the touch, fizzing faintly like soda left in the sun. They ooze so slowly you can barely see them move, feeding on the minerals in the steam and excreting tiny flecks of black glass that the faint updrafts carry into the ash, to become the seed crystals for new spires. If you were foolish enough to reach into a vent, one might ooze around your finger, warm and soft as honey, leaving no mark when you pull away. Take one off the mesa, and it will turn to inert gray dust in an hour; it exists only in the heat and mineral vapor of Shard-Mesa. When a distant sheet shatters, all the Vent-Drifts on the mesa will pulse bright yellow for a few seconds, like embers flaring in a draft.

Sometimes, when a crack propagates fast across a peeling sheet, or a spire collapses in a cloud of ash, you will see them: the Fray-Phantoms. They are flickering, ash-and-glass outlines of humanoid figures, standing a hundred paces away in the gloom, silent, still. They last only a few seconds, dissolving into the swirling dust before you can take a step toward them. They are not ghosts, not spirits, not the echoes of people who stood here before—they are the refraction of amber light through clouds of ash and flying glass shards, a trick of the mesa’s unstable geometry and the constant vibration. They mean nothing, and they mean everything: proof that even the empty dark of Shard-Mesa wears the shape of what you expect to see, if only for a heartbeat.

None of these things are separate from the mesa. They do not live on it; they are its process made visible, its unmaking given a face. The Sift-Weavers carry the ash that feeds the vents; the Vent-Drifts make the glass that grows the sprites; the Lamina-Wights weaken the rock that peels into sheets; the Core-Singers hum the thrum that drives it all. There is no predator, no prey, no cycle of consumption—only a single, endless loop of becoming and unbecoming, of stone turning to ash turning to steam turning to stone, over and over, as the mesa unbuilds itself into something no mind has yet thought to imagine.
