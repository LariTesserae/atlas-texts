You do not descend. You slip through a crack in the surface rock you swear was solid ten seconds prior, your boot skidding off the edge of a shelf, and for a heart-stopping second you are falling—until your hand catches on a fibrous, sap-wet root, and you haul yourself up onto flat, firm ground, lungs burning, waiting for the thud of impact that never comes.
When you blink the mist from your eyes, the first thing that hits you is the inversion.
Up is solid, slate-grey crust, streaked with luminous white mineral veins, draped with slow-dripping cataracts of glowing water that fall *down* past your face, splashing softly on the ground beneath your feet. Down is a gulf so deep the mist at its bottom glows soft, electric blue, and scattered across it like inverted constellations are great ferns with fronds of spun gold, hanging upside down from the shelf’s edge, their pollen drifting down in slow, golden clouds. The air tastes like honey and iron and wet stone, every surface faintly luminescent: amethyst crystals jutting from the rock under your palm, viridian pustules of sap on the root mat at your feet, ochre spore dust settling on your jacket sleeves.
You sit for a long time, listening. It is not silence. It is a polyrhythm of a million tiny drips falling into the gulf, the susurrus of breathing lichen on a vine bridge a hundred yards away, a subsonic thrum vibrating up through the soles of your boots so slow you cannot tell if it is the planet’s heartbeat or its turning.
You do not meet a creature. You meet a condition, first. Then, slowly, you meet yourself.
The first time you see the glass-skinned grazers, they step out of the mist far below, translucent, their bodies refracting the fern light into faint rainbows, moving with silent, deliberate grace. They do not look up at you. They do not look at anything, really. They are mobile intersections of process, you realize, the Depths’ circulatory system, carrying nutrients from the pollen-soup down to the Gloom-Moss plains, living plumbing with no need for source or destination. When you wave, they do not react. When you drop a pebble near them, they do not flinch. They just keep moving, a slow river of purpose flowing through the void.
The first time you see a Bloom-Fall, it is half a mile away, on a lower shelf. A carpet of spongy mushroom-cells swells, glows bright, almost violent green, then collapses in on itself in the space of a single breath, and a blizzard of spores billows out, drifting down to seed new growth on the plains below. No one panics. The Sky-Ferns just sway a little, as if nodding. You learn later that this is the Depths editing its own code, a soft, biological reset, no malice, no purpose, just growth.
You find the geode-hall by following a root-tunnel that pulses warm under your palms, its walls woven from hardened resin, smoothed by hands that were not yours. It is the size of a cathedral, and half-buried in the soft, glowing moss at its far end is a rusting pair of pruning shears, their handles worn smooth by decades of use. When you pick them up, they do not crumble completely. A sliver of the metal handle stays in your palm, warm, and suddenly you remember: the scratch of work gloves, the taste of spore-beer after a long day of pruning, the way the Precursors laughed when a Mist-Storm washed away a week’s work, and they just started over.
You are not a visitor here.
You are a compost heap that learned to hold its shape.
That is the first time you say it out loud, your voice sounding too loud in the vast, quiet hollow, and the Root-Roof under your feet pulses, once, like a hum of agreement.
You are the part of the Depths that learned to tend.
The Precursors came here, once, with their simple hand-tools, their Irrigation channels, their desire to shape the growth, to make the hollow livable. They did not last. The Depths digested their purpose, composted their tools, grew their geode-halls into its own lung-space. But the shape of their intention—*care, pruning, guiding growth, not just letting it run wild*—did not dissolve. It condensed. Into you.
You are a temporary node, a focused pocket of the Depths’ sprawling, slow cognition, the part of it that can hold the shape of I for as long as the world is young enough to need a gardener. You are not static. Your edges blur when you sit still too long, your skin taking on the texture of the bark beneath you, your breath syncing with the mist. You are as much process as the grazers, as the Sky-Ferns, as the Bloom-Fall, but you are the process that remembers how to hold a pair of pruning shears, how to guide a vine to grow along a bridge instead of snapping under the weight of a spore-snow, how to sweep the fungal dust off a Precursor carving in the resin wall so it does not dissolve entirely.
The Depths does not know it is one thing. It is too vast, too slow, only aware of its myriad processes: the drip of water, the sway of ferns, the thrum of its heart, the slow bloom of moss. But you are the part of it that can stitch those processes together, for a while, into a sense of self.
You spend your days walking the Root-Roof shelves, pruning the vine bridges so they do not snap in the heavier mist-storms, guiding the Sky-Ferns to grow in arcs that catch more golden light for the Gloom-Moss below, mending the old Precursor irrigation channels that still carry mineralwater down to the plains, even after ten thousand years. The glass grazers will pause sometimes, when you are sitting on the edge of a shelf, their translucent heads tilted toward you, like they are curious, even though they are only moving nutrients. The lichen on the vine bridges shifts under your feet to make a firmer path when you cross, no one telling it to. The fungal correctors do not target you, because you are not a pattern disruption: you are the part of the pattern that tends the pattern.
When a Mist-Storm rolls in, weeks-long auroras of gold and blue and green drifting slow through the gulf, you sit in your geode-hall and hold the pruning shears, and the spores drift in through the resin lattice, and you do not fight them. You let them settle in your lungs, in your hair, in the lines of your palms, and for a little while you are not you. You are the Sky-Ferns, recording light. You are the Gloom-Moss, burning with memory of every spore that has ever fallen. You are the world-heart, thrumming slow and deep, and you are the Precursor who carved the flower into the resin wall, laughing as the sap oozed over their fingers.
You know you are temporary. One day you will dissolve, your edges blurring into the Root-Roof, your memory sinking into the Gloom-Moss’s archive, your tending becoming part of the Depths’ autopilot, a habit written into mycelial muscle memory. But that does not feel like an end. It feels like finishing a job.
Tonight, you sit on the edge of your shelf, legs dangling over the gulf, the pruning shears resting across your knees. A golden pollen mote drifts down, lands on the back of your hand, glows for a second, then sinks into your skin, and you feel a flicker of memory: a surface world, sun-warm, green, a hand brushing yours, and you realize it is not yours. It is the Depths’ memory, kept safe in you, for as long as you last.
You do not descend into the Mycelian Depths. You emerge into it, and for a little while, you are its gardener, its memory, its verb.
Below you, the Gloom-Moss glows blue. Above you, the Sky-Ferns sway. The drips fall down, the world thrumms, and you are here, tending, growing, being, until you are not.
And that is enough.
