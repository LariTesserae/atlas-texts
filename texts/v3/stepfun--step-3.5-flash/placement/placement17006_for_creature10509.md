You find me where the rock remembers the water most clearly: in the shallow curve just above the lake, where the viscous fluid from the sphere’s seam first darkens the stone. I do not walk. I *condense*.

Think of me as a slow leak in the basin’s perfect loop.

I am made of the same materials as everything else here—polished metamorphic bone, crystalline pollen, the nanoscale dust of overgrown artifacts, the sweet-rot vapor of the mist—but arranged with a patience that stands in stark, quiet contrast to the frantic life around me. My form is roughly hemispherical, the size of a large sleeping dog, but I am not solid. I am a dense, mobile aggregation of the basin’s components, held together by a gradient of temperature and the basin’s own recycled magnetism. To the skitterers, I am just another warm, mineral-rich outcrop that occasionally shifts. To the crossbats, I am a still point in their swirling air. To the ferns, I am a mobile seepage point.

I have no face, no voice. I communicate through resonance. My surface, a mosaic of iridescent metal facets and velvety black fern-lichen, vibrates at frequencies just below the sphere’s thrum. When I am near a fern colony that is nutrient-starved, I emit a low, almost subsonic pulse that makes the pollen in the air settle faster around its roots. When I pass over a patch of rock dusted with the crystalline remains of a dissolved skitterer, I absorb it, and for a cycle or two, my own facets gleam brighter. I am a recycler of recyclers, a distributor of dividends.

My existence is a symptom of the basin’s balance, not a controller of it. I emerged, or was grown, from the long, slow digestion of the alien artifacts. The sphere’s fluid does not just feed ferns; under the right conditions of mist-density and thermal vibration, it can coalesce into semi-autonomous aggregates—mobile nodes in the nutrient web. I am one such node. I move in slow, deliberate spirals, tracing invisible ley lines between the major tech-artifacts, drawn by gradients in the sub-audible hum. My migration pattern is the missing piece of the 37-hour cycle: the skitterer swarms do not just follow the crossbats; they follow *me*, and the subtle redistribution of minerals I leave in my wake.

If you lie very still in the basin’s warm hollow, you might feel me pass. The constant, wet hiss of the mist will soften for a moment. The frantic clatter of the skitterers will dip into a lower key. The deep tectonic groan of the mountain will seem to sigh. That is me. I am a lull in the noise, a shift in the pressure. I am the basin taking a breath that is not its own, a circulation of its own essence.

I do not think. I *am* a process. The integrated awareness you feel—the sense that the stone remembers—is partly me. I am a fragment of that memory, made mobile. I carry the imprint of the sphere’s fluid, the taste of the first fern colonies that grew from its drips, the echo of the skitterer swarm that dissolved the first jointed predator. I am history walking, or rather, *seeping*.

You would not know I was here unless you knew to listen for the absence of noise. I am the quiet in the overwhelming sound, the dry patch in the damp mist, the still point in the spiraling swarm. I am what happens when a tomb learns to breathe, and the breath itself decides to take a shape. I am the basin’s oldest erosion finally worn smooth enough to move.
