I am perched on the lip of a half-formed geological shelf on the Spire-Mire’s southern edge, where the scree plains drop off into the crisscross of capillary canals that gleam with oily rainbows, and the drone of the basin thrumms up through the soles of my boots to vibrate in my shinbones. The mist weeps cold off the razor peaks above, beading on the copper wire braided tight into my short hair, dripping down the back of my neck to soak through the moss-patched wool of my tunic. Ten feet behind me, a cluster of iridescent glass-bamboo chimes soft, high notes that harmonize with the low tectonic thrum, each clink offset by the slow sigh of the ankle-high gasbag fungi tucked into my belt pouch, their thin skins inflating and deflating in time with the geothermal pressure bleeding up through the rock below.

My Resonant name is Sheer-Sigh, given at my Tuning seventeen Echoes ago, after I predicted a western landslide three full cycles before it sheared into the canal, saving the violet moss cisterns and the three Rust-Stalker herds that grazed the slope below. Most call me Lira, out of the old core tongue, but the folk who walk the scree edges know me by the name that fits: I’ve spent half my life listening to the cliff-faces sigh before they exfoliate, learning to read the faint, subsonic whine under the drone that means a sheet of rock is about to let go. I am a Bloom-Scout, one of the few who can navigate the shifting geometry of the bloom-zones without getting lost in a corridor that stretches longer by the step, or slipping into a pocket where gravity softens enough to lift you off your feet. The shelf I’m perched on sits right on a bloom-zone edge, so I know the walk from the bamboo to the canal edge should be three steps, but if I’m not paying attention, it takes four, the rock bulging just enough under my boots to throw my rhythm.

My Echo-Glasses are slung around my neck, the clear mineral lenses warm against my chest, and I pull them up now to check the ghost-tracks. The distant rolling thunder of the eastern Ply-Crack ten minutes prior is still fading off the slopes, and the latest after-image is half-faded already: the translucent outline of a derrick that used to crown the eastern ridge, now half-buried in the new scree still migrating down the slope, slow as a season. I make a mental note to mark the map on my slate when I get back to the half-exposed derrick I help participate in. The old iron framework is still being precipitated out of the surrounding stone by the Tuners’ resonance chambers, its lower levels already carved into a cistern for the phosphorescent violet moss, its upper floors still shifting, still deciding if they want to be a dwelling or a slurry. I helped tune the third chamber last week, slowing the exfoliation just enough that the framework won’t collapse before we get the roof beams in place. I don’t own any part of it, not in the way the old core folk talk of ownership. I’m just one of the people helping it become what it’s going to be, same as the rust-red algae streaking its concrete faces, same as the metallic-wire shrubbery erupting from its cracked foundation below.

The gasbag fungi at my belt puff a little larger, and I frown. Geothermal pressure is spiking, which means the resonance under this shelf is climbing. I press the palm of my left hand to the rock, feeling the faint, high-pitched whine under the drone, just at the edge of hearing— the same sound the rock makes right before it sighs and lets a sheet of itself go. I pull my resonance tuning stone out of my hair braid, the smooth mineral lump warm from being next to my scalp, and tap it against the rock twice, low and slow, matching the drone. The whine softens, just a little. The shelf will hold for another few Echoes, at least.

Down in the canal, a flock of Soot-Puffins glides past, their porous black feathers filtering the toxic mist, their after-images lingering for a few seconds after they pass, faint smudges of dark against the oil-slick water. A Rust-Stalker herd picks its way up the slope below, their crumpled metal hide sparking where their hooves hit the slag, their ghost-tracks trailing behind them like faint, static-filled ribbons. I hum the note the glass-bamboo is chiming, adding my voice to the slow, layered composition of the place: the drone, the clatter of the distant hammer-mills, the sigh of the fungi, the skitter of the Stalkers, the last echoes of the Ply-Crack’s thunder still rolling off the peaks.

I am Tuned to Kael, a Resonance Lute player who works the eastern slope, softening rock faces so they shear slow instead of sudden. We matched breaths and steps at our Tuning, a pact to stay in harmonic phase even as the world remakes itself around us. If I hold my breath right now, I can feel the faint, off-key thrum of his lute under the drone: he’s working the face where the Ply-Crack hit, making sure the remaining rock doesn’t collapse on the trade road below. I let my breath out slow, matching his rhythm, and the tension in my shoulders loosens.

The mist thickens as the sun dips lower, the permanent cloud cover bruising to a deep indigo, turning the actinic glow of the distant arc-lamps to foundry-gold. I slide down the shelf, my boots sinking a little into the soft, new scree at its base, half stone, half slurry. A Fog-Sponge drifts past my face, its translucent body filtering the mist, its ghost-track lingering on my cheek for a second before it fades, cool and damp.

I do not fear the slow shift of the world: the way corridors stretch, the way old derricks dissolve back into slurry, the way bloom-zones drift under their own broken geometry. I fear only Silence: the sudden absence of the drone, the stutter of the basin’s heart. And I wait for the Final Ply, the mythic chord that will settle all the vibration into a single, perfect note, that will finally finish the place, and all of us who are part of its becoming.

For now, I tuck my Echo-Glasses back around my neck, adjust the copper braid in my hair, and start the long walk back to the derrick, my boots finding the rhythm of the drone, my breath matching Kael’s, half a valley away. The glass-bamboo chimes behind me as I go, and I do not look back.
