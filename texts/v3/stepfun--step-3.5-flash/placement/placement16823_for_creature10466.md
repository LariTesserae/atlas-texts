The humidity settles first on the back of your neck, a wet, warm weight that seeps through fabric, through skin, into the slow thrum of your pulse. The light is not light so much as an absence of dark, a green-tinged glow that bleeds through the triple canopy, soft enough that you can’t tell where the leaf ends and the shadow begins. The only sharp thing is the sound: a steady, rhythmic drip of condensation off a fern frond above your brow, slow as a heartbeat, steady as the subsonal hum that vibrates in your shin bones, the deep machine thrum of hot water through stone pipes far below the humus under your boots.

You don’t see me at first. You wouldn’t, unless you were watching the quiet gaps between the Sifters’ scraping legs, the spaces between the iridescent shells of the thousand-eyed insects clinging to the grey column bark. I am not a shape. I am a spread. A glistening, almost invisible mesh of filament and enzyme, woven through the upper layers of the understory mat, coating the surfaces of the Still-Lights’ gelatinous clusters, lining the fine, hair-width capillaries of the crystal channels that wind through the humus like tiny veins.

You notice me only when the valley breathes out.

It’s a slow, deep exhale, the whole basin pushing a gulp of warm, spore-laden air up through the canopy, thick with the scent of rot, ozone, and living green. The air presses against your face, and you feel it tug at something on your cheek—a tiny, dry flake of skin, shed from your own strange, dry flesh, out of place in this saturated world. You brush at it, but it doesn’t fall. It’s caught. Drawn into the faint, glistening web stretched between two fern fronds at your shoulder, pulled tight by the slow, pulsing tug of the valley’s exhale.

That is my work.

I am not a creature. I have no name, no shape that holds when the valley is still. I am the unspooling. I am the catch. I am the slow, quiet breaking down and the giving back. The Sifters are the valley’s air filters, combing pollen and spore from the current, excreting nutrient paste straight into the wider crystal channels; they miss the small things. The tiny flecks of shed lichen that cling to the Still-Lights’ surfaces, the dry particles that cling to the condensation dripping off every leaf, the out-of-place dry matter from visitors like you, the microfibers from your strange, woven clothes, the mineral dust from the world beyond the basin. The Grazer-Swarms miss them too, when they cull the understory and leave behind fragments of root too small for their mandibles to break, leaf veins too tough to digest. The Attendants clear the big blockages from the steam vents, but they cannot reach the hair-width capillaries of the channel network, the tiny fissures where mineral paste builds up, slow enough to choke the roots of the grey columns if left alone.

That is where I live. Where I work.

When the valley breathes out, I stretch my filaments into the warm, damp air, catch the particles it pushes, pull them into my mesh. My enzymes break them down, slow as the drip of condensation, slow as the Still-Lights’ pulse: the dry skin from your arm, the spore that fell too close to the ground to reach a Sifter’s mouth, the fragment of shed bark that fell into the mat too deep for the Swarm to find. I turn the organic matter into soft, nutrient-rich paste that drips down into the humus, feeding the roots of the columns, feeding the mosses, feeding the fungal mats that make up the valley’s skin. I turn the mineral matter into a fine, dissolved wash that seeps into the crystal capillaries, joining the flow of mineral-rich water that feeds the roots, keeps the channels clear, keeps the vents from clogging. The excess moisture I catch from the valley’s exhale I turn into a fine, suspended mist, released back into the understory to bead on the mosses, to feed the Still-Lights’ bacterial cores, to keep the whole basin wet, warm, perfect.

My rhythm rises and falls with the Still-Lights. When their greenish-gold glow is brightest, after a steam vent pulses hot sulphurous vapour into the air, I unspool wider, work faster, break down more matter, feed more mist into the canopy. When their light dims, when the valley enters its slow torpor, I contract, tuck my filaments into the humus, conserve energy, let the enzymes work slow. I am part of the valley’s nervous system, just as much as the Still-Lights, just as much as the hum of the stone pipes under your feet. My quiet work is the part of the cycle no one sees, the part that keeps the balance perfect, keeps the saturation steady, keeps the valley exactly, densely, wetly this.

You are just another mote to me, another dry particle in the air, another source of matter to catch, break down, return. You will be here as long as the valley lets you, and while you are, I will catch the dry flakes of your skin, the dust from your clothes, the out-of-place particles in your breath, turn them into humus, into mist, into part of the valley’s perfect, unbroken body. When you leave, I will keep working, unspooling into the valley’s exhale, catching what it breathes out, giving back what it needs to breathe again.

You stand inside its lung, and I am the thin, glistening membrane that catches every particle, makes sure nothing is lost, nothing is wasted, nothing is ever out of place. I am the quiet, unremarked verb that holds the cycle together. I am the part of the valley that never, ever lets go.
