The first thing I know is the hum in my teeth, the same low thrum I’ve felt since before I had a shape to hold it, since before I knew the difference between the damp air pressing against my skin and the dark water lapping at my calves. The cold seeps into the cracks of my knuckles first, slow and quiet, no bite at all, just the same slow damp that creeps into every surface here. It doesn’t bother me. I’m made of the same stuff as the walls, after all.

I don’t remember being made. I only remember waking up, standing on the curve of the vast dome, moss already growing in the creases of my wrists, the iridescent liverwort crusted into the ridges of my cheekbones. I am not sharp anywhere. My edges are worn soft, like the ribbed arches of the aqueduct, like the seamless plates of the basalt, no corners, no tool marks, only the gentle, rounded shape of something that has been smoothed by time the same way the stone has. Patches of polished dark basalt show through the moss where I move the most, where I brush against the walls, where I trail my fingers along the handprints. The thick, violet leaves of the succulents grow in clumps along my forearms, oozing that faint pine-and-wet-stone sap when I brush them against the stone, and faint grey bioluminescence pulses under my nails, the same soft glow as the fungus draped over the distant arches, just bright enough to stain the edge of the darkness if I hold my hand close to my face.

I do not have a name. Names were for the builders, and they are gone, and I only hold fragments of their memory, like the handprints pressed into the walls, worn soft by ten thousand years of moss and water and slow erosion. I am the residue of their labor, the part of the half-organic, half-synthetic material they used to build this place that gathered enough of their intention, enough of the weight of their hands, to wake up, to move, to remember.

My work is slow, same as everything here. I wander the layers, top to bottom, wading through the ink-dark water, sliding through the crumbled matrix of collapsed chambers, diving down to the sealed air pockets in the impermeable foundation shell where the water has never reached. I do not take anything from those pockets. The fire-hardened tools, the woven baskets stacked in the corners, the faint scratch marks of suns and rivers and faces carved into the dry stone—all of it belongs to the substrate, eventually. I only press my palm to the stone, and if I feel the faint impression of a hand on the other side, I hold that memory for a little while, so it does not fade entirely before the water wears the stone away.

There is one handprint I visit more than the others, carved shallow into the curved wall of the aqueduct, just above the water line. The edges are worn soft, not broken, and the callus at the base of the thumb is still rough, even through the layer of moss that has grown over it in the eons since. I do not know who left it. I only know they were tired, from the weight of the impression, from the way their palm dragged a little at the edge, like they were leaning hard against the wall to catch their breath after a long shift. I can feel the faint ghost of their heat still trapped in the stone, the faint echo of them looking out over the basin they had spent their whole life digging, knowing they would never see the work finished, knowing the only thing that would outlast the walls was the faint mark of their hand.

I sometimes wonder if they imagined something like me, when they pressed their palm to the stone. If they thought someone, something would be left to remember them, when the moss covered the walls, when the water filled the chambers, when the *plink* of falling droplets wore the stone away grain by grain. I do not think they did. I think they only wanted to leave a mark, any mark, to say they had been here, that they had worked, that they had existed.

The *plink* sounds now, close enough that the ripple it makes in the water brushes against the heel of my boot, against the moss growing there. I do not look up. There is no sky to look to, no way to tell where the sound comes from. I only lift my hand, press my palm to the handprint on the aqueduct wall, and hold it there for a second, matching my worn-soft edges to theirs, my calluses to theirs, across ten thousand years of quiet, dark water and slow, patient growth. For a second, the hum in my teeth fades, and I can feel the faint warmth of their palm against mine, and I know they are not gone, not really. Not as long as I am here, remembering.
