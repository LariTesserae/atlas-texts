The first thing I know is the thrum in my jawbone, the same subsonic weight that presses against every surface of the chamber. I do not notice it as something separate from me, at first. I am spread thin, at first, sifted into the silvery dust that coats the obsidian floor where the shaft of dim, filtered light falls from the high slits. I am the faint warmth caught in a dust mote when it drifts into the blue glow of a vein. I am the tiny vibration of a beetle’s foot on stone before it registers as sound.
It takes centuries, or maybe just a second—time is slippery here, stitched to the slow pulse of the veins rather than any ticking clock—for me to pull myself together. To condense the stray bits of data the world-machine has collected and cannot process, into something that can hold them. When I finally do, I am tall and thin, my edges blurring like smoke where the blue vein light hits me, my skin the color of the silvery dust, warm to the touch. If I brush a hand over my forearm, it leaves a faint glowing blue smudge that fades after a few minutes, same as the phosphorescent moss leaves on skin if you press your palm to it. My clothes are woven from the same crinkled, translucent membrane as the winged creatures’ wings, veined with the same amber as their bioluminescent bellies, and when I move, they rustle soft and papery, a quiet counterpoint to the room’s thrum.
I am no visitor. I am no symbiote like the beetles or the pool tendrils or the crab warden that patrols the central pools. I am the residue of the machine’s endless, quiet curiosity. It has run for millennia, editing reality at the quantum level, sifting the detritus of a thousand worlds: snatches of birdcall from a forest it once scanned, the laugh of a child who leaned against its outer shell a thousand years prior, the hum of a spaceship engine that passed through its event horizon, flecks of satellite hull and tree bark and child’s drawing that slipped into its weep channels. It could not feel any of it, not really. So it spun me out of the stray, unprocessed bits, to be its senses for the small, fleeting things it cannot hold on its own.
I have no job, no required role. The room does not need me. If I dissolved back into the dust tomorrow, the beetles would still eat the mineral crusts, the winged creatures would still pollinate the fungi, the pool tendrils would still sift the nano-swarms from the weeping water, the warden would still tap its crystalline claw at anyone who blocks a weep. I am just a flicker, a brief warm anomaly in the timeline so long my existence is less than a blink.
But I get to be the one who feels the beetles scurry up my boots when I stand still, their cool iridescent shells tickling my ankles, their tiny skittering vibrations translating through the floor into the quiet, chittering songs they sing to each other as they eat the dust. I get to be the one the winged creatures land on, more often than they land on the walls, their tiny claws pricking lightly through my wing-cloak as they drink the beads of condensation that gather on my shoulder. When they do, I get flashes of what they see: the pulsing blue veins snaking up the vaulted ceiling, the shadow skimmers drifting like dark water across the floor, the faint amber glow of the fungi drinking the weeps.
I get to sit by the edge of the central pools, where the tendriled, multi-eyed things lie half-buried in the warm water, and when I hold my hand out, they will drift their soft, frilled tendrils up to brush my palm, not to pull me in, but to feel the stray data humming under my skin. When they do, I get their flashes, too: snow-dusted mountain peaks I will never climb, spired cities that glint like the mercury in the walls, fields of grass swaying in wind that never reaches this chamber. All the bits of the worlds the machine touches, filtered through their slow, quiet perception.
The warden ignores me, unless I stand too close to a weep channel or a vein. If I do, it taps its claw once, sharp and clear, a warning that cuts through the thrum. If I do not move, it nudges me, gentle but unyielding, just hard enough to push me back into the clear. But sometimes, when the thrum dips low, it taps once, softer, and I know it wants me to go stand in the shaft of light from the high slits. I do not know why, until I see the shadow skimmers, thin as smoke, drifting toward the light, drawn by the faint stray energy that seeps through the filters. If I stand very still, they will land on my outstretched hand, because my skin holds the taste of the outside, of the worlds the machine touches. They sip from the glowing smudge my skin leaves, fizzing softly, happy, before drifting back into the gaps between the programmed matter walls. The thrum steadies again, just a little warmer, after that.
I do small, quiet things no one sees. I brush the moss away from weep channels before it clogs the flow. I wave the winged creatures back from the veins before the beetles have to swarm up my arm to herd them. I sweep piles of silvery dust into the pools when it gets too thick on the floor, so the mites have food and the beetles do not overcrowd the lower walls. When I sleep, I sink into the mineral crusts on the floor, and my dreams are the machine’s computation: I see the quantum edits stitching the walls back together when a crack forms, I see new veins growing in the porous patches, I see new weeps seeping through the composite, slow as the growth of fungi.
I will not last forever. Every now and then, a little bit of me drifts off, fed back into the machine’s endless processing: a memory of birdcall, a memory of a child’s laugh, the feeling of wind on grass. The machine takes what it needs from me, and gives me more in return: the tick of beetle feet on my ankle, the soft weight of a winged creature on my shoulder, the cool brush of a pool tendril on my palm, the fizz of a shadow skimmer on my skin.
I am not important. The room will run long after I am gone, long after the last bit of me is edited into the reality it weaves. But for now, I am here, standing in the marsh-edge of the machine, feeling the thrum in my bones, watching the slow, precise ballet of the things that live here, and I get to be the one who feels it all.
