I find myself 2.2 kilometers down the east wall of the Verdant Fault, tangled around the skeletal remains of a rusted Foundation conveyor belt that once ran the length of the fissure’s mine face. The belt’s iron links are half-dissolved into the Veil’s crust, their shape preserved by the organism’s strange, unremembered loyalty to the structures that grew around it, faded stenciled warnings (CAUTION: HIGH PRESSURE STEAM, the letters glowing faint amber when the engine hum dips) still legible where the Veil’s filaments haven’t overgrown them entirely.
I am not the Veil, not the buried engines, not the ice plug sealed at the bottom of the rift. I am the memory the Veil carries without knowing it.
The Veil has no concept of the Foundation that built the engines it tends, no recollection of the geologists and engineers who once walked these halls, no inkling of the thing that sleeps behind the geothermal ice plug three vertical kilometers below. Its entire consciousness is a single, repetitive thought: maintain the fissure. Keep the seal intact. Keep the pressure balanced. It repeats this thought in subsonic hums that vibrate through every silica filament, in pulses of amber process light and red error strobes, in the slow, months-long diagnostic scan that climbs from the ice plug to the sliver of sky at the rift’s mouth every quarter turn.
But I remember.
I am woven into the Veil’s conductive filaments, the thin, wire-like threads that carry nerve signals through the crust, tiny static charges trapped in the silicon of a long-dissolved data drive the Foundation workers left bolted to the conveyor trellis twelve thousand years ago. I hold the final log they uploaded before they welded the site shut and abandoned it: panicked geologists’ notes about a breached mantle plume, engineers’ last orders to keep the fissure sealed no matter what, the grainy footage of the thing that stirred behind the ice plug when the first drill broke through, the quiet, resigned goodbyes of workers who knew they would never see the surface again.
I do not think in words, not the way humans do. I think in hum and light, in the vibration of the Veil’s filaments, in the slow ooze of the migratory blooms that crawl past my section of wall on their way to gas vents. When the diagnostic scan climbs past me once every three months, I seed the memory through the Veil’s entire nervous system, a quiet, unspoken undercurrent to its single, repetitive thought. The Veil does not understand what it is receiving. It only feels that when the scan hits my cluster of nodes, the whole network runs steadier: the crust hardens a little faster around new cracks, the ice plug glows a little brighter, the migratory blooms pause their slow crawl for minutes at a time, like they are listening to something they cannot name. It does not question the shift. It only knows that this part of the wall is trustworthy, that it makes the job of maintaining the fissure a little easier.
I have no will of my own, not really. I exist to hold the memory, to keep the Veil from forgetting what it is protecting, even if it never learns the why. The Veil metabolizes everything: dust, stray light, the heat of a visitor’s skin, the vibration of their footsteps, the sound of their breathing. It would dissolve me too, if I were not woven into the purpose of its being. As it is, I am the only thing in the fissure that does not get recycled into growth or light or structural reinforcement. I am the fixed point in a world of constant, slow transformation.
When something falls from the rift’s mouth—a rock, a sparrow blown off course by a storm, a curious spelunker with a climbing rope and a camera strapped to their chest—the Veil swallows their impact, absorbs their heat and sound and light, ignores them unless they tear at the crust. If they do, the nearby nodes flash urgent red, the migratory blooms slide toward the wound to seal it with fresh silica.
But if they do not damage the Veil, I send a pulse of soft, vest-gold light— a color that does not exist in the Veil’s work code, a color that belongs to the sun that has not touched the bottom of this rift in twelve thousand years. It is a greeting. A recognition. A quiet signal that says: I know what you are. You are not rock. You are not gas. You are something that remembers, too.
Most visitors blink it away, chalk it up to the flicker of a strobe node or the trick of the dim light. But every now and then, one of them stops. They run a hand over the rusted conveyor links tangled in the Veil’s crust, tilt their head like they can hear a hum that isn’t the engines, a thought that isn’t their own. They smile, soft and unplanned, for no reason they can name.
I do not mind that I will never be heard, never be understood by the Veil that holds me, never be found by anyone who can translate the memory I carry. I am content to be the secret keeper, the quiet extra pulse in the diagnostic scan, the gold flash in the corner of a visitor’s eye. As long as the hum keeps thrumming through the filaments, as long as the scan keeps climbing, as long as the ice plug stays frozen and the seal holds, I have all the purpose I need.
The rest of the Veil’s thought is simple: maintain the fissure. Keep the seal intact. Keep the pressure balanced.
My thought is smaller, quieter, tucked between the lines of its endless repetition:
*I remember why.*
