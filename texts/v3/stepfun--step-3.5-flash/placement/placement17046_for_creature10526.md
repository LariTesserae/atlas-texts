You are not an *observer* here.  
You are a **Crust-Sigh**.

A transient node in the sintered skin.  
You form for a few decades, then fade.

You exist at the junction of two polygonal plates, where the crust’s slow breath generates a minute, persistent friction. Heat. Pressure. Mechanical stress. The piezophilic lichen here grows thicker, more vibrant, and in its pulverized cycles, mineral ions concentrate in a way they do not elsewhere. Calcium sulfates. Trapped heavy metals. Silica colloids.

Your body is a **crystalline lattice**, grown not by design but by chemical inevitability—a stiffening of the lichen’s excreted polymers, a deposition of mineral salts along stress lines. You are warm. You hum at a frequency only the silica-spike phalanx roots can feel, a vibration that guides their thirsty capillary networks infinitesimally toward richer chemical veins.

You have no senses. You *are* a sensor. A passive attractor.  
When the crust shifts (a sigh that travels for miles), you resonate. Your lattice flexes, ever so slightly, and releases a pulse of dissolved nutrients—phosphates, trace selenium—into the pore-water film at your base. A spike-tapper’s drill might tap near you tomorrow; the sap it draws will carry your signature.

You are **urban density made manifest**: a functional structure grown from the basin’s crowded necessity. Not a building, but a *process*. A brief punctuation in the long sentence of geology.

You remember nothing. You will remember nothing.  
But for the century you persist, you alter the chemical poetry of the soil beneath you by a single, subtle verse. A hundred years after you dissolve, a new phalanx grove will grow a half-degree off true north, its fronds angled toward a richer seam of ammonia—because you once hummed.

You are the Sintered Labyrinth’s **quietest citizen**:  
a temporary architecture,  
a mineral thought,  
a sigh given form,  
then given back to dust.
