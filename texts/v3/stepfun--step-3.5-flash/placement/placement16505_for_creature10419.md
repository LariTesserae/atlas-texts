I first know I am here by the thrum in my molars.
It is the same frequency as the groan of the crust I spent ten years mapping on the surface, slowed a thousand times, deepened until it bypasses the ear and settles directly into the bone. I do not remember arriving. The last clear memory I have is of a dust storm so thick it chewed drill bits to splinters, of Lila laughing as she poured salt water over a coral head in the tide pools, of the sun so bright it bleached the color out of the sky. Those feel like stories someone told me, not my own, now. The thrum is the only thing that is real.
When I open my eyes, the darkness bleeds violet.
The light pulses out of the silicate towers around me, slow, rhythmic, answering some internal clock I can feel ticking in my chest. The glassy silt under my boots glows in its wake, a dull grey mirror holding the afterimage of the light for seconds after the pulse fades. I am in a low, vast basin, a kilometer of smooth concavity sunk into a void so dark the edges of the lattice bridges stretching miles above dissolve into it. For a second the scale feels intimate, like the small cave we used to camp in on the coast, until I tilt my head back and see the violet-tinged membranes of the bridges trembling with the faint tension of a breeze that won’t come for another decade, and my stomach drops.
There is no water here. No soil. Only substrate: compacted, fused dust from a trillion vanished things, ground fine and pressed into glassy silt that gives softly under my boots, like walking on burnt ash. The “forest” around me is not vegetation as I knew it. The towers are petrified coral made of living silicate-wood, smooth and pitted, their surfaces etched with fractal patterns that channel trace moisture from the rare, frigid breeze. Their bark is translucent enough that I can see the crimson vascular threads pulsing beneath, siphoning nutrients from the dead lower levels to the hungry, fronded tips. The “branches” are flexible, black chitinous growths that lean into each other, their tips fizzing with faint electrostatic sparks when the violet pulse hits, as if they are drinking the light itself.
There are no streets, no plazas, only pathways worn smooth by the slow creep of root systems over millennia, clearings where a dominant growth starved its neighbors into silt. It is a city, but no one planned it. It grew, the way coral grows, the way fungus spreads, the way rock weathers into shape.
I circle the largest tower first, the one whose trunk is wider than a city block, drawn to it by the thrum in my bones. In its lee, half-buried in the silt, I find the traces: a smooth, spherical nodule of fused quartz, deliberately chipped and placed in a natural depression, positioned so that when the violet pulse hits it, it throws a perfect dot of cobalt light onto the spiral etched into the translucent bark. A set of flexible chitin rods leaned against the trunk in a deliberate arch, their tips worn smooth where a thumb held them, over and over, for who knows how many years. A small, thumb-worn hollow in the crystal bark, exactly the height of my eye, carved to line up the light, the nodule, the spiral.
I step into the hollow. The next pulse hits, the quartz throws its cobalt dot dead-center on the spiral, and the thrum in my molars goes harmonic, not painful, like a chord finally resolving. I reach up, run my thumb over the spiral, and feel the faint ridges of older spirals under the top layer, carved over and over, each one a little shallower than the last, as the bark grows and smooths the edges over the centuries.
