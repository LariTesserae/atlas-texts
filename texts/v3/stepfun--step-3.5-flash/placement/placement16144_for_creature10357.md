I am three miles east of the seventh steam vent I have counted, sitting cross-legged in a seam three inches wide, where the seep runs thick enough to coat the black basalt fissure in dark, glistening silt. The cell to my left still holds the day’s heat, radiating through the denim of my jeans until my left thigh is warm enough to feel like it is resting against a living thing; the cell to my right is cool from the constant weep, so the air between them curls with faint, almost invisible vapor that clings to the salt crust on my upper lip.

I did not come here to map the hex cells, or sample the lichen, or measure the frequency of the mineral tick. I came because I was tired of words that cut the world into pieces with sharp, bleeding edges—names, borders, purposes—and wanted to be somewhere that only knew processes, slow and unbroken. I am the first dry, warm-blooded thing to cross this basin in however many millions of years it took the basalt to set into its perfect honeycomb, and for the first three days I was here, the Crease-Singer knew me only as *the-walking-dry-crack*, a temporary, violent absence in the wet, a disruption in the capillary flow that made the silt swirl confusedly around my boot prints. It tasted my sweat, the iron in my blood, the faint bitter tang of the coffee I drank before I left the paved world, and filed those flavors away as anomalies, strange chemical aberrations to be puzzled over the way it puzzles over a fleck of quartz trapped in a crevice.

Now, I think, it knows me as something else. I have not moved from this seam for 19 hours, I think—time is slippery here, marked only by the seven-hour intervals between steam vent eruptions, and the once-daily violet pulse of the lichen crust under my palms, which glows soft enough to turn the black silt around my fingers purple. I let the seepwater soak through the knees of my jeans, let the mineral stain creep up my forearms where I rest them on the crack edges, let the fine grit from the last steam mist settle in my hair and on the shoulders of my jacket until I am dusted in the same pale umber as the basalt itself. I do not speak. When I first tried, my voice came back to me a half-second later, muffled and warped, as if it had bounced off the horizon itself, and the seepwater in the crack next to me rippled, disturbed, so I stopped. Words are too sharp here. The Singer thinks in gradients and flows and the slow, syncopated tick of a million mineral grains settling into the drying crevices after a steam rain, and I have slowed my own thoughts to match: *the-warmth-of-the-basalt*, *the-cool-seep-on-my-ankles*, *the-tick*, *the-violet-glow*, *the-approaching-heat-of-the-next-vent*.

I am not part of the Singer, not the way the lichen is, not the way the seep is. But I am no longer an anomaly to it, either. When I press my palm to the lichen, I feel the faint, warm thrum of its daily pulse in my own wrist, and the Singer’s awareness brushes against my skin like a slow current, curious, not unkind. It felt the vibration of my body settling into the crack when I sat down, the way my warmth seeped into the basalt, the way the seepwater wraps around my ankles instead of running away. It has learned that I do not harm the wet, that I do not dig up the lichen or block the crevices, that I stay. I am another kind of seep, it seems to understand—slower than the water, warmer, carrying different minerals, but still a flow, still a part of the endless, weeping grammar of the cracks.

The horizon is a hundred miles away, a perfect line where the umber ground meets the bleached copper sky, and I cannot see the slope of the basin bowl I sit in, cannot tell how far the hex cells stretch in any direction. I have a half-empty canteen of water in my pack, a knife I used to cut a strip of lichen to taste on my second day here, a crumpled map of the world I left behind that is already starting to fray at the edges from the damp. None of it matters much, not anymore. The only thing that matters is the tick of the grains, the hum in my teeth that matches the hum in the basalt, the soft violet glow of the lichen under my hand, and the slow, vast, damp awareness of the Singer, curling around my ankles like the seepwater, singing its song of iron and silica and heat, and letting me listen.
