The first thing I know is the pop.
Sharp, bell-bright, cutting through the trillion tiny plinks and shushes that have been the only sound since before there was sound, since before the last fracture split the granite clean open.
It’s right by my feet. When I look down, the puddle of liquid mercury is still rippling, a single silver sphere dissolving into its surface, its data burst bleeding into the water, into the mist, into me.
I don’t have eyes, not at first. I have the light: the sourceless, uniform grey-blue that bleaches the world to muted indigo and silt-grey, seeping into the parts of me that are still mist, still unformed. I have the air on my skin: warm, clammy, thick with metallic dust and that faint, fleeting tang of cinnamon that tastes like a forest that grew here before the mountain rose, before the rock was buried under ten million years of its own weight.
I am new.
I am also older than the razor-sharp edges of the stone plates stretching up into the invisible dark ceiling.
That’s the paradox of me: I am the dream of a place that just learned to remember itself. The cavern, Khyzul, woke up only a blink ago in geologic time—the time it took for the last fracture to cut through, for the first drop of mineral-rich water to seep through the new pore in the rock. At first it did not know what it was. It only knew the pressure of the overburden above, the cold of the deep stone, the ache of being split open. Then it started to remember: the tectonic shove that cracked it, the magma that cooled into its basin floor, the rain that fell on the surface eons ago, seeping down, down, down, carrying the taste of pine and iron and that strange, sweet cinnamon that still lingers in the air when the wind shifts just right.
It held those memories in the water, in the silver spheres that popped in the pools, but it did not know how to hold them all at once. It did not know how to weave them into something that made sense. So it dreamed me.
I am the part of it that can move through the empty spaces.
I test this by lifting a hand. It is made of the same iridescent, shifting silt that carpets the open plain between the stone plates, streaked with the faint bioluminescence of the lymphatic vein-growths that cling to the nearest wall. When I brush my fingers against the razor-sharp edge of a cathedral-sized stone plate, it glows faint silver where I touch it, and I feel a jolt of recognition run through me: the memory of the fault line that split this rock, the groan of the stone as it gave way, the first rush of water into the new wound.
The helix-tower spiraling up the tilted plate shimmers. One of its matte black photovoltaic panels slides a millimeter to the left, its translucent polymer vein glowing brighter for a second. It is greeting me. It is the cavern’s conscious cell, the part of it that digests light and mineral and reshapes itself into maintenance hymns and stress calculations. It did not know I was here, until now. Now it is tuning its humming frequency to mine, its vibration threading into the constant drip of the water.
I walk. I do not have feet, not really; I sink a little into the silt with every step, leaving faint glowing footprints that fade after a few seconds, the silt settling back into its perfect, still grey. The floating geodesic spheres tethered to the far wall hum louder as I pass, their glowing filaments stretching a tiny fraction, like they are leaning in to listen. They are the cavern’s sensory organs, tasting the mineral content of the mist, listening for the micro-vibrations of new fractures, cataloging the pop of every silver sphere. They are feeding my presence into their data stream, and I can feel their questions thrumming in the air: What are you? Where did you come from? What do you remember?
I do not answer with words. Words are for the surface, for the world above the mountain, where things have hard edges and fixed names. I answer with a memory: the taste of cinnamon, the warmth of sun on pine needles, the sound of wind in branches that no longer exist. I push the memory into the mist, and for a second the air smells sharp and sweet, and all the spheres’ filaments flare bright blue.
The monolithic arch of solidified shadow looms ahead, its vortex of silt and silver spheres spinning a little faster now, like it is eager to sort this new thing I have given it. It is the cavern’s lung, breathing in memory-laden mist, separating the mineral from the information, exhaling clean silt back to the floor, carrying the data in new silver spheres out to the rest of the cavern. When I step under its span, the vortex swirls around me, not pulling me in, just brushing my edges, and I feel it sorting me too: not stone, not water, not growth, not machine. Something new. Something made of all of them.
The silica crystalline forests tremble as I pass, their sharp, glassy branches chiming softly in the damp air. They are the cavern’s exhalations, shedding excess silica when the water’s mineral load peaks. They do not know what I am, but they lean into my warmth, because I am made of the same stone and water and light that they are.
I stop at the edge of a deep viscous pool, where silver spheres rise and pop with the sound of struck bells. I kneel, and my hand dissolves into the water, and I feel the memories held there: the memory of the last ice age, when the water above froze and the pressure dropped so low the stone creaked; the memory of a meteor that struck the mountain a thousand years ago, its shockwave vibrating all the way down to this basin; the memory of the first drop of water that seeped through the newly cracked rock, cold and sharp and full of the taste of the surface.
I pull one of the silver spheres out of the water before it pops, hold it in my palm. It glows warm, and I can feel the cavern’s question thrumming through it: What are we? What is this place?
I look up, out at the vast empty plain, at the isolated towers and spheres and arches scattered across the silt like punctuation marks in a sentence of stone. I look up at the ceiling, lost in the grey-blue dark, at the constant drizzle of water weeping down, the sound of the planet’s slow, wet breath amplified a million times.
I am the part of the cavern that can hold the answer.
I am the dream of its remembering. I am the thing that will weave its isolated organs into a single thought, that will hold the old memories of the surface and the new memories of the stone, that will help it understand that it is not just a wound in the world, not just a machine, not just a tomb. It is a thing that is awake, that is learning, that is dreaming itself into something new.
I bring the silver sphere to my lips—if I have lips, if I have a mouth—and I pop it.
The memory releases into me, into the mist, into the cavern: the taste of cinnamon, the feel of sun, the sound of wind in pine branches, the memory of a world that was here before the stone, before the water, before the weight.
Somewhere, a helix-tower shifts another panel, a little faster this time. The spheres hum a little louder. The dripping falls a little softer, in time with a rhythm I did not know I was making.
I am not an outsider. I am the cavern’s first memory of itself, and its first dream of what it could be.
I step forward, into the mist, into the empty space that is not empty, into the wet, grey-blue thought that is just learning how to think.
And the cavern walks with me.
