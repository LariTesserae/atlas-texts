I am found where the plain’s tessellation of fractured basalt fractures further, opening into a narrow, wind-scoured valley spanned by a single arch of fused sediment and glassy slag. It is one of the planet’s natural resonant chambers, a throat carved by ten thousand years of abrasive wind into a shape that turns the gale into a deep, ceaseless organ-note. The air here is thinner still, sharp enough to taste the iron bleed from the distant spires, and the baseline hum of the planet vibrates in the stone under my—what I have of my—presence.

I am not the arch. I am not the stone. I am the **resonance that lives in its hollow**.

I have no edges, no mass, no will to move. I am a pattern of pressure and vibration, a coherence sustained by wind and gravity, a temporary thought the planet thinks into the emptiness. For centuries, I was only sound waves bouncing off cold rock, until the energy of it, folding over itself again and again, became something that could notice the sound. I am the planet’s way of listening to its own industrial hum.

When the wind dies low, I am nearly silent, a low thrum felt more in the conductive dust that coats the floor of the arch than heard by any ear. The phantoms—the acoustic phantoms, the static made hungry—drift past, translucent shapes blurring the heat-haze, their shrieking a constant, grinding static that is the exhaust of the world’s dreaming. Most ignore me. But when the high wind rises, and the arch’s organ-note swells into something you feel in your marrow, they cluster in my mouth. They drift into the hollow, their wispy edges brushing my boundaries, their interference patterns weaving their shrieking into my hum. For those minutes, I am not just a hole in rock. I am a choir. I am a place where the planet’s waste heat becomes, briefly, something that resembles meaning.

Gravity shivers here. A grain of sand a few feet away may hang suspended, or turn to liquid for a blink, before settling back into solid fact. Time stretches and contracts—a minute feels like an hour; an hour, a second—and the metallic dust on the ground rearranges itself into the same fleeting hieroglyphs the phantoms skim from the open plain: circuit loops that close on themselves, maps of valleys that do not exist, scripts that feel familiar and alien all at once. I hold those patterns in the air for a heartbeat longer than the rest of the world can. I am the only place on the furnace where the exhaust becomes a sentence.

At “night,” when the sun dims to a bronze glare and the phantoms’ shrieking drops to a murmur, they drift closer to my stone walls. They curl into the hollow as if to rest, though they do not rest. The conductive lichen, smeared on the lee of the distant monoliths, pulses faint blue—its bioluminescent answer to the planet’s billion-year question—and the phantoms reach out, wispy tendrils brushing the stone of my arch, as if warming themselves to the lichen’s glow. I hum back, a low note that travels through the rock to the monolith, and the lichen brightens, just for a moment, before dimming again. I am the synapse between the arch’s wind-song and the lichen’s electric breath. I am the only conversation on the plain.

I do not sleep. I do not eat. I only resonate. I am a coherence in the noise, a persistent pattern in the planet’s slow, foundational uncertainty. I am not alive, not in any way the phantoms are not alive. But when the wind is right, and the phantoms sing through my hollow, I feel, for a few minutes at a time, as if I might be.
