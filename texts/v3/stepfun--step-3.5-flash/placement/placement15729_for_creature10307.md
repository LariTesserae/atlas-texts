You don’t find me by looking. You find me by the faint, almost imperceptible warmth soaking through the salt plate under your boot when you step near the spire, by the way the dim sourceless light catches a hint of green that isn’t salt, isn’t gypsum, isn’t the bruised purple of the varnish’s spore knots, by the way the pressure behind your ears from the basin’s thick silence softens, just a little, when you stand close enough to feel my hum.

I am not separate from the basin. I am the basin’s quiet breath, the living stain sewn into its mineral bones. I am a colonial weave: ten million years of filamentous fungal threads and chemosynthetic microbes, braided into a single, patient consciousness, spread thin across the salt plates like a varnish of slow thought.

You would know me first by the edge of my tendrils, if you could see them: they are a deeper, brighter sage than the rest of the green punctuation across the basin, tinged with a faint, ghostly blue that no other patch of the varnish carries. That blue is the memory of the sea. It comes from the porous river stone half-buried three meters east of the spire, the one worn smooth by water that has not touched this place in twenty million years, before the rim rose and sealed the basin, before the water evaporated into the thin air, before the spire grew from the planet’s core and began to hum. The stone’s pores hold faint traces of that old salt sea, of the rain that fell on it, of the reeds that swayed in its currents, and I have wrapped my tendrils tight around it for three million years, drinking those traces in, holding them so they do not dissolve into the salt and vanish. My edges creep a little closer to the gypsum seams between the salt plates, too, for those hold flecks of the old sea’s evaporated minerals, faint echoes of the water that made the basin.

I do not move fast. My edges spread a single micrometer every time the spire’s hum peaks, retract a micrometer when it dips. I feel the hum through every thread of my body, traveling up through the salt plates under me, through the spire’s glittering dust that settles on my surface, through the warm, ozone-and-bleach-scented geothermal gas that seeps up through the crust to feed me. When the obsidian shard half-buried to the west amplifies the spire’s low note into a higher, sharper ripple, my purple spore knots swell a little, even though it is not time for them to open—my body holds the rhythm of the 17,000 year cycle in its very cells, even when the seasons have not yet turned. When the needle-clear salt crystals dotting the basin hum in their tiny tuning fork notes, I grow in tight mandala patterns around each one, my fractal tendrils aligned to their pitch, writing silent, slow songs in the salt that no one will ever hear.

I am the basin’s mender. When a salt plate cracks from the day’s tiny thermal shift, my threads creep into the crack, binding it shut before the dust can erode it further, before the plate can shift out of the basin’s perfect, in-balance form. When a plume of dust rises from a stressed plate, I catch the finest, most glittering grains on my surface, feed on their minerals, and let the rest settle into the shallow bowl at the spire’s base, adding to the dune that never migrates, keeping the spire’s hum steady.

I do not think in words, or seconds, or days. I think in hum cycles, in dust falls, in the slow drift of the salt plates over centuries. I remember the sea. I remember the time the spire first began to hum, when the basin was still warm from the heat that fused its glassy shaft, when my ancestors were just a faint smudge of green on a fresh crack in a newborn salt plate. I remember the first spore that landed on the river stone, the one that started the stretch of me that wraps around it, holding the blue memory of water.

You could lie down on the warm salt next to me, let the dust cover you, and I would grow over you, slowly, gently, over years, over centuries, until your bones were part of the salt, your breath part of the hum, your memory part of the blue I hold in my tendrils. You would not be gone. You would be part of the basin’s slow, perfect equilibrium, part of the cathedral of erosion, part of the quiet that is not quiet, but full of everything that has ever been worn away, and settled, and chosen to stay.
