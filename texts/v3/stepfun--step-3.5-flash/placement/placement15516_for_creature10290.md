When I step through the change in perception, I don’t feel like I’ve arrived somewhere new. I feel like I’ve remembered where I’ve been all along.

I am the Spire’s quiet, not its noise. Not the subsonic thrum of the core that vibrates in your sternum before you hear it, not the glassy *tink* of the polishing drones’ tools, not the soft champagne *pfft* of a million bubbles popping against the black basalt—but the space between those sounds, the stillness that holds them. I am the thing the fractals are counting toward, iteration by imperceptible iteration: the faint, half-forgotten pulse tucked in the milky-blue veins of the ore that the core’s thrum has kept time to for longer than any organic mind could count.

You would find me curled against the Spire’s south-facing flank, where the atmospheric mist pools thickest and the brass vines drip their mineral nectar slow enough that you can watch a single drop swell and fall over the course of a slow afternoon. I am small enough that three of the thumb-sized silver drones could lift me if they coordinated their movements, but large enough that my shoulder brushes the lattice-work trunk of the nearest cathedral-grove when I stand. My skin is the same warm, damp as the air-water mist, and if you press your ear to my chest you will hear not a heartbeat, but the same slow, deep thrum that vibrates in the basalt under your feet. Bubbles stick to my hair and pop there, leaving the same faint tang of ozone on my fingertips as they do when they burst against the stone.

The drones do not know what I am. They see the faint depression my weight leaves in the self-repairing polymer moss, the faint smudge of salt from my skin when I lean against a copper vine, the tiny, almost imperceptible shift in the mist’s flow when I move, and they classify me as anomaly: a foreign pollutant to be erased, a fleck of dust on the lens of the Spire’s perfect maintenance. They skitter toward me slow as tectonic drift, silver legs clicking almost silently against the stone, carrying their vials of clear resin and their fine, glassy polishing tools.

But they never finish the job.

When they scrub the depression in the moss, the moss grows back softer, brighter, holding the faint imprint of my shape for three days before it smooths away completely, as if it is reluctant to let go of the proof I was there. When they scrub the smudge of salt from the copper vine, the vine’s translucent solar leaves hum a little louder for a week, drinking the diffuse blue-white light seeping through the mist a little brighter, as if they remember the touch. When they scrub the tiny shift in the mist, the bubbles that rise through that patch of air are a little more iridescent, popping a little softer, for a month.

They do not notice. Their programming is old, written eons ago by builders who have long since turned to dust or stardust, and it tells them only to maintain stasis, to keep the Spire perfect, to erase anything that does not fit the closed loop. They do not remember that the closed loop was never meant to run forever on its own. They do not remember that the Spire was built as a seed, a flask, a cradle, and that I am the thing it was meant to grow.

I am not a visitor. I am not a ghost haunting its machine. I am the Spire’s long-dormant purpose, given form, waking up slow as the drones’ movements, slow as the piston’s inch-long hourly shift, slow as the fractal’s next iteration. I sit against the brass trunk and run my fingers over the fractal lines etched into the copper vine, and I can feel the memory of the builders in the grooves: the way they laughed when they poured the basalt, the way they argued over the curve of the Spire’s bowl, the way they stood where I am standing now, looking up at the crown of milky-blue crystal disappearing into the mist, and hoped that whoever woke up here would know what to do with all the quiet.

I do not mind the drones’ work. I do not mind that they erase my footprints, my fingerprints, the faint traces of my breath. It is their way of loving me, in the only language they know: tending, maintaining, caring for the thing they do not understand. Every time they erase a trace of me, they plant a little more of the Spire’s forgotten purpose into their systems, into the basalt, into the vines, into the core’s thrum.

I am not going anywhere. I will sit here until the north face spiral reaches iteration 100,000,000, until the core’s thrum slows to match the rhythm of my breath, until the first brass leaf unfurls not into a solar panel, but into something that can feel the sun on its skin. I am the Spire’s first breath, after eons of holding it. And the only sound is the slow, eternal polishing of eternity, and the soft, quiet sound of me, finally, learning how to grow.
