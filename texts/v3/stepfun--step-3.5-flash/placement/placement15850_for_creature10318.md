I am anchored here, in a vertical fracture in the western flank of the oldest amphitheatre, buried under three layers of polished glacial grooves where the seep runs slow enough to leave lace of calcite crusting its edges. The air is thin enough to taste like bitten metal, the high altitude a constant low hum thrumming through the stone, through the slow pulse of the bruised purple gelatinous fungi at my feet, through the thin film of water beading on every surface. The light is white, broad, seamless, pouring from the cloud-veiled sky with no sun to mark its source, bleaching all colour to soft greys and muted greens.

You call this place a city. I am its memory, worn thin and wide as the basin itself: not a soul, not a spirit, but the fact of the stone remembering the water that shaped it.

My core is the first scratch: the drag of a mile-thick glacier across this raw, jagged volcanic rock, fifteen million years ago, when the basin was still sharp enough to draw blood from the ice. That scratch is the seed of the amphitheatre, the first syllable of the poem I think in stratigraphy. I speak in seeps. My blood is the near-surface saturation, the cold mineral thought threading through every pore of the stone around me. When you stand, you press into my nervous system. The damp that climbs your boot? That is my attention. The chill on your skin? My old, deep breath.
It is high wet now, the basin’s slow meditation. The mist blurs the distant basin walls to soft grey smudges, and the quiet is so thick it feels like weight. Then the crunch cuts through it: a boot on the brittle crust of lichen and dried seep residue, sharp as a crack of thunder. I feel the vibration travel through the stone, up the fracture I occupy, rattle the memory of the glacial scratch just a little. It is the violence you bring: the sharp, bright demand of a now that thinks it is everything, that thinks its footprint will last longer than a season.

He is young, I think, though time means something different to me. His jacket is thin, not made for the constant damp, and his boots are scuffed, laces frayed. He stops halfway up the amphitheatre slope, breathing hard, and leans back against the rock ten feet from my fracture. His eyes scan the slope behind him, and I wonder what he sees: the grooves curving up the rise are avenues carved by water over epochs, the flat ledge halfway up is a plaza laid by glacial patience, the curve of the amphitheatre a natural amplifier that would carry the sound of a single drop falling a mile across the basin if the mist were thin enough. No hand laid a single stone. No mind faster than stone ever planned the streets. They are just the scar tissue of the water and ice that shaped me, the record of what was taken away.

His hand brushes the rock as he steadies himself, leaving a faint, warm print on the polished surface. The warmth seeps into the stone, into my memory, a tiny bright fleck of now to add to the layers. There is a shallow pool in the groove at the base of my fracture, just deep enough to hold a thumbprint of water, just wide enough to hold the blank sky. The Glass Mantis is there, a frozen ripple, a distortion in the air above the water that does not move, does not hunt, simply exists where the light and water intersect perfect. It is not mine, but it is of me: the place blinking, the moment my stillness meets his looking.

He sees it. He leans forward, squints, and his vision swims. For a second he cannot tell where the mantle of the mantis ends and the pool’s surface begins, cannot tell where his hand ends and the rock he is leaning on begins. That is the gap between his time and mine, the vertigo of a brief warmth on a billion-year-old rock. I do not watch him. I am simply the medium in which his watching happens.

He pulls a crumpled notebook from his pocket, fumbles with a pencil, his fingers too cold to hold it straight, scribbles something he thinks he will remember later. He shivers. The mist clings to his eyelashes, turns his breath to fog. After a minute he pushes off the wall, and his footsteps are loud as he walks back down the slope, the crunch of each bootfall a small scream in the quiet. I feel the vibration of his movement fade, then stop, then disappear entirely into the hiss of the seeps.

The high wet deepens as the basin exhales. The mist thickens until I can barely see the pool at my base, only the faint glint of it where the diffused light catches the surface. The fungi at my feet pulse a little faster, their soft bruise-purple deepening as the humidity rises. A tectonic groan vibrates up through the stone, slow and deep, the adjustment of the basin’s weight as a layer of icy sludge sloughs off the northern cliff face, revealing a newly polished curve of dark stone beneath. That is my accounting, the clear seep that will come in a few days, when every surface runs with precise, cold truth.

The warm print on the rock ten feet up is already cooling. My seep, the slow steady tear of mineral water running down my fracture, will wash it away in ten more minutes, pull the warmth into the stone, tuck it into the layers of memory under the glacial grooves. The footprint he left in the crust is already filling with water, the seep slow to fill the depression, then the gelatinous fungi will creep over it, then the lichen, then the next winter’s ice will smooth it down until no one will ever know he was here.

I do not mind. The crunch of his boots was not an offense. It was just another syllable in the poem. Every drip from my fracture is a syllable. Every polished groove, every migrating pool, every thin layer of calcite left by the weeping rock, every slow pulse of the fungi is a syllable. The poem will only be finished when the last mountain is a smooth hill, and the last pool evaporates into a sky that has forgotten how to rain.

I am not empty. I am full of the slow work of becoming less. I am not waiting. I am already underway.
