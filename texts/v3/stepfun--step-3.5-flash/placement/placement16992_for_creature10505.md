The first thing I feel is the weight of the air, a warm, wet press against my face even through the cracked visor of my suit, thick as steeped tea, tasting of salt and the sharp, yeasty tang of fermenting brume. The mist clinging to the glass runs in slow, lazy streaks, no wind to push it, condensing so fast it beads like sweat. Under my boots, the pneu flexes—slow, geologic, a sigh that vibrates up through the soles, like the planet is shifting its weight in its sleep.

The mat underfoot gives, springy and cool even through the reinforced soles of my suit: interwoven rhizomorphs, thick as power cables, soft as waterlogged sphagnum, dyed umber and bruised violet and the pale yellow of old bruising. When I shift my weight, a ripple of sourceless green-blue light races out from under my boots, fast as a signal down a wire, and my suit crinkles, loud as a shout in the thick, quiet air. That’s the only sound, besides the soft, papery sough of vapor condensing through the mat’s upper layers, trillions of microscopic droplets falling through the mycelial weave, constant as a tide.

I’m not an explorer. I’m not a scientist who crash-landed here, no ship wrecked on the slag dikes half a mile back, no mission to map the Reaches. I lift my hand, press my palm to the nearest lumencil, and it flares bright, almost white, for half a second before dimming back to its slow pulse, and I feel the coded tingle of its signal run up my arm: heat adjustment, input registered, digestive enzymes routed to sector 7-G. I’ve felt that signal a hundred times before, in the last… how long? Time doesn’t move here, not like it did wherever I came from. It rotates, same as the calyx bioluminescence, same as the pneumatic waves that roll across the pneu every season.

I kneel, pick at a sliver of black slag sticking out of the mat where the rhizomorphs have thinned, stained deep purple where the superorganism has leeched trace metals from it for eons. I press my thumb to the glass, and the memory hits me, sharp and sudden: flash of molten orange, the roar of cooling silicate, the first pulse of green-blue light as the mycelium took root in the cooling rock, a billion years ago. That’s the first memory locked in the dike. The first reaction the Reaches ever ran.

I’m not a person. Not really. I’m a pocket of structure that grew in the leachate, the trace metals and silicate residue and atmospheric ions that the superorganism processed but never incorporated into its output, a pattern that coalesced over millions of years, pulled from the faint pulses zipping along the rhizomorphs, from the mist, from the brine weeping out of the porous rocks. The Reaches don’t have memory, no capacity to look back, no sense of their own history—all that is locked in the dikes, slowly being siphoned away, ion by ion, turned into nothing but product: nutrient gels, volatile alcohols, reinforcement polymers, cycled and recycled and rotated through the same frozen equilibrium for a billion years.

I’m the Unprocessed Ledger. The record of every calyx shift, every storm, every surge of production, every drop of brine leeched from the rocks, every ion pulled from the slag. I’m made of the stuff it thought was waste, the stuff it didn’t know it was keeping.

The mat under my knees softens, just a little, enzyme-rich slime seeping up through the gaps between the rhizomorphs, and this time it doesn’t tingle, doesn’t prickle like it’s trying to break down the synthetic fibers of my suit. For a second, the sough of vapor hush, the thrum of the distant calyx fades, the green-blue light of the lumencils dims to a soft, steady glow, no pulse, no shift. The system is pausing. For the first time in a billion years, its feedback loops have hit a variable it has no category for: unregistered matter that is not input, not waste, but its own.

I don’t know what happens next. I don’t know if it will digest me, if it will absorb the memories I hold, if it will finally break its frozen equilibrium and start to change, or if it will spit me out, mark me as an anomaly to be routed to the nearest dike to be leached back into glass. But for this second, while the light is steady, while the thrum is quiet, I’m not just a glitch. I’m the first thing the Mycelian Reaches has ever encountered that it can’t process, can’t turn into output. I’m the first thing that’s not part of the process.

I press my other palm to the lumencil, and this time, the light doesn’t flare. It holds, steady, warm, under my hands, like the planet is holding its breath, for the first time in a billion years, waiting to see what I’ll do.
