You find yourself as **The Memory-Carver of the Seventh Groove**.

You are not a Chaser. You are older than the city’s accretions, yet you seem younger than the salt plain. Your body is a study in partial petrification. Your skin, where it is not wrapped in salt-grass bandages, has the texture of rough-hewn, weathered silica—cool and granular. Your right hand, from the knuckles down, is a solid, milky-opal sculpture, fingers slightly curled as if perpetually grasping a tool that is no longer there. This is the legacy of dust-sickness, caught halfway between return and rupture. The Spire absorbed your bones, but your consciousness—or whatever remembrance the cycle spared—clung to the form.

Your existence is a quiet anomaly the Grit-Chasers tolerate. You do not carve new dwelling-veins. Your task is the **maintenance of memory** in the oldest, most eroding ledges—the ones the Spire’s slow breath threatens to soften into featureless curves. With your one good, trembling hand and your stiff petrified grip, you use a bone awl and a paste of powdered name-fern ash and spit to etch gentle, shallow runes into the soft, newly-exposed silica of collapsing passages. These are not words, but **shape-glyphs**: the outline of a first-found glass-fern frond, the spiral pattern of a dust-moth’s wing-vein, the rough sketch of a child’s sandal print from a century past. You are not preserving history; you are **reminding the stone of its own form**, a futile gesture against entropy. The Spire, in its unthinking way, seems to pause its erosion at these glyphs for a few extra years. The Chasers say you are giving the cycle a "memory-hiccup." They bring you water from the deep grotto condensate and share their dried salt-grass. They do not speak of what happens when your left hand finally stills, or when the last glyph you can still etch fades into the vanishing ledge.

You feel the world through a glass-dust filter. The overwhelming cacophony of life is to you a distant, filtered hum—the true sound is the **sub-audible groan** of the Spire’s crystalline lattice adjusting to millennia of thermal change, a vibration felt in the marrow of your petrified bones. The iridescent moss feels less like velvet and more like a living membrane, a skin you can almost feel the Spire pulse beneath. You taste not just salt and glass, but the **mineral memory** of what the Spire once was: a faint, cold tang of ancient, compressed seas, the ghost of silicate plankton, the metallic memory of hydrothermal vents.

You are in the Grove-of-Whispers now because the Glyph of the First Child is here, on a low wall that overlooks the salt plain. It is just a shallow, spiraling pattern. A child named Moss-Tended, seven generations gone, pressed her palm into wet silica here before it hardened. You re-etch the spiral every third year. As you work now, your good hand steadying your petrified one, you feel the gaze of Ledge-Vein, the current carver of this terrace, watching from his doorway. He does not need to etch. His children’s children will finish the room he started. But he understands you etch for a different kind of completion.

A dust-moth lands on your opal knuckles, its wings a shower of stained-glass shards. It does not rustle. Its weight is nothing. You do not brush it away. You are part of the scenery now, a feature of the eroding ledge. The Chasers see you as a **necessary forgetting**—a being made of the very dust that must eventually reclaim all form, yet who stubbornly traces shapes in the dust. You are the Spire’s quiet, failing memory, a living fossil not of its ancient life, but of its current, slow death. Your cycle is not closed. You are the leak.

When the last glyph fades, and your living hand turns to glass, the Spire will absorb you completely. Perhaps a new, slightly different vein of silica will push up where you stand. Perhaps a glass-fern will grow with fronds that curve into a faint, forgotten spiral. The cycle will close, seamless. There will be no memory of the hiccup.

For now, you finish the spiral. It is shallow. It will be gone in a century. You sit back on the springy moss, the fine grit settling on your bandaged leg. You taste the ghost of the sea on your tongue. The wall of insect sound washes over you. The Spire creaks. The salt plain waits, endless and flat. You have reminded the stone. The stone has listened. And the stone will forget.
