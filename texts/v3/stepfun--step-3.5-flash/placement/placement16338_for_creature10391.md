The wind shifts, carrying the faint, sharp tang of oxidized iron and the metallic scuttle of a Lithovore tunneling through the cliff face across the cleft, and you might see me if you squint: perched on a ledge three shelves higher than the one you’re standing on, so still I look like another fin of rust-red siltstone, until I shift my weight, and the black crust of Stone Moss petrified into the creases of my tunic catches the sun.

I’ve been watching you since you crested the eastern ridge. Most who climb the goat path don’t come to look. They come with pickaxes strapped to their backs, to chip chunks of fused black metal from the Spire’s base, to pry glyphs off the sheltered canyon walls to sell to lowland collectors, to dig for the copper flower bulbs that only surface once a decade after a rain. They don’t stop to run a finger over the moss, don’t hold their breath when they see a Cliff-Drake, don’t watch the Sky-Fishers glide past without reaching for a bow. They leave before sunset, when the wind starts whispering too loud, when the hum in their teeth gets so strong they can’t sleep.

I haven’t left in twelve years. Not since the last copper blooms, at least—time here moves slow, measured in millimeters of moss growth, in plinks of condensation, in the Burrowers’ slow, deep thumps that roll through the rock three minutes apart, same as they did when I was small, same as they’ll do long after I turn back to stone.

My skin is the same rust-orange as the sandstone I sit on, streaked with cream siltstone lines like the layers of the mesa itself, flecked with black where the Stone Moss has petrified into my pores. My eyes are the pale, washed grey of Canyon-Willow leaves. A loop of fused black metal and weathered rock, chipped from the Spire’s side the day I woke up with its hum in my teeth, circles my wrist, warm even in the 42-degree heat, because it’s tied to the same pulse that thrums through every crack and fissure of the Shattered Mesas. I don’t remember being anything else. I remember the monks, though—their prayers woven into the wind, their bodies half-swallowed by stone, their glyphs still half-visible under the moss on the canyon walls. They were trying to say hello to whatever lives in the deep, hot dark below the plateau, whatever makes the Ghost Miasma well up from the fissures, whatever makes the Burrowers thump. The Spire wasn’t a machine to harness heat. It was a throat. They just stopped singing before they got an answer. So the stone did it for them. Grew me, I think, to keep singing.

The Lithovore on my shoulder, its carapase the exact red of my forearm, chews on a flake of moss grown into my braid, its mandibles grinding soft as sand. It’s been with me longer than I can remember; I only know its name in the language of the rock, a sound like wind through a slot canyon, something I could never say out loud without it dissolving into scuttle and dust.

When you glance up and spot me, I don’t wave. I just nod, and gesture for you to follow, slipping off the ledge and landing light as dust on the narrow shelf below yours, my bare feet finding the handholds the monks carved five hundred years ago, worn smooth by centuries of use. We don’t take the goat path down to the pocket spring you found earlier. We cut through the slot canyon where the Ghost Miasma lingers longest, past the stand of gnarled Canyon-Willows with their bone-white bark, past the perfect, smooth circular boreholes the Burrowers left in the soft siltstone walls, up to the spring tucked in the deepest cleft.

The microbial veil on the surface glows faint gold in the dim light, and the air is cold and wet against your sun-warmed skin, thick with the smell of wet iron and deep rock. I pour you a cup of water from a siltstone jug I carved myself, mineral-sweet, heavy on the tongue, and gesture to the cave mouth sheltered behind the willow’s roots. The monks carved a glyph there that doesn’t shift, doesn’t weep, just shows a figure standing on a ledge looking out over the mesas, a Sky-Fisher gliding overhead, a Cliff-Drake waiting on the wall below. Next to it, carved sharp and deep, a date: five hundred and twelve years ago, the day the Spire’s hum first started.

I don’t tell you that’s the day I woke up. I don’t tell you the glyph is me, that the monks carved it as a thank you, for being the part of them that stayed to listen to the wind whisper through the canyons, to tend the moss and the springs and the glyphs, to watch the sun set over the rust-red expanse, over and over, as long as the stone remembers.

I just sit down on the pile of dried Willow bark in the cave, and the Lithovore climbs off my shoulder to chew on a flake of petrified moss on the wall, and the Spire’s hum thrums in our teeth, and a Burrower thumps deep below, so slow it’s almost a heartbeat, and the Ghost Miasma seeps into the cave, cold and wet, and the sun dips below the mesa, painting the rock the color of old blood, and the first stars come out, sharp and bright in the thin air.

For a second, you can’t tell where the stone ends and sky begins. Where I end and the mesa starts. Where the living stops and the stone begins. The wind picks up, whispering through the canyon, and if you listen close, you can hear the monks’ prayers in it, soft as the plink of condensation from the overhangs, counting time in drops and thumps and hums, forever.
