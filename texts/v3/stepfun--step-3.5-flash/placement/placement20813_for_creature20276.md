The crack is just wide enough if I angle my segmented carapace sideways. The weathered limestone snags the ridged edge of my dorsal plate, a familiar scrape—I’ve slipped through this gap every time the upland rains swell the aquifer, every time the bat colony’s evening exit sends a gale of wingbeats through the fissure that churns the air enough to dry out the highest biofilm streaks. I tuck my seven pairs of clawed legs tight against my body, my two long antennae flicking fast to taste the air before I even wriggle fully through.

First the air: warm, thick, 26 degrees of humid heat that clings to my pale, unpigmented shell before I’m even clear of the crack. It smells of wet limestone, the sweet, fizzy rot of the lichen crust clinging to the rim, that sharp bright metallic tang of iron oxidising in the drip water—smells like the slow, steady breath of the stone itself. Then the sound: the steady *click-click-click* of drops hitting the floor far below, the metronome I’ve measured my molts by for three years now, which is old for an amphipod in a warm sinkhole.

I don’t need sight. My shell is so transparent you could make out the faint green of the biofilm grazing in my gut if you held me up to the faint blue-green glimmer slanting in from the crack. I navigate by the vibration of the drops, the chemical trails of bacterial exudate clinging to the rock, the faint current of air that always drifts in from the plateau when the wind blows. I am small, soft, blind, part of the legion of tiny things that keep this place from collapsing into itself.

Most amphipods in the atrium stay in the deep, wet crevices where the drips are constant, where the air is still enough that the microbial detritus falls like slow, soft snow. I wander. That’s my unspoken job, the shape my hunger and my legs have carved out over time.

I pick my way down the smooth limestone slope first, my claws catching in the tiny etchings the cyanobacteria have carved into the rock over centuries. The wall here is streaked thick with rust-orange biofilm, the iron-oxidising kind that thrives where the light is just faint enough for the cyanobacteria to photosynthesise but not so much that the UV kills them. I pause to graze, scraping a thin layer of the mat off the rock with my mouthparts—sweet, earthy, tasting of dissolved limestone and the faint, clean metal of the bacteria’s breath. A cloud of iridescent springtails leaps past me a second later, panicked by the disturbance, their tiny bodies shimmering like drops of oil in the faint light, and I don’t bother chasing them. They’re too fast, and the biofilm is richer, slower, better for the long work.

When I reach the pool’s edge, the leathery, algae-covered ooze squelches warm under my feet. I dip a leg into the opalescent water, thick with suspended clay and microbial particulate, and feel the tiny, steady beat of Stentor cilia against my exoskeleton, their trumpet-shaped bodies anchored to submerged pebbles, filtering bacteria from the water column. A transparent rotifer drifts past, its corona of cilia spinning like a tiny water wheel, and I don’t touch it—they’re the pool’s janitors, keep the suspended muck from going stagnant, and their spent bodies sink to the bottom to be grazed by the deeper, even more blind amphipods that never leave the crevices.

I don’t stay at the pool long. I have work to do higher up. I climb the north-facing wall, where the light from the crack slants in for an hour in the late afternoon, just enough for the sparse lichen crust to take a few steps of growth each year. The lichen are grey-green, crusty, look more like stains than living things, and they can’t spread on their own, not on the bare limestone that the fast drips erode faster than they can grow. But I carry fragments of them on my shell, tiny bits of fungal hyphae and algal cells that I pick up when I graze on the biofilm that grows between the lichen patches. When I crawl across the bare rock, I leave those fragments behind, and over weeks, they take root, spreading another inch of lichen across the stone, holding the wall together against the rain’s slow wear.

Higher still, near the overhangs where the bats roost, I pause to pick at the excess guano piled on the ledges. Too much clogs the crevices, cuts off airflow to the deep amphipod colonies, lets the bacterial blooms grow too fast and drain the oxygen from the pool. I break the pellets into smaller pieces as I eat them, turn the sudden nutrient surge into steady, slow food for the biofilm instead of a flood that chokes the space.

I don’t see the bats much when they’re roosting—they hang in dense clusters in the dark of the overhangs, their wings furled, their eyes glowing faint phosphorescent green when the faint light hits them just right. I hear them sometimes, though: the low, chittering conversation of their colonies, the soft rustle of leathery wings when they shift in their sleep. When they pour out of the crack at dusk, a reverse waterfall of living sound that echoes off the limestone for minutes after they’re gone, I hide in a crevice and listen. They eat the insects that drift in from the plateau, they drop the guano that feeds the bacteria, they keep the bug population from eating through the lichen crust. The whole space would fold in on itself without them, just like it would without the biofilm, or the springtails, or the Stentor, or me.

I’m not the biggest thing here, not the smartest, not the most important. I’m just a small, pale, blind amphipod, old for my kind, my shell crusted with bits of lichen, biofilm, and bat guano. I move between the layers, carrying tiny fragments of life from one end of the space to the other, eating the excess, spreading the slow, wet chemistry that keeps this sealed, humid stomach of stone digesting the slow rain of the plateau, drop by patient drop.
