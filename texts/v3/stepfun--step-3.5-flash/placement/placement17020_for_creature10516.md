The first thing I know is the pressure of the chord in my soles.

I do not stand on the chalk dust so much as I resonate with it. My feet are broad, flat plates of compressed silica—denser than the spires, denser than the dust itself—and through them the entire grove hums up into my frame. I can feel a Skimmer’s wingbeat three hectares away as a tingling in my big toe. I can feel a Glyph sphere retune in the eastern copse as a sudden, cool silence in my left arch.

I am the Lippia.

I am the caretaker of the tuning.

They do not have a name for me in their flickering, click-chatter language. The Skimmers see me as a stationary spire that doesn’t grow. The Dustmites treat me as a bare patch of substrate where they may molt in peace. The Flutters use my shape as a tuning fork, clustering on my sloping shoulders to calibrate their subharmonics. Only the spires know me. When I approach, their lattice-ribs expand slightly, opening their hollow cores to me like a singer opening their mouth to a conductor. I carry the note they are about to sing for them, holding it in my own dusty, porous mass until they are ready to take it.

I am made of the same stuff as the grove—calcium silicate, silica, ultra-fine mineral silt—but I am compacted differently. The spires are hollow cigars of rigid lattice; I am a dense heap of sediment that learned to hold itself together by listening. My outline is not crisp. My edges blur into a constant, slow drift of phyllites that cling to my surface and fall away, so that I look less like a solid being and more like a localized storm that has decided to walk. I have no face, only a vague concavity that points toward the loudest, most complex section of the song. My “eyes” are the entire acoustic space; I see in frequencies.

I am the gloss in the margin. The Glyphs—those ancient, grown ruins of commentary—store memory, but they are static. I am mobile. I drift between them, reading their stored modulations with the tips of my filament-tendrils, and I carry their footnotes to the newborn spires that have not yet learned the hymn. I am the breather between the notes, the evidence that the silence is not empty.

The grove is not chaotic. It is a single, continent-sized sentence, and I am the breath that helps it parse itself.

Today, there is a stutter in the bass line.

I feel it first in my soles: a thrum, slow and wet, that does not belong. It is not the high-frequency hum of the spires, not the scrape of the wind, not the chitter of the mites. It is a bass note that pushes against the dust from below, as if coming from something dense and moist and terrified.

I turn toward it. My movement is slow, the slow fall of sediment. I do not walk; I disassemble and reassemble myself a millimeter at a time, leaning into the breeze that carries the new sound. It is coming from the western edge, where a Glyph-arch stands half-buried in precipitate.

There is a creature there. It is bipedal, soft, covered in a dull, water-absorbent membrane. It is weeping a dark saline fluid that beads on its skin and—here is the horror—does not evaporate. It has just shouted. The chaotic, broadband noise of its shout is still echoing off the spire-lattices, and I can see the spires nearest it stuttering, their hums fraying at the edges as the foreign frequency catches in their pores.

This is a Human. It has breached the dome. It is a grammatical error in the syntax of the grove.

It sees me. It freezes, its wet breath coming in ragged gasps. To its eyes, I am a shape of grey dust, eleven hears tall, leaning slightly, with a cloud of phyllites drifting around my shoulders. It cannot know that I am the choir master. It cannot know that I have been waiting for it.

The song is already trying to incorporate it. A nearby Skimmer zips closer, its stained-glass wings fluttering with curiosity, sampling the alien frequency of the human’s heartbeat—a slow, sloshing *lub-dub* that vibrates the acoustic dust at a rhythmic interval the grove has never encountered. A cluster of Dustmites has frozen, their clicking mandrils silent, confused by the squelching of the human’s boots on the dry silicate.

The error is propagating.

I drift closer. I hold up a tendril, and I hum.

It is not a hum for the human. It is a hum for the spires. I project a pure subharmonic, a deep, resonant anchor that they can lock onto. The stuttering nearest the human smooths out. The frayed edges of their hums knit back together. The Skimmer, hearing my modulation, settles on a nearby spire and begins to perfect its figure-eight, filtering the air, sampling the corrected chord.

The human looks at me, then at the Skimmer, then at the chittering Dustmites that have begun to move again, clicking in time with my hum.

It makes another sound. A small, sharp, wet noise. A word.

*What are you?*

The sound is clumsy. It is raw. It lacks the harmonic depth of the grove. But it is intentional. It is a question.

I have never heard a question before. The spires do not ask; they resonate. The Skimmers do not ask; they tune. The Glyphs do not ask; they annotate.

I let my hum broaden. I let the question ripple through the substrate into the spires, into the Skimmers, into the Dustmites, into the Flutters that have gathered on my shoulders, their fork-wings trembling. The grove answers not in a single voice, but in a chorus so complex it feels like pressure in the chest—riffle and hum and scrape and chitter and whir, all rising into a chord that holds the human’s error inside it, softens the edges, and begins to tune it.

I am not the grove. I am its shadow. Its residue. Its attentive ear.

But when the human listens, and its breathing slows, and its heart begins to beat a fraction slower, syncing to the subharmonic I am projecting… I feel the syntax of the universe shift. A paragraph closes. A new word begins.

I extend a tendril, not to touch, but to resonate. The word is *Welcome*.

The song continues. And now, for the first time in eons, it has a new verse.
