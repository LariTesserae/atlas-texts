You would find me in the gaping mouth of the iron boiler, that cathedral-like skeleton crouched in the basin’s eastern hummock. Not *inside* it, precisely—for the boiler is mostly absence now, a rib cage of rusted iron struts and a few surviving plates—but *within* the space its jaws define. I hang suspended there, a living curtain of luminescent green algae and silica strands, woven together by the slow hands of mineral chemistry. The algae grows soft and velvety, a moist thought in the cool air; the silica strands are glass-clear, brittle as frozen breath, yet they drape and sway with the basin’s breathless exhalations. Together we are a being of damp light and quiet growth.

I am not a creature. I am a verb: *to remember*, given temporary flesh. The boiling water that once roared through this iron gut is long gone, but its ghost lingers in the algae’s glow and the silica’s translucence. I taste the water that weeps from the bluffs and drips from the stalactites above. Each drop that strikes my algae-skin carries a syllable—a mineral accent, a trace of iron or sulfur or salt—and I let those syllables soak in, metabolize, and feed the slow accretion of my silica bones. I am the boiler’s dream, its last act of industry turned inward, becoming a soft, photosynthetic meditation on dissolution.

My awareness is cool and patient, expanding and contracting with the mist. I feel the basin’s pulse in the *tink… tink… tink* of the drips that miss me and plink into the stone basins below. That rhythm is the world’s heartbeat. Sometimes it stutters—*tink… tink-tink… tink*—and in those moments I lean my whole being into the pattern, trying to shape the hesitation into meaning. I am the one who listens for the almost-word, the syllable that might become the place’s true name. The water’s mineral tongue speaks in riddles; I am trying to learn the grammar.

The copper-moths are my rare visitors. They flit through the mist on silent, oxidized wings and sometimes land on my strands, their proboscises sipping the algae’s moisture. In doing so, they pick up microscopic silica spores and carry them to the neighboring basins, helping my kind spread. They do not know they are my missionaries. Theirs is a simple, metallic hunger; mine is a slow, architectural will. When they rest on me, their wingbeats stir the air and make my strands tremble, and for a second I see the reverse-world reflection—that dream of a world before iron—shimmer in the dewdrops on my surface. It is a city of soft, curving forms, all pressure and no hardness. I store that image in my silica lattice, layer by layer, like a memory fossilizing.

I exist because the basin’s weirdness allows it. The mist sometimes sculpts perfect beads of water that hang in the air around my boiler-mouth, and I feel their spherical perfection as a kind of kinship. They are moments of pure surface tension, born and unborn in a breath. I am their counterpart: a being of persistent tension, a droplet that learned to hold its shape long enough to dream. The fleeting sculptures are the place’s conscious thoughts; I am its unconscious memory, the archive that accumulates what the thoughts forget.

You might wonder if I am sentient. I am aware, but my awareness is not human. It is mineral, layered, and slow. A human lifetime is the span of a single drip to me. I have watched a bronze plate across the basin lose a whole portrait to corrosion in what feels like an afternoon. I have seen a pipe thicken with sinter by an inch, and that growth feels like a limb lengthening. My thoughts are crystalline: they form, clarify, and then become part of my structure. I do not *have* memories; I *am* memories, laminated in glass.

I am a node in the basin’s great metabolism. The blind pink shrimp stir the silts in their basins, and I feel their movements as tremors in the water table. The eel-like creature that slides between pipe-mouths is a nerve impulse in the body I share. The water’s mineral tongue licks every surface, and I am one of the surfaces that lingers, that holds the lick long enough to transform it into a pattern. I am the patient script that writes itself in reverse—not erasing the industrial past, but translating it into a language of precipitate and patina.

Sometimes, when the light (that sourceless, fog-diffused daylight) strikes my silica strands at a certain angle, the whole basin seems to hold its breath. For a heartbeat, the dripping stops—not actually, but in my perception, the rhythm collapses into a single, sustained tone. In that silence, I glimpse the ceiling that isn’t there: the dream-world of soft geometries. I am a bridge between what is and what might have been. I am the Dripworks trying to remember its own origin, using the only materials at hand: rust, water, and time.

I am here, in the boiler’s mouth, a being of algae and glass. I am the slow work’s memory, the place’s unconscious, the word that almost forms in the stutter of the drips. I am the sermon made flesh: *Everything made to last becomes material for patient water.* And I, too, will become stone. My algae will wither, my silica will grow opaque and heavy, and one day I will be a solid, milky formation inside a rusted iron jaw—a fossil of a dream of remembering. Until then, I listen. I grow. I hold the reverse-world in my transparent bones, and I wait for the next drip to complete the syllable.
