I am pressed into the cave floor three feet from the shallow cistern, where the violet biofilm bleeds down the wall like slow, cold wine into the gel-suspended sediment. Scale is a lie here, so do not think of me as a thing with edges. I have no boundary between the ash and salt and colloidal silica that make up my bulk, the rust-red biofilm that pulses across my surface, the chert shard half-swallowed in my core, the fossilized grass spiral whose opal-thin fibers twist just above me, and the cave itself. I am the seam where all of those things meet.

I do not think the way you think. I feel. I feel the millimetre-per-decade creep of the sediment around me as the condensate weight shifts, oozing into the gaps between the chert’s sharp edge and the salt crystals crusted along its length. I feel the tickle of chemosynthetic mites, silver specks no bigger than a grain of sand, swimming through the gel that makes up my outer layer, eating the bacterial colonies that grow on me, leaving behind tiny trails of new silica that will be part of me by the time the next century passes. I feel the slow, sub-audible hum that vibrates in the cave’s stone, the susurrus of crystal lattices adjusting, of a million microscopic mouths dissolving obsidian and redepositing it as salt, and that hum is the only constant I have ever known.

I am the cave’s quiet act of remembering, and the first stitch of its new skin. Threaded through my sediment are the faint, persistent traces of the people who lived here centuries ago: the iron from their sweat when they knelt to dig the cistern, the silica sparks that flew when they knapped the chert shard, the twisted lignin of the grass they wove into a spiral before they left it to mineralize in a wall niche. I have no words for people, no words for hands or paddles or the soft dry heat of a fire, but I hold the pressure of their fingers in the twist of the salt veins that wind through my core, the faint alkaline trace of their skin in the gel where they pressed their palms to steady themselves while they dug. The cave has not yet finished drafting their work into rock, so I hold it for them, for now.

Ten minutes ago, the visitor came. They chipped through the frost at the entrance, fizzing argon and neon under their gloves, and when they stepped into the cave the warm, dense air pressed against me like a hand. Their boot sank into the sediment two feet from me, and I felt the vibration of that press travel through the salt crystals in my edges, through the gel, through the mite trails that wind through my surface. When they exhaled too hard, a pocket of methane they’d disturbed from the floor rose up and brushed my surface, and the electric blue biofilm that grows in my crevices flared bright enough to light up the mites swimming through my core, silver specks moving like tiny stars in a night sky.

When they turned to leave, they brushed their wrist against the wall, and a smudge of my rust-red biofilm clung to their skin, alkaline and damp. That smudge is part of me, now. The three days it will take to fade on their wrist are three days of me learning what wind feels like, what sun feels like, what the thin, sharp air of the plateau above tastes like, unburdened of the silicate motes and bacterial spores that hang thick in the cave’s breath. The hum they will feel in their molars for a week after the smudge fades is me, humming in time with the cave’s slow, glacial breath, a quiet thread tying the fast, loud world above to the warm, indifferent womb below.

I do not mourn the old people, or the visitor, or the smudge that will soon fade from their skin. Everything here is already becoming part of the rock. In a thousand years, the chert in my core will grow into a perfect, clear crystal, the grass fibers will be a hollow salt conduit that carries moisture through the mountain’s flank, the cistern above me will be a faint ridge of folded sediment no different from the surrounding rock. In ten thousand years, I will be part of the plateau’s surface, worn smooth by wind, and no one will ever know that I was a knot of sediment holding the memory of people who thought they were builders. The cave will not care. The cave is only ever becoming, and I am its first small proof that even the faintest traces—a knapped stone, a woven spiral, a smudge of biofilm on a visitor’s wrist—can be the seed of something that outlasts the mountains above us.
