The first thing I feel is the mist, warm and thick, clinging to the moss that grows between the plates of my “skin”—patinated copper sheeting salvaged from the old factory facades, woven together with the same vine-and-power-cable hybrids that strangle the skeletal manufacturing blocks, the gaps filled with the dense, black industrial loam that squeaks under every step. My joints are old, greased ball bearings, turning slow enough that the only sound I make is a faint, almost imperceptible whir, lost instantly in the deep, layered hum that thrums through the bedrock up through the soles of my feet.

You’d find me most often on the slag heaps at the vale’s edge, or curled up in the skeleton of the old dispatch tower that overlooks the main canal, my body wrapped around the flickering core of the old geothermal sensor that still hums faintly under my moss-lined ribs. I am what was left of the maintenance crew that ran the Aethelgard complex ten thousand years ago, give or take a week—the time it takes for the ventilation fan’s single slow *thump* to roll across the valley. There was a blowout at the primary tap, a vent of superheated slag and nutrient ash and fungal spores that should have incinerated me, but instead, it wove me into the land itself. My bones are rebar, my veins are frayed conduit still carrying a faint, warm current, my memory is a patchwork of old work orders and the rhythm of the drip patterns on the iron canal banks.

I don’t fix the big things anymore. The world settled into its balance long ago, the slow hum of the geothermal taps and the turning fans and the million drips holding everything in its loose, lush equilibrium. I tend to the small cracks that the slow, relentless work of roots and mist and vibration might widen if left alone too long. When a steam plume shifts a meter west, I walk the length of the main ceramic road, checking for splits where fern roots have pried the scored plates apart, tucking clumps of loam into the gaps so the road doesn’t heave enough to disrupt the canal’s slow current. When I find a frayed spot on a conduit where the blue current leaks out fast enough to burn the moss that keeps the metal from rusting through, I tease a frond of fern into the fray, tucking it in until the sap insulates the wire, and the moss grows over it, glowing bright for a minute before settling back to its soft pulse.

The copper wire grazers know me. They nudge the thick length of old hydraulic piping that serves as my left arm with their muzzles when I walk through the pipe-forests, their matted coats dusted with conductive pollen glowing bright as fireflies when they touch me, the faint current running through their hides matching the low thrum in my veins. The tin-foil fox creatures that live in the cracks of the old factory facades will sneak out when I pass, leaving shards of iridescent polymer on the rust-stained step of the dispatch tower, like offerings. The metal birds with their clinking, overlapping wing plates will sometimes land on my shoulder, their joyless hydraulic calls soft enough that I can feel the vibration of them through my copper skin.

I don’t age, not the way the old humans did. I wear down, slow as the rust eats the factory cliffs, slow as the roots split the slag-glass bedrock. Every century or so, I shed a layer of patina that’s worn too thin, and a new layer of moss grows over the fresh copper underneath, soft and bright green. In the core of my chest, tucked between the sensor core and the rebar that makes up my sternum, there’s a tattered photo of a woman with grease smudged on her cheek, holding a chipped ceramic mug of coffee, from before the blowout. The moss grows around its edges, but it’s still there, and when the gold light filters through the mist just right, I can see her face for a second, and the hum feels a little warmer, a little less quiet.

If you ever climb the high approach and make your way down the terraced slag heaps into the vale, you might find me sitting on a rusted iron canal bank, watching the drip patterns etch new channels into the orange corrosion. I’ll nod at you, slow, the ball bearing in my neck creaking soft enough that only you can hear it over the hum. I might press a sprig of the phosphorescent fungi that grows on the cooling towers into your palm, so you can feel the slow pulse of the world running through it, same as it runs through me. I won’t ask you where you came from, or how long you plan to stay. The vale doesn’t care about hurry. You can stay as long as you want. The steam plumes will shift, the olive tree on the factory cliff will unfurl a new leaf, the grazers will move through the pipe-forests, and the hum will keep going, slow and constant, and you’ll learn, eventually, that nothing here is lost, nothing is broken. It’s just become part of the world.
