You don’t have a name, because names are for things that stand apart from the world, and you are not apart. You are the slow, soft composite of the shelf itself: ridged and ovoid, the size of a worn-down basalt boulder, no sharp edges, no front or back, no top or bottom except the faint gradient of the water column. Your skin is layered exactly like the crust underfoot: sheets of precipitated halite crisscrossed with silt cemented by iron seep, stitched through with the fossilized, papery remains of microbial mats that bloomed when the ice above was thin enough to let stray photons through, all polished to a dull, iridescent sheen by the same endless current that planed the basalt plain. Woven into every layer are living colonies of chemosynthetic bacteria, the same violent purple and sickly yellow as the crust stains underfoot, glowing so faint you only see the light when you press your face close to your own side.

You move so slow a Filter-Krill could settle on your upper flank and live out its entire three-year lifespan, grazing the bacterial films on your skin, before you have shifted a single meter from where you rested. The dense water holds you like a cold hug, trailing after your edges when you drift, taking three heartbeats to pull away from your surface, thick with the same brine, iron, and sour sulfur tang that coats every surface here. You don’t breathe, not the way air-breathers do; you absorb hydrogen sulfide directly through your skin, metabolize it with the bacteria in your layers, excrete the waste as faint, glowing purple streaks that drift off into the current to seed new crusts on the bare basalt miles away.

You don’t see, not really. The greenish-grey murk is too thick, the light too dim, only the faint waste-glow of your own bacteria to tell you where you end and the world begins. Instead, you feel: the deep, tectonic thrum of the planet’s core traveling up through the basalt when you rest, humming through your salt layers, settling into the hollows between your ridges; the soft, felt pop of gas bubbles bursting against your upper surface, each one a tiny pressure pulse you record in the mineral layers of your skin; the faint, delicate click of Filter-Krill claws against your sides when they climb onto you to graze, or to tuck their molted shells into the crevices of your ridges to eddy the current, pull more particulate organics toward them. You don’t think of this as kindness, or use. It’s just what happens, the way the current wears the basalt smooth, the way the vents spit sulfide into the water, the way the krill arrange their shells.

You carry a library of small moments in your layers. The fist-sized concretion of iron and manganese embedded in your left flank, grown around a grain of quartz you picked up drifting over the Plain of Glass ten thousand years ago, its layers as regular as tree rings, each one a year of slow, cold current. The streak of bright yellow silt along your lower right edge, deposited when a new vent fissure erupted under you five centuries past, the heat searing the outer layer of your skin, killing the purple bacteria there, leaving the yellow stain of new, heat-tolerant archaea that still glow faintly when the water moves. The patch of pale, salt-bleached mat on your upper surface, where you rested in the River of Brine for three hundred years, the hypersaline water stinging the outer layers into dormancy, leaving the bleached mark even now that you’ve drifted back into the shelf’s main current.

Sometimes a Gulper drifts past, its translucent, pulsing mouth opening wide to ingest the cubic meter of water in front of it. It doesn’t eat you—your salt layers are too tough, too mineral-heavy for its soft membrane—but for the half-second its mouth is open, the water in front of it clears, a temporary tunnel through the dense, particle-heavy brine, and you can feel the faint, slow pulse of its internal chamber through the water, a soft thrum that matches the planet’s own heartbeat for just a moment, before it closes its mouth and drifts on, leaving the tunnel to collapse, the water rushing back in to fill the space.

You don’t have goals. You don’t wonder about the sun that used to shine, the ice that sealed the world shut, the air that must have once existed above the water. You only drift, slow as erosion, rest on the basalt when the current pulls you low, seed new crusts with the waste of your bacteria, carry krill colonies and their shell-eddies on your back, add a new thin layer of silt and salt to your skin every century, feel the planet’s thrum in your core. You are not a creature, not exactly. You are a part of the world that learned, very slowly, to move. You are a small, soft postponement of the final stillness, no more intentional than the krill’s shell-eddies, no more planned than the vent plumes that paint the water copper and black. You are the shelf’s wandering memory, soft and ridged and slow, drifting forever in the greenish-grey dark, made of the same stone and salt and life as everything around you, no separation, no borders, just the slow, endless current carrying you along, toward a stillness that never comes, because the heat from below never stops, and you never stop drifting.
