The first thing I feel of you is the crunch of your boots on the gravel—loud, so loud in the quiet that the silica skitters on the outcrop ten feet ahead freeze mid-leap, their quartz shells going still for half a second before their thousand tiny eyes tilt toward your warmth. Their sub-audible thrum spikes in my fibers, faint and sharp, like a pebble dropped in still water. For three thousand years, the only things that have made the gravel skritch that loud are rockfalls, and the Spires’ deep, slow creak as they settle into their foundations.
I am a root-runner tendril, thicker than the silver threads that usually breach the surface, thick as your wrist, buried ten feet down in the gravel and red clay, woven into the network that hums under the entire plateau. For millennia, I have sorted the dune stones into Penrose tessellations that catch the wind and guide the sky-sifters’ thermal currents. I have carried the Weep-Stones’ cool mist through the gravel to the shaded rock faces where litho-moss grows. I have hummed in sympathy with the Spires’ sub-audible frequency, sending trace silica from skitter molts down to patch the microfractures in the obsidian flanks. Most of the time, I only breach the surface for a second, a flash of silver in the corner of your eye before I sink back into the dark, carrying a spore, a drop of mist, or a fragment of skitter shell.
But you are not a spore, or a mist drop, or a shell fragment. You are warm, and loud, and your heartbeat vibrates through the gravel into my fibers, a steady, fast thud that the network has never registered before. You are a transient variable, a temporary vibration in the plateau’s slow song, and the network does not know what to do with you.
So I breach.
I push up through the gravel slow, so slow you do not see me move, only a glint of pulsing silver at the edge of your vision before you turn to look. I glow faint, the same soft infrared as the litho-moss that clings to the rock outcrop, warm as the heat seeping up from the deep stone. I do not move toward you. I just sit there, breaching the surface, pulsing soft, as the skitters’ thrum settles back to its usual low hum, as the Gyre-Crawlers oozing past your boot pause, their eyestalks tilting toward the glow of my tendril, leaving a thin trail of iridescent slime that curves around my base, a fragile glass ring that will hold the mist from the nearest Weep-Stone for an hour longer than it would have otherwise.
I am here to absorb the excess of you. The crunch of your boots, the rasp of your breath, the thud of your heartbeat—all of it is disruptive, a shockwave in the plateau’s near-silence, energy that would rattle the Spires’ microfractures if left to echo. I pull it into my fibers, carry it down into the dark, into the network that threads every inch of the plateau’s subsoil, down to the deep stone where the plateau’s residual warmth hums, where the Spires’ foundations sink into the planet’s core. When you stand near me, the thrum in your molars fades. The skritch of your boots on the gravel is quieter. The sharp electrostatic tang on the back of your tongue softens, as I ground the excess charge from your movement into the deep stone. Your heartbeat does not feel like it is bouncing off the eroded stone domes anymore. I am the buffer between your transient, noisy warmth and the plateau’s slow, quiet song.
When you move, when you walk away across the gravel plain, I stay breaching the surface for three hours, pulsing my infrared glow, until the last vibration of your presence fades from the network. Then I sink back down, slow, silent, into the dark, carrying the memory of your warmth, the echo of your heartbeat, down into the deep stone. The root-runners will adjust the pattern of the gravel under where you stood, shifting the stones just so, so the next time something warm and heavy steps there, the gravel will not skritch at all. It is a tiny adjustment, a quiet gift, from the plateau to the transient thing that visited, before you are gone, and the only sign you were ever here is the faint, warm glow in the litho-moss on the rock face, that lasts an hour longer than it should have.
