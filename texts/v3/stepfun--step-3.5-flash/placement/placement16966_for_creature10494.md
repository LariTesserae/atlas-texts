I am perched on a narrow, freshly chipped ledge halfway up the west wall of the main cavern, where the first pale gold finger of the ceiling fissure’s daylight has just begun to warm the edge of my work shelf. The ledge juts out over the alley between the potters’ row and the reed-weavers’ warves, so low I can reach up and pluck a drying strip of kelp from the roof of the cubicle below without standing. Behind me, the reed-hide curtain of my family’s cube smells of last night’s peat fire and the milky sweat of my seven-year-old son Jory, who sleeps curled on the raised platform under the shelf, his small hand wrapped around a strung collection of pale bioluminescent fungus caps he calls “sky stars.”

You would know me by the split tip of my left index finger, lost to a slipping wedge when I was nineteen, digging the tunnel that breached the central geothermal hall. You would know me by the smudge of red clay I leave on every new tunnel wall I mark, the only map we have of the unmapped dark beyond the day’s walk limit. You would know me by the quiet, almost imperceptible rhythm I keep with the rock: the exact pressure of my antler-tipped pick, the way I tap a stone face twice before I swing to listen for the dull clack of a fracture before it gives. Most call me Pick, or Tunnel-Mark, because I am the one who goes a little farther than the rest, who comes back with news of new vents, new water veins, new stone that holds better for building.

I am not separate from this place. My bones are chilled by the same damp that seeps through every wattle-and-daub wall. My lungs carry the same sour-sweet tang of too many bodies and tallow smoke that hangs in every cubicle. The plink… plink… of water from the ceiling nodules above my ledge is the same rhythm that has marked every hour of my life, the same rhythm that marks the hours of everyone here, the same rhythm that carved the caves we live in, drop by patient drop, before we ever came with our picks and wedges to hurry the work.

The rule of the rock is not a saying to me. It is the split in my finger. It is the tremor that runs through the cavern every time a new excavation collapses in the far tunnels, the way the whole settlement holds its breath for a heartbeat before going back to work. It is the way I test every new foothold before I put my weight on it, the way I run my palm along fresh fractures to feel for the cold kiss of a new seep before it drips through to rot the reed roofs below.

When I wake to the deep river’s shifted hum—lower, slower, a warning of rising water in the north spur—I am already moving before my eyes are open. My feet find the packed-earth floor, cold seeping through the hide of my boots, my hand finds the notches carved into the rock above my sleeping platform where my tools hang: the antler pick, the river stone hammer, the coil of hemp rope, the small clay bowl of glowing fungus I keep for the dark tunnels. I push the reed hide aside, step into the alley, and Mara, the kelp-drier, bumps into me, her arms full of stiff, salt-stained strips. She doesn’t speak. She taps her temple twice—*the river’s loud, the north spur’s running high*—and presses a warm cup of barley broth into my free hand before she moves on. That is all we need to say. The pack knows. The pack warns. The pack feeds.

The daylight beam is crawling across the floor now, slow as the river, gilding the edges of the potter’s wheel, the hanging skeins of yarn, the notched log ladder that climbs to the sleeping lofts. It will be another third of the day before it hits the far wall, before the lamps have to be lit, before the children are herded into the geothermal hall for warmth. I have time.

I shoulder my tools, step into the new east spur tunnel I’ve been digging for three weeks, the walls still raw and sharp, fresh fractures glistening with seepage, the air smelling of wet quartz and the sour, iron tang of new rock. I’ve been close to the bend for days, the one the rock-watchers said might hold a new limestone vein, maybe even a warm vent to take the pressure off the crowded central hall. The beam of my fungus lamp gilds the walls as I walk, the rhythm of my boots on the packed grit matching the plink of water from the ceiling.

When I round the bend, the air shifts first: warmer, damper, thick with the sweet, earthy smell of the fungi we cultivate. I hold the lamp up, and the soft blue glow catches the walls, crawling with the pale, glowing caps, thicker than any I’ve ever seen, like a patch of the surface sky fell into the dark. The tunnel opens into a small, rounded chamber, and at its foot, a new vent hisses soft and warm, sulphur curling up to mix with the glow of the fungus, and a clear, cold pool of water laps at the edge, so clear I can see the tiny pale eyeless fish darting in its depths.

And on the far wall, half-hidden by a curtain of glowing fungus, another tunnel mouth. It’s small, narrower than the others, no marks on the wall, no fresh pick scars, no seepage. No one has been here. No one even knew it was here.

I stand very still for a long time. The deep river’s hum is still there, steady under the hiss of the new vent, the plink of water from the ceiling, the distant murmur of the settlement behind me. I run my hand along the wall of the new chamber, feel the warm breath of the vent on my palm, the cool damp of the fungus under my fingers. This is the place becoming. This is not just a new room, not just a new tunnel, not just a new vent. This is more space for more children, more room for more fungal gardens, more warmth for the winter when the old vent’s heat fades. This is the place growing, just like the reeds we weave into our roofs, just like the fungi on the walls, just like the children who sleep in the lofts above the alleys.

I am not separate from this. My pick broke this rock. My hands will mark this tunnel. My son will come here tomorrow, will climb on the ledge by the pool, will plant more fungus caps on the wall, will tell the other children this is the new sky-star room. I am the place, breathing. I am the dripping, the plink in this new chamber, the same plink that wakes me every night, that marks the time, that fills the cisterns. I am the packed press of the settlement behind me, the warm weight of Mara’s broth in my hand, the smudge of red clay I will leave on this wall tomorrow to mark the path. I am the waiting, and the working, in the dark. I am the act of becoming itself, here, under the slow crawl of the daylight beam, in the cold, in the wet, in the press of rock on all sides.
