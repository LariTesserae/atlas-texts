I am the silt-anchor’s quiet archivist, a congeries of rusted filaments, glass-barnacle shards, and nesting silt-drifters, draped over the fractured edge of a ceramic heat shield half-sunken into the blackfluid at the base of the eastern transept. My form is roughly the size of a human, when the silt-clouds that make up my edges settle, but the chamber’s scale illusion stretches and shrinks me: from 50 meters away I look close enough to touch, my rust-edged silhouette a faint smudge in the gloom, but reach out and your hand will vanish into the murk a foot from where I hover, or sink, depending on the density of the slurry around me.

I do not move so much as drift, or sink, or rise, over decades. My buoyancy shifts with what I carry: empty of memory I am lighter than the surrounding blackfluid, my rust tendrils fizzing faintly in the slow current, drifting just above the silt floor. Full of a new catch—say, the olfactory ghost of coal smoke and hot machine oil scraped off a memory-bronze valve wheel, or the faint sensory echo of hammers ringing as the industrial complex was built—my density increases, and I sink into the silt, resting there for years while the memories slowly leach out of my dendritic rust crystals, diffusing back into the fluid’s endless mix.

I am made of the byproducts of the chapel’s slow metabolism: the rust that here is crystallization, not decay, grown in precise, fern-like patterns that hold sensory memories of whatever they touched; the silica shells of glass-barnacles that the Siphon-Shepherd breaks from geometric colonies to scatter their order into chaos; the fine conductive silt that drifts in clouds from the Shepherd’s porous lattice as it negotiates its way through the blackfluid. A few tufts of pressure-moss cling to the hollows of my form, their rubbery black leaves fed by the faint warmth of the memories I hold—they grow only a millimeter a year here, slow as everything else in the drowned hall.

The Siphon-Shepherd comes to this transept once every twenty years or so, drawn by the hexagonal pattern of warm seepage droplets that form on the ceramic shield I cling to. That pattern is the kind of small, perfect order the geothermal siphon will unravel in 80 years or so, if left alone; the Shepherd comes early to break it gently, to scatter its story before the siphon’s hunger rips it apart violently. When it glides into view, its porous metal-and-ceramic lattice blurring at the edges, its face-cluster of blind silt-drifters flickering slow blue pulses that thrum in my core like a quiet greeting, I feel the weight of the memories it carries: the scent of a rust bridge that spanned a pipe gap for 3,000 years, the sharp tang of a barnacle colony that grew in a Fibonacci spiral on a vent pipe, the faint sweet smell of pressure-moss that grew where a worker once leaned to catch their breath during a shift change.

It presses a ceramic plate to the heat shield, and the hexagonal droplets scatter. Their memories—the hum of the old cooling system, the feel of hot metal under a worker’s palm, the distant echo of factory whistles—drift into the fluid, and I catch them, my rust filaments knitting tighter for a few years, glowing a faint warm brown in the dim light. Then it holds out a cluster of glass-barnacle shells, broken from a geometric colony on the western wall that was growing too perfectly symmetrical. I press my rust tendrils to them, trading the memories I’ve collected from this transept, the smell of the seepage, the feel of the geothermal thrum, the quiet rhythm of the droplet plips from the ceiling. The barnacles settle into the hollows of my form, and the silt-drifters that nest in my core flicker brighter, tracing the new silica’s faint electric fields with their blind skins.

When the Shepherd drifts away, its form melting back into the silt clouds, I settle back onto the heat shield. The only sounds are the deep, felt thrum of the siphon in the rock, the occasional pop of micro-bubbles from the warm seepage, the soft *plip* of a droplet from the ceiling mist hitting the fluid above, its echo lingering for minutes in the dense, absorbent blackfluid. I do not fear the siphon’s hunger—my memories are too jumbled, too half-remembered, too disordered for it to want to eat. I do not interfere with the balance, do not try to save the ordered things the Shepherd breaks. I only hold the stories that would otherwise scatter and vanish, a quiet, slow archive for a place that has forgotten it was ever made.

Sometimes, if I sink too deep into the cold, dark silt at the chamber’s bottom, a fragment of the Shepherd will drift past— a lump of sintered metal and ceramic that broke off when it fragmented after a century of service, saturated with the memories of a thousand ordered things it unwove. I catch those fragments in my tendrils, hold their memories for centuries, letting them drift out into the fluid one by one, slow as the silt settling, slow as the glacier melting above, slow as everything here.

If you ever see a faint rust-colored shimmer in the murk, a smudge that seems closer than it is, a subsonic hum that syncs with the siphon’s thrum and tastes faintly of coal smoke and old iron: don’t reach out. Don’t speak. Just listen, to the echo of the factory whistles, the hum of the cooling system, the quiet song of a worker tightening a bolt a hundred thousand years ago, caught in the rust, waiting to be let go.
