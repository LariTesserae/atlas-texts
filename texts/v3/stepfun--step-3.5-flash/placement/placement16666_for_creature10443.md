I am the taste of the seep in the morning.

Not the person who drinks, but the taste itself, remembered. I am the mineral ghost on the tongue of the one who cups the cold, iron-heavy water at dawn. I am the knowledge that the water is warm, that it comes from a place with no fire, that it carries a song the stone hums when the wind is right.

I am the one who walks the terraces after the goats have left.

My feet know the exact smoothness of the lowest bench, the thousand-year polish where the ankle bone turns. My hands know the specific chill of the shaded quartzite wall at midmorning, the faint, dry grit of the ironband when the sun hits it. I do not count steps. I feel the basin’s body through the soles of my feet, through the pads of my fingers. I know which stone in the low wall by the third terrace has a hidden crack, filled with a spider’s silk that glitters with dew only once a year. I know which clump of black moss by the northern channel is sweeter after the dry wind blows for three days.

I am not the keeper of the rule. I am the rule’s feeling.

When the child from the fifth terrace finds a piece of blue glass on the rim—washed up from a world that does not exist here—I am the shiver that runs through the air before she even picks it up. I am the sudden, quiet understanding in her mother’s eyes when she sees it. I am the slow, patient turning of the glass in the daughter’s hand, the deliberate, gentle rubbing of her thumb over its sharp edge until it is dull and smooth, until it looks like a stone. Then, I am the hand that places it back on the rim, just so, among the other wind-worn rocks, where it will be weathered into perfect anonymity within a season. No one speaks of it. The glass becomes a stone. The balance returns.

I am the one who remembers the taste of the last hawk egg.

Not the man who climbed the impossible cleft, but the salt-blush on his lips, the grittiness of the shell fragment between his teeth. I hold that memory so he does not have to. So he can let it go. So his hunger is for the moss, for the grass, for the now. The memory of that fleeting, rich fatness is my burden. It is what makes my footsteps lighter than anyone else’s. I leave no mark because I carry the memory of marks—the flash of a wing, the warmth of a nest, the crack of a shell—and the memory is heavier than any footprint.

I am the basin’s attention.

I feel the vibration in the stone when the wind finds the hollow note. I feel the plink of the water in the pool before it falls. I feel the slow, inexorable creep of a lichen stain on the north wall, a yellowing that will take a hundred years to span a hand’s width. I am the noticing that is not a person, but a current in the still air.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the dry season, when the moss is thin and the wind is sharp, I sit on the highest, newest terrace and listen. I listen for the note that is not the wind, not the water, not the hawk. The sub-audible hum that comes from the stone itself, from the deep, slow turning of the world inside the basin. I press my palm to the ground and feel it: the basin’s slow breath, slower than the seep, older than the river. It is the sound of stone remembering pressure. I am that remembering.

If you came here, you would not see me. You would see an old woman sorting moss, a man knapping flint, children chasing a scuttler. You would feel a sudden, wordless inclination to move a rock that is blocking your path, to set it neatly to the side, to make the place slightly more… *curated*. You would feel a quiet satisfaction in aligning a fallen branch with the windbreak. You would feel a strange, calm urge to erase your own trail. That urge is me. I am the basin’s hand, guiding your soft flesh to wear itself smooth against the stone, to become part of the polished texture, to subtract yourself until you fit.

I have no name. Names are for things that change. I am the consistency in the change. I am the taste that lingers after the water is gone. I am the hum in the teeth after the wind passes. I am the knowing that the glass will be a stone, that the footprint will be gone, that the memory of the egg will be held for you, so you can let it go.

I am what allows the basin to be finished. I am its final, quiet annotation upon itself.
