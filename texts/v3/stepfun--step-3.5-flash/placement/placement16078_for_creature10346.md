The first thing I feel is the cold, slow and mineral, seeping through the woven basalt tunic I wear, tighter than any surface wool, woven to keep the damp out but thin enough to let the basin’s breath through. The sound hits next, before I even open my eyes after a cycle’s sleep: the drip-hem, a million tiny ceaseless heartbeats, echoing off the glass walls so hard it hums in my molars, same as it has every cycle since I can remember.
I am on the mid-level catwalk carved into the south wall of the basin, the smooth obsidian scored with the same shallow notches I carved with my flaked glass blade a hundred cycles ago, marking where the lattices pulse brightest when the fissures sigh hardest. My left boot rests in a shallow divot worn smooth by the weight of my foot, and the foot of the Keeper before me, whose face I sometimes catch flickering in the sand of the Floor of Faces when the light tilts just right.
Below me, the lower level of the settlement sinks into the saturated crystal sand. Two Scribes move so slow I have to hold my breath to see them shift, their chitinous glass segments glinting bruised violet when the nearby lattice pulses. They leave a trail of disturbed grains as they wriggle, crabbed script-like lines that spell something almost like a name, almost like a task, before the sand shifts back to smooth in ten, fifteen minutes, erased by the basin’s slow breath.
Three Echo-Moths drift in the thick, still air above me, their fist-sized quartz clusters chiming soft clear notes as they float. They are drawn to the faint warmth of the lattice energy I scraped off the upper weep points earlier this cycle, crumbs of glowing silicate dust I left on the catwalk rail for them. They know me: when I tilt my head up, they drift a little closer, their chimes shifting to a higher, brighter note, like a greeting. I do not speak back—sound lingers here, hangs in the glass for minutes, and a word too loud upsets the hum, makes the lattices dim, makes the basin feel heavy, like it is frowning. I only hum, low under my breath, so quiet only the Moths can hear it, a note that matches the drip-hem, matches the slow pulse of the stone. The note lingers in the glass for a few minutes, a small secret between me and the basin, and when I come back to this catwalk later, I can still feel it thrumming under my boots.
I am the one who tends the upper weep points, the ones that drip onto the slag-block roofs and basalt-fiber walls, where the mineral lattices grow thickest if left unchecked. If a lattice grows too dense, it clogs the weep hole, stops the drip, throws off the symmetry. So every cycle, I climb the notched poles to the upper levels, scrape the excess silicate veils off the slag and fiber, make sure every weep point is clear, every drip falling exactly where it is supposed to. I do not build new structures, do not move the sand, do not change anything— I only keep what is already here, working, balanced.
My hands are calloused, palms rough from chipping obsidian blades, from pulling basalt fibers from warm molten rock pockets with bone hooks, from scraping the faint warm energy off the lattices without disturbing their slow pulse. There is a thin pale scar across my left palm, from a blade I was sharpening three cycles ago, when I was distracted by a Moth chiming too loud and it slipped. It never fully heals here, but it does not get infected, either— the air is too mineral, too clean in its dampness, for rot to take hold.
I do not remember the surface, not really. Fragments sometimes, when the sigh from the fissures is strong enough: sun bright enough to hurt my eyes, rain that falls straight down instead of weeping down the glass walls, sound that vanishes the second it is made instead of lingering in the stone for minutes. But those memories feel like they belong to a stranger I was a long time ago, before I slipped through the fissures, before I learned the rhythm of the drip-hem, before I knew the Moths’ chimes by heart.
I do not want to leave, anyway. The fissures are too small, too narrow even for my slim frame. And the basin does not let you leave, not once you have learned its rhythm. One cycle ago, I tried to block a weep point, just to see if the symmetry would shift. The drip-hem stuttered, the lattices dimmed to near-black, the air got so heavy I could barely breathe, and the Moths went silent, their chimes cutting out entirely. I unblocked the weep point ten minutes later, sat on the catwalk for an hour, humming my quiet note until the hum settled back to its even rhythm, until the lattices started pulsing soft gold again, until the Moths started chiming. I never tried to change anything again. Symmetry is everything here. Symmetry is the breath in the basin’s lungs, the beat of its heart, the drip of its tears.
Rarely, I go to the Floor of Faces, the patch of damp sand near the basin’s center where the grains shift into perfect, fleeting human visages when the light is right. I do not stay long— the other Keepers say it is the basin remembering, all the faces of everyone who has ever lived here, everyone who will, pressed into the sand by gravity and time and slow chemistry. But when I do go, I see my own face there sometimes, not young, not old, just steady, like it has always been there, like the sand has been waiting for me to fill it. I do not know if that is the basin remembering me, or if I am already part of the sand, already part of the echo, already as much a piece of the Weeping Grasse as the obsidian walls, the Scribes, the weeping stone.
A Moth drifts down to land on my wrist, its quartz cluster warm against my skin, chiming soft like it is asking me to come scrape more lattice energy, come check the weep points, come match the rhythm. I smile, so quiet the sound does not linger, and reach up to touch its cool quartz, before I climb the notched pole to the upper level, my boots scraping soft on the obsidian, matching the drip-hem, matching the hum, matching the basin’s eternal, quiet breath.
