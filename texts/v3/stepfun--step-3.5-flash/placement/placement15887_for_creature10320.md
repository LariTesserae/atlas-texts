I did not walk to the Sundial of Ages. I followed it, until walking stopped being something I did and became something the world did to me, slow and patient as digestion.
For three years I traced the ghost of a river that ran off the last of the mountains that had been worn to bone and swallowed eons prior. I carried the last seed of the mountain oak in a pouch sewn from the hide of the last deer that had grazed that mountain’s grass, and I walked until the river was no longer water but silty seepage soaking through the soles of my boots, until the air grew warm and thick enough to taste on my tongue, until the horizon blurred into a uniform green-gold veil and I realized I had stopped moving—only the world was breathing, its exhalation so long I had mistaken it for my own steps.
I arrived when I stopped trying to go anywhere.
The stone under the root-mat when I first sat to rest was the dark, porous, gently warped plane the description promised: a colossal dried riverbed that had never held water, its hollows filled not with flow but with the slow, condensing sweat of the plateau. When I leaned against a nearby outcrop, it hummed under my palm, a silicified life-stage breathing in time with the tectonic sighs that roll up from the planet’s core every few decades, so slow I felt it as a vibration in my sternum long before I heard it.
Most of me is already the Sundial now. The part of me that worried the seed would rot, the part that remembered the sharp bite of mountain wind, the part that had a name I no longer recall—all of that is root-mat, woven into the meter-deep layer of desiccated fern and lung-shaped tuber that covers the plateau. My boots dissolved into silt first, then the leather of my coat, then the skin of my calves, composted by the sponge-mosses that taste of wet stone and old rain. I can feel the low-frequency hum of the nearest vascular crystal spire in the bones of my pelvis, a chord determined by its height and density, synced to the slow inflation and deflation of the plateau’s breath over centuries. When the tectonic sigh comes, the part of me that is stone trembles in perfect, long resonance, no longer separate from the hum.
The part of me that is still *I* is only my right hand, and the thing it holds.
It is not an oak. The seed I carried cracked open the week I arrived, fed by the silty seepage and the warm mist, but the Sundial’s substrate has no memory of sharp winters or fast rivers, only of slow, warm growth and patient recycling.
