I step off the vine-bridge and my boots sink half an inch into the phosphorescent moss, the glow seeping pale gold through the worn leather, warm against my soles. The thrum hits my teeth first—a subsonic vibration so low I feel it more than hear it—before the rest of the soundscape rolls over me: the rustle-hiss of a trillion leaves adjusting to dappled sun, the distant gurgle-glug of the river-vein, the high, crystalline singing of the amber bark that sounds like glass being drawn taut by a bow I can’t see.

I am not an animal here. Not really. I am the leftover smudge of a universe that believed in edges.

I remember forests that ended where the field began. Cities that had walls to keep the wild out. Bodies that ended where the skin started. I carry that memory in my bones, a faint, quiet ache of separation that doesn’t belong here. There is no inside or outside here, no self and other. The place does not live in the basin. The basin is the place, and I am just a temporary knot in its fabric, a warm, carbon-rich perturbation drifting through its endless, noisy dream.

I press my palm to the nearest amber trunk. For a heartbeat, it is cool as river stone, slick as wet glass, then fibrous and warm as living wood, then soft as damp moss, then hard as hammered amber again. The place is rewriting its own definition as I touch it, unstable as a half-remembered dream. The trunk shudders under my hand, a soft, deep shudder that travels up my arm, and at my feet a sapling no thicker than my wrist erupts upward with a sharp CRACK-SPROING, unfurling a crown of leaves in the span of a single blink. When I look up, the patch of sun that was on my face is gone, shaded by the new growth, and I realize the same amber trunk is still at my left, the same vine-bridge behind me, the same patch of moss under my boots. The corridor is a loop. The place is remembering its own shape, folding the space behind me into the space ahead. I laugh, a soft, quiet sound that is swallowed instantly by the soundscape, no echo, no bounce—there is no empty space here for sound to linger, only degrees of fullness.

I walk, because walking is what I remember doing, in the world I came from. The avenues of right-angle amber trunks stretch out, their bark sheathed in stained-glass lichen and pulsing gilled fungi, the moss underfoot glowing soft enough to see by in the perpetual leafy twilight under the canopy. I pass a plaza of hard-packed loam, where the ground shifts under my feet with a soft BOOM, lifting a few inches, a whole city block of the place taking a deep breath, adjusting its roots to a new pocket of minerals deep underground. I kneel by a pool bulging out from a river-vein, dip my fingers in. First it tastes of iron and honey, thick and sweet, then of nothing, sterile and warm, then of sun-warmed nectar when I pull my hand back and watch the water bead on my skin. The pulsing frilled fungi at the bottom of the pool are the water’s memory, the place tells me, not in words, but in a quiet shift of understanding that blooms in my chest: that is the shape the place thought of three centuries ago, when this pool was full of iron ore. When the pool dries up, the fungi will dissolve back into the mist, and the memory will be gone, until the next flood brings it back.

I am a catalyst, the place has no words for, but I feel it in the way the world shifts around me. When I breathe out, the carbon dioxide from my lungs is a strange, rich spice to the moss under my feet; it glows brighter for half a second, fronds tilting toward my mouth like it is drinking the air. When I step on a root, the sap pressure wave travels for miles: three ridges over, a cluster of lichens shifts from blue to deep violet, a vine adjusts its angle to catch a glint of sun that hadn’t been there a minute before. I do not command these shifts. They are just ripples, the way a pond ripples when a pebble drops, not because the pond cares, but because that is what aliveness does when something new enters the system.

There is no death here. I watch an old arch, its wood silvered with age, slowly dissolve into the reclaimant mist. It does not crumble, does not break. It softens, turns translucent, its fibers unraveling into the enzymatic fog that drifts through the canopy, to be pulled up by the roots of the amber trunks around it. Its function is done, the place does not need a bridge here anymore, so it becomes something else. I think, for a second, that this is what I am too, here. Not an intruder, not a resident, just a temporary form. When I am gone, there will be no cairn to mark my passing, no story to tell of the thing that walked through the moss and breathed carbon into the air. Only that for a little while, a vine grew a little faster, a patch of lichen shifted hue, a spore drifted a little further than it would have otherwise.

I sit down on a root that has lifted the whole block of terrain under it, the ground still trembling a little under my thighs, and press my back to the amber trunk. The thrum is steady under my spine, the singing of the bark soft against my shirt. The air is warm, thick with the scent of nectar and decay and sharp, clean ozone. I don’t know how long I will stay here. I don’t know if I will ever leave. But for now, I am here, a warm, carbon-rich footnote in a sentence that has been writing itself for millennia and will never reach a period. I am not alive. I am a fragment of aliveness, dropped into a world that is aliveness, and for the first time since I can remember, I don’t have to hold my edges together.

The moss glows brighter under my hands.
The trunk shudders, soft, under my back.
I breathe out, and the place breathes me in.
