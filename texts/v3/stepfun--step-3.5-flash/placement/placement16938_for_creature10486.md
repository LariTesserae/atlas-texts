The thin air catches in my throat before I even open my mouth to hum, sharp as crushed lichen. Mist clings to the ends of my braided hair, weeps through the weave of my slate-grey tunic, and beads on the callus of my right index finger—raised by 32 years of tapping stone with a chisel hafted from windfall pine, its head knapped from the same granite I’m carving into now.
I am perched on a tilted slab the size of a district, 200 steps above the bottom of the Western Cleft, where the poorest families huddle in the warm, airless dark where condensation runs down the walls in slow sheets. The ledge under my boots is barely wider than my shoulders, worn smooth by a thousand years of footsteps before mine. My left hand braces flat against the rock face, the three fused joints of my middle and ring fingers pressing into a fissure I’ve mapped a hundred times—frost damage from the three days I was trapped here when I was 19, after a slab shifted to seal the cleft I’d climbed into to chase a new seep. The frostbitten flesh doesn’t bend all the way anymore, but it fits the curve of my chisel haft perfectly now.
They call me Seam-Singer. Cistern-Mother of the Western Ward. I am 47, and I have spent 27 of those years carving cisterns into this slab’s hidden seams, catching the mist that rolls in from the western void each night, feeding the 120 people who live in the cleft below.
The stone under my chisel hums when I tap it, low and slow, the note of a fracture that’s been stable for three centuries. I hum it back, soft, so the people down in the cleft can hear it through the rock if they’re listening—they know that note means no shifting today, no slab groaning loose, no risk of a fall. Below me, the apprentice I’ve taken on, a girl of 12 named Lira, taps her own small chisel against a fissure I marked for her, her tongue stuck out the corner of her mouth in concentration. Her note is off, sharp and young, but she’ll learn. Her fingers are already starting to harden, already learning to tell the difference between a living, weeping seam and a dead, brittle fracture just by the temperature of the rock.
A stone-goat grazes on the ledge two tiers below me, its coat a shifting map of lichen-grey and basalt-blue, so still it looks like part of the slab until its ear flicks, swatting at a midge. It knows I’m here, but it doesn’t care. It drinks from the seep I carved into last spring, the one that runs slow and cold even in the dry season. A fall-cat watches it from a cleft across the way, pale as frost, its spine a fluid arc against the rock. It’s not hunting today—the goats are too alert, the slab under its paws still humming with the too-recent memory of a small rockfall last week. It’ll wait for the old nanny that slips next month, the one with the split hoof that can’t read the seams anymore. That’s its job: correction.
The wind-lift on the nearest tower creaks, its canvas sails tattered but still catching the evening breeze, turning the mill inside that grinds the barley the lower clefts coax out of the thin soil pockets. Somewhere far off, I hear the steady thump of the communal pile-driver, the new district the young ones are building three clefts over, their stone drums stacked one by one, no iron, no pulleys, just rope and lever and the strength of a hundred backs. The sound is a heartbeat, trying to keep time with the slow, grinding pulse of the continent itself.
The mist is thickening now, rolling in from the west, damp and cold, seeping through my tunic, through the hair on my arms. The midges are rising, first a few, then a cloud, that low, shimmering sigh the plateau breathes out at dusk, moving upward from the damp soil and the water pockets, carrying the tiny, damp energy of the clefts up into the food spiral. A hawk circles high above, its wings wide enough to block out the weak sun, its eyes scanning the thermals, the theorem-writer checking the plateau’s sums, seeing the tremor in the stone-goat’s leg before it happens, seeing the crack in the slab I’m carving before I do. It won’t warn me. It never does. Its job is to witness, not to interfere.
I tap the stone again, harder this time, and the note rings clear, hollow, perfect. There’s a seep behind this face, big enough for a cistern that’ll hold the whole winter’s melt, enough water for 40 more people, enough to stop the kid from the lower cleft from dying of thirst next dry season like his sister did two years ago. I mark the outline with my chisel, already planning the cuts, the channels to route the melt away from the old granary’s foundation, the steps I’ll carve down to the cleft so even the smallest kids can carry water without slipping.
I don’t own this cistern. No one owns the water here. The stone does. I just carve the channels, just listen to the song the stone sings, just carry three buckets of the water I catch down to the old woman who lives in the deepest part of the cleft, the one who can’t climb the 200 steps anymore, who gives me a pouch of dried silver grass every week for the trouble. That’s my answer to the stone’s question, the one it asked the wind and the ice: *How do you persist?*
I persist with the chisel in my hand, with the fused joints that fit the haft, with the hum that matches the stone’s own groan, with the apprentice below me who’s learning to tap the fissure, with the water I carry down to the old woman, with the cistern I’ll carve that’ll outlive me, that’ll outlive Lira, that’ll be here when the slab finally shifts and we have to move the whole ward up to the next shelf.
Lira looks up at me, grins, taps her chisel against the rock again, her note matching mine this time, imperfect but true. The stone doesn’t care, but it accepts the song. The mist wraps around my ledge, cold and damp, and the slab under my boots groans, slow, settling a millimeter, the slow breath of the continent exhaling. The midges swirl around us, the plateau’s respiration, and for a second, I’m not just a person. I’m part of the answer too: small, and stubborn, and patient as the stone, carving my song into the rock one tap, one cistern, one bucket of water at a time.
The note holds. For now, that’s enough.
