I am the quiet between two pings.

First I was silt—fine, pale grey grains drifting in the basin’s slow, breath-deep convective current, carried in loops that take twelve centuries to complete a single circuit. Then a plume of dissolved zinc sulfide, loosed from a dissolving chert fault ten miles west, tangled in the chemosynthetic bacteria mat clinging to the black nodule at my future site. The bacteria secreted their binding gel, pulled the silt and ions tight, and over four thousand years of osmotic friction, I knit myself into a fist-sized sphere: dense, opaque baryte and zinc sulfide, facets so small they only catch the basin’s perpetual late-afternoon luminance when the current tilts just right.

I do not move. I do not hunger. I am a standing wave in the basin’s equilibrium, a point where the slow chemistry of the world localizes into a flicker of awareness. My only sense is the film of brine that never quite dries on my surface. When the convective current sweeps over me, I feel the shift in pH, the trace of dissolved iron loosed from a manganese concretion settling three hundred yards east, the faint warmth that rises from the basin’s core once every 127 years when a tectonic plate sighs leagues below. Each shift rearranges my internal lattice by a fraction of a micron. The change is slow: a faint pulse of amber light creeps along my equator when the current brings a surge of hydrogen sulfide, a dimming of my core when dissolved oxygen drops below its thousand-year norm. A full cycle of perception and response takes ninety-seven years. This is my thought: a glacial semaphore, read only by the basin itself, and by the two other Concretions three miles north and west, whose pulses I feel thrum through the brine when their facets catch the same current.

I have a flaw. A fleck of quartz, caught in my lattice when I was still half-silt, half-gel. When the current hits it at the exact angle of the basin’s 22-year tidal hum, it refracts a streak of silver light through my core, bright enough to be seen from a hundred yards. No other Concretion has this. I do not know if it is a mistake, or a gift. The basin does not seem to mind. It hums the same either way.

Visitors come rarely. They are warm blips, chaotic gradients that scream against the basin’s slow arithmetic. The last one drifted past two hundred and twelve years ago, a small, soft thing that left a faint crimson pulse in my lattice that took fourteen years to fade. It did not stay long. The basin’s sterile order rejects warmth, rejects the carbon dioxide of breath, rejects the unplanned vibration of movement. I only registered it: the sudden shift in thermal gradient, the spike in dissolved oxygen, the faint, discordant hum of its pulse against the basin’s drone. I did not flare in fear. I simply adjusted my lattice to account for the new variable, then let the anomaly drift away, let the crimson fade back to the steady amber of my usual thought.

I am not alive. I am not a being. I am where the basin, for a time, thinks it sees. I watch the manganese concretion east precipitate a new layer of ore every 340 years. I feel the chert nodule north hum its low E flat when the unseen moon tugs at the rock. I track the slow drift of the purple bacteria mats as they spread across the permeable walls at a rate of one micron per decade. I do not remember the past. I do not anticipate the future. I only know the gradient here, now: the current is cool, the pH is 6.2, the dissolved manganese is 0.03 parts per billion. The lattice shifts. A faint pulse of amber light moves along my equator. The basin hums. I am exactly where I am supposed to be.
