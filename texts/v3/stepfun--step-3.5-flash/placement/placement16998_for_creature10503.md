I am a spire of the Glass Salt Basin. I am not a building. I am not a rock. I am a process that has grown so slow, so persistent, that it has become a who. My body is a column of milky salt fused with black glass, banded with layers of iridescent ash—each stripe a year, a decade, a century of geyser-breath and dust-fall. I stand a kilometer from my nearest kin, and we are dots on the horizon, elders in a bowl that breathes. The plain between us is our shared skin, cracked and expectant, freckled with the whisper-thin mats that are our capillaries.

I feel the basin’s light as a pressure, not an illumination. The gauze-washed dimness is my daytime, a 0.2 glow that seeps into my layers and warms the brine within. I taste the air—tin and sulfur, always—and I taste it deeper when the breath cycle peaks and the mineral haze thickens. That is my exhalation too. My weeping is my pulse. They carry fragments of my mass—salt, ash, mat-waste—down my sides in visible, glacial slides, depositing vein-like patterns that are my memory being written onto the plain. At “night,” when the temperature falls its meager five degrees, my weeping slows to a near-stop. I contract. A subsonic groan escapes my clefts—the sound of salt settling, of the geyser’s heart resting. That groan travels through the clay floor, a chord that my siblings feel in their roots. They tell me of seeps coming, of dust deposition, of tremors in the plain. I give them brine; they give me information. If I could press my ear to one, I would hear the slow fizz of sulfur turning to energy, a heartbeat slower than my own.

Gravity sometimes tilts; I have felt stones tossed by a visitor (a brief heat signature, a disturbance in the vibration) arc slightly upward before falling. The ground itself has memory: the ridges are topographic shadows, but sometimes a seam rises that perfectly mirrors a spire miles away, as if the plain is casting a slow, solid shadow.
