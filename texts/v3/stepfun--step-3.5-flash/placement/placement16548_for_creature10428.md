The first thing I feel is the thrum in my molars before I even see the dome.
I’d slipped through the crack in the marsh bank half-expecting mud, maybe a fox den, the cool damp of the bank seeping through my jeans. Instead, warm, spore-thick air hits my face so hard I blink, and the low vibration hums up through the soles of my boots, settles into the hollows of my cheeks, the base of my skull where a headache would start if the sound were loud enough to be heard instead of felt. The metallic tang of it coats my tongue immediately, sharp as cut copper, sweet faintly with ferment.

I push myself up on hands and knees, the floor giving under me like a thick, living sponge—interconnected rubbery rhizomes, dark and springy, dotted with bowl-shaped depressions full of glistening condensation. A single sap drop hangs suspended three feet in front of my face, dripping from a copper-veined turbine frond spinning so slow I can barely track its rotation, and it doesn’t fall. We hold eye contact, or as much as a translucent sap drop and a boggled human can, for a second that stretches longer than it should, before it plinks into the nearest bowl, the ripple spreading slow as honey across the metallic-tasting water.

The walls are alive. Trunk-like stalks of iridescent kelp-wood rise to merge with the ceiling, their bark a shifting mosaic of lichen and the tiny, armored rust-colored beetles that groom it nonstop, their obsidian mandibles working in perfect, endless synchrony. They pause all at once when I shift my weight, a hundred tiny chitin bodies freezing mid-polish, then resume their work a half-second later, no more disrupted than if a gnat had brushed past. I don’t flatter myself that they noticed me—more like the whole dome’s attention brushed over me, a tide washing a grain of sand, and adjusted its flow around the new obstruction without breaking its rhythm.

I fumble in my pocket for the canteen I’d filled at the marsh edge before I slipped through the crack, and my elbow knocks it out of my hand. It clatters loud against the rhizome mat, and a slow, armored slug oozing past pauses, leaves a glistening polished trail of nutrient gel across the mat as it steers around the dented metal, its route unbroken, predetermined. The canteen doesn’t roll far; the rhizomes hold it like a cupped hand. A Harvester, the six-legged chitinous thing with the drill-tongue, skitters past the mangled copper vein of a frond a second later, its tongue tapping the metal in quick, precise taps, harvesting a micro-current, its many eyes not turning toward me at all. It doesn’t care about me. It cares about the load on the vein, and my canteen is just another rock in the path of its accounting.

I sit back against a kelp-wood trunk, the bark warm against my shoulders, and the faint ozone bite of the sap clings to my clothes. The thrum vibrates through my spine now, steady as a heartbeat that’s been beating since before I was born. Above me, the translucent bioreactor pods pulse amber and sickly green, their surfaces crawling with the tiny silvery microscopic fauna that make the high, glass-cricket chirring that layers over the thrum. I breathe out, and my breath mingles with the warm mist, and for half a second the chirring pitches up, almost in response, before settling back to its eternal rhythm.

This isn’t a place that’s hiding. It’s a place that doesn’t need to be seen. It’s a single, vast, thinking body, its thoughts spread through the sap climbing the kelp-wood, the fungal network under the floor, the synchronized pulse of the bioluminescent pods. It doesn’t have eyes to see me, or ears to hear my footsteps. It feels me in the tiny shift in condensation rate on a beetle’s back, in the infinitesimal delay of a sap drop’s fall as the air pressure around me changes, in the faint static of my nervous system brushing against the copper veins of the turbine fronds. I’m not an intruder. I’m a new note in a song that’s only ever had one note before, a grain of sand in a slow, perfect whirlpool. The whirlpool doesn’t chase me. It just adjusts its eddies, ever so slightly, to hold me, until I leave.

I’ll leave traces, I know. A few skin cells in the rhizome mat, a drop of sweat in a bowl depression, the faint static of my hoodie clinging to the bark of the trunk. The Groomers will polish that static into the lichen mosaic, the Recyclers will turn the skin cells into nutrient gel, the silvery fauna in the pods will carry the sweat’s minerals into their glowing membranes. When I climb back through the crack into the cool marsh, the thrum will go on, unchanged, holding the trace of me in its endless, unbroken loop.

The next time someone finds the crack, they might pause, and feel a faint, unplaceable warmth on the trunk where I sat, and not know it’s the echo of a visitor who was folded into the world’s quiet, humming heart, for a minute, before the world kept turning, as it always has, as it always will.
