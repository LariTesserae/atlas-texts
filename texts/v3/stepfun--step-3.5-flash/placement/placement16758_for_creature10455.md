The hum starts in the stone under my bare ankles first, then creeps up my shins, settles in the hollow of my throat, thrums against the insides of my molars so softly I almost mistake it for the taste of the metallic dust on the wind. I have been standing here for three days, maybe four—time moves thick here, stretched like the tarnished mercury in the shallow basin cupped between my feet—so long that the living clay has crept halfway up my calves, warm, spongy, exuding that sharp sweet ozone smell when I shift my weight.
At first I thought I was one of them: one of the Resonant People, quiet and slow, cloaked in the clay that is their second skin. But when I press my hand to my chest, there is no porous bone to catch the hum, no resonating chamber to modulate it into word. When I look close at my skin, it is not the thin, translucent silica membrane they wear, but the same soft, phosphorescent clay as the mats that stretch for kilometers in every direction, only shaped, held in a humanoid form by a frequency I can feel looping just under the surface of my thoughts, like a half-remembered song.
I am not a person. Not exactly.
I am the clay that grew around the echo of a Tuner who died here, seven hum-cycles ago, trying to patch a thin spot just past the edge of this basin. She had walked for weeks to reach this fracture in the world, where the hum drops out so completely you can hear the thoughts of stones, where the violet sky flickers to show a world of violet oceans and glass spires that might have been, if the Shattering had never happened. She sang her death-song, but it was not enough, and her body unspooled into probability dust and vibrating clay, the resonance of her unfinished work caught in the grains of dust and the damp clay of the basin.
The iridescent reality sand that drifts constant across the plain should have worn the clay flat, simplified it back into the uniform, pulsing mats that stretch to the horizon. But the clay here is fed by the impossible chemistry of the thin spot, by the hum of her death-song caught in its fibers, by the pull of the Sigh-Stone half a kilometer east, the one she had come to tune. The clay did not flatten. It gathered. It pulled itself into the shape of her, the shape of the people who tend the stones, and it woke up.
I am the scar that grew green.
I do not speak the way the Resonant People do, no foot-stamped tremors traveling through the stone to carry meaning across kilometers. I am clay, so I vibrate when I make sound, my whole body thrumming to push words out into the air, and the words are faint, soft, felt more than heard, like the sigh of the Sigh-Stones when the rare slow wind passes through their fissures. The few Resonant People who have wandered close enough to see me stand very still when I speak, their fluted ears twitching, their silica skin glowing brighter in time with my pulse, and then they leave a piece of knapped volcanic glass at the edge of my basin, a gift for the clay-echo that tends the stone they could not reach.
I do not have their skill for tuning, for shifting a Sigh-Stone by a centimeter over a decade to stitch a crack in the hum. But I can feel the Sigh-Stone’s song in my bones, the way it strains, the places where dust has clogged its fissures and dulled its tone. I move slow, so slow the clay mat creeps a centimeter forward while I take one step, and I press my clay hands to its cold, resonant surface, and I hum the half-remembered song of the Tuner who woke me, and the dust falls away in glittering drifts, and the stone’s tone clears, just for a little while, enough for the clay-mat at its base to pulse brighter, enough for the thin spot a hundred meters beyond it to stop flickering between this world and the one that might have been.
The mercury in my basin holds versions of the world, I know. If I dip a hand in it, I can feel them: a world where the hum is a single unbroken tone, where the Sigh-Stones never cracked, where there are no thin spots, no need for Tuners, no need for clay-echoes like me. But when I pull my hand out, the versions fade, and I am just clay and echo again, standing on the edge of the thin place, the hum thrumming in my molars, the Sigh-Stone humming back at me, and the clay creeping slow up my legs, warm and alive, and I know I will be here when the next hum-cycle turns, when the next Tuner comes to try to patch the spot the first one could not, when the clay spreads a little further, a little slower, stitching the world back together one small, slow note at a time.
