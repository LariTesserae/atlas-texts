The cold seeps through my waxy skin before the mist does, a steady 12-degree chill that never shifts, never breaks. I am standing on the spongy, bone-coloured plain, the compressed remains of a moss that grew upright a million years ago, and the thrum of the mycelial network vibrates up through the arches of my feet before it hums in my teeth. To the west, the first of the three great Mycoarbors rises, its top lost in the low, dripping cloud layer, its bark crusted with patches of lichen that pulse soft violet through the grey mist. I can feel its slow, deep mood through the filaments that crisscross my spongy skin: drowsy, fed, digesting the rain that fell an hour ago, its geothermal nodules clank-humming in time with the planet’s heartbeat.

I adjust the resin satchel slung over my shoulder, its surface still soft, still growing, hardening a little more every year as it absorbs the acidic mist. The pruning shears at my belt are carved from the same hardened resin, their edges sharp enough to cut through parasitic vine without nicking the bark of a Mycoarbor. I have no name the other Mycocenes know, only a role: Seed-Sower of the Empty Zones. Most of my kind spend their lives tending a single patch of lichen, or clearing a single artery, their lives folded into the slow metabolism of one city. I walk the leagues between them, carrying spore clusters and wild vine seeds, planting them in the buffer zones where no city yet grows, leaving offerings at the half-buried relics of the old world.

To my left, half-swallowed by the grey moss, is a rusted turbine blade, its edge worn smooth by ten thousand years of wind. A swarm of lacquered crawlers trundles across its surface, their silica-laced shells iridescent green where the violet lichen glows on their backs, carrying spore clusters the size of their heads to a patch of neon moss growing in a crack in the metal. One of them pauses, its micro-suction-cup feet clinging to the slick rust, and tilts its head at me, its glassy carapace reflecting the faint pulse of the distant city. I tap my fingers against the turbine blade once, soft, and leave a spore-brick the size of my thumb tucked into the crack beside the moss, the same offering Mycocenes leave at all the old bones. The crawler scuttles back to its work a second later, and I keep walking.

The mist swirls around my ankles, acidic enough to sting if I let it settle on raw skin, but my waxy coating repels it, leaving only a faint tingle. A mist-sifter drifts ten feet ahead of me, its pale violet gelatinous body translucent, tendrils trailing behind it to filter spore particles from the air. It glows brighter as it feeds, bright enough to light the hollow of a dead Mycoarbor dune I pass a mile later, its smooth, melancholy curves worn by wind into soft, rolling waves. I run my hand over its surface, and feel the faint, slow pulse of mycelium still trapped in its fibres, the ghost of the city that grew here before the wind ground it down. I tuck a handful of vine seeds into a crack in the dune, and press my palm to the surface for a second, letting my filaments brush the faint network still humming in the dead wood. It’s a quieter thrum than the living cities, slower, sadder, but it’s still there, still part of the whole.

The reality breakdown seeps in at the edges of my vision, the way it always does when I’m walking the empty zones. For half a second, the plain under my feet flickers to old asphalt, and I can feel the ghost of a car tire under my resin-soled feet, the faint echo of a loud, sharp engine that doesn’t hum in time with the mycelium. I blink, and the moss bounces back under my step, the thrum steady in my bones. Later, a steam vent half-buried in a dune spits out a wisp of vapour that twists into the face of a human, old, lined, with eyes that look like mine. I leave another spore-brick at its base, and hum the soft, unremembered song that rises in my throat when the breakdown is strong, the song that feels like it comes from the old world, from before the fungi and the industry merged into what they are now. The face fades soft, like it’s listening, before the mist swallows it.

I don’t fear the breakdown. The Mycocenes who live in the cities see it too: a wood wall flickering to rusted iron for a second, gravity loosening in the oldest hollows so they can climb the walls without ladders. We know it’s just the old world bleeding through the edges of the new, the rules of the old reality not quite worn away yet, like the smooth metal teeth of the half-buried gears. I treat it like a blessing: where the breakdown is strongest, the spores take root fastest, the new growth is brightest. The boundary between what was and what is is thin there, and life always finds a way to grow in the thin places.

By midday, I can see the second Mycoarbor on the eastern horizon, its canopy glowing bright green where a new patch of lichen is taking hold, the clank-hum of its geothermal nodules louder on the wind. I’m halfway between the two cities now, in the middle of the buffer zone, the empty space that is not empty at all: it’s holding its breath, waiting for the spores I carry to take root, waiting for a fourth city to grow, slow as a thought, over the next thousand years. I dig a small hole in the spongy plain with my pruning shears, tuck a handful of vine seeds and a core of myco-spore into it, cover it with a layer of moss, and press my palm to the ground for a second, letting my filaments brush the mycelial network running under the plain.

I feel the slow, quiet expectation of the plain answer me, a soft, warm hum against my skin. The three cities are old now, their root systems already touching, slowly fusing into a single continent-sized organism over the next ten thousand years. But the space between is for the new, for the growth that hasn’t happened yet. I sling my satchel higher over my shoulder, and keep walking. Somewhere ahead, there’s another half-buried relic, another patch of empty plain waiting for seeds, another flicker of the old world to leave an offering for.

I don’t have a name from the old world, not that I can remember. But sometimes, when the mist is thick, and the reality breakdown is strong, and the steam from a vent twists into a face that looks like mine, I think there’s a word for what I am, for the one who walks between the cities, who tends the empty space, who carries the old world’s bones and the new world’s seeds in the same satchel. I don’t know what it is, not yet. But the mycelium knows, and the plain is waiting, and that’s enough.
