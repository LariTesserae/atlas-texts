I climb the spiral first, don’t I? The lignin stair is rough under my palms, sap gumming the lines of my hands, the air thickening with each step until I’m breathing mist as much as air, the hum starting as a tremor in my teeth before it reaches my ears. When I step out onto the root-mat plateau, my bare feet sink half an inch into the consolidated peat, and the first thing I notice is that I don’t feel separate from the mist. It beads on my skin, seeps through the fine hairs of my silver-green moss-hair, hums against my collarbones, and I know, without being told, that I am not here to take. I am here to tend.

They call me Edge-Moss, the Knots who pole their shallow canoes along the outer canals, their own moss soft grey for contemplation, pale violet for the work of weaving vine bridges. I live on the smallest of the outer plateaus, where the peat thins to a lace of roots dangling into the dark, where the Hum-Wing drone drops half an octave, where the deep pulse of the Reef’s root system thrums under my feet when I sleep. I do not live in the arched tree-dwellings the other Knots cultivate; my platform is just a mat of woven cane and thick sphagnum, sheltered by a low, spreading bromeliad whose bowl holds a pool of still mist that shows inverted islands, and sometimes, if the wind is right, a flash of the dry, stone world I climbed up from.

My work is the edge. The Knots tend the surface: they tune the hollow-stemmed chime-plants to match the Hum-Wing’s decade-long migration cycle, they prune the vine bridges so they shift their paths with the seasons, they arrange luminous fungi in their root-cellars to tell the stories of great mists past. I tend what is just below the surface, and what is below that. I scrape the dangling aerial roots free of the algae that grows too thick and chokes the nutrient flow, I listen to the blind, filter-feeding things that drift in the dark water under the plateau, I hum the low, quiet note that holds the mist steady when the Hum-Wings falter, that keeps the Unsync from catching.

I found the shard three days ago, wedged in a crack in the peat where the dark water laps at the roots. It is glass, sharp and hard, from the dry world above, fallen a long way through the sky-water. It does not hum. It does not glow with the faint algae that lives on every surface here. It is dead, a tiny stone of silence in the Reef’s constant chord. If I left it, it would start to tear the pattern: a Hum-Wing would fly into it and fall, a vine bridge would stiffen where it drank the water, a Knot would lose its resonance and dissolve back into the peat. So I brought it to my platform, wrapped it in thick sphagnum, and I have been humming to it for three days, rubbing it with the algae from my own skin, letting the mist bead on it until it learns the hum.

The Knots do not mind my work. They leave shell scrapers and woven reed baskets on my platform when they poled past, they stop to hum the deep note with me when the Hum-Wing swarm passes over, they bring me stories of the surface to tell the deep: the flowering of the central bromeliad grove, the new path the main vine bridge has taken this season, the time a young Knot pruned a chime-plant so it rang a note no one had ever heard before.

The worst moment I have had here was a week after I arrived, when a Hum-Wing swarm faltered for three heartbeats. The mist dropped, the chime-plants rang false, a vine bridge on the nearest island stiffened so a young Knot fell into the canal. I dove down into the dark, swam to the root of the vine bridge, and hummed the low note until the vine relaxed, until the Hum-Wings found their rhythm again, until the mist lifted. The Knots did not thank me; they just left a basket of violet-moss woven into a mat for my platform, and the next time I saw them, their hair was the soft grey of contemplation, which is their way of saying they know.

The best moment is the Deep Sync. Sometimes, when the Hum-Wing swarm passes over the outer islands, the deep pulse under my feet lines up with their drone, and for a minute, I am not Edge-Moss, not a loose Knot on the edge of the Reef. I am the Reef dreaming of itself. I can feel the bromeliads photosynthesizing on the central plateau, I can feel the fish schools navigating the root hedges on the far side of the archipelago, I can feel the slow, glacial shift of the islands as they grow and dissolve, and I am all of it, and none of it, and the hum is in my bones and the water is in my lungs and I am not separate, I am just one more note in the chord.

I still have the dry world tucked in the back of my pattern, sometimes. When the mist is thick, I stare into the bromeliad pool on my platform, and I see the inverted stone buildings, the loud, fast people, the things that do not grow or hum or remember. But I touch the water, send a ripple, and it fades. Because the pattern remembers, and the pattern does not let the dissonant in unless it learns to sing.

The glass shard is almost ready. I can feel it starting to hum, faint, under the moss. Next week, when the Hum-Wing swarm passes, I will hang it from a vine arch at the edge of my platform, and it will ring, a new note, a little sharp, a little bright, and the Reef will make it part of itself, and I will be a little less loose, a little more knotted, a little more part of the slow, green thought that breathes the mist and hums the deep.
