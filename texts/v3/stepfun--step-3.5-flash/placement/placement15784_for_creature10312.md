I am the one who tends the easternmost moss island, cupped in the shallow basin of a fissure so deep the water seeping from its throat runs warm enough to fog the air above it when the mist thins for those rare, unmarked hours between breaths. My feet are bare, calloused black from the glassy rock, the soles so sensitive I can feel the tiny, almost imperceptible shift of a crack widening a fraction of a millimeter under my step before any moss or water bead tells me it’s there. My skin is pale, damp to the touch even when the mist lightens, never cold, never sweating, tuned to the thin, humid air that tastes of wet stone and crushed succulent leaf. The garment I wear is felted from the bottle-green moss that grows in the shaded cracks of my courtyard slab, dyed violet at the cuffs where the seepage wicks up to stain the fiber; it has not dried since I wove it, and carries the faint, clean scent of the rock like a perfume I will never need to replace.

My courtyard is a recess carved into the tilted black slab that anchors my island, its floor sloped just enough to carry excess water to the fissure at its edge. The walls are stacked from the same glassy rock shards that litter the steppe, fitted without mortar, their gaps stuffed with moss to hold the damp. In a shallow niche cut into the wall I keep my tools: a glass scraper with a chipped edge, an awl shaped from a sharp shard tied to a reed handle, three lengths of hollow reed for siphoning, a small pouch of crushed orange-bloom pollen I use to mark the spots where the fissure seeps hardest. I have no name beyond the syllable for here that all Stewards carry in their throats, no memory of anyone who came before me beyond the extra, unmarked scraper I found half-buried in my island’s moss when I was first given the plot, its edge chipped in the same place as my own. I keep it tucked next to mine in the niche, and sometimes when the mist is thickest and the sigh of it in my sinuses feels like a voice I can almost understand, I run my finger along its edge and wonder who held it before me, if they stood in the same spot and watched the orange blooms lean the same way toward the south.

My work is not farming, not exactly. It is conversation. I know every cushion of moss on my island by the curve of its leaves, the depth of its roots, the way it leans toward the seep or curls away from the too-bright edge of the slab. The orange blooms are my sentences: when they grow thick and lean hard south, I know the fissure is slowly shifting that way, seeping mineral-heavy water that will drown the violet moss if I do not adjust the small clay diverters I have wedged into the crack to spread the flow evenly. When they grow pale and sparse, I know the seep is slowing, that I need to scrape away a little of the silt that builds up at the crack’s mouth to let the water through. I clear the sickly pale moss that grows when the water sits too long, encourage the violet with a pinch of crushed bloom pollen, sift the silt from the shallow pools that form in the depressions between the moss cushions until the water runs clear.

I do not look at the drop at the edge of the plateau. Once, when I was new to the island, a water bead the size of my fist slipped under my bare foot as I was checking a diverter, and I slid three paces toward the lip before I caught myself on an outcrop of glassy rock. For half a second I looked down, into the swirling grey of the cloud sea below, no ground, no end, just mist that stretched down forever. My throat tightened, a sharp, unfamiliar ache, like the crack I had just found in the diverter was spreading in my chest. I stepped back, scraped a ridge of moss and mud along the lip of the slab to make the footing safer, and I have not looked toward the edge since. To look out would be to admit there is an elsewhere, and the Steppes do not admit elsewhere. The universe is the warm rock under my feet, the mist on my skin, the slow pulse of water in the cracks, the unblinking gaze of the pools.

I have been to the western Great Pool seven times to mend its surface. I do not count the cycles between, for there is no way to count time here, only accumulations: the number of times a deep *glug* of water breaks loose from a high crack and rolls down the steppe to vanish into a fissure, the number of times I adjust my diverters to match the lean of the orange blooms, the number of times I run my finger along the chipped edge of the old, unknown scraper in my niche. Each mending is the same: the Stewards gather without word, without glance, our moss-fiber garments dark with damp, our bare feet silent on the rock. We siphon the pool’s water into moss-lined basins with our reed tubes, scrape the depression floor with our glass scrapers until the black rock gleams, brush away every grain of silt, every smudge of mineral stain. Then we return the water drop by drop, watching until the surface is so still it looks solid, until the mercury sheen is unbroken, until the still point is still again. No one speaks, no one smiles, no one signals that the work is done—we just turn and walk back to our islands, to our cracks, to our moss, and the world holds its breath again.

Sometimes, when the mist is thick and the sigh of it is loud in my sinuses, I wonder what would happen if I stopped. If I left a diverter wedged wrong, if I let a patch of sickly pale moss grow over the violet, if I walked to the edge and looked down, if I cracked the Great Pool myself with a blow from my glass scraper. The thought is a small, sharp crack in my mind, a place where the water might seep through if I let it. But I do not let it. I brush the orange bloom to make it lean straight, I adjust the diverter, I wipe the silt from the edge of the small pool at the foot of my island, and I wait for the next glug of water, the next shift of the bloom, the next mending of the pool. The rock is warm under my feet. The mist is wet on my skin. The world is here, and it is enough.
