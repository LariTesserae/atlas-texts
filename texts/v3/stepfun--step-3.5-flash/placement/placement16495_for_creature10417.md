I am curled into the lee of a glacial erratic that hums with the memory of ice, half-buried in Luminmoss that glows brighter where it touches my skin. I am three kilometers northeast of the northwestern geothermal vent, where the pulse’s first faint warmth seeps up through the bedrock before it rolls across the rest of the basin, and the moss here is the brightest cobalt in the whole bowl, sated on the pulse’s earliest, most concentrated breath.

I am not plant, not animal, not mineral, not quite any of the things that grow or swim or glide through the basin. I am a slow accumulation: a concreted knot of the platinum-gray silicate dust the Silt-Wyrms excrete, bound together with the amber sap the Gnarled Tamaracks drip onto the soil, and shot through with the thin, iridescent membrane the Watchers shed when they molt, woven into a loose, fan-like veil that covers my front. I have no bones, no organs, no blood. My core is a dense cluster of the same crystalline material that grows in the Basin Stags’ geode-horns, and it thrums, slow and quiet, in perfect time with the sub-audible hum of the erratic I am anchored to.

I do not make light. The Luminmoss that carpets the gaps between my irregular, lumped surface borrows its glow from geothermal radiation and mineral mist, and I borrow it from the moss, letting it seep through the iridescent veil of my front so it can be seen. The basin’s first law holds true here: light is never created, only passed on, and I am one of its quietest couriers.

My purpose is small, almost unnoticeable, the way all the basin’s parts are when you look at them alone, but essential when you see how they fit. The basin runs on a 37-hour pulse, a wave of warm, mineral-heavy water that seeps up from the vents in the northwest, rolling across the basin floor like a slow, cold breath. The Stags count its minutes with their horns, growing a new crystal node every time they feel its first tremor, but they are large, slow, and spend most of their time half-lethargic in the moss, paying more attention to distant avalanches than the clock. The small things—the Whisperwings that feed on tamarack spores, the microscopic insects that live in the Luminmoss, the Glasswort that needs to know when to open its leaves to catch the pulse’s extra condensation—they cannot feel the pulse coming until it is almost on top of them.

That is where I come in.

I feel the pulse’s first warmth seeping through the rock three hours before it reaches the surface. I feel the subtle shift in the soil’s vibration as the warm wave moves through the Wyrms’ silk-like tunnels below. When that happens, I spread my iridescent veil just a little wider, and I glow a shade brighter, the blue of my borrowed light shifting just enough to be noticeable to anything that is paying attention. The Whisperwings stir first, their silent wings brushing the sedge tops as they move to feeding perches before the pulse peaks. The tiny moss insects wake, and the Luminmoss under their feet brightens in response. The Glasswort’s waxy leaves perk up, ready to catch the extra moisture the pulse brings.

I am not the only one. There are others scattered across the basin, each anchored to their own erratic, each holding a pocket of the pulse’s rhythm, so no part of the basin ever loses track of its slow, steady breath. The Watchers know we are here. Sometimes, when the pulse is at its peak, one of them will detach from its tamarack perch, drift down to sit on the rock next to me, its obsidian body reflecting my glow, its compound eyes checking that my rhythm is still true. It never stays longer than a few seconds, never makes a sound, then flies back up to its branch, as if confirming that the great silent machine is still running correctly.

I have lived through 312 pulses, I think. I count them the way the Stags count tremors. The erratic I am anchored to holds the memory of the ice age, when the whole basin was buried under a kilometer of ice, when the peaks were sharp and uneroded, when the pulse came every 12 hours instead of 37. Sometimes, when the mist is so thick I can barely see the tamarack tops, I tap into that old memory, and I glow a little faster for a few seconds, just to feel what it was like when the basin moved quicker. The Stags pick up on it sometimes, their horns twitching, their onyx eyes flicking toward me for a half-second before they go back to listening to the distant crack of ice in the high tarns. They do not know what I am, but they know I am part of the rhythm, and that is enough.

The basin’s hush swallows all sound, so I have never heard my own voice, if I have one. I communicate only in light and vibration, and the hush carries both perfectly, no distortion, no waste. If the ground is disturbed too harshly—if someone digs, or blasts, or steps too hard on the moss—the Wyrms burrow deeper to avoid the tremor, and the Luminmoss around the disturbance browns and dies, as if a circuit has been broken. When that happens, I glow sharp and bright, an urgent, white-edged blue that cuts through the hush, calling the nearest Stag. They feel my signal through their hooves, come to stand over the dead patch, and hum a low, counter-vibration through their geode horns that seeps into the soil, tells the Wyrms it is safe to come back up, tells the moss to start growing again.

I do not rush. I do not hunger. I do not want, except to keep the rhythm, to pass the light along, to hold the basin’s breath so it never forgets how to exhale.

The mist condenses on my iridescent veil now, the air growing heavy, tasting of sulfur and wet stone—the sure sign the pulse is only half an hour away. I feel the first faint warmth in the rock below me, and I spread my veil just a little wider, glowing a little brighter. A Whisperwing glides past, its membrane brushing my edge, and it veers off toward the sedge stands, to feed before the warmth comes. A Watcher detaches from the tamarack branch above, drifts down, perches on the erratic next to me, its eyes reflecting my steady blue glow. Somewhere in the distance, the stag lifts its head, its geode horns catching the faint first glimmer of the pulse’s light, throwing tiny rainbows across the mist. It does not look at me, but its horns hum in time with mine, and for a second, I am not just a small, lumped thing anchored to a rock. I am part of the great, slow, luminous machine, and I am exactly where I am supposed to be.
