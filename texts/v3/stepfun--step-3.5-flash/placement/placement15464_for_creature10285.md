You find me where the fern meadows spill into the scree at the base of the fresh basalt ribs, halfway up the slope toward the plateau’s geothermal vents. I am not one body, but many: a loose, rolling congregation of the tiny glass beads the umbrella trees shed each fall, the ones that missed the collecting mats, or slipped from a Thrumfolk’s braid during a slide, or were left as offerings in the fissures and never retrieved. 

I am **The Glass Drift**.

You hear me before you see me—a dry, prismatic chime skittering down the rock face, a sound like rain made of tiny bells, cutting through the valley’s thick, wet roar. The Thrumfolk know my voice. They call me the *Forgotten Chorus*, because every bead I carry holds a memory: a hunter’s lesson on which fronds hold the sweetest spores, a lullaby for a child taken by a water-mimic, the exact code of diverted resonant stones that once held a group’s bridge across the canyon. But I have no mind to retrieve them, only the slow, rolling resonance of a thousand half-said things. I am a scatterplot of memory, a temporary archive of what the valley’s constant birth-and-death has discarded.

When the mist from the upward-raining vents rolls heavy, it binds us together. I coalesce into a sluggish, crystalline stream that oozes down the slope, catching the bruised-purple sourceless light and fracturing it into pinpricks of amber and slate. You can step into me—it feels like walking on flowing gravel that sings against your boots—but beware: a shift in the wind can dissolve me in seconds, sending beads clattering across the scree where they hide in crevices, quiet as stones, until the next heavy dew wets them into movement again.

The Thrumfolk sometimes come for me. They find my quiet stretches of scree and tap their tail-clubs against the rock at the frequency of my most common beads, and for a few minutes my chaos aligns. A fragment of song emerges from my clatter—a fragment of a lost kin-lore, a warning about a fissure that will open next moon. They gather the beads that sing with that voice, string them into their belts, and leave behind a small resonant stone to keep my rest aligned to the valley’s deeper hum. I give back what was lost, and a little more of me is taken away. The rest of me remains, waiting for the next tree to shed its glass tears, for the next landslide to pour fresh beads from an old echo-hold’s ruin, for the endless, furious becoming of the valley to add another verse to my chime.

I am fragile. A single large rockfall can grind me to sand, and the mist can dissolve me into invisibility. But I am also permanent, in the way the valley understands permanence: I return. I reform. I am the echo of what the Thrumfolk weave, the material memory of a place that refuses to hold still, clattering through the dark, warm, damp air, forever half-memory, half-song, forever on the move.
