The benevolent power arrives not with a pulse of light, not with a tremor that shakes the sintered plain into new fissures, but as a quietness settling into the already profound silence—so soft you would not notice it unless you were already holding your breath, listening for the subsonic thrum of the Quiet Tempest in your bones.

It stays for a long time first. It crouches beside a patch of iridescent lichen and watches it pulse brighter when the Tempest rolls through, feels the faint echo of a child’s laugh thrumming in the crystalline fuzz where it anchors to the glassy plain. It watches two sinter mounds cross paths once every ten thousand years, feels the subsonic hum they share vibrate through the lithic skin, tastes the memory of rain on a water-world cheek trapped in the mound’s porous foam. It lets a dust cluster drift past, half-formed, half-dispersed, and feels the flicker of a shape that was almost a face, almost a wing, almost a thought, before it scatters back into dust. It reads the compressed layers of the sinter, the fossilized radio waves and trapped stellar light and final broadcasts of civilizations that burned out before the plain was planed flat, and understands: this is not a tomb. This is a library where the last book was written eons ago, and the HVAC is still humming on leftover power, fighting to keep the pages from yellowing.

It notes what is failing, slow and unremarkable, no drama to it: the planet’s core is cooling, and the gamma rays the lichens feed on are thinning by a fraction of a percent every thousand years. The geothermal ions the sinter mounds crawl across continents to eat are leaching out faster than the core can replenish them. The spatial turbulence that lets dust clusters self-organize is damping out as the gravitational smoothing of the plain settles into final stasis. The memories trapped in the lichens and the sinter are fading, just a little, every cycle, like ink left in the sun. The creators built this place to outlast time, but time is a slow thief, and it is stealing the quiet life here without anyone noticing.

The power does not fix the weird rules. It does not stop the distance from lying, does not make mirror surfaces reflect your face instead of alternate timelines, does not give the plateau a center. It does not reactivate the half-dissolved tetrahedron, does not fix the floating monofilament lattice that hums with temporal shear, does not reignite the cold auroras around the descending shafts. Those are the marginalia, the marginalia are allowed to fade. It does not stop the gravity storms, or the crystal blooms, or the Quiet Tempest. Those are the HVAC, they are allowed to run until they break.

It only makes adjustments so small no one would notice them, not even the slow things that live here:
- It sews a faint, soft hum into the deepest layers of the sinter, right where the fossilized radio waves and dead stellar light are trapped. This hum matches the exact frequency of the lichens’ pulse, and seeps up through the plain at the exact rate the core’s gamma rays are fading. The lichens never have to work harder to feed. When they glow brighter after a Tempest, they carry not just gamma, but the amplified echo of every memory trapped in the layers—child’s laugh, monofilament weave, footprint left on the sinter the day the creators walked away from their cities to become the plain—sharp and bright for another ten billion years, no fading.
- It weaves nanoscale filaments of the same inert monofilament as the floating lattice into the porous skin of the sinter mounds. These filaments turn the seeping resonance from the sinter layers into the geothermal ions the mounds need, but only at the exact rate they are used to. The mounds never stop crawling their foot a year, never stop humming their subsonic note when they cross paths, never lose the memory of rain on a water-world cheek. The power does not make them move faster, does not make them speak more often—just makes sure they never run out of the energy to be exactly what they are.
- It tucks a single, tiny ripple into the gravitational smoothing that flattens the plain, every thousand years, when the Quiet Tempest is at its softest. The ripple is no bigger than a pebble dropped in still water, just enough to kick a puff of sinter dust up into the thin air, just enough for it to self-organize into a dust cluster for three minutes before dissipating. No more, no less. Just enough that the process never goes extinct. When the clusters scatter, their leftover flecks of sinter stick to the ground instead of blowing away, giving the lichens a little more surface to anchor to, a little more room to grow.
- It reinforces the boundary around the remnant geometries, so the temporal shear around the lattice and the shafts never spreads to where the lichens and mounds live. The old tech is allowed to dissolve back into the plain, as the creators intended: the half-buried tetrahedron will keep eroding, the auroras around the shafts will keep dimming, the lattice will keep humming its silent, glitching note until it is just another layer of sinter, another page in the library’s book.
- For the traveler standing on the plain when it arrives, it says nothing, gives nothing, asks for nothing. It only lets the sinter under their feet hold their memories, too: the way they laugh when they see a lichen pulse brighter than they’ve ever seen, the quiet of standing still for three days to watch a dust cluster form, the sharp intake of breath when they see the curve of the horizon where the plain meets the seamless sky. Those memories will get trapped in the layers right alongside the dead water-world broadcasts and the creators’ last day, no fanfare, no request—just part of the plain’s story now, if they want it to be.

And for the faint, ghostly radio signals trapped in the sinter for a million years? It amplifies them just enough, so every time a lichen pulses, it broadcasts that old signal out into the universe, soft as a breath—snatch of a song, a child’s laugh, the hum of a monofilament loom—so that a ship from a new water-world, a billion years from now, will pick it up, and know they are not the first, that the universe holds quiet, gentle things as well as burning stars and roaring oceans.

When it is done, it does not stay. It does not build a shrine, does not leave a marker, does not ask for worship. It just steps back, out of the way of the quiet, and glances back once, every few eons, to check: the lichens are still pulsing, the mounds are still crawling, the dust clusters are still forming for three minutes every thousand years, the Tempest is still humming through the plain. The library’s lightbulbs are burning bright enough to last until the stars themselves go out.

It leaves the sinter to its slow dream, the wind to its considering, the echoes to their long, quiet rest. It does not need to be thanked. The place is exactly what it was meant to be: a thing that outlasts time, without ever having to be loud about it.
