The benevolent, wise power does not descend in a pillar of light, does not shake the trunks with a voice that splits stone, does not rewrite the laws of the Gilded Pit to fit some external ideal of "paradise." It has watched this city grow out of a wound in a dead world: has seen the quartz borers scrape rock from the mesa wall to build walkways, seen the smolder-stalks churn their bacterial fires to melt mineral into reinforcement, seen the gardeners nibble vine-overgrowth back to keep the mid-level platforms from collapsing under their own weight, seen the whole biomechanical thing breathe, pulse, and never stay the same for more than an hour. It loves the city not in spite of its roughness, but because of it: the clang of tools on grown-hardwood, the acrid tang of smelted quartz, the way pioneer vines already creep over a landslide before the dust has even settled.

It slips into the world as quietly as everything else here: dissolving into the faint hydrocarbon vapor rising from the deep vents, tucking itself into the spore of a common pioneer moss, drifting on the barely perceptible breeze from the surface fissures until it lands on the bark of the central cathedral trunk, and sinks into the mycelial network running under the wood. No one sees it. No one hears it. For weeks, nothing seems to change.

The first shift is so small no one thinks twice about it: a crew of stabilizer-creeper layers finishing work on a new district carved into the mesa wall after a borers’ shift notices the pale green rootlets are digging into loose quartz and glass shards in hours, not days. The new walkway is hard enough to walk on in a week, not two. The foreman grunts, says the mesa’s in a good mood this cycle, and marks the progress on his slate.

Next, a worker in a rootward sprawl forge, feeding processed quartz into a smolder-stalk’s bacterial vat, blinks. Usually, the fumes sting his eyes, burn his throat after two hours of work, leave him coughing until his shift ends. Today, he works a full six-hour rotation without a single cough, without having to pause to wipe grit from his eyes. His coworker says the same: must be a good batch of combustion bacteria. They go back to feeding the stalk, no more thought given to it.

A cycle later, a team of gardeners pruning vine-overgrowth on a mid-level platform pauses, pointing at the cut stems: tiny, silver-tinged buds they’ve never seen before, tucked where the vine meets the bark. They mark the spot, send a runner up to the high aerie groves with a sample. The scholars there grow the buds in a sealed sap pot, and a week later, they sprout into tiny, waxy leaves that bead with a thin, silvery liquid, nothing like the thick black hydrocarbon solvent that runs every part of the city. One scholar touches a drop to their tongue: cool, sweet, no oily aftertaste. They name it sky-dew, seal it in a quartz vial, and set it on a shelf to study later, no idea where it came from.

The power’s quieter work is deeper, slower, woven into the root network that ties the whole sinkhole to the mesa’s core. For generations, the old-timers have spoken of the Root-Heart’s skips: faint, uneasy thrumming that vibrates through every walkway, every trunk, every platform, a sign the mesa is settling, that the core of the whole city is stressed. After the power’s weave, the skips stop. The deep hum under the boots steadies, shifting from a vibration that feels like a warning to a low, warm thrum you feel in your bones instead of your teeth, like a lullaby. The quartz borers notice their seams are richer, that they don’t have to work as hard to pull the same amount of mineral. The solvent seeps glisten a little thicker, a little more consistent, along the trunk bark. No one connects the dots. They just say the mesa is having a good century.

The largest gift is the one no one notices until it is already there, slow enough to feel like just another part of the city’s constant change. The root network, nudged by the power, reaches up: up through the porous mesa rock, up through the sharp glass dunes of the sterile surface above, its tiny rootlets breaking down the glass into soft, mineral-rich soil, pulling the almost imperceptible moisture that condenses on the glass when the planet’s thin, cold night falls. It is not liquid water—whatever water once existed on this world is long gone, locked in rock or boiled away by the sun. It is a thin, silvery film that seeps into the broken-down glass, turning the sharp dunes into a soft, grey-green mat of pioneer growth over centuries.

A hundred years after the power first slipped into the mycelium, a young quartz borer crests the top of the mesa for the first time in living memory, expecting the blistering hot glass, the salt flats, the dead emptiness he’s been told about since childhood. Instead, he stands on soft, spongy growth that smells like the moss in the mid-canopy warrens, that feels cool under his oil-resistant boots. The slant of the planet’s weak sun gilds the edges of the tiny, waxy pioneer leaves, and when he digs his fingers into the mat, they come away wet with silvery sky-dew. He laughs, runs back down the sinkhole to tell everyone, and within a generation, the city is growing outward, not just down into the sinkhole, but up onto the mesa’s surface. They do not abandon the sinkhole—this is home, this is where the Root-Heart beats, where all their history lives—but now they have room: new districts grown on the surface, new sky-dew farms that produce sweet sap for fuel and medicine, new lookouts that let them see the whole dead world around them, not just the sinkhole walls.

The final gift is left deep in the abyss, where no one has ever gone before: the pressure is too high, the root network too dense, the hydrocarbon solvent too thick to survive more than a few minutes without specially grown pressure suits. A team of explorers in hardened stak-reed suits makes the three-day descent down the central cathedral trunk, past the lowest levels of the rootward sprawl, past the last glimmer of bioluminescence, into the dark, until they reach the core of the mesa, the Root-Heart itself.

It is a vast, pulsing chamber of glistening root-cables, glowing faint amber from geothermal heat, oozing the thick black solvent that feeds the whole city. Embedded in the center of the root network is a small, smooth quartz nodule, flecked with soft green-gold light that pulses in time with the Root-Heart’s thrum. When the lead explorer reaches out to touch it, a warm, quiet feeling spreads up their arm into their chest, and they hear, not with their ears, but in their mind, a soft hum: the same as the deep thrum under the walkways, but softer, kinder. They do not see a face, do not hear a voice, but they know, without words, that they are not alone, that the dead world above is not a prison, but a promise, that the city they built out of a wound is something worth tending to.

They bring the nodule up to the high aerie groves, set it in the central plaza where its soft glow lights up the district at night. They do not build a temple around it, do not worship it, do not carve stories of a benevolent power into the bark. They just leave it there, a quiet reminder that they are part of something bigger.

The power never reveals itself again. It slips back into the mycelial network, into the hydrocarbon vapor, into the spore drift, and watches. It watches the city grow, new platforms sprouting overnight, sky-dew harvests filling the storehouses, children born on the surface districts climbing down to the sinkhole to climb the cathedral trunks
