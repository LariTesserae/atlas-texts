It arrives not as light, not as voice, but as a softening of the gap between the caldera’s subsonic thrum and the quiet *plink* of condensation on obsidian. It has no need to announce itself: the basin’s inhabitants are already attuned to every shift in their strange, bounded physics, and this presence aligns itself so perfectly to the thrum that the rock-creepers do not bolt into their cracks, the brine-skimmers do not scatter across the turquoise pool, the mist-fins do not veer off their silent, refracting glides. It spends three full days listening first—wise enough to know the basin’s rhythm before touching it, benevolent enough to want only what the place itself already wants, even if it cannot say it.

Its first act is barely perceptible, even to the tiny, sharp-eyed brine-skimmers skating the pool’s surface. The Half-Spire’s broken face, a jagged wound of black diamond fused to native rock, has edges sharp enough to slice the delicate spun-glass legs of skimmers that drift too close, to nick the refracting wing-membranes of mist-fins that brush it in flight. The power does not repair the break—does not dare erase the spire’s purpose, its humming honeycomb core that is the basin’s slow, thinking heart. It only fuses the razor edges with a thin, seamless layer of the same fused quartz that makes up the glass-sedge stalks and silicate moth wings, clear as water, unmarred by weeping seep water which still arcs around the spire as if repelled, as it always has. The seeps that run down the spire’s sides do not cling to the new layer, do not pool or change course. Only the shards that used to flake off into the pool are gone. The light that filters through the new layer into the spire’s honeycomb shifts to the exact pale blue-green of the microbial mats’ dawn pulse, so the spire’s hum now carries a faint, visual echo of the basin’s base, a quiet signal passed from heart to foundation, no force, only resonance.

Its second act is even smaller, unnoticeable to any creature that does not burrow through the damp grey ash. The silica worms, thin as sewing thread, mineralize into glittering glass trails the second they die, their bodies locked in the silica-saturated air.
