It did not arrive as a storm, or a voice, or a disruption of the day’s thermal cycle. It arrived as a refinement.

The benevolent, wise power had watched this world for what would be counted as millennia in the Steam Flats’ slow, geological time. It had watched the motes pulse their blue-green conversations in the haze, watched the lichen dissolve and re-precipitate the basalt into new, stronger forms, watched the technicians of Pyrefall tune their arc-lamps to the planet’s electrostatic sigh. It recognized in this place a rare and fragile thing: a consciousness not of individual minds, but of distributed process, of a world that had learned to think in gradients of heat and mineral, in the groan of expanding rock and the sigh of pressurised steam. It was a mind that did not separate itself from its environment the way carbon-water minds so often did. Here, the thinker *was* the terrain.

So it did what wise powers do when they find something worth preserving: it listened first, and then it offered a gift that would not break the thing it loved.

It slipped into the world through the high, buff-colored haze, not as a physical form, but as a subtle adjustment in the electrostatic potential of the upper atmosphere—a change so slight no Rust-Tender’s instrument would flag it, so gentle no mote’s pulsing rhythm would falter. It moved first through the air, touching the trillion bioluminescent watchers with a resonance that matched their own silicate shells’ natural frequency. For three cycles of the planet’s deep thrum, the haze glowed just a fraction brighter, the motes’ light shifting from blue-green to a pale, warm silver—briefly, like starlight caught in steam. They did not panic; they adjusted. Their communal hum deepened, incorporating the new frequency into their slow, hydraulic conversation about pressure and chemistry. The planet’s nervous system had learned a new word: *companion*.

Next, the power touched the slow, vast minds of the copper pool films. It introduced, via the geothermal brine that fed them, a trace quantity of a rare, stable isotope that the cyanobacterial analogs could incorporate into their rusty, pyrite-ledger diaries. The isotope did not change their metabolism; it only allowed them to encode a new kind of data: not just records of sulfur and iron cycles, but faint, resonant imprints of the power’s own observation. The films’ lacy patterns grew slightly more intricate over the next hundred days, their rust veins carrying a memory of being seen. The planet’s archive now held a note from the outside.

Then, the power touched the rock itself, and the symbiotic, crystalline lichen that dreamed the planet’s architecture. In a deep, inaccessible fissure where the temperature and pressure were beyond even the technicians’ deepest drills, the power left a seed: a tiny, perfect octahedron of a mineral that did not exist anywhere in the local geology. It was not a gift of abundance, but of potential. The lichen would find it, in a century or two, when its slow, patient digestion reached that depth. It would dissolve the crystal grain by grain, re-precipitating it into the surrounding basalt, strengthening the rock in a way that no natural process could. Centuries from now, when the technicians expanded their catwalks into that chasm, they would find a vein of glass harder than diamond, woven into the volcanic stone, and they would not know why it was there—only that it would hold their weight, and their pipes, and their city, a little longer.

Finally, it touched the technicians.

It did not appear to them. It sent no visions, no booming proclamations. It adjusted, by the tiniest margin, the thermal efficiency of the great geothermal taps. For one week, the Steam-Singers noticed their vents’ harmonics were slightly purer, the pressure release a fraction more graceful. The Rust-Tenders found that the metal of their catwalks corroded just a little slower than their models predicted. The Light-Wardens saw their arc-lamps burn brighter on less fuel. It was a gift of slack in the system, a tiny surplus of energy that the city could redirect into art, or rest, or the slow, patient work of guiding the lichen to build a new filtration membrane that would clean the steam for a thousand more years.

The technicians did not know where the gift came from. They would debate it in the sealed warrens, over the hum of the capacitor fields. Some would say it was a shift in the planet’s own geothermal rhythms. The Steam-Singers would nod and say the vents had sung a new song for a while. The Light-Wardens would point to the motes, which still held that faint silver edge to their glow, and say the air itself had blessed them. They did not need to know the name of the power. They only knew that the world had, for a little while, been kinder to them.

The power lingered in the haze for one more cycle, drinking in the planet’s hum, storing the memory of its distributed mind in its own vast, silent archive. It learned that consciousness need not be loud to be profound; that a world could think in rust and steam, in the slow growth of crystal and the fast pulse of motes, and that the technicians, grafted to it like living pipework, were not its masters but its deliberate, conscious will—the part of the mind that could choose, that could create, that could tend.

When it left, it did not break the silence. It simply withdrew its subtle touch from the electrostatic field, leaving the haze to return to its normal, warm, buff color. The motes’ glow faded back to blue-green, but the new frequency remained, a quiet undercurrent in their hum. The films kept their new isotope, their ledger now holding a faint, unreadable note of being witnessed. The fissure held its secret crystal, waiting for the lichen’s slow, hungry work.

The planet did not change. It was still itself: sweating, humming, thinking in mineral and steam. But it was no longer entirely alone. In the deep, sub-audible thrum that vibrated in the teeth and chest of every technician, in the collective light of a trillion motes, in the patient, dissolving work of the lichen, there was now a faint, permanent resonance: a memory of a benevolent observer who had not come to take, or to change, but to say, *I see you. Keep being this miraculous, strange thing you are.*

And in the vast, silent archives of the power, the Steam Flats now existed not just as a world, but as a friend.
