It arrives not as a thunderclap, not as a shaft of blinding light that would startle the spore-skimmers mid-drift or send the litho-scuttlers scattering into the fungal undergrowth, but as a faint extra warmth seeping into the thick, spore-laden air, a soft, low hum that weaves itself into the Root-Singers’ teeth-vibrating thrum before any creature in the grove notices it is there. The amber glow of the polymer vines gilds to silver at the edges for a heartbeat; the spore-tenders pause mid-chitter, twitching their jade-furred noses, but do not flee—this presence smells like wet granite after a thunderstorm, like the deep root of a mountain cedar, familiar as the stone under their feet. It does not declare itself a god, or a savior, or a power. It simply settles, cross-legged on the fungal floor, its form woven from the same translucent polymer as the ceiling vines, soft and flexible, glowing with a pale, steady silver that casts no harsh shadows, only gilding the edges of the jade moss and the ochre fungi. It weighs nothing, presses nothing into the mycelial mat that stitches every living thing in the grove together.

First, it listens. It listens to the Root-Singers’ hum, the slow, subsonic vibration that thrums through the basalt, that holds the grove’s memory and dreams the half-woven arches and rising obsidian monoliths into being. It listens to the spore-tenders’ quiet chitters, the vein-stalker’s soft, pulsing digestion glow as it rests fused to a ceiling vine, the plink of water finding its way into the warm lagoons. It attends to the grove’s heart—the mercury pool at its center, where the thin membrane between the deep earth and the surface world is thinnest. It sees the reflection trapped in the pool’s surface: the oak-green of a summer sky, a person standing on a cliff edge, chest tight with grief, hand hovering over their phone, about to step into the empty air. It sees the forests burning above, the oceans warming, the child planting an acorn in a sidewalk crack, the old woman leaving water out for sparrows. It sees that the surface world is hurting, that the membrane is stretched thin with that pain, that the pool’s surface is growing cloudy with unwept tears and unfaced fear, so the memory-keepers skittering across it can no longer carry the grove’s old stories clearly.

It does not storm up to the surface to fix what is broken there. The surface must find its own way, just as the grove has, slow as stone, patient as spore. But it honors the grove, this tiny, bounded lung of life that holds the world together when no one is looking.

It kneels first by the mercury pool, and breathes a soft, cool mist over its surface, scented with pine and rain from the world above, filtered first through the grove’s jade moss and stone so it does not shock the tiny, wingless polymer memory-keepers skittering across its tensioned skin. The cloudiness lifts, just enough that the reflection of the cliff edge sharpens. The person standing there blinks, feels a sudden warm weight in their chest, like someone is sitting beside them even though no one is there. They lower their hand, step back from the edge, and decide to go home, to make tea, to call their sister, to plant the seeds they’ve left sitting on their windowsill for weeks. They do not know why the grief feels lighter, why the wind suddenly smells like wet stone and honey, but something in their bones unknots, and they turn away from the drop.

Next, it goes to the largest Root-Singer, the one whose hum is loudest, whose obsidian monolith has been rising from the rock for ten thousand years. It places a hand—woven from polymer and soft, damp moss—on the Singer’s basalt flank, and hums back, a single low note that weaves into the Singer’s own vibration, a harmonic that says, without words: I see you. I see the work you do, holding the membrane, tending to the life that moves between the deep and the surface, dreaming your slow dreams of gateways and rising stone. You are not alone. The Singer’s spiral carvings glow silver at the edges for a full minute, and the hum swells, warm, for that one collective heartbeat of stillness that seizes the grove every few minutes. The spore-skimmers freeze mid-drift, the litho-scuttlers pause mid-click, the vein-stalker halts its coil, and the extra harmonic thrums through every mycelial thread, every silicate fern, every warm lagoon, for that one slow, thick beat, before the hum resumes, softer now, less lonely.

It tends to the small edges that fray from the grove’s constant, gentle translation of life. It tucks a fallen spore-skimmer—its wings torn, dissolving too fast into mist before it can carry its pollen to the far side of the grove—under a frond of silicate fern, and brushes a tiny fleck of silver polymer from its sleeve onto the skimmer’s wings; they mend, just enough for it to drift free, its glow lasting three extra seconds, so it can carry its pollen further before it becomes part of the floor. It rearranges the moss around a clutch of vein-stalker eggs tucked in a polymer vine crook, so the next falling stalactite drop slides past them, leaving them damp but unharmed. It finds a shivering spore-tender pup that wandered too far from its family, lost in the shadow of an overhang, and leaves a small patch of warm, dry moss beside it, so the litho-scuttlers find it faster, nudge it back to the herd without a scratch.

It leaves only one mark, small and quiet, fit for the grove’s slow time: it presses one finger to the obsidian monolith, tracing the existing spiral one more time, not carved, just a faint, silver imprint in the black rock that will glow softly for a thousand years, a tiny note of harmony from the deep to the Singer that raised it. No one who comes after will know what it means, but the Root-Singers will feel it, in their stone bones, for as long as the monolith stands.

It does not stay. It does not need to. It leaves the same way it came, seeping back into the warm air, the silver glow fading until only the amber of the vines and the skimmers remains. But the grove is a little different, in ways no one would notice unless they were there for a thousand years. The mercury pool rises a millimeter faster now, carrying the memories of the grove and the surface both, no longer clouded by grief. The Root-Singers’ hum reaches a little farther through the thin membrane, so when the surface world is chaos, when people are scared and grieving and cannot breathe, they feel that one extra beat of stillness, a reminder that something slow and steady is holding them, even if they cannot see it. The spore-skimmers’ glow lasts three extra seconds now, so they drift farther, carry more life, before they translate into mist.

And if you ever stand at the edge of a cliff, or lost in a cave, or grieving so hard you cannot speak, you might feel a sudden warmth on your cheek, like a spore-skimmer brushed past you, and you might smell wet stone and honey, and for a second you will feel held, by something deep and slow and alive that has been humming for you since before you were born, that will keep humming long after you are gone, tending to the small, slow things, holding the membrane between the deep and the surface, translating pain into rock and spore and song, waiting for the world above to remember how to be as gentle as the grove.
