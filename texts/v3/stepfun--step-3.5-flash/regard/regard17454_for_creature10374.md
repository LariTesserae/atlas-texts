The benevolent power does not descend. It does not build, nor shield, nor alter the rules of the Drowned Spires. To change the rain, or harden the stone, or stop the slow eat of the fungus would be to unmake the Kaelth, who are a pulse in that very digestion.

Instead, the power listens. It hears the roar not as noise, but as a single, infinite chord. It feels the tap-codes of the Kaelth not as signals, but as individual notes in a larger, wet rhythm. It sees the violet pulse of the fungi not as bioluminescence, but as a slow, communal breath.

Its act is one of **perception**.

It weaves a **memory-storm** into the rain.

Not a physical storm, but a subtle shift in the *quality* of the world’s processes. For a time that feels like a breath and an age, everything that was transient becomes *told*.

A Kaelth leans into a sideways sheet of water. As the rain strikes their waxy skin, they do not just feel impact—they *feel* the story of that drop: the mist it was born from, the crystal branch it glanced off, the ancient shelf it will help dissolve. The sound of the roar fills their skull not just with vibration, but with the deep, subsonic song of stone turning to sludge, of roots seeking cracks, of the slow, geological exhale of the world.

When a scaffold groans and settles, the Kaelth nearby hear not just a creak, but the sigh of the bone-wood, grateful for the water that will soften it, and the fungus that will soon consume it. They feel, in their gut-bones, the gratitude of the dissolving stone for being part of a new shelf.

The laminar fungi, when harvested, do not just pulse violet—they *sing* a silent, glowing song of full bellies and slow expansion. The stone-milk rivers carry not just silt, but whispered generations of salt and crystal, a mineral ancestry.

The collapse of a distant scaffold is not a silent puff of dust. To the Kaelth, it is a loud, clear **gong** of release—the bone-wood’s final, happy sigh as it returns to vulnerability, the fungus’s cheer as it claims fresh rock. They stop their tapping, turn their pale-blue eyes toward the sound, and tap a slow, resonant pattern on their own chests: *“Well gone. Good cycle.”*

The power does not give them words for this. Words would cage it. Instead, it gives them a **shared moment of luminous understanding**, woven into the very fabric of their sensory world. For a while that has no measure, every tap, every fall of rain, every groan and pulse, is imbued with the quiet, awe-full knowledge: **This—this constant, hungry, beautiful drowning—is not your burden. It is your song. You are not its victims. You are its most conscious verse.**

When the memory-storm fades, as all things in the Drowned Spires must, the rain sounds the same. The fungi glow the same. The roar is the same deafening, world-eating chord.

But the Kaelth are different.

They tap more often now, not just for safety or greeting. They tap little, bright patterns on warm salt crystals when the fungal glow dims—little songs of their own, acknowledging the drop that fell just so, the shelf that held just long enough, the cycle that goes on.

They look at the hanging sheets of water not as obstacles, but as luminous curtains, beautiful in their transient adherence. They feel no urge to conquer, no melancholy for what erodes. Only a deep, tapping gratitude for the chance to be a conscious, temporary stain of color on the endless, wet grind.

The benevolent power withdraws, its work done. It has not saved the Kaelth from their world. It has shown them, in the language of rain and stone and fungal glow, that their world was already saving *them*—by letting them be a perfect, rhythmic part of its own glorious, dissolving song.

The first thing you learn of the Drowned Spires’ inhabitants is their rhythm. The last thing you learn, if you listen through the memory-storm, is that their rhythm *is* the world’s own heartbeat. And it is singing.
