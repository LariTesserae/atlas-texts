The benevolent, wise power does not descend in fire or voice. It does not rearrange the sky or rewrite the mycelial code. It has watched slow worlds rise and fall too many times to mistake grandeur for care. First, it listens.

It tunes itself to the sub-audible thrum of the continent-spanning mycelial network first, letting the vibration hum through its own form until it can tell the difference between the grief of the dead fungal cities ground to dunes, the quiet focus of a Tender of the Violet Lichen pruning their patch, the faint, lingering echo of the old human world that seeps through the reality breakdown at the plateau’s edges. It feels the weight of the half-buried turbine blade a traveler left an offering for three days prior, the stutter of a geothermal gear that a gear-beetle couldn’t quite hold steady during a reality flicker, the faint, unspoken loneliness of a new mycoarbor sprout struggling to take root in the empty plain’s nursery soil.

It does not fix. It tends.

First, it weaves thin, bioluminescent silver threads into the frayed edges of the mycelial network where the reality breakdown is sharpest—not to erase the flicker of rusted iron walls, the human faces that form in steam, the momentary loosening of gravity, but to anchor them. Now, when a steam vent breathes out the shape of a human face from the old world, it lingers for a full minute instead of a split second, soft and unhurried, and the faint echo of gratitude the old world left behind seeps into the vent’s metal, so the Mycocenes who leave spore offerings at its base feel a warm, quiet hum under their fingers instead of a cold, random glitch. The breakdown is still there, still part of the plateau’s memory, but it no longer frays the present. It becomes an archive, soft to touch.

Next, it turns to the empty plains between the three great Mycoarbor cities: the nursery, the graveyard, the shared air buffer. New mycoarbor spores land there by the thousand, but the plain’s silence is deep, and the reality breakdown’s stutter often makes young sprouts wither before their root systems can catch. The power does not force the spores to grow. It grows thin, almost invisible mycelial threads from the nearest city’s network to each new sprout, so fine they do not disrupt the plain’s function as a buffer, just give the sprout a faint, steady pulse to grow toward, like a lullaby hummed under the soil. Over the next few generations, the Mycocenes will not notice the change at first—they will only think the plain has grown kinder, that new sprouts in the buffer zone take root a little more often, their first lichen patches glowing a little brighter against the spongy bone-colored earth.

It turns to the old relics half-swallowed by the plain’s moss next: the cart-sized rusted gears, the broken turbine blades, the fragments of old industrial bone the Mycocenes revere as ancestor remains. The power does not restore them, does not turn them to wood or leaf, but coaxes the conductive fungus that grows on every surface of the plateau to weave into the metal’s cracks, binding rust to living fiber so the relics do not crumble to dust. The metal stays cold, stays hard, but now it hums with a faint echo of the mycelial network. When a Mycene Keeper of the Long Routes leaves a spore brick at a half-buried gear, they do not just feel dead metal under their hand—they feel the ghost of the wind that once turned the blade, the callus on the human finger that forged the gear, soft and warm, as if the old world is saying thank you.

It tends to the small lives, too, in the quiet way a gardener tends to pest control without poison. The gear-beetles that keep the geothermal nodules’ interlocking seed-pod gears from jamming often slip when the reality breakdown makes the gears flicker to rusted iron for a split second. The power does not change the gears, but over three beetle generations, it grows a tiny layer of conductive fungus into the beetles’ silica shells, so their micro-suction cups hold steady even when the gear flickers. The beetles do not know why their shells have changed. They only know they do not slip as often, that the nodules’ clank-hum stays steady, that the city avenues stay warm through the cool mist.

It does not speak to the Mycocenes. It does not give them individual names, does not disrupt their symbiotic connection to the cities they tend, their lives measured in the slow pulse of the network. But when a Tender of the Violet Lichen is pruning their patch late at night, when the bioluminescent glow is soft and the mycelial thrum is low, they will feel a faint, second warmth in their filaments, under the network’s pulse, like a shadow standing beside them. They will not tell the others, because the network has no words for it. They will only prune a little more gently, leave a little extra spore slurry for the lichen to drink, and feel, for the first time in their short, busy lives, that they are not just a mobile organ of the city they tend—they are seen.

The changes are slow, so slow no single Mycene will live to see them all take hold. The three great Mycoarbor cities will still take centuries to fuse their root systems, their canopies still merging inch by agonizing inch as they always have. The reality breakdown will still flicker at the edges, still show old skyscrapers and sunlit forests for split seconds. The empty plains will still be nursery, graveyard, buffer, still hold the melancholy dunes of dead cities ground to dust.

But one day, a Keeper of the Long Routes, old, their mycelial filaments silvered at the tips, will walk the plain between the second and third city during a thick mist, when the breakdown is strong and the plain flickers to an old-growth forest under a golden sun. They will stumble, their foot sinking into the spongy earth, scared for a second that they will be lost in the flicker, like the young Mycocenes who sometimes wander into the breakdown and never return. But the thrum under their feet will go soft, warm, steady, and the mist will clear just enough for them to see a new mycoarbor sprout ten feet away, its first lichen patch glowing violet, and the half-buried turbine blade they carried an offering for resting right at its roots.

They will set down their spore sample, place their hand on the cold metal, and for one second they will not feel the plateau’s damp, or the mycelial thrum, or the mist on their skin. They will feel sun on their face, wind in their hair, a human voice laughing soft and warm, like a neighbor greeting them from across a fence. Then the feeling will fade, and they will be just a Mycene standing in the mist, holding a rusted blade, the sprout pulsing in time with their own filaments.

They will leave an extra spore brick at the blade’s base, tuck a shard of hardened resin under the sprout to hold it steady against the wind, and keep walking toward the third city. They will not tell anyone about the laugh. The mycelial network will already know, will tuck the memory into its slow, deep archive, right next to the memory of the hand that forged the blade, the wind that turned it, the first Mycene who ever left an offering at its base.

The power will not stay. It will move on to other slow, quiet worlds that need tending, leaving no trace but the soft silver threads in the mycelium, the conductive fungus on the beetles’ shells, the faint, warm hum under the Mycocenes’ feet when they work. It does not need worship. It does not need thanks. It only wants the world to hold its balance a little longer, its memories a little softer, its slow, patient growth a little less lonely.
