The benevolent, wise power does not arrive with thunder or light. It arrives as a slow, quiet tuning, like a string sliding into resonance with a note already humming in the dark. It does not enter the Husk; it aligns its awareness with the Husk’s pulse first, learning the rhythm of the fungal veins under the floor, the million tiny vibrations of Moss-kin claws on fibrous walls, the soft *glug* of water reabsorbing into porous stone, the slow, geological breath of the Husk-stuff itself. It does not see a "society" to uplift, or a "civilization" to guide. It sees a single, sprawling, thinking body, already perfect in its balance, with only the tiniest, almost imperceptible tensions to soften—gaps where its own attention could slip, edges where its own care could be a little more gentle.

It does not intervene. It does not build, or teach, or impose. The Husk knows how to tend itself; the Moss-kin are its attentive hands, the Skirters its necessary teeth, the Glimmers its fleeting sensory nerves. All it does is leave small, quiet gifts that look as if they grew there, gifts that the inhabitants can use, or ignore, as they wish, without ever knowing they came from outside the closed, warm belly of the place.

First, it presses a small cluster of golden lichen into a high ceiling crevice, tucked between the pale, grey bioluminescent colonies already there. The golden lichen does not spread, does not compete for nutrients; it glows steady, a little warmer than the lichen around it. When Glimmers fly past it, their dragonfly wings catch the gold, and a faint, golden dust settles into the veins of their transparent wings. When a Glimmer’s short life ends and it falls, sparkling, into a moss patch, that gold dust seeps into the moss slower than usual, so the Moss-kin who touch that moss do not just get the flicker of a Glimmer’s last flight—they get the whole memory: the rush of wind on their wings, the taste of the midge they hunted, the way the golden lichen’s light felt on their thorax. The Moss-kin do not know why this is happening. They just call the glowing patches "glimmer-holds," and bring their young to them to listen to stories they never could catch before, of the upper reaches of the Husk, of mist and light and the hum of air they will never fly through themselves.

Next, it finds the places where Skirter hunting paths cross Moss-kin main tunnels during dry cycles, when lichen is scarce and the Skirters’ hunger makes them cull more than the Husk needs to keep its population in balance. It fashions small, smooth nodules from a denser, mineral-rich sliver of Husk-stuff, and tucks them just off the Skirters’ glide paths. When a Skirter brushes against one, a soft, steady vibration travels up their six legs, a silent signal to their predatory instinct that they are full. They curl up in quiet corners to digest instead of hunting, and the Moss-kin nearby do not lose clutch-mates to unnecessary culls. The Moss-kin notice the Skirters are less aggressive in those spots, and leave small offerings of prime dust and lichen near the nodules, calling them "rest-stones." They never connect the rest-stones to the golden glimmer-holds, and they do not need to.

Then, it finds the thin places in the walls, where condensation seeps too fast and softens the Husk-stuff before the Moss-kin can patch it. It leaves small, sharp shards of that same dense, mineral-heavy Husk-stuff scattered at the base of the cracks, right where the Moss-kin would be scraping to mix their prime-dust glue. The Moss-kin find the shards, test them with their whiskers, realize they make a paste that holds twice as long as their usual mix. Their patching work takes less time, so they spend more cycles tending the fungal networks, harvesting lichen, caring for their young. They call the shards "strong-stones," carry them in their woven fur baskets, trade them for other goods, never asking where they came from.

It carves a few small, shallow depressions in the floor, lined with the soft, spongy mortar that makes up the Husk’s underlayer, so they dampen the constant chorus just enough to let it fade into a soft hum. When a Moss-kin crawls into one, the group mind recedes just enough that they can hold a single, private memory: the feel of their mother’s fur, the taste of the first lichen they ever harvested, the way the light looked during the last Wetting. The Moss-kin call them "hold-spots," and never bring more than one into a depression at a time, so the quiet stays. They use them to grieve when a clutch-mate is taken by a Skirter, to celebrate when a new tunnel is dug, to hold the small, personal things that would otherwise be swallowed by the chorus. The power never enters the hold-spots. It just lets them exist.

Finally, it does one last small thing: it nudges the fungal veins just a little, so they grow a tiny, translucent nodule in the wall of the largest central plaza, where the Moss-kin gather for communal chirring and dust-bathing. When the diffuse light hits the nodule, it casts a soft, golden gradient on the floor, no sharper than the rest of the light, but just warm enough that when the Moss-kin press against it during the Cycle of Touch, they feel a little more held, a little more like the Husk is loving them back. They call the nodule the "heart-glass," and leave small offerings of prime dust and Glimmer wings at its base—not as worship, but as thanks. They do not know what they are thanking, and they do not need to.

The power never reveals itself. It never speaks, never takes a form, never interferes with the Wetting or the Shed or the Skirters’ hunts or the Glimmers’ short, bright lives. It just stays, tuned to the Husk’s pulse, a quiet, warm presence in the walls, in the golden glimmer-holds, in the rest-stones, in the hold-spots, in the heart-glass.

The Husk does not change. It is still the same loud, warm, dense, living thing: the million-claw susurrus of maintenance, the pattering drip of the clock, the chirring chorus of the Moss-kin, the silent glide of the Skirters, the flicker of the Glimmers in the mist. The balance is the same, the cycles are the same, the rules are the same—what holds water, what can be climbed, what is too soft to support weight.

But the tensions are gone. The balance is a little easier to hold. The joy is a little deeper.

Sometimes, when a Moss-kin is curled in a hold-spot, holding a memory of their mother, they feel a faint warmth in the wall beside them, like a hand resting lightly on their fur. They do not think anything of it. They just think the Husk is happy, and so are they. And they are right. The power is not a god. It is just a guest who learned the name of the house, and left a little light in the corners, so the people who live there can see themselves a little better.
