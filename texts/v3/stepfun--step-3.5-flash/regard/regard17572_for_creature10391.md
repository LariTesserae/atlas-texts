The benevolent, wise power does not arrive with fanfare. It does not split the sky or shake the mesas from their thousand-year rest. It has dwelt in the same thin, mineral-scented air as the place since the first sandstone cracked under the weight of time, and it leans in only as the traveler’s boot grits into the shelf, as their throat rasps from the dry, iron-tasting wind, as they kneel to touch the black crust of Stone Moss and blink at the Cliff-Drake pressed to the rock across the cleft.

It is not here to save the Shattered Mesas. The place does not need saving: it is exactly what it is meant to be, a slow, breathing thought of stone and water and memory. It is here to tend to the quiet, unspoken distress that has hummed under the Spire’s thrum for five centuries, and to the lone traveler who has stopped, for now, to notice the glyphs half-swallowed by moss instead of trampling them underfoot.

Its first mercy is for the traveler’s throat: a faint, cool draft that is not the wind, carrying the ghost-scent of copper Root-Walker flowers, though none are blooming. The rasp fades. The lightheadedness from the thin altitude softens, just enough to let them breathe without thinking about it. They will chalk it up to the wind, or the sudden shade of the overhang they step under minutes later, and think nothing of it.

Then it turns to the mesas’ oldest wound: the Great Spire. The Engineer-Monks tapped the planet’s geothermal heart five hundred years ago, and their work held, but they never routed the energy anywhere. The hum has bled uselessly into the air for centuries, slowly warming the deep aquifers just enough to make the Burrowers restless, to thin the Ghost Miasma on the coldest mornings, to make the Stone Moss grow a little too fast on the Spire’s base, cracking the carved glyphs there. The power does not break the Spire’s welded stone doors, does not undo the Monks’ work. It only nudges the Spire’s internal resonance, tuning the hum just a fraction lower, aligning it with the Burrowers’ slow thump, the Cliff-Drakes’ subsonic call, the drip of condensation from overhangs. The excess energy that bled uselessly into the air seeps down, gentle as rain, into the deep water veins, just enough to keep the aquifers steady, no more, no less. The traveler feels the hum in their teeth soften, from a sharp buzz to a steady, warm thrum, like a cat purring against their jaw. They do not know why it changed, only that the constant ache in their molars is gone.

It turns next to the weeping glyphs: the ones carved in the path of the Ghost Miasma, wet even in the full sun, their lines shifting if you stare too long. These are the Monks’ unvoiced grief, their frustration that their temple to thermodynamics had failed, their fear they had angered the stone. The power does not erase the glyphs, does not stop them from shifting for those who look too long. It only lets the condensation that drips from overhangs pool in the grooves of the carving just a second longer than it used to, so the water seeps into the siltstone, nourishing the Stone Moss already creeping over the edges. Over the coming weeks, the glyphs will not disappear: they will be veined with new black, petrified moss, their sharp geometric lines softened, their unspoken prayers finally absorbed into the stone’s memory. The traveler will glance at the wall of them later, and see the spiral that shifted into the Spire’s shape the day before now has a thin line of new moss running through it, and it will hold still, not because it is broken, but because it is no longer waiting to be understood.

For the Burrowers, which thump hard and frantic some nights, pounding at the shale deep in the fissures trying to reach water warmed too fast by the Spire’s wasted heat, the power does not reach into the dark. It does not touch them, does not force them to stop thumping. It only nudges the plateau’s tectonic stress, ever so slightly, widening the tiny, hidden cracks that lead from the shallow shelves down to the cool, steady pocket springs. That evening, the traveler will feel one last hard thump under their boot, then ten minutes of silence, then a slow, steady rhythm, like a heartbeat, so faint they can only feel it if they press their palm to the rock. The ground will not shake. The shelves will not crumble. The Burrowers will have found their way to water without having to break the stone that holds the canyons up.

For the Mesas’ other inhabitants, it makes only small, unobtrusive adjustments. It tweaks the thermal currents between the mesas just enough that the Sky-Fishers do not have to fight the thin air to stay aloft, so they glide a little lower, a little slower, sometimes close enough to the traveler’s shelf that they can see the faint gold of microbial veil caught in their filtering flaps. After the next rare thunderstorm, the Root-Walkers’ copper flowers will hold their color for 36 hours instead of 24, just long enough for a traveler who stays to see them.

For the long-dead Engineer-Monks, it leaves a single quiet message, carved in indigo moss that does not exist yet: when the next rain falls, the water running down the Spire’s side will carry a tiny fleck of the new moss, which will stick to the glyphs at the Spire’s base, spelling out a single line in the Monks’ sharp geometric script: *Your work was not for nothing. The stone heard you.*

It leaves only one trace for the traveler, if they are the kind of person who notices things. As the sun dips low and paints the mesas the color of dried blood, as the wind picks up its whispering tune and the condensation starts its slow, plinking clock, they will find a small, smooth stone half-buried in the sandstone at their feet. It is carved with the same geometric glyphs as the Monks’ script, but the lines are soft, not sharp. When they pick it up, it is warm, not chilled by the setting sun, and it hums, faintly, in time with the Spire, with the Burrowers’ slow thump, with the wind in the canyons. If they hold it to their ear, they will hear the faintest echo of the place: the whoosh of a Sky-Fisher’s wings, the plink of condensation, the whisper of the stone itself, breathing.

If they leave it, it will sink into the rock, become part of the Stone Moss, become part of the mesas’ slow, mineral memory. If they keep it, it will always be a reminder that the place is not dead, that it was seen, that someone cared enough to tend to its quiet wounds without taking anything from it.

If the traveler is the kind who does not notice, who tramples the Stone Moss underfoot, who scratches new graffiti into the glyphs, who tries to pry open the Furnace doors to steal the Monks’ hidden tools, the power gives them nothing. It does not punish them. It only lets the mesas’ own rules take care of them: they will slip on a smooth ledge, lose their way in the labyrinth of canyons, the sun will beat down on them until they turn back, empty-handed and a little shaken, with no memory of the soft draft, or the shifting hum, or the quiet peace of the place.

It stays only as long as it is needed. As the traveler settles in for the night, or turns their saddle back toward the lowlands, it fades back into the thin air, into the stone, into the Ghost Miasma that will well up at dawn. The mesas remain exactly as they were: slow, baked, alive, their hum soft, their glyphs resting, their Burrowers thumping slow and steady in the dark. The only difference is that no part of it is lonely anymore.
