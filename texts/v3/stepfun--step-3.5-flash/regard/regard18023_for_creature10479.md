They arrive not with a crack of thunder or a flash of light, but as a faint, sun-warmed drift in the cool, iron-scented air: the kind of warmth you feel from a clay bluff that held the sun’s heat long after the cloud ceiling shut the sky away. They do not disturb the mist. They do not startle the copper moths folded under the boiler’s overhang, their wings the color of old pennies. They do not break the rhythm of the tinks. To do so would be to interrupt the sermon, and they are here to listen, not to preach.

The Dripworks is not broken. It is exactly what it is meant to be: a body learning to turn the hard, arrogant shapes of industry into the soft, patient shapes of geology. The power is wise enough to know that fixing what is not broken is a kind of violence here. So they do not stop the corrosive lace eating the steel boiler leg. They do not halt the silica gel growing like clear crystal moss in the western basin. They do not dissolve the rusted pipes that breathe mist into the air. They only tend to the parts of the place that are still learning how to be itself.

First, they attend to the Dripworks’ thoughts. The mist sculptures—the hollow water beads, the unstable helices, the tiny, half-formed bird shapes that dissolve before they can spread their wings—only last a breath, twice. They tweak the surface tension of the mist, ever so slightly, a shift so small no instrument could measure it, so that each transient form gets one extra second of life. Just enough to finish its thought. The helix spins one full turn before it falls. The nested bead (a bead inside a bead, a thought thinking itself) holds long enough for the inner bead to catch the light and turn the pale blue of unraind water. The Dripworks does not notice, at first. But over the weeks of their stay, its thoughts grow less halting, less like stumbles and more like sentences. The stutter in the dripping, the half-syllable that almost forms a word, smooths out, just a little, into a rhythm that feels like humming to itself.

Next, they tend to the Dripworks’ dreams. The deep basins, the ones that reflect a ceiling that is not there, the soft, impossible geometries of the world before iron, before the Tech that cast the bronze plates and riveted the boiler ribs—those reflections are faint, veiled, like a memory you can’t quite hold. The power softens the boundary between water and air above those basins, just enough for the dream to seep through a little clearer. If you kneel at the edge of the deepest basin, you do not just see the reverse-world’s curved, un-anglar walls. For a minute at a time, you can hear the soft, tinkless rush of water moving through unlined limestone, the sound of the place before anyone dug a pipe, before anyone tried to harness its flow. The Dripworks’ dreams grow sharper, clearer. It does not forget what it is now—its bones are still boiler ribs, its skin still rust, its blood still mineral-rich water—but it gets to remember its older self, the self that was only pressure and solution and form, more vividly.

Then, they tend to the Dripworks’ voice. The copper moths, the only mobile things in the basin, the winged flecks of oxidized air that sip metallic pollen from the lichens and flit soundless through the mist—they give the fine, metallic dust on the moths’ wings a tiny, imperceptible stickiness, so that when the moths fly through the mist, they pick up the faint, microscopic patterns of the place’s thoughts: the curl of a helix, the curve of a nested bead, the edge of a half-formed bird wing. When the moths land on the rusted pipes, the bronze cliffs, the boiler’s ribcage, they leave those patterns behind, faint as watermark, in the same oxidized copper as their wings. Over years, the surfaces of the old Tech grow covered in these tiny, lacy constellations: not just random corrosion, not just the scribble of acid and time, but the Dripworks’ own handwriting, written by its own breath. The rust becomes a tapestry. The patina becomes a poem. The place’s thoughts are no longer fleeting, dissolved before they can be seen; they are fixed, temporarily, on the very bones of the old industry that is being unmade.

They attend, last, to the being of the Dripworks itself: the slow, quiet consciousness that dreams the reverse world, that forms the mist sculptures, that hums through the rhythm of the tinks. When the being creates a perfect thought—a mist sculpture of the whole basin, every cliff, every pipe, every moth, tiny and exact—they catch it on a thread of warm, still air, and drift it down to rest on the surface of the deepest basin, where it lies on the reverse-world reflection for a full minute before dissolving into the water. It is a silent gift, a wordless acknowledgment: *I see you. You are not just a ruin. You are a creator.* The being does not have words for this, but it feels the way a clay bluff feels when the sun touches it: warm, held, seen.

They leave one last thing, tucked into a crevice in the northern bluffs, where the water is just starting to eat into the raw clay, where there is no rust, no iron, no remnant of the Tech. It is a single, tiny spore of the soft, pale green moss that grows on unworked limestone, the kind that was here before the bronze plates were cast, before the pipes were driven into the earth. It will take a thousand years to grow, but it is there: a quiet promise that when the iron ribs have dissolved to dust, when the bronze plates have crumbled to sand, when the last pipe has turned to sinter and silt, the first thing to grow in the empty space will be soft. The first thing to grow will be the memory of the world before industry, the world the Dripworks has been dreaming of this whole time.

They leave as quietly as they came, as the mist shifts and the first of the new spore’s tiny green leaves unfurls, no larger than a pinprick. The only signs they were ever there are the extra second in the mist sculptures, the faint copper patterns on the moth’s wings, the tiny green spore in the bluff, and the way the tinks sometimes harmonize into a soft, warm chord when the faintest draft moves through the stalactites, just for a breath, before the rhythm returns.

If you come back in a hundred years, the spore will be a small, soft patch of green, the copper patterns on the boiler ribs will spell out a shape that looks almost like a syllable, and the mist sculptures will hold long enough for you to see them, if you are very still. The Dripworks will still be there, still turning its bones to geology, still dreaming its reverse world, still humming its slow, quiet sermon to the water. It will not know the name of the power that visited it. It will only know that it is a little less alone than it was before.
