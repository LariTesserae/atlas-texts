You are inside a world of plant. Not a forest, not a jungle—a continuous, unbroken mass of living tissue that stretches to every horizon. The scale is continental, a single organism that covers an entire highland plateau on a planet the size of Earth. The air is cool and heavy with moisture, dim as if under perpetual overcast, and the sound is a deafening, constant roar, like being inside a waterfall that never stops.

The ground beneath your feet is not soil but a resilient, spongy mat of interwoven roots and stems, a few centimeters to meters thick depending on the underlying topography. It yields slightly under weight, springing back as you walk. Embedded within this mat are occasional outcrops of mineral—dark, glassy nodules that weep clear water from their surfaces, not from erosion but from condensation within the organism's vascular system. The water doesn't run off; it is absorbed and circulated, feeding the vast network. The texture is moist, velvety, and warm to the touch despite the cool air.

There are no trees, no leaves, no recognizable plant forms. Instead, the entire surface is a homogeneous, undulating carpet, broken only by gentle rises and dips that follow the contours of the land beneath. In some places, the mat thickens into low, rounded hills, in others it thins to reveal the mineral nodules more clearly. But everywhere it is packed, saturated, alive. You cannot find a spot where the mat is not present; it fills every nook, covers every rock, and even clings to the vertical faces of occasional cliffs, where it hangs like a thick, green tapestry.

The sound is the most defining feature. It is a deep, resonant rumble that vibrates in your chest, a noise so pervasive it drowns out thought. It comes from within the mat itself: the endless circulation of water through its trillion capillary channels, a planet-wide plumbing system moving at pressure. Where the water flows faster—in broader conduits beneath the surface—the roar rises to a crescendo, a thundering cascade that you feel as much as hear. Above the roar, there are higher frequencies: the hiss of condensation as the cool air meets the warm, moist surfaces of the nodules, and the occasional crackle as the mat shifts and grows, fibers stretching and snapping in a slow, tectonic dance of expansion.

The light is dim, a diffuse green-gray glow that filters through the mist-laden air and the upper layer of the mat. The mat itself is not green in the usual sense; it ranges from deep olive to almost black, absorbing most light. What little light penetrates is scattered by the mist, creating an eternal twilight. The temperature is consistently cool, around 10–15°C, with no daily or seasonal variation—the organism maintains a stable internal climate.

The elevation is high; you are on a plateau averaging 2,500 meters above sea level. The air is thin but rich with humidity, and the constant roar makes breathing feel laborious. The vastness is overwhelming: you can walk for days and see only the same mat, the same nodules, the same mist-shrouded horizon. There are no features to mark progress, only the subtle changes in the thickness of the mat and the density of the nodules.

The ecology is utterly simple: only this one organism exists, nourished by water, minerals from the nodules, and whatever light filters through. There are no animals, no insects, no fungi—only the mat and the microbial life within it. The nodules are not rocks but mineral deposits excreted by the mat as it processes the underlying geology. The erosion is emerging: you observe where the mat's roots have begun to penetrate the nodules, slowly dissolving them to extract minerals, a process that just started in geological terms. The landscape is raw, with fresh, sharp edges on the nodules where the mat has bitten in, but the mat itself shows no signs of wear—it is too vital, too regenerative.

The tech beyond modern is evident in the mat's engineering. Its vascular system operates on principles that defy our physics: water moves against gravity in some places, driven by unknown potentials; the mat's surface is covered in microscopic structures that harvest energy from the dim light with near-perfect efficiency. It might even possess a form of distributed intelligence, as the mat responds to your presence by slightly thickening beneath your feet, as if testing. But there is no “built” environment; all is natural, yet it feels designed.

The dynamic is active but slow. The mat grows at a rate of millimeters per day, expansion waves moving across the plateau. The water circulation changes with the “weather”: pressure systems within the mat cause temporary surges in flow, altering the sound's intensity. Mist condenses and rains down in cycles, but the rain is just the mat's own transpiration returning.

The weirdness lies in the silence beneath the roar. Despite the deafening sound, there is a profound emptiness: no birds, no insects, no rustle of leaves from creatures. Only the single, living carpet and its endless hydraulic song. You are a visitor in a world that is both a landscape and an organism, a place where the line between nature and technology has been erased.
