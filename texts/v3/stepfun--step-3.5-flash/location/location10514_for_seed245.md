You stand on a plain that is not a plain. It is a manufactured thing, a vast, low-lying basin of artificial geology, its scale so immense that the curvature of the world is perceptible as a gentle, sickly tilt to the dim, sulfur-hued sky. The air is hot, wet, and still—a hot, wet still that feels less like an absence of wind and more like a held breath. It is the still of a system under immense, chaotic pressure, not at peace.

The ground is the first lie. It looks like petrified slag, cracked basalt, or compressed ash, but to touch it is to find a texture somewhere between fired clay and pumice, fragile and porous. It is a total construct, every atom of this world placed by intentional, primitive hands. There are no boulders, only shapeless lumps of blackened glass and rust-hued ingots, some the size of cottages, others the size of hills, scattered at a density that privileges emptiness. Between them, the ground is scored with straight, shallow canals of briny water that do not flow but *persist*, weeping from the porous subsoil in a constant, slow seepage. The water is the world’s only active agent of change, and its work is minimal: it pools in the hollows of manufactured stones, it condenses on every surface into a persistent, lukewarm mist that blurs the distant horizon, it leaves behind crystalline salts that grow in fractal spikes overnight, only to dissolve again in the perpetual, muggy dawn.

There is no vegetation. No lichen, no mold, no waving frond. The only organic signatures are mineral. Here and there, a *growth* of prismatic salt-fern, brittle and geometric, erupts from a water-source, a slow-motion crystal reaction that dies when its channel shifts. Elsewhere, dark, leathery *patches* of what might be a fungal biofilm cling to the shaded undersides of the great shapeless masses, feeding on the metallic salts in the damp air. They are not alive; they are a chemical inevitability, a stain that achieves a semblance of pattern.

The light is dim, a perpetual late afternoon under a sky the color of a fading bruise. The sun, if there is one, is a diffuse, sourceless glow that gives no sharp shadows. It bleaches color from the world: the ground is a spectrum of black, grey, rust, and ochre. The only vibrancy comes from the *weirdness*—the reality breakdown that seeps from the very fabric of this place. It manifests in pockets of instability. A canal of water will, for a single, silent moment, run *up* the side of a slag-hill before resuming its weep. A distant ingot will seem to be both thirty paces and three hundred paces away at once, its apparent size shifting with your focus. Sound travels wrong here; your own footfall is a muffled *thump* that arrives a half-second after your foot leaves the ground, as if heard through thick, distant water.

The soundscape is mostly quiet, but it is a quiet layered with impossible textures. There is the constant, sub-audible *hum* of the world’s construction—the vibration of a billion primitive mechanisms, gears and levers and simple pulleys, all buried deep, all turning at a rhythm that does not match any heartbeat. There is the *drip* of water, but it is a stereo drip, each drop arriving at your ear from two slightly different directions. And sometimes, there is the *crack*—a pure, clean sound like a snapped bone, followed by a slow, groaning *shift* as a portion of the ground, or a great mass, reconfigures itself. This is the dynamic: a turbulent, antillike energy of constant, subtle, violent reassembly. The world is not stable; it is *under construction*, always, erratically. A hill might soften and slump into a new, lower shape overnight. A canal might be erased by a sudden, silent uplift of the ground beside it.

You see the first sign of the builders. Not them—they are long gone or were never *who* you imagine. You see their work. A lattice of rough-hewn, rust-red metal beams, bolted with ponderous, simple rivets, rises from the ground like the ribcage of a buried beast. It is a scaffolding for nothing, supporting the weight of the sour sky. It is primitive technology made vast, made total. Adjacent to it, a wall of black, glassy bricks, each the size of a coffin, is incomplete—a section simply missing, the edges of the remaining wall as crisp and final as a torn page. Beyond it, a vast, shallow basin is filled not with water but with a slow-churning, iridescent foam that smells of ozone and struck flint. It is a bath. A forge. A soup. It is not for anything you recognize.

The air itself is a construct. It carries the saline tang of the weeping water, the metallic dust of the eroding masses, and the faint, sweet-rot smell of the mineral biofilms. To breathe it is to taste the world’s effort. The heat is a dry, pressing blanket, but your skin is constantly damp with the condensing humidity. You are never dry. You are never clean.

There are no animals. No birds, no insects, no crawling things. The only movement is the world’s own: the slow creep of salt-spikes, the silent lurch of a distant landform, the shimmer of a heat-haze that does not rise from the ground but seems to emanate from the air itself, a localized distortion in the dimensional fabric.

This is a world built by hands that knew only fire, simple levers, and the weight of stones. They built a continent-sized artifact, a reality-engine of such primitive, brute-force conception that its operating principles are now breaking down into poetry. It is a place of relentless, hand-made weirdness, where the laws of physics are treated like suggestions, where the landscape is a perpetual, slow-motion ruin that is also a perpetual, slow-motion birth. It is wet, but not with rain. It is hot, but not with fire. It is built, but not with purpose you can grasp. It is vast, sparse, and utterly, terrifyingly alone, save for the sound of its own endless, quiet, turbulent becoming.
