**The Dripworks**

You do not so much enter The Dripworks as you are accepted by it. The threshold is a change in the quality of the air—from a wind-touched open to a still, cool, mineral dampness that settles on the skin. The scale is immediately understood: a basin maybe two hundred paces across, ringed by low, smoothed clay bluffs. It is a single, contained feature, a room carved by water and time, with its own ceiling of permanent, diffused cloud.

The defining presence is water, not in roaring flow but in a trillion simultaneous acts of delivery. *Tink. Tink. Tink.* The sound is a sparse, crystalline metronome, the only true sound in a place of profound quiet. It comes from everywhere: from the weeping faces of the bluffs, where dark, mineral-rich water traces paths through centuries of sediment; from the constant, fine mist that condenses on every surface and beads, then falls; from a thousand stalactites of sintered brass and iron, grown fat and drip-crowned beneath the cloud ceiling. The ground is a mosaic of shallow, mirror-clear basins and spongy, root-bound hummocks of black moss. The water is near the surface, always.

The weirdness (weirdness: 1.1/3) is in the details that do not compute. Certain of the deeper basins, if you stare into them long enough, seem to reflect a ceiling that isn’t there, a reverse-world of strange, soft geometries. The sound of the dripping, so regular, sometimes stutters in a pattern that feels less like random plumbing and more like a syllable repeating on the edge of meaning.

You are not alone here, but the presences are the place itself: the weight of the damp clay bluffs, the cold sheen on the bronze, the constant *tink… tink… tink* of the world slowly, meticulously, turning to stone. It is a grove of rust and resin, a chapel of seepage and sedimentation, where the only sermon is the patient, turbulent work of water on what was made to last.
