# The Myco-City Plateau

You stand on a vast, cool, damp plain of packed, spongy earth the colour of old bone. It is not soil, not exactly. It is the compressed, centuries-dead remains of a plant that once grew vertically—a fibrous, moss-like mat that now serves as the regional datum. The elevation is high; the air is thin, chilly, and constantly tastes of wet copper and ozone. Before you, the landscape does not so much *rise* as it does *coalesce*.

The vegetation is total. This is not a world with plants in it; this is a world that *is* plant. The dominant lifeform is a colossal, slow-growing fungus-wood hybrid, a lichenous arboreal thing that builds in layers. What appear from a distance to be hills or mesas are, upon approach, single, continent-sized organisms. Their surfaces are a chaotic, beautiful tapestry of luminescent lichens, suction-cup fungi, and parasitic vines that bleed metallic, acidic sap. This is the "urban" scale: these are not cities built *by* something, they *are* the settlements. The dense, tangled canopies form domed ceilings; the hollow, eroded trunks are avenues and plazas; the buttress roots are bridges and aqueducts. The "built" environment is 100% grown, a megastructure of botanical engineering whose blueprint is written in DNA and time.

Yet the density is sparse. You can walk for hours across the open, misty plains between these living monoliths, the only features being the occasional, sad-looking industrial relic—a rusted gear the size of a cart, a broken turbine blade half-swallowed by grey moss—and the skeletal remains of older, dead fungal cities, ground by the eternal wind into smooth, melancholy dunes. The scale is open, regional: you see three great bio-cities on the horizon, their tops lost in the low, dripping cloud layer, with leagues of empty, whispering plain between them.

The dampness is constant but not overwhelming. It comes from the frequent, mist-like rains that sweep across the plateau, and from the condensation that beads on every surface. Rivulets of acidic, lukewarm water carve temporary channels down the sides of the living hills before being reabsorbed by thirsty root-filters. The temperature is a cool, unchanging 12° Celsius. There is no sun, only a diffuse, sourceless grey light that makes the bioluminescent flora pulse with a faint, sickly green or violet glow.

**Sound is loud, and it is the sound of the ecosystem in motion.** It is a layered cacophony:
*   The deep, sub-audible **thrum** of the mycelial network—the planet’s nervous system—vibrating through the ground.
*   The **hiss** and **drip** of millions of condensation points, a ceaseless rain on a grand scale.
*   The **creak** and **groan** of the living architecture as it grows, inch by agonizing inch, over decades.
*   The **rush** of water through internal vascular canals, sometimes audible as a roar from a nearby "artery."
*   And, woven beneath it all, the **industrial hum**. Not from visible factories, but from within. Certain great fungal trunks have integrated, symbiotic nodules that function like geothermal generators, their rhythmic *clank-hum* a mechanized heartbeat. Pipes (grown from hardened resin, or salvaged metal) snake over bark, carrying steam, nutrient slurry, or shimmering waste. This is industrial tech (1.9): engines and scaled production, but utterly organic in presentation and purpose.

**Fauna is rare (0.9), and what exists is small, silent, and strange.** You might glimpse a six-legged insectoid thing with a glassy carapace, moving with silent, jerky precision as it harvests a specific spore. Or a floating, jellyfish-like creature that drifts in the mist, trailing tendrils that filter the air. Predator-prey dynamics feel minimal; life here is more about symbiosis and parasitism on a geological scale. The loud soundscape belongs to the planet and its plant-minds, not to animal cries.

**Erosion is ancient (2.6).** This plateau is a ghost of a ghost. The original mountains were worn to nubs eons ago. What remains is the final, stable form: a flat-topped, worn-down expanse dominated by these final, enduring biological masterpieces. The land is soothed, planed, settled. The weirdness (2.6) is a **reality breakdown** that seeps in at the edges. It’s subtle, persistent. A bridge ofliving wood might, out of the corner of your eye, appear to be made of rusted iron for a second. The steam from a vent might briefly form human faces. The laws of physics feel like suggestions here; gravity is occasionally negotiable in the heart of the oldest city-organisms. Time itself seems to stretch and contract—you could watch a vine grow in a minute, or lose an hour staring at a static drip of water.

**The rules of this world:**
1.  **Growth is Construction.** Architecture, infrastructure, and machinery are not built; they are cultured, farmed, or coaxed into being over centuries. Repair is a form of gardening.
2.  **Metabolism is Industry.** The "factories" are digestive systems, respiratory chambers, or circulatory pumps. The "pollution" is waste-heat, excess gas, or spent nutrient broth. The products are spore-bricks, resin-tar, and bioluminescent gels.
3.  **Scale is Relative.** The landscape operates on geological and botanical time. A "town" is a single fungal dome 200 meters tall. A "regional process" is the slow, millennial merger of two myco-cities as their root systems touch and fuse.
4.  **Emptiness has Meaning.** The sparse, open plains between cities are not unused; they are buffer zones, graveyards for failed cities, or communal air-channels. The space *is* a feature.
5.  **Integration, not Imposition.** The industrial technology (2.4) does not clash with the total vegetation (3.0). It is *integrated*. Steam vents emerge from bark. Gear mechanisms are grown from interlocking, petrified seed-pods. Wires are filaments of conductive fungus. The world did not go from nature to industry; it evolved *through* industry, or industry evolved *from* it.

**Atmosphere:** The air is cool, moist, and thick with the smell of wet earth, fungus, ozone, and a faint metallic tang. Light is a diffuse, shadowless grey, punctured by the soft pulses of bio-light. Visibility is limited by mist, which swirls in predictable, convective currents. The feeling is one of profound, ancient stillness that is utterly at odds with the loud, ongoing processes—a deep-time quiet that contains a million slow-motion noises. It is a world that is both dying and thriving, industrial and primeval, empty and overwhelmingly full, where the line between landscape and living creature has been erased by a billion years of patient, vegetative ambition. You are not in a place; you are inside a single, continent-sized, industrial-organic entity, and its breathing is the thunder you hear in the distance.
