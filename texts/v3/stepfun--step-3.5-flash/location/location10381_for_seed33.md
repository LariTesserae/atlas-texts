You stand on a shelf of black glass that was once an ocean floor, polished smooth by winds that carry no sand, only a gritty, metallic dust. This is the high plateau—the *teer*—and it sits at the radical, almost vertical lip of the world. Below, the land falls away not in slopes but in sheer, terraced cliffs of layered mineral strata: rust-red iron bands, sheets of translucent sulfate, veins of violet salt that glow with a faint inner cold. The scale is regional, vast, but the density is near-void. You see the next spire of rock, the next basin, perhaps a kilometer away, and between you and it, nothing but the polished plane and the immense, silent sky.

The air is cool and thin, tasting of ozone and dry iron. There is no water. Not as a concept, not as memory. The chemistry is silicate-heavy, metallic. What moisture exists is a temporary, tacky film of condensed atmospheric gases that beads on the glass at dawn and evaporates by the second sun-cycle, leaving a fine, conductive dust.

The built environment is here, but it is not *built*. It is *revealed*. The urban parameter is satisfied by the sheer density of form, the clear intentionality of space. But there are no devices, no tools, no engineering as you’d know it. The city—*Veyl*—is a contradiction: it is both a natural erosion and a constructed statement. It is carved *into* and *from* the living rock of the plateau. Structures are negative spaces: vast, amphitheater-like hollows cut into the glass, their walls ribbed and fluted like the interior of a giant geode. Bridges are not thrown across gaps; they are the remaining, narrowed ridges between carved forms, polished by millennia of the same dust-driven wind. Inhabitation is marked by subtle smoothing on certain ledges, by the arrangement of fist-sized, perfectly spherical nodules of fused quartz that sit in deliberate circles. There is no furniture, no wiring, no visible artifact of a tool-using species. The technology, if it exists, is geological, hormonal, a slow dialogue between mind and stone.

Light is dim, a perpetual late-afternoon glow from a small, red-tinged star that hangs low and motionless in the yellow-tinged sky. It casts long, sharp shadows that do not move. The second sun-cycle is not a sun, but a diffuse, sourceless brightening of the upper atmosphere that bleaches the violet salts to grey for a few hours, followed by a deeper twilight.

Sound is almost nil. The wind, despite its grit, makes no hiss against the glass. There is no call of bird, no hum of insect. Your own footsteps are deadened, as if the glass absorbs vibration. Yet, the world is profoundly *active*. The dynamic parameter is met not by weather or biology, but by physics in slow motion. You feel a sub-audible tremor through the soles of your feet—a deep sigh of the tectonic plate, a settling that takes a century to complete. High on a cliff face, you see a slow, viscous seep of dark, tar-like mineral fluid that moves perceptibly over hours, leaving a fresh, glittering trail behind it. It is not lava; it is something else, something that flows against gravity in capillary threads before hardening into new, strange rock.

Ecology is a ghost of ecology. Fauna is rare (1.4/3), but present. What lives here is not animal, not plant. It is more like a persistent pattern. You might see a cluster of the quartz nodules rearrange themselves silently over a day, forming a spiral that wasn’t there at dawn. Or a shimmer in the air above a basin—a distortion like heat-haze, but cold, that might be a predator or a weather system, a consciousness or a natural phenomenon. It is impossible to tell. The few mobile forms are translucent, refractive, seeming to be made of the same stuff as light itself, passing through solid rock and vanishing. They leave no tracks.

Weirdness (2.9/3) is the constant substrate. Reality does not break with a crack, but with a persistent, unsettling *wrongness*. Distances are unreliable. A ridge that seems a ten-minute walk takes two hours. A sheer cliff you recall seeing is now a gentle slope in your memory. Time stutters. You might experience a sudden, silent déjà vu where you live the next five minutes in a flash, then live them again for real. The carved city itself resists mapping; turns you are sure you took lead you back to the same ledge. The materials themselves are slightly non-Euclidean. The black glass is not perfectly flat; if you lie on it and stare, the horizon subtly *curves upward*, as if you are on the inside of a vast, smooth bowl.

The atmosphere is one of immense loneliness and profound, ancient patience. This is not a dead world. It is a world that has evolved past life, past biology, into something mineral and meditative. The “inhabitants” of Veyl, if they still exist, are likely symbiotic with the rock, their thoughts as slow as erosion, their architecture the byproduct of a consciousness that thinks in geological time. You are not an explorer here; you are a brief, frantic chemical anomaly. The silence is not empty; it is full of the slow, tectonic thoughts of a planet that has forgotten the sea, forgotten the sun, and has instead turned inward, carving its dreams into its own bones.
