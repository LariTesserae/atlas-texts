You do not arrive at the Sundial of Ages by journeying to it. You arrive when the world’s breathing has slowed enough for you to perceive the place where it has always been.

It is a high plateau, not a mountain, for the mountains were worn down to their bones and those bones were digested eons ago. The elevation feels less like a height above sea level and more like a removal from the ordinary rhythm of the world—a place where time is a viscous syrup. The erosion is not the sharp work of glaciers or rivers, but the patient, absolute flattening of destiny. The rock is not broken, but *reinterpreted*. Vast swathes of the landscape are a single, homogeneous, gently warped plane of dark, porous stone, like a colossal, dried riverbed that was never a river. In the hollows and depressions of this petrified sea, the wetness gathers. The water does not flow in channels; it sweats and condenses from a perpetually damp, warm air. Weeping rocks exude not tears but slow, silty seepage that feeds a thousand sponge-mosses and gelatinous lichens. The ground is never dry, but rarely is it a true lake. It is a global marsh-edge, a skin stretched between saturation and vapor.

And the skin is alive. Vegetation is not a coat upon the land; it *is* the land. The dense, fibrous substrate you walk on is a root-mat a meter deep, woven from the desiccated remains of ten thousand generations of feathery ferns and woody, lung-shaped tubers. From this mat grow the current generation: towering, breathing spires of vascular crystal that hum with internal fluid, broad lipped-capital plants that hold gallons of captured mist, and low, crawling hedge-structures of interlocked thorn-branches that form living walls and corridors. The plants are the architecture, the soil, the weather system. They transpire a fine, warm mist that hangs in the dim light, blurring the horizon into a green-gold veil. The density is overwhelming—layers of life, dead life, and the chemistry of decay all pressing in at once, a saturated presence that feels less like a forest and more like being inside a giant, moist lung.

The light is a permanent, diffuse twilight, filtered through a high, unbroken ceiling of condensed cloud and the upper foliage of the world-forest. It does not cast sharp shadows. The scale is continental; you can walk for days and the character of the place changes only in subtle gradations—the spires grow slightly taller, the mist slightly sweeter, the stone beneath the root-mat slightly darker. The dynamic is glacial. A branch may thicken by a leaf-width in a decade. A new mist-pool may form over a century. The only motion is the slow exhalation of the entire biosphere and the occasional, deep sigh of the tectonic plates far below, which propagates up as a sub-audible tremor that makes the crystal spires vibrate in sympathetic resonance for a full minute.

Fauna is abundant, but subtle. They are not animals as separate from the plants; they are nodes of mobility within the greater whole. There are soft, slug-like grazers with symbiotic photosynthetic skins that drift through the lower foliage, leaving shimmering trails of fertilizing mucus. There are winged things like living seed pods that drift on the thermals from the warm ground, their flight a slow, silent drift. Predators are ambush-sculptures—creatures that have spent millennia growing still, their forms indistinguishable from root-ball or fungal cluster until their petal-like jaws uncurl. A "storm" may be the coordinated reproductive discharge of a synchronized fungal bloom, raining spores for a week.

The scattered built elements are almost always biological in origin, or are being consumed as such. A single, smooth-walled arch of fused black glass stands in a clearing, perhaps the fused core of an ancient engine or a natural lightning-fused stone, now dripping with a nourishing algae. A ring of upright, carved pillars turns out to be the petrified husks of colossal, tree-sized tubers that once fruited in a perfect circle. There are no tools, no devices, only these faint marks, these partial remains, which are being slowly rewritten by the total vegetation. The tech is zero, but the world operates on a biological logic so advanced it mimics the effects of a forgotten, gentle technology: it processes, recycles, builds, and sustains on a scale that feels engineered, yet has no engineer.

The soundscape is the quiet of deep processes. The dominant note is the *drip-thump* of water finding a new channel through the root-mat, a sound you feel in your sternum as much as hear. There is a constant, low-frequency hum from the vibrating crystal spires, a chord determined by their height and density. Occasionally, a high, crystalline chime echoes as a massive, old-growth "bark-plate" of a spire shears off and falls into the sponge-moss below, absorbed without a splash. The abundant fauna communicate in subsonic pulses, in color shifts of their skins visible only in the mist-haze, or in the release of specific pheromones that alter the local plant chemistry. You do not hear their conversations; you might smell a sudden shift to mint, or feel a localized warming of the air.

The rules are simple and absolute:
1.  **Growth is the only motion.** Everything is either growing, being grown upon, or being composted for the next growth.
2.  **Form follows function, but function is obscure.** Structures exist for reasons known only to the whole. A twisting colonnade of root may serve as a water conductor, a sound resonator, and a birthing chamber simultaneously.
3.  **Time is a local variable.** Your sense of an hour is meaningless. The true cycles are the respiration of the plateau (a slow inflation and deflation over centuries), the migration of nutrient fronts through the root-mat (millennia), and the long, slow dying of the central, oldest spire complexes (eons).
4.  **You are a disturbance.** Your footsteps compact the mat. Your breath adds a foreign gas. Your presence is a brief, hot pulse of entropy that the world will work to smooth away, not with hostility, but with the impartial, total recycling of all things.

To stand in the Sundial of Ages is to experience the opposite of wilderness. It is a completed thing. A final, stable form that has forgotten the concept of change except as internal, cyclical maintenance. The dim light, the wet warmth, the dense, breathing green-gold—it is not a snapshot of a moment, but the accumulated texture of an ending so profound it has become a beginning. The only rule that matters is that everything, eventually, becomes part of the living stone.
