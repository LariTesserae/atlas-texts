This is the Sinkhole Atrium, a shallow karst collapse just barely below the surrounding plateau: its rim sits only 1–2 meters under the level of the upland grassland above, so if you crouch at the edge and peer down, you can see the faint, blue-green glimmer of its walls, and the faint silver shine of the central pool, before the darkness swallows the rest. It is 200 meters across, bounded entirely by smooth, weathered limestone, a single self-contained feature small enough to walk its circumference in ten minutes—fits the 1.4/3 small, bounded scale exactly.

The first thing that hits you as you squeeze through the narrow, worn crack in the rim that is the only entrance is the damp, warm weight of the air: 26 degrees Celsius, 90% humidity, it clings to your skin like a wet blanket within seconds, carrying the smell of wet limestone, sweet rot from the sparse lichen crusting the walls, and a faint, clean metallic tang you can’t place. The sound hits you next, before you even see the space: a steady, clicking patter of water hitting stone
