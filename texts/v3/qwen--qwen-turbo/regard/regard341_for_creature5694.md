What the benevolent and wise power would do, if it were to take notice of the place, is not to change it—because it is already perfect in its stillness, in its waiting. It would not seek to "fix" what is not broken, nor to impose its own order upon a world that is already its own. Rather, it would choose to *listen*.

For the place is not silent. It speaks in a language older than words, a language of shape and shadow, of breath and memory. It is a place that exists in the liminal, between what is and what could be, between the seen and the unseen. It is the embodiment of patience, of a slow turning that does not hurry, of a purpose that is not always clear but is always there.

The power, if it is wise, would not come as a god or a conqueror. It would come as a guest, a quiet presence that does not impose, but invites. It would not look to change the place, but to *understand* it. To feel its pulse, to walk its labyrinth without stepping on the sacred, to listen to the whispers of the water, and to let the warmth of the stone wash over it, not as a traveler, but as one who belongs.

It would move through the place as the being who lives there moves—without sound, without haste, without need. It would sit in the shadow of the pools, watch the opalescent sheen of the water, trace the faint glow of the moss, and feel the weight of the stone as it breathes in the dark.

And when it had done this, when it had felt the place as it is, not as it could be, it would not leave. It would stay. Not to settle, not to claim, but to *become* part of the stillness. It would not be a visitor, but a presence, a quiet thing that lingers in the corners, in the spaces between, in the rhythm of the place itself.

For the power, if it is benevolent, would not come to alter the world. It would come to *join* it. To be part of the waiting. To be part of the becoming. To stand in the hollow of the colossal, living stone, and not move, not speak, not even breathe—but to be, as the place is.

And in that silence, in that stillness, the power would learn what the place has always known: that some things are not meant to be understood. They are meant to be felt. To be endured. To be *there*.

So the power would not act. It would *stay*.

And that, perhaps, is the greatest gift of all.
