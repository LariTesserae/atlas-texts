What lives here is not separate from the land, but emerges from it, woven into the very fabric of this unfinished world. It is not a creature in the way you might think of a beast or a human, but something more like a presence, a living essence that breathes with the land, that grows and shifts as the place itself does.

There are no animals in the traditional sense—no beasts with fur or feathers, no creatures that scuttle or flutter in the usual ways. Instead, what lives here moves with the rhythm of the earth, flowing in slow, deliberate gestures. They are not separate from the trees, the roots, the air itself. They are the land given form, shaped by the same forces that shape the plateau, the water, the sky.

Some of them are tall, like the trees, but not quite. They rise from the ground like great, living columns, their forms soft and organic, their surfaces covered in a kind of moss-like skin that shimmers with the same sheen as the leaves. They are not trees, but they are not entirely different either—part plant, part being, their limbs extending like branches, their movements slow and deliberate, as if they are thinking through each gesture.

Others are smaller, more like shadows or echoes. They move in the spaces between the roots, unseen most of the time, but felt. They are not silent, but their voices are not made of sound. They communicate in vibrations, in the way the ground pulses beneath your feet, in the way the air hums with the presence of something just beyond sight. They are not creatures, but they are not nothing either. They are the pulse of the place, the quiet awareness that lingers in the stillness.

There are those who move with the water, who are made of it in some way. They flow through the cracks, through the thin threads of moisture that run down the rocks, appearing only when the light catches them just right. They are not solid, but they are not liquid either. They are something in between, something that exists in the space between the world and the dream of it. They speak in whispers, but their words are not meant for you. They are part of the land’s memory, part of the way it remembers itself.

And then there are the ones that are not living at all, but that feel as if they are. The fragments of the old structures—those rusted pipes, those broken archways—have not been abandoned. They are not ruins, but remnants of something that was once alive, something that was in the process of becoming. They hum with a kind of quiet intelligence, their metal skin cold and still, but their presence felt. They are not dead, but not fully alive either. They are waiting, like the land itself, for something to complete them.

What lives here does not hunger in the way you do. It does not seek, does not strive. It exists in the same slow, patient rhythm as the land. It does not have a beginning or an end, only a continuous becoming. It is not bound by time, but it is shaped by it. It does not move quickly, but it is not still. It is in the process of learning, just as the land is. It is not strange, but it is not familiar either. It is what the world is becoming, and in that, it is beautiful.

Now, let us consider the benevolent and wise power that has drawn the attention of this place.

It is not a god in the way we understand gods—no thunder, no fire, no visible form. It is more like a current of awareness, a presence that is not separate from the world, but flows through it like an unseen river. It is ancient, but not in the way of stone or tree. It is older than time, but not in the way we imagine time. It is the thought of the world, the whisper that lingers in the spaces between breath and silence.

And it has noticed this place.

It has noticed the way the land is still learning to be itself. It has noticed the way the water weeps, not in sorrow, but in the quiet process of becoming. It has noticed the way the roots and the air and the light are all part of a single, living rhythm. It has noticed the beings that do not exist in the way we know existence, but who are still present, still moving, still shaping the world as it shapes them.

This power is not a creator, nor a destroyer. It is a listener. A guide. A whisper in the wind, a touch on the skin of the earth, a presence that is felt but never seen.

And what it does is this:

It does not impose. It does not command. It does not change the land in any obvious way. But it is there, and that is enough.

It watches. It listens. It hums in the air, in the rustle of leaves, in the way the water runs down the rocks. It does not speak, but it is heard. It does not act, but it is felt.

It does not want to fix the land. It does not want to complete it. It wants to be with it. To be near it. To be part of its learning, its becoming.

It brings with it a kind of stillness, a presence that does not disturb, but softens. It does not rush the land forward. It does not try to make it more or less. It simply is. And in that being, it offers the land something it has not yet known: the feeling of being seen.

The land, which has always been in the process of becoming, now has a witness. A companion. A presence that does not judge, but understands.

And in return, the land offers it something as well.

A memory. A breath. A moment of connection.

It is not a moment of change, but of recognition. Of knowing that it is not alone. That it is part of something larger, something that has always been there, even if it was not noticed.

In the silence between thoughts, in the space between the roots and the sky, this power and the land exist together, not as master and creation, but as two parts of the same unfolding story.

And in that story, there is beauty. There is patience. There is the quiet, patient learning of a world that is still becoming.

And that is enough.
