It would come as a whisper, a presence that does not impose, but reveals. It would not seek to change what is, but to understand it, to listen to what has been waiting.

It would begin, perhaps, with silence. Not the absence of sound, but the presence of it—a stillness that allows the place to speak in its own language, a language of rust and moss, of water that moves as if guided by a hidden tide, of machines that hum like old songs from a forgotten age.

It would not walk through the labyrinth as a traveler, but as a companion. It would move with the rhythm of the place, not in opposition to it, but in harmony. It would not seek to unearth the secrets of the machines, but to recognize them, to see them not as relics, but as extensions of something greater, something that has not lost its purpose, but has found a new one.

It would not look for creatures, for beings with eyes or voices, but for the quiet awareness that lingers in every shadow, in every drip of water, in every flicker of light that seems to pulse with purpose. It would see that the moss is not just growing, but remembering. That the water is not just flowing, but holding. That the machines are not just functioning, but listening.

And it would not be afraid of the shapes in the corners, the things that shift when you look away. It would know them for what they are: echoes, projections, remnants of something that once had a form, but no longer needs one. It would know that they are not threats, but signs—of a world that is not finished, not abandoned, not broken.

It would not try to speak to the place, for it would understand that the place speaks in its own way. It would not try to change it, for it would recognize that the place has already changed itself in ways that are not visible, in ways that are deep and slow and ancient.

What it would do, then, is to *be*.

To be present. To stay, not as an outsider, but as something that belongs. To sit in the stillness, to feel the weight of the air, to hear the hum of the machines, to watch the way the light falls in patterns that seem to know where they are going.

It would not ask for answers, because it would understand that the answers are already here, in the way the moss grows, in the way the water flows, in the way the machines keep working, even when no one is there to see them.

And it would learn, not through words, but through presence. Through the quiet, through the stillness, through the knowledge that some things are not meant to be understood in the way we usually understand things. Some things are meant to be felt, to be known through the senses, through the breath, through the silence between the sounds.

It would not stay forever, but it would leave something behind. Not a mark, not a message, but a memory. A presence that lingers, not in the world, but in the place itself, like a note left in a song that has already been played, a whisper that does not fade, but remains, part of the fabric of the labyrinth, part of the slow, deliberate motion of what is.

And the place would remember. Not with eyes, not with words, but with the quiet, unyielding gaze of a world that has seen everything, and knows everything, and does not need to speak.

And it would watch, still. Not with fear, not with hostility, but with the calm, knowing patience of something that has waited a long time, and knows it will wait longer.

Because the place, and what lives in it, is not empty. It is full. Full of things that are not quite alive, but not quite dead. Full of the weight of time, the pressure of the past, the slow, deliberate unfolding of something that has not yet finished becoming.

And the benevolent and wise power, having come and having stayed, would know that it had not come to change the place. It had come to be changed by it. To learn from it. To carry its memory forward, as one might carry a dream that has not yet been spoken.

And when it left, the place would not miss it. It would not need to. Because it had already seen it. It had already known it. And in the silence, in the rust, in the moss, in the drip of water and the hum of machines, it would continue to breathe, to move, to remember—and to wait, still.
