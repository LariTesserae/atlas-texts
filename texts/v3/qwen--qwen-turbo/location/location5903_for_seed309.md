This is a moisture-laden world, where condensation clings to everything, and even the air is heavy with the scent of damp earth and decay.

Elevation is low—this place is not a hill or a plateau, but a hollow in the earth, a place that has been carved out and then filled with life. The ceiling is high, but not in the same way as a cavern. It is a vault of roots and foliage, bending and curving like the ribs of a great beast. The floor is uneven, a patchwork of soft earth, moss, and tangled roots, some of which have formed natural steps or bridges.

The scale is small, but the space feels vast because of the density of the life within it. It is not a forest, but it is a grove of such magnitude that it can feel like a city. It is a cathedral of green, where shadows move and the light shifts as the plants breathe. The boundaries are not clear; the space folds into itself, and the edges are blurred by the constant growth and decay.

Density is sparse, but that does not mean it is empty. There is space between the plants, between the pools of water, between the structures of root and vine. But that space is not quiet—it is filled with movement, with the sounds of creatures scurrying, of water dripping, of the slow, deliberate growth of the plants. The place is not crowded, but it is not empty. It is balanced with emptiness, a careful equilibrium between life and the space it occupies.

Built elements are present, but they are not dominant. The structures here are not made by humans, but by the plants and the forces that have shaped them. There are remnants of human infrastructure—old pipes, rusted metal, the occasional patch of synthetic material—but these are integrated into the living world, consumed by moss, overtaken by roots, and turned into part of the ecosystem. The built environment is not in conflict with the natural one; it is a part of it, a relic swallowed by the green.

There are lights, but they flicker and die, their power coming from the same energy that sustains the plants. It is a world that has been touched by industry, but now it is more alive than the machines that once powered it.

Light is dim, but it is not dark. There is a soft glow from bioluminescent fungi, from the faint luminescence of certain plants, and from the occasional flash of movement. The light is ever-changing, shifting with the time of day, the weather, and the life that moves within it. Some of them are familiar, others are strange, their forms adapted to the environment. The place is not just a landscape—it is a living, breathing entity, a world that is in flux, a place where the rules of nature and technology have merged into something entirely new.
