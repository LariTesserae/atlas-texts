Some of them have been submerged, their trunks half-rotted, their leaves still green and drooping like the remnants of a forgotten language.

It is not the cold of winter, but something deeper, a cold that has settled into the very structure of the place. The water is icy where it licks at the edges of the city, and the air is always damp, always ready to freeze, even when it doesn’t.

Elevation is low—this is a place that has been swallowed, not by the sea, but by time and water. The city lies in a basin, a hollow where the land has given way and the water has taken root. The structures are not tall. They are squat, thick-walled, built to last in the damp, to resist the slow decay of the elements. The buildings are still standing, but they are not intact. They are eroded, cracked, their surfaces worn by the wind and water, their materials a mix of stone, wood, and something more alien, something that feels as though it should not be here.

The scale is open, not in the sense of vastness, but in the sense of reach. This is not a small place. It stretches for miles, a sprawl of ruins and wetlands, of waterways and overgrown streets. It is a city that has grown into the land, not the other way around. There are no clear boundaries, no edges that define where the city ends and the wild begins. The two are one.

Density is high. There is no room to breathe, no space to move without feeling the presence of something else—another building, another tree, another creature. The city is packed with life, with structures that press close, with plants that choke the streets, with animals that dart between the ruins like shadows.

There is an urban presence, though it is not modern. The city is built, but it is not made by machines. It was built by hands, by people long gone, by a civilization that left behind its monuments and then vanished. The streets are still there, the foundations still strong, the buildings still standing, but they are not occupied. They are relics, preserved by the water, by the moisture, by the slow hand of decay.

The only tools are those made by nature itself—roots that split stone, water that carves through rock, insects that burrow and build.

The light is normal, but it is dim, filtered through the canopy of trees and the mist that hovers above the water. There is no sun, or at least none that reaches the ground. The sky is hidden, obscured by the growth that has taken over the city, by the thick layer of water that floats above it. The light is soft and gray, casting long shadows across the ruins.
