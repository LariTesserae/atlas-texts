Cracks run through it like veins, and in those fissures, green grows.

Some plants are tall and rigid, like skeletal trees with metallic bark, others creep and curl in slow, deliberate movements, as if they are aware of their own presence. The air is filled with a musty, earthy scent, not of rot, but of something older, something that remembers.

The sun is high, but it does not burn. Its light is soft, as though the sky is made of something other than air—maybe a thin veil of gas, or a film of static. The light is normal, but it feels heavy, as though the sky is pressing down, not with weight, but with presence.

Erosion is minimal. The place has not had time to wear down. The edges are sharp, the features are new, as if the land was only just formed, and it is still figuring out how to be. Yet there is a presence here, a sense that this place is not just made, but *grown*. The rock is not inert; it shifts slightly, as if the ground is breathing. The vegetation does not simply grow—it *expands*, pushing through the mineral, as though the land itself is alive and the plants are its way of speaking.

The scale is small. Not in the sense of being insignificant, but in the sense that you are inside it, surrounded by it. You are not looking at a landscape from above, but within it, as if you have stepped into a cathedral of green and stone. The plateau is a single, enclosed space, with borders that are not clear, but felt. It is a grove, a room, a hamlet of life, but not in any human sense. It is a place that exists on its own terms.

The density is high. There is a crowdedness to the place, not in the way of buildings or people, but in the way of life. Every inch of space is filled with something—roots, spores, leaves, or the faint shimmer of unseen movement. The air is thick with the presence of things. You can feel them, even if you can’t see them. There is a sense of being watched, not by eyes, but by the weight of the ecosystem itself.

The structures are not made; they are *grown*, and they serve purposes that are not immediately clear.

The flora and fauna have created a system that is self-sustaining, self-regulating, and self-aware. The animals are not domesticated or tamed—they are part of the ecosystem, moving in patterns that are only barely perceptible, their sounds few and low, as if they are trying not to disturb the balance.

They are large and slow, with bodies that seem to merge with the landscape. Some have skin that shifts color, blending into the rock or the vegetation. Others are small, almost invisible, flitting between the roots and the leaves. They do not make much noise. The sound is quiet, almost absent. The weirdness is subtle, but there. It is not the kind of horror or chaos that you expect from the strange, but rather a quiet, uncanny quality—things that are almost familiar, but not quite.
