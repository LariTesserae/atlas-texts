To prepare for a visit to this hidden, subterranean expanse — a place that feels more like a dream than a real world — the ordinary human multiverse traveler must do more than pack a flashlight, a canteen, and a few snacks. The journey here is not one of mere physical traversal, but of mental, emotional, and perhaps even existential preparation. This is not a place to be entered lightly.

First, the traveler must understand that this is a world that does not follow the familiar rules. The laws of physics may be altered, time may be fluid or fixed, and the boundaries between the organic and the mechanical may be blurred. The traveler should not approach this place with the expectation of control — the world beneath the surface will not be tamed by maps, GPS, or even instinct. It is a place that resists categorization, and perhaps even resistance.

The traveler should pack not only practical gear but also tools of the mind and spirit. A notebook and pen are essential. This is a place that demands observation — not just of the physical environment, but of the subtle, almost imperceptible shifts in tone, in rhythm, in the way the air and light behave. A traveler who can document even the smallest details may find themselves better equipped to navigate or understand what they encounter.

The sense of sound is particularly important. The world is filled with a deep, continuous hum, a sound that is not just background noise, but a language. The traveler should bring recording equipment — a high-quality microphone, a portable recorder, perhaps even a device that can capture infrasound. This is a place where silence is rare, and the sounds are not random, but part of a system. Learning to listen, to discern pattern and intention, may be as vital as any physical survival tool.

The traveler should also bring a form of protection against the unknown. While there is no visible danger, the environment itself may be unsettling. The air, thick with spores and the metallic tang of ozone, may have an effect on the mind. Some travelers may experience a strange disorientation, a feeling that they are not quite in their own body. A small vial of something to ground the traveler — a calming herb, a crystal, or a simple object of personal significance — could be useful. But more importantly, the traveler must be prepared to accept that their sense of self may be altered. This is not a place that tolerates arrogance or detachment.

Clothing should be chosen with care. The terrain is dense, the air is cool, and the water flows in a way that cannot be predicted. Clothing should be both practical and adaptable — layers that can be removed or added as needed, sturdy boots that can navigate uneven ground, and a waterproof jacket that can withstand the damp, condensing flow of water that clings to every surface. But the traveler should also be prepared to shed their usual sense of identity. This is not a place where the familiar will always fit.

The traveler should also prepare for the psychological weight of the place. The sense of being watched, the slow, deliberate movement of shapes in the dark, the feeling of being part of something ancient and silent — these can be overwhelming. The traveler should cultivate a sense of calm, a deep awareness of the present moment. This is not a place for panic. It is a place for presence.

The light, too, is a guide and a challenge. The blue-green glow of the water is constant, but it is not steady. It pulses, it shifts, it may lead the traveler in directions they aren’t ready for. The traveler should avoid relying too much on this light. They must learn to navigate by other senses — by touch, by sound, by the way the air moves. But they should also be aware that this light is not just illumination. It is part of the world’s heartbeat, its rhythm. The traveler may find themselves drawn to it, or repelled by it, depending on their state of mind.

Finally, the traveler must understand that this is a place that is waiting. Not just for someone to find it, but for someone to understand it. This is not a place to be taken lightly, but not necessarily a place to be feared. It is a place of meaning, of history, of something that has existed beyond human comprehension. To enter it is to step into a world that may not have a purpose, but that has a presence. And that presence may change the traveler in ways they did not expect.

In the end, the traveler should not go with the expectation of discovery, but with the willingness to be discovered. This is not a place you visit — it is a place that visits you. And when it does, it will leave its mark.
