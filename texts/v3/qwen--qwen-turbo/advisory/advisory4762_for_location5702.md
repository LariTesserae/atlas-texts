To prepare for a visit to this submerged, living city, you must approach it not as an explorer, but as a guest—perhaps even as a student. This place is not just a location; it is an ecosystem, a memory, a myth made tangible. It is not a place you can simply "get through" or "see." It is a place that will see you. To enter, you must first understand that you are not entering a city of human origin. You are entering a city that has *become* something else, something that has grown, breathed, and adapted to the water. Here, the rules are not written—they are felt.

### First, you will need a way to *breathe*, but not in the way you expect. You will need a suit, yes, but not a typical diving suit. This is not a place you can simply "dive" into. You must be equipped with something that allows you to function in the deep, but also to *move* in the water—something that mimics the fluidity of the environment. A rebreather of some kind, perhaps, or a suit that allows you to glide and maneuver as if you are part of the current. But more than that, you will need a *mind* that is open to the slow, deliberate motion of this place.

You will not be able to walk in the traditional sense. The streets are not paved, but they are covered in silt, sand, and the shifting weight of the sea. You will move by drifting, by letting the currents carry you. Your movements must be graceful, deliberate, as if you are part of the water itself. If you move too quickly, you may disrupt the balance of the city, and you may not like the consequences.

Your tools must be simple, but not useless. This is not a city of technology in the human sense. There are no machines, no wires, no batteries. But there are tools—stones shaped by hands that have long since vanished. You must find a way to carry these, or to replicate them. These tools may be used for cutting, weaving, or building. They are not made from metal, but from bone, shell, and coral. If you bring anything with you, it must be something that can be used without disrupting the harmony of the place.

You will need a way to *see* in this place, but the light is not the same as on land. The surface above is a constant shimmer of sunlight, refracted through the waves. It is soft, indirect, and ever-changing. You may need a device that can help you adjust to the light, or you may need to learn to rely on your senses. The city is not dark, but it is not always bright. You must become accustomed to the shifting patterns of light and shadow, the way the city seems to breathe with the movement of the water.

The sounds of the city are constant, but they are not the same as the sounds of the open ocean. The city has its own rhythm, its own song. The walls hum with the movement of the water, the structures pulse with the life of the sea. You must listen—not just with your ears, but with your entire being. This is a place where sound is as much a part of the architecture as the coral and kelp. If you do not listen, you will miss the message that is being sent to you.

You must also learn to *feel* the city. The water is not just a medium; it is a part of the city itself. The pressure is heavy, but it is not oppressive. It is a constant presence, a reminder of the depth. You must learn to move with it, to let it guide you, to let it shape your movements. You must learn to move slowly, to let the city *move* you, rather than the other way around.

The fauna is abundant, but not always visible. You will see fish, eels, jellyfish, and other creatures, but there are also things that move in the periphery—creatures that may not even be fully visible. You must be cautious, but not afraid. This is not a dangerous place in the way you might expect. It is a place of patience, of slow motion, of living things that have adapted to the city as much as the city has adapted to them.

You must also prepare yourself for the *weirdness* of the place. It is not unsettling, but it is strange. The architecture is not human, and the technology is not mechanical. The city is not built—it is *grown*. The buildings are not made of stone and wood, but of coral, kelp, and living structures that pulse and shift. You must be willing to accept that this is not a place you can understand in the way you understand the world above. It has its own logic, its own rules, and it will reveal itself to you only if you are patient, if you are open, if you are willing to listen.

Finally, you must be prepared to leave. This is not a place that you can stay in forever. The water is not just a medium; it is a force that shapes and re-shapes the city. You may feel the pull of the deep, the slow rhythm of the tides. You may feel the city trying to hold you, to keep you, to make you one of its own. But you must remember that you are from another world, and that you must return. You must prepare yourself for that, not with fear, but with gratitude. Because this place is not just a city. It is a living memory, a place that has existed long before you, and that will continue long after you are gone.

In the end, visiting this city is not about exploring, but about *becoming*. It is a place that will change you, just as it has changed everything that has come before. If you are ready to listen, to move, to feel, and to understand, then you will not just visit this place—you will become part of it.
