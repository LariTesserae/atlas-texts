To prepare for a journey to this vast, flat world, the ordinary human multiverse traveler must approach with a mind both open and grounded, with a body fortified against the unknown, and with a heart willing to confront what lies beyond the limits of comprehension.

First, **mentally**, one must let go of the familiar. This place does not obey the laws of space and time as we know them. The horizon is not a boundary, but a deception. The traveler must surrender to the idea that their perception of distance, direction, and even time itself may be unreliable. This is not a place to be mapped or tamed. It is a place to be experienced, to be felt, to be *sensed*. Meditation, visualization, and a strong sense of self-awareness will be essential. The traveler may need to practice mental anchoring—techniques to remain present, to resist the disorientation that comes from being in a place that feels like it is breathing.

**Physically**, the traveler must be equipped for a world that is not entirely hostile, but not entirely welcoming either. The ground is made of fused minerals that may be abrasive, sharp, or unstable underfoot. Sturdy, multi-layered footwear is a must, with reinforced soles and possibly some form of heat resistance, since the warmth is not oppressive, but it lingers. The atmosphere is thin, so a breathing apparatus or air supply may be necessary, depending on the traveler's tolerance for low oxygen. However, the traveler should not become too reliant on technology, for this world is not designed for human comfort. It is a world of endurance, of patience, of presence.

**For the journey itself**, the traveler should carry supplies that are minimal but meaningful. Water is not required, but something to sustain the body and mind—perhaps a small amount of food, a notebook, a journal, and a way to record observations, even if the world resists being recorded. The traveler should carry a light source, not because it is dark (though it is), but because the bioluminescent lichen provides only faint, intermittent illumination. The traveler’s own light may be a way to orient themselves, to measure the distance between themselves and the horizon, which remains forever just out of reach.

The **technological aspect** of the world is not to be ignored. The traveler should bring devices that can detect energy signatures, electromagnetic fields, and subtle vibrations. The world hums, vibrates, and pulses, and these signals may be keys to understanding what is going on. However, the traveler must also be prepared to let go of the idea that technology can fully explain the place. This is not a world to be analyzed, but one to be *felt*.

**Socially**, the traveler should prepare for the fact that they may be alone. The creatures here are not human, and they do not communicate in any familiar way. They are not hostile, but they are not friendly either. They are echoes, shadows, remnants. The traveler must be prepared to be watched, to feel watched, and to accept that they may never know why. The feeling of being observed, of being part of a larger, unseen presence, is part of the experience. The traveler may need to develop a sense of quiet confidence, a calmness that does not shrink from the unknown.

**Spiritually**, this place demands a kind of acceptance. It is not a place that is meant to be conquered or understood in a traditional sense. It is a place that exists, that has been built, that has been forgotten, and that continues to exist in a state of quiet decay. To visit it is to confront the idea that not all things are meant to be known. The traveler may need to carry a sense of reverence, of humility. This is not a place for ego. It is a place for observation, for reflection, for the quiet contemplation of something that is not alive in the way we think life is.

**In terms of navigation**, the traveler must accept that traditional maps are useless. The world is vast, and it is not structured in a way that allows for direction. The traveler must learn to move in a way that is not linear, that is not bound by north or south. They must learn to move with the flow of the place, to trust the rhythm of the hum, the pulse of the lichen, the movement of the creatures. The traveler may need to develop a sense of spatial intuition, a way of sensing where they are in relation to the world, even when the world itself refuses to give them a fixed point.

**The emotional preparation** is perhaps the most important. This is a place that will unsettle the traveler. The subtle weirdness that lingers in the back of the mind is not something that can be ignored. It is a constant, a background hum, a reminder that this place is not like any other. The traveler must accept that they may feel a sense of unease, of displacement, of alienation. They must also accept that this is part of the journey. There is no need to fight it. There is no need to explain it. There is only the act of being there, of walking on the blackened stone, of feeling the pulse of the world beneath their feet.

In the end, the traveler should not go to this place with the expectation of finding answers. They should go with the intention of *being*—of encountering something that is not human, not natural, not entirely mechanical, but something else entirely. A place that is vast, ancient, and alive in its own way. A place that is not meant to be understood, but to be felt.

And when they return, they will not return as the same person. Because this place does not simply exist. It changes those who walk its surface.
