To prepare for a visit to this world, an ordinary human multiverse traveler must approach the journey not as a mere expedition, but as a transformation. This is not a place to be simply observed or even explored—it is a place to be *experienced*, to be *reoriented* within. It is a landscape that does not conform to the familiar scales of space, time, or perception. To step into it is to step into a realm where the laws of the mundane are not broken, but reimagined. Here, the human body, mind, and psyche must be attuned to a rhythm that is not its own.

First, one must prepare the body. The environment is warm, almost oppressively so, but not in the way of a desert. It is not the heat of the sun, but the heat of a system—of a place that has been engineered to be in a state of equilibrium for eons. The traveler should not arrive in heavy clothing or with the expectation of cold. Instead, light, breathable garments—preferably made of moisture-wicking fabrics—will allow the body to adjust to the constant, ambient warmth. The air is thick with humidity and the scent of ozone and something faintly sweet, like metal and earth. The traveler must be prepared for this, for it is not just a smell, but a sensation that lingers in the lungs and the mind.

Second, the mind must be prepared for the psychological weight of the place. There is no horizon in the traditional sense, only suggestions of distance. The eye is constantly deceived by depth and scale. This is a place that is designed to be *felt*, not just seen. The traveler should not expect to map it in their mind, but to *immerse* in it. They may need to accept that they will not understand it fully, that it will defy their usual ways of perceiving space and time. This is not a place of illusions, but of *realities* structured in ways that are alien to human intuition. A traveler should bring a journal, not just to record, but to *think*—to write down impressions, to test the limits of their own perception.

Third, one must be ready for the density of the place. Everything is close, layered, interwoven. There is no empty space, no open field. The traveler should be prepared for a sensation of being *surrounded*, not just by structures, but by the presence of the place itself. It is not a city, but something much more vast, more intricate. The traveler must be able to navigate through this density without becoming disoriented. A sense of direction is important, but not in the conventional sense. The traveler must develop a kind of spatial awareness that is more intuitive, more attuned to the subtle shifts in the environment. They may need to practice moving with slow, deliberate steps, listening to the rhythmic pulse of the place, sensing the gentle vibrations of the ground.

Fourth, the traveler should be ready for the light. It is bright, but not harsh. It is diffused, soft, and everywhere at once. It is not the light of the sun, but something else—something that seems to emanate from the world itself. This light may feel unsettling at first, as if it is not natural. The traveler may need to adjust their eyes, to learn how to see in a way that is more attuned to this kind of illumination. It is not just a visual experience, but a psychological one. The light may affect mood, perception, and even the sense of time. The traveler should be prepared for moments where they feel as if they are inside a dream, or a memory, or something that has always been there.

Fifth, the traveler must be ready for the sound. It is not loud in the way of noise, but it is present, constant, and reverberating. It is the sound of a place that is alive, that is breathing. It is a low hum, a kind of background vibration that is felt more than heard. This sound is not disruptive, but it is inescapable. The traveler should bring noise-canceling headphones, but also be prepared to *listen* to it. It is not just background noise—it is the heartbeat of the place. Learning to recognize the patterns in this sound may help the traveler feel more at home in the space.

Sixth, the traveler must be prepared for the fauna. These are not wild animals, nor are they domesticated. They are part of the system, part of the design. Some are mechanical, some are organic, some are neither. They move through the landscape with grace and purpose. The traveler should not be alarmed by their presence, but should observe them with curiosity and caution. They may be passive, but they are not to be ignored. They may be part of the place’s ecosystem, but they are not necessarily benign. The traveler should carry a small amount of food or water, not for themselves, but for the possibility of interaction—though it is unlikely that the fauna will be interested in human offerings.

Seventh, one must accept that this place is not meant for humans. It is not a place where one can simply walk in and expect to fit. It is a place that was built by something older, more patient, and more relentless than any human civilization. The traveler should approach with humility, with wonder, and with a willingness to be changed. This is not a place for conquest, for control, or for understanding in the way we usually understand. It is a place that resists explanation, that demands adaptation.

In short, the traveler must prepare for a journey not just through space, but through perception. They must be ready to let go of the familiar, to embrace the strange, and to walk through a world that is not made for them, but that is still open to them. This is not a place to be conquered, but to be *experienced*. And in experiencing it, the traveler may find that they are changed—not just by what they see, but by what they feel, what they remember, and what they carry with them when they leave.
