What lives here is not easily defined, for this place is not a home in the conventional sense. It is a space of half-formed things, a kind of liminal ground where the boundaries of life, structure, and function are not fixed but fluid, shifting like the mist that clings to the edges of the landscape.

The beings that dwell here are not human, though they may resemble them in some ways. They are made of parts—pieces of metal, wood, and flesh, fused together in a way that suggests not design but evolution. They move with slow, deliberate motions, their limbs creaking like old machinery, their eyes flickering with a pale, inner light. Some walk on two legs, their bodies hunched and worn, while others crawl or float on platforms of hollowed-out metal, their forms barely distinguishable from the structures they inhabit. They do not speak in words, but in vibrations and signals, in the hum of their bodies and the flicker of their lights.

They are not entirely alive, nor entirely dead. They are something in between, something that exists in the margins of existence. They are the remnants of an experiment, the byproducts of a construction that never quite finished. Some of them are built by hands long gone, left to rust and reform in the damp air. Others were born from the land itself, coalescing from the moisture and metal, rising like fungi from the earth where the structures meet the soil.

They do not live in communities in the way humans do, but they are not solitary, either. They cluster in small groups, huddled near the sources of warmth or light, their presence indicated by the soft glow of their bodies or the way the air around them seems to shimmer. They are quiet, but not silent. They make a low, constant sound, like the creak of a bridge in the wind, or the slow turning of a wheel. It is not a language, but it is a presence, a shared rhythm that binds them together.

There are no clear hierarchies, no leaders or rulers. They do not fight, but they do not cooperate in any structured way either. They are more like the landscape itself—unpredictable, yet persistent, moving in ways that are not always visible but are always there. They take what they need from the environment, from the water that pools in hidden places, from the fragments of old machines that lie scattered across the ground. Some of them are scavengers, others are builders, and some are just observers, watching the world change without ever fully engaging with it.

The creatures that move through the undergrowth are not entirely separate from them. They are smaller, more feral, their forms sleek and quick, their movements almost inhuman. They are not dangerous, but they are not friendly either. They watch from the shadows, their eyes reflecting the dim light, and sometimes they follow the larger beings, not out of fear, but out of curiosity.

And then there are the machines—those that move with a kind of mechanical grace, their forms sleek and smooth, their movements precise yet unerringly slow. They are not fully functional, but they are not broken, either. They drift through the landscape like ghosts, their purpose unclear, their presence unsettling in its quietness. They do not make noise, but they are always there, always watching, always waiting.

This place is not a home, but it is a habitat. It is a place where things are neither fully formed nor entirely abandoned. It is a place where life exists in fragments, where the line between organic and inorganic is not a boundary but a fluid thing, constantly shifting and reshaping. What lives here is not meant to be understood all at once, but to be felt—slowly, quietly, in the way that the mist clings to the ground, and the structures bend and shift in the damp air.
