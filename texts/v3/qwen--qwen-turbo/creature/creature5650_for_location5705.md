What lives in this place is not easily named. It is not a collection of creatures, but a living, breathing thing that has grown in on itself, layer by layer, until it becomes something that defies the categories we use to understand the world.

There are trees here, but they are not simply trees. They are great, slow-moving sentinels, their trunks thick with moss and lichen, their roots winding like veins through the silt and stone. Some of them have grown so low that their canopies form a ceiling, not just of leaves, but of a dense, living shroud that filters the light into golden threads. These trees do not just grow; they *hold* the air, the water, the very breath of the place. Their bark is etched with the slow, patient marks of time, and their branches seem to reach not just for the sky, but for something deeper, something unseen.

There are fungi here, too—giant, sprawling, their caps like cathedral domes, their stalks thick and sturdy. They are not merely decomposers; they are architects of the soil, weaving the earth together with their mycelium, creating a network that pulses with life. Some of them glow faintly in the dark, their bioluminescence a soft, greenish light that flickers like a heartbeat in the undergrowth. They are not passive. They are watching, sensing, responding. They are the quiet minds of the place, the ones who remember what the land has been and what it might become.

And then there are the creatures—those that move, those that hide, those that are neither wholly animal nor plant, but something in between. There are birds, yes, but not the kind you see in the cities. These are larger, more secretive, their feathers mottled with the colors of the swamps, their calls deep and resonant, echoing through the trees like a language only the place understands. There are insects, too—thousands of them, buzzing and humming, their wings catching the filtered light, their bodies shimmering with the moisture of the air. Some are large, almost like spiders, with wings that fold like origami, while others are tiny, almost invisible, darting between the roots and the leaves.

But what truly lives here is the place itself. It is not just a habitat, but a presence. It is the slow, patient growth of the roots that have taken over the stones, the way the water pools and shifts, the way the air is thick with the scent of algae and moss. It is the way the land feels settled, not in collapse, but in something deeper—something that has chosen to exist, to persist, to be. It is a world that has grown in on itself, that has turned inward, that has found a way to thrive where others would wither.

There are no roads, no signs of human hands, but the place is not empty. It is full, packed with life, with movement, with the quiet hum of things that are not seen but felt. It is a place of layers, of density, of growth that has found its way through the cracks of the old city, through the bones of the industrial past, into the present, into the future. It is a place that has learned to live without being seen, to grow without being noticed, to persist without being acknowledged.

And so, what lives here is not just the creatures or the plants, but the whole thing—this wet, tangled, living architecture, this place that has become a city of its own, a world that is neither fully natural nor fully built, but something in between. It is the kind of place that holds its breath, that waits, that grows, that survives. It is not a place of endings, but of continuations. And it is alive.
