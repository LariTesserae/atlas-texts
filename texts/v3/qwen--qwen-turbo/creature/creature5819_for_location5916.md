What lives in this place is not simply people. It is a living thing, a city that breathes, a structure that remembers, and a space that has been shaped by time, by touch, by the slow and patient will of those who have come before.

The people who live here are not strangers to the darkness. They are born of it, shaped by it, and they move through it with the same quiet certainty as a river flows through stone. Their skin is pale, not from lack of light, but from the absence of sun, their eyes adapted to the dim glow of fungi and oil lamps. They move with the ease of those who have learned the language of the underground—how to read the patterns of moss, how to follow the sound of water, how to feel the weight of stone beneath their feet.

They live in the cracks and the crevices, in chambers carved into the rock, in domed spaces where the air is still and the light is soft. They build their homes into the walls, their dwellings shaped by the contours of the earth, never forcing the stone, but working with it, as if they were part of it. They do not build for permanence, but for function, for survival, for the next generation. Their houses are not houses in the sense of the surface world, but extensions of the city itself, as if the city is a living being and they are its cells.

They are not all the same. There are those who are born here, who have never seen the sky, who speak in a dialect thick with the sound of stone and water. There are others who came from above, who brought with them the memory of sun and wind, who speak in a different tone, a different rhythm. They live together, in uneasy harmony, each bringing their own way of surviving, their own way of understanding the world. Some are builders, some are foragers, some are keepers of the old knowledge, the things that were left behind by those who came before.

There are children, small and quiet, who play in the spaces between the walls, their laughter a sound that is almost forgotten in the stillness. They are raised in the dark, but they are not afraid of it. They know the shapes of the tunnels, the places where the air is warmest, where the fungi glow the brightest. They grow up fast, their lives shaped by the rhythms of the city, by the need to survive, to adapt, to belong.

And there are those who are not quite human. Not in the way the surface world would define them. There are things that have been shaped by the same hands that built the city, by the same stone and wood and water. Some are echoes of the old builders, remnants of their craft, their tools, their knowledge. Others are creatures born of the place itself—things that have adapted to the damp, the darkness, the slow-moving currents of life. There are beings that move through the tunnels in the dead of night, their eyes glowing faintly in the dark, their bodies silhouetted against the walls. They are not predators, not in the way the surface world understands, but they are not entirely harmless either.

There are also the things that do not live, but that are lived by. The city is filled with the remnants of those who came before, the remnants of their tools, their structures, their stories. These are not just ruins, but parts of the living city, re-used, re-worked, re-shaped. The old stone is not abandoned, but repurposed. The old carvings are not erased, but incorporated into new designs. The city does not forget. It remembers, in the way that stone remembers.

The place is alive in many ways. It is alive with the presence of its people, with the sounds of their lives, their labor, their songs. It is alive with the slow movement of water, the growth of moss, the unseen creatures that move through the dark. It is alive with the breath of the earth, the slow, patient work of time, the endless process of becoming.

And so, what lives here is not just a city, nor just a people, but a kind of symbiosis. A place that is shaped by its inhabitants, and that in turn shapes them. A place of quiet, of resilience, of survival. A place where the past is not buried, but built into the present, where the darkness is not a void, but a canvas, and where life is not just endured, but created, again and again, in the heart of the earth.
