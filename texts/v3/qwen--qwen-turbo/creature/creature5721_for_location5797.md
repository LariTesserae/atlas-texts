What lives in this place is not easily seen, but it is certainly felt. It is not merely a collection of creatures or a settlement of the dead, but something deeper—something that has settled into the bones of the chasm like a memory that refuses to fade.

There are no people, not in the way you would recognize. But there are echoes of them, phantoms of a civilization that once thrived here. Their buildings remain, hollowed and weathered, their streets winding like veins through the stone. Some of the walls still bear the faint outlines of figures, as if they were carved into the rock and then somehow eroded away, leaving only the suggestion of presence. These figures are not human, but something between human and something else—tall, with elongated limbs and faces that are almost, but not quite, recognizable. They are the builders, the ones who shaped this place, and they have left behind their silence.

In the cracks of the stone, where brine pools like liquid gold, there are small, luminous growths—neither plant nor animal, but something in between. They pulse faintly, as if breathing, and they seem to react to movement, their glow shifting when you pass by. They are not sentient, but they are alive in a way that defies easy classification. They grow in clusters, clinging to the walls and the broken remains of structures, their surfaces slick with a viscous, iridescent fluid that glows faintly in the dim light.

The insect-like creatures are everywhere, but they are not insects in any conventional sense. They are small, no larger than a thumbnail, and their bodies are smooth and segmented, made of a hard, chitinous material that shimmers like polished bone. They move in slow, deliberate patterns, as though following some unseen rhythm. Their movements are not random, but purposeful, and they seem to be drawn to the luminous growths, clustering around them in small, writhing masses. Their eyes are large and reflective, and they do not blink. They watch you, or at least they seem to, their dark, glassy surfaces catching the amber light and reflecting it back in a way that feels almost like recognition.

Beneath the surface, where the heat rises like breath from the earth, there are things that do not move. They are not alive, but they are not dead either. They are structures—some natural, some built—buried deep in the earth, their forms obscured by layers of dust and time. They are not ruins in the way you might expect, but something more like remnants of a dream. Some are smooth and rounded, as if shaped by water and time, while others are jagged and angular, as if torn from the earth by some ancient force. They do not emit sound, but they vibrate, faintly, like the hum of a distant bell.

The place itself seems to be watching, though it has no eyes. It is not alive, but it is aware in a way that is difficult to describe. It is a place that remembers, that holds onto the past like a weight in the chest. It is not malevolent, nor benevolent, but it is present. It is the silence between sounds, the space between thoughts. It is the deep, slow rumble of stone shifting, of time unspooling, of something waiting.

And in that waiting, something stirs. Not a creature, not a voice, but a presence—something that has been here longer than the chasm itself, longer than the stone, longer than the light that filters down from above. It does not move, but it is there. It is the reason the place exists. It is the reason the dust clings, the reason the brine pools, the reason the creatures move in their slow, rhythmic way.

It is not a god, not in the way you might imagine. It is not a being, not in the way you understand. It is a memory, a resonance, a vibration in the bones of the earth. It is what remains when everything else has been worn away. And it is watching.
