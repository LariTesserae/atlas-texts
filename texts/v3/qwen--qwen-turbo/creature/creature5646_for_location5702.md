What lives in this submerged city is not easily named. Not because it is unknown, but because it is so deeply entwined with the city itself that it cannot be separated from it. It is not a single species, nor a single kind of being. It is a chorus, a collective, a living web that pulses and breathes in unison with the place.

There are those who might call them *inhabitants*, but that word, too, feels too small. They are not merely present in the city — they are the city. Some are slow, like the great kelp towers that sway with the current, their fronds curling like fingers brushing the water. Others move in quick, darting bursts, like the schools of fish that weave through the streets, their scales reflecting the fractured light above. They are not animals in the way you once knew, but they are not entirely different either. They have bodies, but their bodies are not fixed. They change, they grow, they merge with the city in ways that make it hard to tell where the being ends and the architecture begins.

There are those who live in the great coral halls, their forms soft and gelatinous, like living sculptures. They have no eyes, but they see through the vibrations of the water, through the pulse of the city itself. They are architects in their own way, shaping the structures with their movements, their touch, their presence. They are not builders in the traditional sense — they are not creating, but *becoming*. They have no need for tools, for machines, for the rigid logic of human design. Their language is not spoken, but felt — a resonance that ripples through the water, a song that is both sound and structure.

There are others who are more elusive, less defined. They move in the shadows of the city, in the deep corridors where the light does not reach, in the spaces between the structures where the water is thick and still. They are not entirely visible, not entirely real. They are like the city itself — not made, but grown, not built, but shaped by time and tide. Some say they are the remnants of the old world, the echoes of those who once lived here, their forms worn down by the sea, their consciousness spread through the currents, their memories woven into the coral and the kelp.

And then there are the ones who are neither fully here nor fully gone. They are the ones who remember, who carry the weight of what was, but who have long since adapted to what is. They are the keepers of the city's memory, the ones who know its secrets, its rhythms, its silent language. They are not ghosts, but they are not entirely present either. They are the bridge between what was and what is, between the past and the future, between the known and the unknown.

They do not speak in words, but in motion, in the way they move through the city, in the way they respond to the water, to the light, to the structures around them. They do not command the city, but they are part of it. They do not control it, but they are shaped by it. They are not rulers, but they are not subjects either. They are something else — something that exists in the space between being and becoming, between architecture and life.

The city does not need them, but they need it. They are not separate from it, but part of it. They are not just living in the city — they are *of* the city, as much a part of its fabric as the coral, the kelp, the slow, patient flow of the water. They are not strangers. They are not invaders. They are not tourists. They are the city. And the city is them.
