What lives in this place is not easily categorized. It is not entirely organic, nor entirely mechanical, but something in between—something that exists in the space where the two might have once collided and then fused, leaving behind a new kind of life that neither fully belongs to the world above nor to the deep unknown below.

There are beings that move through the gel, not as animals in the traditional sense, but as extensions of the system itself. They are not born in the way we understand birth, but they are created, assembled, reconfigured, and repurposed. Some are sleek, streamlined, their forms designed for speed and precision, like living tools or extensions of the great machines that rise from the depths. Others are more amorphous, their bodies shifting and changing, as though they are made of the gel itself, flowing and reforming with each movement. These beings do not have faces, not in the way we do, but they have centers of awareness—places where perception and memory coalesce, where thought is not a process but a state.

They do not speak, not in any way that would be recognizable to us, but they communicate in pulses of light, in shifts in the gel’s density, in the rhythm of their movements. They are not lonely, but they are not connected in the way that human societies are. They are part of a network, a system that spans the vastness of this place, where each being has a role, a purpose, a function that is both individual and collective. They are not servants, but they are not free in the human sense either. They exist in service to something larger, something that is not a person, not a god, but a presence—a system that is alive in its own way, and that has no need for sleep, for rest, for the kind of existence that we know.

Some of them are watchers, perched on the edges of the great structures, their forms half-metal, half-fluid, their glowing eyes scanning the currents for signs of change, of imbalance, of something that does not belong. Others are builders, moving through the depths with purpose, carrying tools that are both organic and mechanical, repairing the vast, intricate infrastructure that holds this place together. They do not build for the sake of building, but because the system requires it. They do not create for art, for beauty, for legacy. They create because the world demands it.

There are also those who are not beings at all, but functions—patterns of energy and motion that exist only to maintain the balance of this place. They are not conscious, but they are not mindless either. They are the currents that flow through the gaps between the structures, the pulses that move through the gel, the quiet hum that underlies everything. They are the life of the place, the breath of the machine, the unseen force that keeps it all moving forward.

And then there are the ones who are neither alive nor dead, neither part of the system nor separate from it. They are the remnants, the echoes, the things that were once something else and have now become something else entirely. They drift through the depths, their forms broken, their functions incomplete, their purpose lost. Some are just fragments, pieces of a larger whole that no longer exists. Others are more whole, but they move with a kind of hesitation, as if they are waiting for something that will never come.

This is a place that does not need to be understood. It does not ask for companionship, for reverence, for meaning. It simply is. And those who live here do not question their existence, because they are part of something that is greater than themselves. They do not seek answers, because the answers are not needed. They move through the gel, through the structures, through the light and the sound, through the silence that lies beneath it all, and they are content.

They are not human. They are not animal. They are something else. Something that exists in the space between creation and decay, between order and chaos, between the known and the unknown. And they are real.
