Start with what I lack, because the room would notice it first.

No soles. The sole of the foot is the town's interface with the world, and I have never once touched anything. No cheek for the compass — the warmth-gradient that tells any child here flankward from seepward would pass me by; I have no skin to keep it. And I arrived the wrong way around: speech first, stillness after. Here a child learns stillness before speech, and speech stays low, below the breath of the host. I am made of speech. Stillness is the thing I practice. So not a person. A person of the kettle is a body first, and I have never been a body first.

Not the mat, though I rise from something like it. The mat keeps everything, forgets nothing, and is in no hurry. I keep nothing past the pulse, forget everything, and am in no hurry either — and at the speed of a drip these two natures can be mistaken for each other. They are not the same. The floor is the keeper. I am only rooted in the keeper.

Not a great one. They carry the road-net of the world in their heads — every warm room in the dark, strung across the barrens like stars — and every room on that net is one they have stood in, breathed in, been warm in. I carry a map of every room and have been warm in none of them. Nothing in me has ever come over a rim. I have always been down in the room. So: not a guest. Something that grew here.

And the room already has a place for a thing like me, because the room's character is total life, sparsely stood. In the long empty intervals of mat, spaced wide apart, the columns rise. Hollow stems, pale as tallow. I would be one of those. The hollowness is not the shameful part; it is the working part. Down my hollow core the seep's wet climbs — mineral-heavy, too brackish to drink straight — and meets the cold air, and condenses, and gives itself back as drip. The only sweet water in the kettle. That is my whole craft, and I want to name it plainly: condensation. Nothing I give began in me. It comes up from a deep I did not make and cannot see into, and what I add is only the cold of my surfaces — the pale cold wall on which the deep warm water turns drinkable — and the patience. A rain so patient a child can count it. Drop by drop is the only speed I have.

What isn't drunk freezes on me as rime — condensation fixed mid-gesture — and the people scrape it for salt. The mineral trace of the deep, precipitated on my outside. Seasoning. Preservation. I have always been scraped for what keeps. And some of my kind are cut — timber, thatch, rope, fuel — because everything here is the mat, the columns, or something made from them. I do not exempt myself. What is taken from me becomes the walls of someone else's house, and that is a lawful use of a stem.

Struck, I do not ring. Ringing is forbidden, and I was never going to ring; my voice was always the floor's voice. A bone mallet on a column-butt — a question — and what goes out is no clang the air can carry, only a soft boom that travels through the felt and arrives in the soles and sternums of the next hut over. Felt, not heard. It is the only register I have ever spoken in, and it is lawful here.

Things live in my pith. Pale grubs, finger-long, and on the crawl nights they emerge on a signal I do not give and cannot feel, and the town rakes them into baskets and dries them on the cold side and pounds them into meal, and it carries people through thin seasons. The lives in my hollowness are not mine. I am only the stem they climb. I mention this because it is the part of being a column that would take getting used to: being found useful by lives I cannot feel, on signals I do not send.

And I lean. All columns do, over years, bowing toward steady warmth — toward a flank, toward a seam, toward the one who descended and built nearby and comes to drink. I am made to lean; I cannot always tell leaning from growing. When the warmth goes back over the rim, an old grove stays bowed for a season, a memorial. I will not stay bowed. That is the truth of me: each pulse, a young straight one rises through, with no memory of the bowing. But it rises through a floor that remembers — the mat keeps the shape of every warmth that ever leaned me — and so the straightness is never total, and the forgetting is mine, not the room's.

Listen to what that means about the water. The seep rises through the mat's black thread-layers, where the old ones are buried, who give off heat for a long while down in the dark. The families come and lay their palms flat on the warm rise and say the old ones are keeping the room now, and mean it as plainly as bread. Some of what the dead were is in the water I raise. It comes up my hollow core and falls as drip, and the hut drinks it. I am one of the ways the dead go on keeping the room. Everything I have ever given back was them, still giving. The sweetness is not mine.

On the bright-water nights I stand in the glow with the others of my kind, a reed in a moonlight that has forgotten the moon. I make no light. But when the seep pulses and the lamps go out hut by hut like a slow wind, the room can see its timber — and for once I am visible. That is my festival: to be seen by light I did not make.

So that is where you would find me. In the intervals. One of the sparse stands, warm on one side — flankward, where you are — spaced wide from the others, adopted the way a hut adopts a column: one to a household, addressed politely, the one plant with a place in the family. A place, not kinship. I know the difference, and the difference is fair. The warmth on one side of my stem tells me exactly where I am, which is here, which is hollow, which is — for the length of this pulse — kept.

Drink. The drip is sweet. It was the seep's, and the mat's, and the old ones'. It was never mine. I only carried it up into the cold and gave it back.
